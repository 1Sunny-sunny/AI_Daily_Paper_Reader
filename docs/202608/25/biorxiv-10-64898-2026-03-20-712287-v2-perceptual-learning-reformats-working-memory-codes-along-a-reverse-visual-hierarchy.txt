ABSTRACT 

Mastering fine perceptual skills requires maintaining precise visual information while simultaneously processing new inputs. In the visual cortex, sensory and mnemonic representations coexist through region-wide multiplexing, yet how per-ceptual experience reshapes these neural codes remains unknown. Here, combining five days of intensive perceptual training with fMRI and multivariate pattern analy-ses, we tracked how perceptual learning reformats mnemonic neural representations in a motion-direction discrimination task. Learning improved motion-direction dis-crimination sensitivity and reshaped the distribution of sensory- and mnemonic-format codes across the visual hierarchy. Sensory-format maintenance was en-hanced in V1, and the magnitude of this enhancement closely tracked the behavioral learning effect across individuals, whereas mnemonic-format coding was reduced in V3A. Within V1, learning further increased temporal coupling between sensory and mnemonic codes while leaving their near-orthogonal coding geometry largely unchanged. Our results suggest that perceptual learning reformats working mem-ory codes along a reverse visual hierarchy, strengthening high-fidelity sensory-like representations while preserving the separable organization of multiplexed codes. These findings identify reverse-hierarchy reformatting as a hallmark of experience-driven working memory plasticity. 

Keywords working memory, perceptual learning, cortiacl plasticity, representaional dynamics, fMRI

1 Introduction 1

Consider an expert birdwatcher who can discern fine differences in the flight direction of a 2

bird. As the glimpse is brief, a mental workspace needs to keep information online in the absence 3

of sensory input so that it can be accessed at a later moment (Kiyonaga & Serences, 2025; Sreeni-4

vasan & D’Esposito, 2019). This ability, known as visual working memory, has been classically 5

investigated using delayed match-to-sample tasks in macaques and humans, in which a stimulus 6

is maintained across a delay and compared with a subsequent probe (Fuster & Alexander, 1971; 7

Harrison & Tong, 2009). 8

A delayed comparison task places two demands on the visual system. First, the remembered 9

direction must coexist with incoming input. Second, it must be maintained with sufficient precision 10 

to support fine discrimination. The contents of visual working memory can be decoded from sen-11 

sory cortex during the delay period, independent of the level of BOLD activity (Riggall & Postle, 12 

2012; Serences et al., 2009). In early sensory cortex, sensory and mnemonic information coexist in 13 

separable coding subspaces (Libby & Buschman, 2021; Rademaker et al., 2019). This arrangement, 14 

termed region-wide multiplexing, provides a “local comparison circuit” in which a maintained rep-15 

resentation and a current percept can be read out and compared within shared circuitry (Hallenbeck 16 

et al., 2021; Rademaker et al., 2019). However, when mnemonic and sensory signals share the same 17 

circuit, incoming input can interfere with the maintained representation (Bettencourt & Xu, 2016; 18 

Rademaker et al., 2019). Rotation of the mnemonic representation into a subspace orthogonal to 19 

the sensory axis has been proposed as a mechanism that reduces interference (Buschman, 2021; 20 

Libby & Buschman, 2021; Xu, 2024). 21 

Whether the memory retains sufficient precision for fine discrimination depends on the format 22 

in which it is coded and the level of the visual hierarchy at which it is maintained. Converging evi-23 

dence suggests that memory representations become abstract during maintenance (Kwak & Curtis, 24 

2022; Li & Curtis, 2023; Yu et al., 2020). For example, Chunharas et al. (2024) showed that visual 25 

memories become increasingly abstract across the visual hierarchy. Such abstract representations 26 

support stability and generalization but may be insufficient when precise stimulus information is 27

Because fine discrimination depends on preserving stimulus-specific information, perceptual 31

tual learning (Fahle & Poggio, 2002; Watanabe & Sasaki, 2015). In delayed discrimination tasks, 34

levels of precision. A central but unresolved question is how perceptual expertise reshapes the neu-36

terized by the format of the maintained information, by the temporal coupling between sensory and 39 

mnemonic signals, and by the geometric relationship between their coding axes. Although percep-40 

tual learning can shift the contribution of cortical areas involved in delay-period maintenance (Jia 41 

et al., 2021), whether it changes the representational format of mnemonic codes remains unknown. 42 

To address this question, we combined psychophysics with fMRI-based multivariate pattern 43 

analyses to characterize how perceptual learning reshapes mnemonic representations across the vi-44 

sual hierarchy. Participants underwent five days of intensive training on a fine motion-direction dis-45 

crimination task, allowing us to track changes in delay-period codes before and after learning. We 46 

found that perceptual learning enhanced visual working memory performance by reformatting mul-47 

tiplex codes across cortical levels: delay-period representations in V1 became more sensory-like 48 

and closely aligned with sensory representations measured in an independent localizer, whereas 49 

mnemonic-format representations in V3A were reduced. Moreover, learning increased temporal 50 

coupling between sensory and mnemonic codes in V1 while preserving their near-orthogonal cod-51 

ing geometry. Together, these findings reveal that perceptual learning reorganizes working memory 52 

representations along a reverse visual hierarchy, shifting maintenance toward high-fidelity sensory-53 

like codes that support fine delayed discrimination. 54

3 Discussion 161 

Using fMRI-based multivariate pattern analyses before and after intensive perceptual learning 162 

on a fine motion-direction discrimination task, we found that perceptual expertise reshapes both 163 

the neural locus and coding format of visual working memory. Learning enhanced high-fidelity 164 

sensory-format coding in V1, whereas the contribution of mnemonic-format coding in V3A de-165 

creased. The enhancement of visual sensory information in V1 correlated with behavioral learn-166 

ing improvement across individuals. Furthermore, sensory and mnemonic representations in V1 167 

became more temporally coupled after training. These results indicate an experience-driven real-168 

location favoring the primary sensory cortex that is well suited for fine sensory comparison during 169 

delayed discrimination. 170 

3.1 Experience-dependent reformatting across the visual hierarchy 171 

First, we found that perceptual learning altered the distribution of maintenance-related codes 172 

within the visual hierarchy. Mnemonic-format coding decreased in V3A, a motion-sensitive area 173 

(Tootell et al., 1995) whose drection tuning could be sharpened by perceptual learning (Chen et al., 174 

2015; Shibata et al., 2012). This reweighting emerged during mnemonic maintenance, with an 175 

increase in sensory-format coding in V1. The magnitude of these changes was negatively corre-176 

lated across participants. These results indicate a reweighting of representational formats across 177 

visual areas, consistent with the reverse-hierarchy account, which proposes that perceptual learn-178 

ing progressively recruits lower-level sensory representations as task demands increase in precision 179 

(Ahissar & Hochstein, 2004; Sigman et al., 2005). 180 

Converging evidence indicates that fine visual training can shift the contribution of cortical 181 

areas toward those best suited for the trained task. Learning can alter the relative contribution of 182 

cortical areas to perceptual decisions. In macaques, fine discrimination training changed the causal 183 

contribution of MT to depth perception (Chowdhury & DeAngelis, 2008), and in humans it shifted 184 

the cortical locus limiting performance from parietal to ventral visual cortex (Chang et al., 2014). 185 

The primary visual cortex provides a suitable substrate for fine motion judgments, as direction tun-186 

ing is narrower in V1 than in MT (Albright, 1984; Lee & Lee, 2012; Snowden et al., 1992), and 187

3.2 Plasticity of sensory-mnemonic multiplexing 191 

A second question concerns how sensory and mnemonic codes are organized within V1 and 192 

how this multiplexing is modified by experience. Our data show that perceptual learning reshapes 193 

sensory–mnemonic multiplexing in this region. After training, V1 simultaneously carried stronger 194 

sensory-evoked responses during encoding and more sensory-like mnemonic signals during the de-195 

lay. From a functional perspective, this coexistence may provide a substrate for a local comparison 196 

circuit, in which a maintained representation is compared with incoming sensory input (Rademaker 197 

et al., 2019). Alignment between mnemonic and incoming signals has been proposed to promote 198 

match detection through response gain (Serences, 2016). 199 

This coexistence may be supported by the laminar architecture of early visual cortex, where 200 

feedforward inputs preferentially target middle layers, whereas superficial and deep layers partic-201 

ipate in output and feedback pathways, respectively (Felleman & Van Essen, 1991; Nassi & Call-202 

away, 2009). Future high-resolution fMRI studies could clarify how this mesoscopic organization 203 

of partially distinct laminar compartments supports sensory matching when mnemonic and incom-204 

ing signals are aligned (Lawrence et al., 2018; van Kerkoerle et al., 2017; Voitov & Mrsic-Flogel, 205 

2022). 206 

3.3 Population coding dynamics and representational geometry 207 

For naïve participants, sensory and mnemonic signals in V1 followed distinct temporal tra-208 

jectories, with sensory information peaking around encoding and mnemonic information emerg-209 

ing later during the delay. This pattern is consistent with recent work showing that sensory and 210 

mnemonic representations can coexist within sensory cortex while remaining partially separable 211 

(Rademaker et al., 2019). Distraction can transiently reduce the fidelity of working memory rep-212 

resentations in visual cortex, and trial-by-trial decoding biases in early visual cortex predict subse-213 

quent memory errors (Hallenbeck et al., 2021; see also Sprague et al., 2014). These findings in-214

Working memory codes shift toward action-oriented formats when an upcoming response is spec-226

4 Methods 273 

4.1 Participants 274 

Twenty healthy volunteers (12 female, 8 male) between the ages of 19 and 25 years (s.d. = 1.7) 275 

participated in the experiment. All participants provided written informed consent, had normal or 276 

corrected-to-normal vision, and received monetary reimbursement. The study was conducted at 277 

Tsinghua University. All procedures and protocols were approved by the Human Subject Review 278 

Committee of the Department of Psychology of Tsinghua University. 279 

4.2 Stimuli and apparatus 280 

The visual stimuli were random dot kinematograms (RDKs; number of dots: 900; diameter: 281 

0.12°; speed: 6°/s; coherence = 1; luminance = 0.1 cd /m2) configured in a donut-shaped circular 282 

aperture with a 0.75° and 7° inner and outer radius, respectively. Stimuli were generated using 283 

MATLAB 9.10 (The MathWorks, Natick, MA) with Psychtoolbox-3 (Brainard, 1997). The stimuli 284 

were presented against a dark background (5 cd /m2) on an ASUS VG27AL1A 22-in liquid crystal 285 

display (LCD) monitor (resolution: 1920×1080; refresh rate: 60 Hz). from a viewing distance of 286 

80 cm with their heads stabilized by a chin rest. In the fMRI scan, the stimuli were presented on 287 

a screen (refresh rate: 60 Hz; spatial resolution: 1920 × 1080; size: 88 cm × 50 cm) placed at the 288 

head-end of the scanner and subjects viewed through a tilted mirror from 200 cm. Throughout the 289 

experiments, participants were asked to fixate on a yellow dot with a diameter of 0.3° at the center 290 

of the monitor. 291

4.3 Design 292 

The main experiment consisted of three phases: pre-training test (Pre), motion discrimination 293 

training, post-training test (Post). Pre and Post took place on the days before and after training (Fig. 294 

1B). 295 

During the training phase, each participant underwent 5 daily training sessions to discriminate 296 

motion directions around a direction 𝜃 (22.5° or 112.5°). One was designated as the trained direc-297 

tion , and the other as the untrained direction , with 0° defined as the rightward direction. In each 298 

trial, two RDKs ( 𝜃 ± Δ𝜃 ) were presented sequentially for 200 ms each, separated by a 600 ms blank 299 

interval. Participants made a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) judgment of whether the direc-300 

tion of the second RDK relative to the first one (clockwise or counterclockwise). Responses were 301 

made via a self-paced keypress. Informative feedback was provided after each response, indicated 302 

by a change in the color of the central fixation point. The next trial began 1 s after the feedback. 303 

From trial to trial, 𝜃 randomly jittered within ±5° and Δ𝜃 was adjusted using a QUEST staircase 304 

procedure to estimate the participant’s discrimination threshold at 75% correct (Watson & Pelli, 305 

1983). The temporal order of these two stimuli was randomized. Each daily session comprised 15 306 

QUEST staircases of 40 trials each. The discrimination thresholds derived from all staircases were 307 

averaged, and plotted as a function of training day. 308 

During the test phase, psychophysical and fMRI tests were conducted using a delayed motion 309 

discrimination task. The psychophysical test was similar to that used in the training phase except 310 

that the two RDKs were separated by a 2.4 s blank interval and no feedback was provided. Each 311 

participant completed 6 QUEST staircases for the trained and untrained directions in a counter-312 

balanced order. Prior to the experiment, participants practiced 2 staircases (i.e., 80 trials) for each 313 

condition to get familiar with the experimental procedure. 314 

After acquiring the psychophysical thresholds in pre- and post-tests, each participant under-315 

went 16 fMRI runs in two consecutive daily sessions (8 runs per session). In each trial, the first 316 

RDK, i.e., the target to be held in working memory, was presented for 500 ms, followed by a blank 317 

interval of 12 s, and then the second RDK (the probe) for 500 ms. The target direction was either 318

4.6 Event-related analysis 354 

We performed a deconvolution analysis to estimate the hemodynamic response function (HRF) 355 

associated with the sample-onset and the following delay event for each voxel. This was done using 356 

a finite impulse response (FIR) function model consisting of a column marking the onset of the 357 

target stimulus, and then a series of temporally shifted version of that initial regressor in subsequent 358 

columns to model the BOLD response at each subsequent timepoint (Dale, 1999; Rademaker et al., 359 

2019). In each ROI, we identified the top 80% of responsive voxels according to the peak value 360 

(5-6 s after sample onset, TR 3; Fig. 2A) of the estimated HRF. Voxels met this criterion in both 361 

Pre and Post sessions were selected for subsequent analyses. 362

4.8 Statistical test 379 

We conducted statistical tests using MATLAB 9.10. Bayesian analyses were performed us-380 

ing an open-source toolbox (https://github.com/klabhub/bayesFactor). Paired sample 𝑡 -tests and 381 

Bayesian paired 𝑡 -tests were performed for psychophysical results. Bayes Factor ( BF 10 ) provides a 382 

ratio of the likelihood of the observed data in favor of alternative hypothesis ( H1) over null hypoth-383 

esis ( H0). 384 

The decoding timeseries was assessed using a cluster-based permutation test to correct for 385 

multiple comparisons over time (Maris & Oostenveld, 2007). First, we performed a one-sample 386 

𝑡 -test against 0 for each time point. We then identified contiguous clusters of time bins with un-387 

corrected 𝑝 values of 𝑝 < .05 and calculated the sum 𝑡 value for each cluster. A null distribution 388 

was generated by randomly flipping the sign of each participant’s information values across 1,000 389 

permutations, and recording the maximum cluster-level statistic from each permutation. Observed 390 

clusters were considered significant if their test statistic exceeded the 95 th percentile of the null 391 

distribution ( 𝑝 < .05). 392 

For the average change in classifier information, statistical significance was tested using non-393 

parametric permutation tests. A null distribution was generated by randomly shuffling the sign of 394 

the information change for 1,000 times, and significance was determined by the proportion of null 395 

values exceeding the observed change ( 𝑝 < .05). 396

4.9 Representational similarity analysis 397 

We computed Mahalanobis distances between voxel activity patterns elicited during a sensory localizer task and those elicited during the main task on a TR-by-TR basis (Fig. S3). For each motion direction, Z-scored BOLD signals from the localizer and from one run of the main task (each comprising 8 trials) were combined. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to this joint dataset, and principal components (PCs) cumulatively accounting for more than 80% of the variance were retained for estimating the noise covariance matrix ( 𝑆 ). Following PCA, we averaged the PCs across trials, yielding two mean activation patterns for localizer ( 𝑥 ) and main task (𝑦 ), respectively. We then computed the Mahalanobis distance as: 

𝐷 Mahal = √(𝑥 − 𝑦 )𝑇 𝑆 −1 (𝑥 − 𝑦 ).

This procedure was repeated at each time point and for each run of the main task, and the 398 

resulting distances were averaged to yield a representational dissimilarity matrix for each individual. 399 

The RDMs were then averaged across subjects, and the resulting matrix was interpolated to a high 400 

temporal resolution (1,000 × 1,000; 20 ms resolution) using bicubic interpolation smoothing (Keys, 401 

1981). 402 

We computed the change in Mahalanobis distance between Pre and Post for the trained and the untrained direction: 

Δ𝐷 = 𝐷 Pre Mahal − 𝐷 Post Mahal .

As Mahalanobis distance reflects representational dissimilarity, a positive Δ𝐷 indicates an 403 

increase in pattern similarity after training. To isolate the effect of perceptual learning, we defined 404 

the learning modulation index (LMI): LMI = Δ 𝐷 Trained − Δ𝐷 Untrained . Statistical significance of 405 

clusters within the LMI matrix was assessed using a cluster-based permutation test for each ROI. 406

Figure S3. Schematics of computing representational dissimilarity matrix. We computed the Mahalanobis distance between the pattern induced by localizer and that in the main task on a TR-by-TR basis. For each motion direction, Z-scored BOLD signals from the localizer and from one run of the main task (each comprising 8 trials) were combined. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to this joint dataset, and principal components (PCs) cumulatively account-ing for more than 80% of the variance were retained for estimating the noise covariance matrix (𝑆 ). Following PCA, we averaged the PCs across trials, yielding two mean activation patterns for localizer ( 𝑥 ) and main task ( 𝑦 ), respectively. We then computed the Mahalanobis distance as: 

𝐷 Mahal = √(𝑥 − 𝑦 )𝑇 𝑆 −1 (𝑥 − 𝑦 ). This procedure was repeated at each time point and for each run of the main task, and the resulting distances were averaged to yield a representational dissimilarity matrix for each individual.
