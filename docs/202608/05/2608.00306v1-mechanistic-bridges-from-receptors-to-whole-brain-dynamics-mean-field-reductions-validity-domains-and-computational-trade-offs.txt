Abstract – Many pharmacological and pathological mechanisms act at molecular, synaptic, or cellular scales, whereas the resulting phenomena of interest are often measured at the level of cortical populations and whole-brain recordings. This scale gap motivates reduced models that remain biologically interpretable while being tractable enough for whole-brain simulation, parameter exploration, and comparison with empirical signals. This review examines the receptor-aware whole-brain framework of Sacha et al. (2025) [1] as a representative example of such a mechanistic reduction strategy. We reconstruct the mathematical lineage leading to this model, from the master-equation formalism of El Boustani and Destexhe (2009) [2], through the semi-analytical transfer-function framework of Zerlaut et al. (2016) [3], the conductance-based cortical mean-field model of Zerlaut et al. (2018) [4], and the adaptive extension of Di Volo et al. [5], before reaching the connectome-coupled whole-brain implementation of Sacha et al. [1]. We make explicit the assumptions on which this reduction rests and give deliberate space to the derivation and interpretation of the equations , so that the whole-brain model is understood as the endpoint of an explicit chain of reductions and embeddings rather than as a black box. Going beyond a single lineage, we situate this framework within a broader landscape of reduced-population models and we survey the directions in which the lineage is now being extended, including heterogeneity, scientific machine-learning and data-driven surrogates. Finally, we introduce algorithmic simulation cost and memory traffic as explicit hardware-independent benchmark dimensions .Using a FLOP-equivalent and byte-traffic convention, we compare spiking networks, low-dimensional mean fields, adaptive master-equation models, local versus global second-order covariance closures, and learned surrogates. This comparison highlights that local mean-field models mainly differ by node-local constants and share a dense-coupling scaling, whereas global covariance propagation changes the scaling class and becomes prohibitive at whole-brain resolution. The review therefore frames receptor-aware whole-brain modeling as a trade-off between mechanistic transparency, biological detail, predictive flexibility, and computational burden.

1. Introduction 

Understanding how microscopic neural mechanisms give rise to large-scale brain activity remains one of the central challenges of computational neuroscience. At one end of the modeling spectrum, detailed spiking-neuron and conductance-based circuit models can represent membrane dynamics, synaptic ki-netics, adaptation, and cellular diversity with high biological realism. At the other end, macroscopic recordings such as EEG, MEG, fMRI, VSDi, or LFP report aggregated activity at the level of pop-ulations, regions, or whole-brain networks. The central difficulty is therefore not only to model the brain at one scale, but to build principled bridges between scales: from microscopic descriptors such as synapses and neurons to macroscopic effects of collective dynamics and empirical observables. This scale gap is especially important in pharmacology and brain-state transitions. Many interventions of clinical or theoretical interest act at the molecular or cellular level, for example by modifying excitatory or inhibitory receptor kinetics, spike-frequency adaptation, or neuromodulatory tone. Yet their observable consequences, such as slow waves, altered responsiveness, perturbational complexity, or changes in structure-function coupling, are often measured at the level of cortical populations and whole-brain recordings [1]. A useful model must therefore preserve enough microscopic structure to remain biologically interpretable, while being sufficiently reduced to be simulated, analyzed, and embedded into large-scale brain architectures. Direct microscopic simulation is not always the appropriate solution to this problem, especially be-cause of dimensions of the brain [6]. Large-scale spiking simulations are increasingly powerful and scientifically valuable, but they remain high-dimensional, computationally expensive, and difficult to invert or scan across parameter space. Reduced population models therefore occupy a central position in computational neuroscience. Depending on their assumptions, they replace explicit microscopic dy-namics by a small number of collective variables, such as firing rates [7], synaptic gating variables [8], mean activity and covariances [2], population densities [9, 10], or exact low-dimensional variables un-der specific assumptions [11]. Their cost is a loss of microscopic trajectory-level detail; their benefit is tractability, interpretability at the population scale, and compatibility with whole-brain modeling. The present review focuses on one particularly instructive realization of this program: the receptor-aware whole-brain framework proposed by Sacha et al. [1]. Their contribution is not to introduce an entirely new local mean-field theory, but to assemble previously developed components into a coherent multiscale architecture. Microscopic perturbations of synaptic receptors are mapped onto a conductance-based spiking circuit, reduced to an adaptive mean-field node, and finally embedded into a connectome-driven whole-brain model using The Virtual Brain [12]. This framework makes it possible to study how receptor-level manipulations, such as changes in GABAergic inhibition, NMDA-mediated excitation, or adaptation strength, reshape whole-brain dynamics associated with wakeful-ness, anesthesia-like states, and NREM-like slow-wave activity [1]. To understand this framework, one cannot start directly from the whole-brain implementation. Its key object, the adaptive mean-field node, is the endpoint of a theoretical lineage. El Boustani and Des-texhe [2] introduced a master-equation formalism for finite-size asynchronous irregular networks, yield-ing a mesoscopic description in terms of mean activities and covariances. Zerlaut et al. [3] addressed the neuron-transfer-function bottleneck by deriving semi-analytical firing-rate functions from membrane-potential fluctuation statistics. Zerlaut et al. [4] then integrated this strategy into a conductance-based cortical two populations mean-field model, and Di Volo et al. [5] promoted spike-frequency adaptation from a stationary ingredient of the transfer function to an explicit mesoscopic dynamical variable. Sacha et al. [1] inherit and combine these ingredients in a receptor-aware whole-brain model. This review has three aims. First, it clarifies the mathematical structure of this lineage by recon-structing the logic of the master-equation reduction and the assumptions that make it possible. Sec-ond, it situates this lineage within the broader landscape of reduced and surrogate models, including Wilson–Cowan-type neural masses, Wong–Wang reductions, Fokker–Planck and population-density ap-proaches, exact low-dimensional reductions, scientific machine learning, and data-driven whole-brain surrogates [7–11, 13]. Third, it introduces algorithmic cost and memory traffic as benchmark dimen-2Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

sions for mesoscopic and macroscopic models. This last point is important because different models may be comparable in predictive target but differ by orders of magnitude in simulation cost, memory traffic, and scalability. The review is organized as follows. We first introduce the broader modeling landscape and explain why reduced population models are especially relevant for receptor-to-whole-brain questions. We then summarize the Sacha et al. framework as a chain of assumptions, reductions, and embeddings. Next, we revisit the El Boustani–Zerlaut–Di Volo–Sacha lineage in detail, from the master-equation formal-ism to adaptive conductance-based mean-field nodes and receptor-aware whole-brain modeling. We then discuss limitations and future directions, including first-order truncation, heterogeneity, obser-vation models, plastic mean-field formalisms, scientific machine learning, data-driven surrogates, and benchmark design. Detailed mathematical derivations and algorithmic-cost estimates are provided in the appendices. 

2. Background: modeling scales and reduced-population approaches 

Mechanistic brain models can be placed along a continuum of spatial and dynamical resolution. At the microscopic end lie conductance-based and morphology-aware spiking-neuron models, which represent membrane dynamics, synaptic kinetics, dendritic geometry, and cellular diversity with high biological realism. At the mesoscopic end lie neural-mass, neural-field, population-density, and mean-field mod-els, which replace explicit spike-by-spike simulation by a small number of collective state variables. At the macroscopic end, such reduced nodes can be coupled through anatomical connectivity to form whole-brain dynamical systems. Each level serves a distinct purpose: detailed spiking models are indispensable for questions of dendritic integration, synaptic microstructure, spike timing, or the syn-thesis of electrophysiological signals, whereas reduced population models are preferable for systematic parameter exploration, bifurcation analysis, model inversion, and large-scale connectome embedding. No class is universally superior; each optimizes a different trade-off between realism, interpretability, and tractability. The framework of Sacha et al. [1] sits at the reduced end of this continuum: it embeds a biologically grounded, conductance-based mean-field node into a connectome-level whole-brain architecture. To situate it, this section does not attempt an exhaustive survey of reduced models, but is organized around three questions: why reductions remain necessary even as spiking simulations grow more powerful (Sec. 2.1); what the main families of reduced models are (Sec. 2.3); and why this review focuses specifically on the master-equation lineage (Sec. 2.4). 

2.1. What large-scale spiking network models already enable 

Detailed spiking models should not be dismissed as merely local toy systems. Driven by advances in high-performance computing, such as large CPU clusters, GPUs, or dedicated supercomputing resources, mechanistic simulations now reach organism-, microcircuit-, and even hemisphere-scale regimes. BAAIWorm couples a biophysically detailed model of a C. elegans sensorimotor circuit to a closed-loop body–environment simulation [14, 15]; in Drosophila , Shiu et al. [16, 17] built a brainwide leaky integrate-and-fire model from the FlyWire connectome ( ∼ 10 5 neurons, 5 × 10 7 synapses); and human-hemisphere models now combine layered cortical columns, diffusion-imaging-based long-range connectivity, millions of neurons and billions of synapses [18]. These simulations can generate synthetic data, test mechanistic hypotheses, validate analysis pipelines, and reveal how anatomical structure shapes dynamics. Yet they also expose the central practical limitation of explicit spiking approaches: even when feasible, they are computationally heavy, high-dimensional, difficult to invert, and poorly suited to repeated parameter sweeps or subject-specific fitting. The cost is concrete: the human-hemisphere model of Pronold et al. [18] requires on the order of 200 core-hours on a 768 -core machine to produce only 10 s of biological activity. For 10 min of biological activity, the biophysically detailed model of rat primary somatosensory cortex of Laquitaine et al. [19–21] requires approximately 6 days on large clusters containing 120 CPUs. The lesson for 3Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

this review is therefore not that spiking models are inadequate, but that their growing power does not remove the need for reduced descriptions: the challenge is to retain enough biological meaning to study receptor-to-whole-brain mechanisms while shedding the full cost of spike-by-spike simulation. Further detail is given in Appendix A.1. 

2.2. Why reduced population models are especially relevant here 

A conceptual motivation complements this computational one. Although an isolated neuron can be described by deterministic equations such as the Hodgkin–Huxley model, a neuron embedded in a cortical network is continuously driven by fluctuating synaptic bombardment and other microscopic sources of variability, and cortical activity displays substantial trial-to-trial variability [22]. At the network level, exact spike-by-spike prediction is therefore both computationally prohibitive and, in many contexts, less informative than the collective statistical behavior of populations. Reduced pop-ulation models exploit this directly: by deliberately discarding intrinsic single-neuron variability and trajectory-level detail, they describe a local circuit through a few mesoscopic variables, such as firing rate, mean membrane potential, aging, adaptation or synaptic gating, gaining tractability and a more natural interface with macroscopic observables. This interface matters because experimental signals are themselves aggregate quantities: EEG, MEG, LFP, VSDi, and BOLD do not record individual spikes but spatially and temporally pooled activity, filtered by the measurement process. Mesoscopic variables are thus closer to such signals than spike trains, even if an explicit observation model is still often required to map latent states onto data. This is precisely the regime in which the question of Sacha et al.[1] lives. Their goal is not to reproduce generic whole-brain dynamics, but to trace how changes in local physiological parameters reshape large-scale activity. These parameters include synaptic kinetics, receptor timescales, and adaptation strength, which are engaged by anesthesia, NREM sleep, and other brain-state transitions. This demands a model simpler than a full spiking connectome yet mechanistic enough to preserve a meaningful mapping from microscopic perturbation to macroscopic consequence. 

2.3. Canonical families of reduced population models 

Within this reduced regime, several distinct modeling families have been developed. They differ in their state variables, assumptions, biological ambitions, and mathematical convenience; for this review it is useful to distinguish five. These families are not mutually exclusive, nor do they partition the space along a single axis: some are distinguished by modeling philosophy (phenomenological versus mechanistic), others by mathematical formalism, and several are formally related (especially Fokker– Planck and master-equation descriptions). The grouping is therefore pragmatic, chosen to organize the landscape around the lineage of interest, not a claim that these are five orthogonal classes. 

2.3.1 Phenomenological neural-mass models: Wilson–Cowan 

Wilson and Cowan [7] proposed that macroscopic brain dynamics could be modeled by tracking the activity of interacting excitatory and inhibitory populations. The Wilson–Cowan model is phenomeno-logical (or heuristic): it is built top-down from population-level reasoning rather than derived directly from the biophysics of individual spiking neurons. The model describes excitatory and inhibitory populations through low-dimensional coupled ODEs, de-tailed in Appendix A.2, in which weighted inputs are passed through nonlinear gain functions Eq. (22). Its main strength is simplicity. It is analytically transparent, computationally cheap, and easy to embed into large-scale whole-brain architectures. However, its parameters are only indirectly related to cellu-lar biophysics. It is therefore useful when the goal is to study low-dimensional population dynamics, but less appropriate when one needs an explicit receptor- or conductance-level interpretation. 4Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

2.3.2 Biophysically motivated neural masses: Wong–Wang type reductions 

A second family, the Wong–Wang-type models [8], is more tightly tied to the underlying spiking dynamics. The reduction rests on a separation of timescales: because NMDA-receptor kinetics are much slower than AMPA, GABA, and spike generation, the fast variables are not tracked explicitly but replaced by their quasi-steady-state (adiabatic) values, leaving slow NMDA-mediated synaptic gating as the dominant collective variable. This is a deliberate simplification rather than an attempt to represent every channel: the aim is to retain only the slow variables that govern the relevant timescale. Through the mean-field reduction detailed in Appendix A.3, the high-dimensional spiking network is reduced to ODEs for synaptic gating variables, rather than only firing rates. These models retain more biological structure than Wilson–Cowan models and are particularly useful for slow phenomena such as decision-making, working memory, and fMRI BOLD modeling. Their main limitation is that the reduction is selective: it privileges slow synaptic variables and does not aim to preserve the full fluctuation structure of the microscopic network. 

2.3.3 Exact low-dimensional reductions: Montbrió–Pazó–Roxin 

A third family is represented by exact low-dimensional reductions, such as the Montbrió–Pazó–Roxin (MPR) formalism for globally coupled quadratic integrate-and-fire (QIF) neurons [11]. Under specific assumptions (e.g., QIF neurons, global coupling, a Lorentzian distribution of excitability, and the thermodynamic limit) the microscopic spiking network can be reduced exactly to two macroscopic ODEs for the population firing rate and mean membrane potential. The derivation is summarized in Appendix A.4. This makes the MPR framework especially attractive for bifurcation analysis and theoretical work, be-cause it preserves a direct mathematical link between microscopic spiking dynamics and low-dimensional population variables. Its limitation is not mathematical weakness, but model specificity. The exact closure relies on a particular neuron model, coupling structure, and current-based interactions; it there-fore does not automatically extend to more biologically detailed conductance-based or receptor-aware settings. Moreover, the QIF description is less realistic for spike initiation than exponential integrate-and-fire-type models, since Badel et al. [23] showed that the spike-initiating voltage rise is better described as exponential rather than quadratic. For these reasons, MPR is an elegant theoretical refer-ence point, but it is less directly suited to the receptor-aware whole-brain modeling strategy considered in this review. 

2.3.4 Population-density and Fokker–Planck approaches 

A fourth family does not posit a few macroscopic variables from the outset, but instead tracks the time evolution of a probability density over neuronal states, typically the membrane potential V , and in richer formulations additional variables such as adaptation, conductance, or refractory age. In this sense, Fokker–Planck and related population-density approaches are the most direct descendants of statistical mechanics in theoretical neuroscience. It allows one to shift from individual stochastic realizations to a probability density that evolves according to a deterministic linear evolution equation [24]. The underlying logic is that a neuron receiving many weak, approximately independent synaptic inputs can be treated, under the diffusion approximation, as a stochastic drift-diffusion process, yielding a Fokker–Planck equation for the density P (V, t ) from which stationary firing rates follow under suitable threshold-reset conditions. In sparse balanced networks, this description can be closed self-consistently to obtain stationary rates and state diagrams, as in the classical analysis of Brunel [9]. The same density can also serve directly as the dynamical variable of the population itself. Full derivations are given in Appendix A.5. The main strength of these approaches is that they remain close to the microscopic spiking dynamics and faithfully represent the stochastic nature of neuronal firing, while retaining rich information about 5Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

the population state [25]. Their main limitation is mathematical and computational: the models are partial differential equations with nontrivial threshold and reset boundary conditions, and they grow rapidly heavier once adaptation, conductance-based synapses, or additional state variables expand the state space. For this reason, later work has sought either to reduce Fokker–Planck descriptions to low-dimensional ODEs [13] or to develop alternative finite-size formalisms, such as the refractory-density approach of Schwalger et al. [10], which restores the finite-size fluctuations that the infinite-size Fokker–Planck limit discards. 

2.3.5 Master-equation-based stochastic population models 

The final approach we consider is the master-equation description of stochastic neural activity. As noted above, the Fokker–Planck and master-equation descriptions are not independent. Under the Kramers–Moyal expansion a Fokker–Planck equation arises as a second-order approximation of a master equation, so the two should be read as connected branches of a single stochastic, statistical-mechanical approach rather than as disjoint families. We nonetheless treat them under separate headings because they differ in their natural state space and in the regime they describe most naturally: continuous densities in the (often infinite-size) diffusion limit for Fokker–Planck, and discrete, finite-size population activity for the master equation. This makes master-equation the natural formalism when intrinsic finite-size fluctuations, rather than infinite-size mean dynamics, are the object of interest. The approach is older and broader than the specific lineage studied here. Ohira and Cowan [26] introduced a master-equation treatment of stochastic neurodynamics in which population activity is a stochastic process rather than a deterministic rate, and subsequent field-theoretic and system-size expansion methods derived finite-size corrections, correlations, and higher-order fluctuations around deterministic neural-activity equations [27–29]. Across these formulations the state variable may denote binary activity, the fraction of active neurons, spike counts, or spatially distributed neural-field activity; in the large-N limit they typically recover deterministic rate or neural-field equations, while at finite 

N they retain variability through fluctuation and covariance terms. The El Boustani–Destexhe formalism is one specific biologically grounded branch of this broad stochastic-population tradition. It writes a master equation for finite-size asynchronous irregular population activity, derives equations for the mean activity, the covariance matrix, and stationary lagged corre-lations, and closes the system with a single-neuron transfer function, the input-output map giving a neuron’s stationary firing rate as a function of the statistics of its synaptic input (treated in detail in Sec. 4.3.1). This closure links the mesoscopic activity variables to microscopic neuronal biophysics. In the taxonomy of deterministic, stochastic, and mean-field PDE models [25], the resulting description sits closest to deterministic ODE-based population models, since the final object is a closed set of ordinary differential equations for the mean activity and covariance. Its distinctive feature, relative both to the other master-equation methods above and to the Fokker–Planck family, is the pairing of finite-size master-equation dynamics with semi-analytical transfer functions derived from conductance-based neurons, which is what later makes the conductance-based, adaptation-aware, and whole-brain extensions of Zerlaut, Di Volo, and Sacha possible. The remainder of this review follows precisely this branch. 

2.4. Why this lineage, and the scope of the review 

Against this landscape, the focus of the review can be stated precisely. We do not claim that the El Boustani–Zerlaut–Di Volo–Sacha lineage is the only route to whole-brain modeling, nor that it is superior to other approaches; nor is this a survey of the entire master-equation tradition, which also encompasses stochastic neural-mass, neural-field, and field-theoretic methods. Each alternative is preferable in its own setting: Wilson–Cowan for simple phenomenological dynamics, Wong–Wang when slow synaptic gating dominates, Fokker–Planck and population-density methods for statistical-mechanical rigor, and MPR for exact low-dimensional dynamics within its assumptions. The goal is therefore not to establish a universal hierarchy of models, but to use this lineage as a structured pathway to understand how receptor-aware whole-brain models are built through successive reductions, 6Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

approximations, and embeddings. This approach allows us to identify the promises and limitations of a strategy that is more biologically grounded than phenomenological neural masses, yet more scalable than explicit spiking simulations. Two features make it especially instructive. First, it makes the assumptions of the reduction unusually explicit: coarse-grained Markovianity over a finite time bin, quasi-stationarity of the local response, conditional independence across the population, diffusion-like synaptic bombardment at the single-neuron level, and Gaussian closure of mesoscopic fluctuations. Fokker–Planck theory still appears, but only at the single-neuron level, where it motivates the approximate transfer functions that are then inserted into the mesoscopic master equation. Second, through the semi-analytical transfer-function strategy of Zerlaut et al., the local node moves beyond current-based LIF models toward conductance-based, adaptation-aware dynamics while remaining interpretable in terms of synaptic and cellular parameters and compact enough for connectome embedding. One caveat should be stated plainly. The whole-brain frameworks of Di Volo et al.[5] and Sacha et al.[1] do not retain the full second-order formalism; for tractability they rely mainly on first-order adaptive dynamics. The value of the master-equation derivation is therefore not that its covariance structure survives intact to the whole-brain scale, but that it supplies the mathematical genealogy of the reduced node, clarifying what the mesoscopic derivation gains, what first-order truncation discards, and when that simplification is acceptable. This sets up the detailed reconstruction that follows. 

3. Overview of the modeling chain: assumptions, reductions, and limitations 

The whole-brain framework of Sacha et al. [1] can be understood as a sequence of successive reductions and embeddings, from microscopic neuronal biophysics to macroscopic brain activity. The aim is not to simulate every neuron of the brain, but to construct a mechanistic bridge from microscopic cellular and synaptic properties to macroscopic whole-brain activity: the model must therefore preserve enough biophysical detail to remain interpretable while being low-dimensional enough to scale to many brain regions. Concretely, individual neuron dynamics are replaced by the collective dynamics of P

populations, each comprising Nα neurons. Each step below relies on specific assumptions that make the model tractable but also restrict the regimes in which it should be trusted; we state each step in compact form, with its assumptions and limitations made explicit. 1. Operating regime: the asynchronous irregular state. The El Boustani–Destexhe formal-ism [2] is motivated by asynchronous irregular (AI) states, in which firing is irregular, synchrony is weak, and population autocorrelations decay exponentially. This is the regime in which pairwise correlations are weak, conditional independence is a reasonable approximation, the Markovian coarse-graining is plausible, and the stationary activity distribution is close enough to Gaussian to justify a second-order closure. Because AI dynamics are fluctuation-driven, the framework de-scribes the network not only by the mean activities ⟨mμ⟩ but also by their covariance matrix cμν ;the second-order terms are useful precisely when finite-size fluctuations and covariances carry dy-namical information, rather than being strictly necessary in all regimes. Although the formalism is derived under the AI assumption, the resulting equations form a dynamical system that can be integrated outside its original derivation domain. The framework is most justified in AI regimes but can be used beyond them, especially once adaptation is made explicit (step 7); its accuracy is nevertheless expected to degrade when synchrony becomes strong, when activity becomes very sparse, or when the diffusion and Markovian approximations are substantially violated. 2. Mesoscopic state variables. Individual neuron dynamics are replaced by the collective be-haviour of the P populations μ ∈ J1, P K, each described by its mean population activity ⟨mμ⟩

and, in the second-order formalism, the covariance matrix cμν , and later by slow variables such as the population adaptation current Wμ(t). Tracking covariances is informative in the AI regime because fluctuations are not mere noise: they help sustain the dynamical state and carry infor-mation. 3. The exponentially decaying autocorrelation of AI states erases microscopic memory on a timescale that motivates the choice of T . This choice is the most critical free parameter of the model, subject to two competing constraints: 

• Lower bound. T must be large enough that spike-to-spike temporal correlations (e.g., re-fractory or synaptic-delay correlations) are negligible, so that the Markovian assumption holds and discrete spikes are smoothed into a continuous rate consistent with single-neuron Fokker–Planck theory.

The model further assumes time invariance: transition probabilities depend only on the elapsed time T , not on absolute clock time, which requires the neuronal transfer functions to be stable on the modeled timescale (no plasticity or non-stationarity). The master equation does not break without this assumption, but relaxing it would substantially complicate the model. 4. Local circuit model: a biophysically grounded node, used as calibration reference. 

The local circuit is instantiated as a detailed spiking network, for instance an asymmetric two-population RS-FS AdEx circuit with conductance-based AMPA/NMDA/GABA A synapses. The transfer functions Fμ are fitted on single-neuron simulations using a second-order polynomial effective threshold, and the resulting local mean-field model is then calibrated and validated against full spiking-network simulations before being scaled up. Crucially, the spiking network is therefore used as a calibration and validation reference, not as a component of the final whole-brain simulator: once the mean-field node is validated, the spiking network plays no further role in the large-scale dynamics. 9. Scaling to a whole-brain network. Once validated locally, the adaptive mean-field circuit becomes the node model of a larger network of K nodes. In Zerlaut et al.[4] such nodes were arranged on a one-dimensional cortical ring with lateral connectivity and delays to reproduce propagating VSDi waves; in Sacha et al. [1] the same logic was scaled to the whole brain by embedding adaptive mean-field nodes into The Virtual Brain [12], with 68 cortical regions coupled through empirical structural connectivity and conduction delays. Long-range coupling is assumed purely excitatory, while inhibition remains local to each node. At this scale the second-order covariance equations are dropped, and only the first-order dynamics are retained (Sec. 9.1). Propagating covariances between all population variables of the network would require an O(P 2K2) covariance matrix and an O(P 3K3) update cost (Sec. 10.2), which is prohibitive at whole-brain resolution; even the cheaper node-local covariance closure raises the per-node cost without commensurate benefit once the targeted observables are dominated by first-order activity rather than finite-size fluctuations. The truncation is thus defensible in this regime, and the external Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise partly compensates for the unresolved fluctuations. 9Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

A further simplification is that all 68 nodes share the same transfer function, with no region-specific biophysical specialization. 10. Molecular-to-macro mapping and observation model. A key strength of the framework is that pharmacological and physiological states map onto specific model parameters: τi ↑ for GABAergic anesthesia (propofol), τe ↓ for NMDA antagonism (ketamine), and be ↑ for NREM sleep (reduced cholinergic tone). The mean-field variables (νe, ν i, W ) are not themselves observ-able signals, so a final observation model is required. For BOLD/fMRI, the excitatory firing-rate time series is convolved with a hemodynamic response function via the Balloon–Windkessel model; for VSDi, the mean membrane-potential deflection δV N is used directly; for complexity measures such as the PCI, a localized perturbation is applied and its spatiotemporal propagation is quantified. Each observation model introduces additional assumptions that are external to the mean-field derivation itself. The framework is therefore best viewed as a tractable and interpretable approximation chain rather than an exact derivation of brain dynamics. The following sections unpack this chain step by step: we first derive the master-equation formalism, then examine the semi-analytical transfer function, adaptation, and whole-brain embedding, before returning to the limitations and possible extensions of the framework. 

4. Master Equation formalism for finite-size mesoscopic dynamics 

El Boustani and Destexhe (2009) [2] provide the theoretical starting point of the lineage studied in this review. Their goal is to describe asynchronous irregular cortical activity at a mesoscopic scale, between microscopic spiking simulations and macroscopic neural-mass models. The key idea is to model the population activity of finite-size networks as a stochastic process governed by a master equation. In contrast with first-order rate models, this formalism explicitly keeps track not only of mean population activity, but also of finite-size fluctuations, covariances, and temporal correlations. This contribution is important because it gives a principled way to derive population-level equations from microscopic spiking probabilities, under assumptions of coarse-grained Markovianity, conditional independence, quasi-stationarity, and large but finite population size. Additionally, it separates the general mesoscopic formalism from the specific neuron model: once a single-neuron transfer function is known, the same population-level equations can in principle be applied to different microscopic models. However, this generality comes at a cost. The predictive power of the formalism depends strongly on the quality of the transfer function, which is easier to obtain for current-based integrate-and-fire models than for more realistic conductance-based networks. Moreover, the approach is primarily justified in asynchronous irregular regimes, where pairwise correlations are weak and population activity distribu-tions are close to Gaussian. The following subsections reconstruct the mathematical assumptions and derivation underlying this formalism. 

4.1. Asynchronous irregular states and the need for second-order statistics 

In awake, behaving animals, cortical activity is characterized by considerable subthreshold fluctuations of the membrane potential and highly irregular, asynchronous spiking [2]. It is widely believed that it is within this dynamic, noisy regime that the brain performs its primary computational tasks. In computational neuroscience, this behavior corresponds to the Asynchronous Irregular (AI) state, a regime typically exhibited in networks where dense recurrent connectivity creates a dynamic balance between excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs [9]. The AI state is of particular theoretical interest because its macroscopic characteristics closely replicate the spontaneous activity observed in the awake cortex. Dynamically, the AI state is defined by several core properties: very low average firing rates, highly irregular spike trains, and remarkably weak synchrony between individual neurons. Crucially for macroscopic modeling, the activity autocorrelation exhibits an exponential decay, allowing the system to be treated as a Markov process over specific time scales. Furthermore, when sampled over suitable 10 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

macroscopic time bins, the population activity distributions in the AI regime approximate a Gaussian profile, a statistical property that fundamentally justifies the use of second-order closures in mesoscopic models. Classical first-order population models, such as Wilson-Cowan equations (cf Appendix A.2), describe the evolution of mean population activity but do not explicitly track finite-size fluctuations or covari-ances. This can be sufficient for some regimes, but it is not ideal for AI states, in which activity is strongly fluctuation-driven and where population variance and correlation structure carry impor-tant dynamical information. El Boustani and Destexhe therefore proposed a second-order mesoscopic formalism in which the state of the network is described not only by the mean activity of each pop-ulation but also by their covariance matrix cμν introduced below. In this framework, fluctuations are not treated as negligible background noise; they are part of the dynamical mechanism sustaining and shaping the AI regime. 

4.2. Coarse-grained Markovian dynamics over a finite time bin 

Biological neural systems are not intrinsically memoryless. Refractoriness, membrane filtering, synaptic time constants, adaptation, and plasticity all introduce history dependence at their own characteristic timescales. However, a process can often be treated as approximately Markovian after an appropriate coarse-graining: if the observation window (T) is long enough compared with the relevant microscopic correlations, the future state can be approximated as depending mainly on the present coarse-grained state rather than on the full preceding history. In the master-equation formalism, this Markovian 1assumption is therefore not an absolute claim about neurons or networks, but a modeling approximation applied to the mesoscopic activity variables over a finite time bin ( T ). In AI states, population activity autocorrelations decay exponentially, making such a coarse-grained description plausible. The choice of ( T ) is crucial and has to be done on the order of the relevant network time constants: if ( T ) is too small, residual spike-history effects and correlations violate the approximation; if ( T ) is too large, relevant fluctuations are averaged out. The formalism is thus valid only on an intermediate timescale where microscopic memory has sufficiently decayed but mesoscopic dynamics are still resolved.

4.6. Limitation and Validation of the Framework: From Toy Models to spiking networks 

The derivation above provides a formal second-order mean-field description, but the framework also needs to be tested on explicit examples. El Boustani and Destexhe therefore validated the approach in two complementary settings: a linear toy model, which clarifies the role of finite-size fluctuations analytically, and spiking-neuron networks, which test whether transfer functions extracted from mi-croscopic dynamics can reproduce population-level behavior. 

Linear model: The linear model is not intended to be biologically realistic; it is a pedagogical benchmark for the master-equation formalism. Because the transfer function is linear, the mean dynamics decouple from the covariance, allowing the fixed point and its stability to be solved exactly. The model shows that the stability condition remains ∆ < 1, exactly as in the first-order analysis, but that finite-size fluctuations and correlation times become very large near the critical point ∆ = 1 . It also shows that these fluctuations vanish as N → ∞ , thereby recovering the first-order mean-field limit. Thus, the role of the toy model is not to reveal a new instability threshold, but to illustrate clearly how the second-order formalism captures finite-size effects around the classical mean-field critical point. More details are given in Appendix B.3.1. 

Spiking Neural Network: El Boustani and Destexhe then tested the formalism on explicit sparse excitatory/inhibitory spiking networks. The main question was whether a transfer function derived from microscopic neuron dynamics is sufficient to reproduce the mesoscopic behavior of recurrent populations. They considered two standard cases: current-based (CUBA) and conductance-based (COBA) integrate-and-fire networks. In the CUBA case, synaptic inputs are treated as Poisson shot noise, and under a diffusion approxi-mation, the membrane potential dynamics can be summarized by their mean and variance. This leads to a tractable Fokker-Planck-based approximation of the transfer function, which can then be inserted into the mesoscopic ODEs. The resulting mean-field predictions reproduce the activity statistics of simulated current-based networks over a broad region of the asynchronous irregular regime. This case therefore provides the cleanest demonstration of the micro-to-meso bridge: microscopic neuronal and synaptic properties determine a transfer function, and this transfer function determines the mesoscopic population dynamics. 17 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

In the COBA case, synaptic inputs act through conductance changes rather than additive currents. This is more biologically realistic, but mathematically harder, because no exact analytical transfer function is generally available. The authors therefore introduced an effective approximation and then improved it using a phenomenological transfer function fitted to simulations. This demonstrates both the flexibility and the limitation of the formalism: the mesoscopic equations can in principle accommo-date more realistic neuron models, but their quantitative accuracy depends on how well the transfer function captures the underlying microscopic dynamics. Taken together, these examples show that the master-equation framework is not tied to a single neu-ron model. The same mesoscopic ODE structure is preserved, while the neuron model enters through the transfer function and its derivatives. Current-based networks provide a relatively clean validation case, whereas conductance-based networks reveal the central bottleneck of the approach: once a re-liable transfer function is available, the framework can reproduce finite-size population dynamics at much lower computational cost than direct spiking simulation; when the transfer function is poor, the mesoscopic predictions degrade accordingly. More details are given in Appendix B.3.2. 

Limitations: The main limitation of the master-equation formalism is that its quantitative accuracy depends on the transfer function used to close the model. While this function can be derived or approx-imated relatively cleanly for current-based integrate-and-fire networks, more accurate networks require stronger approximations or phenomenological fitting. Structurally, the formalism is relatively flexible as long as its assumptions remain satisfied. It does not require one specific connectivity matrix, but it does require sparse enough interactions for conditional independence and weak correlations to remain plausible. El Boustani and Destexhe also showed that local connectivity and heterogeneous transmis-sion delays can be incorporated. In particular, random delays on the order of the coarse-graining time (T) can reduce global oscillations and enlarge the stable asynchronous irregular region. In addition, the formalism is best justified in asynchronous irregular regimes, where coarse-grained Markovian-ity, weak correlations, conditional independence, and approximate Gaussian activity distributions are plausible. By contrast, asynchronous regular, synchronous regular, or strongly synchronous irregular states introduce periodicity, synchrony, or residual correlations that can violate the assumptions of the model. The original formalism should therefore be understood primarily as a theory of finite-size asynchronous irregular activity, rather than as a universal description of all brain states. Extensions to sleep, anesthesia, or strongly oscillatory regimes require additional mechanisms and specific validation.

A practical advantage of the approach is that the analytical expression can be inverted. Given the measured firing rate F(⟨V ⟩, σ (V ), τ V ) from a complex simulation or in vitro data, one can compute the corresponding effective threshold:

This makes the fitting procedure straightforward: first, the effective threshold is reconstructed from data; then the coefficients (P0, P μ, P σ, P τ ) are estimated by linear regression and refined by nonlinear least squares optimization. Zerlaut et al. validated this framework on several synthetic neuron models, including the leaky integrate-and-fire model (LIF), the exponential integrate-and-fire model (EIF), the inactivating leaky integrate-and-fire model (iLIF), a LIF model with spike-frequency adaptation (sfaLIF), and the inacti-vating adaptive exponential integrate-and-fire model (iAdExp). They found that a constant-threshold description achieved a mean goodness of fit of approximately 84 .6% , whereas the linear four-parameter effective threshold increased the fit to approximately 99 .0% . A second-order polynomial threshold with ten parameters further improved the fit to approximately 99 .6% . However, the four-parameter version was retained as a good compromise between accuracy, simplicity, and interpretability. The same template also provided a robust characterization of real layer-V pyramidal neurons recorded 

in vitro . Importantly, the study revealed strong functional heterogeneity across cells: neurons differed not only in baseline excitability, but also in their sensitivities to μV , σV , and τV . Zerlaut et al. therefore established a compact semi-analytical framework able to capture both theoretical and experimental firing responses in the fluctuation-driven regime. 

Conclusion. El Boustani and Destexhe had already shown that effective transfer functions are nec-essary, especially for conductance-based models. Zerlaut et al. made this strategy more systematic and experimentally grounded by introducing a fluctuation-dependent effective threshold. Their study does not yet build a full population mean-field model, but it provides the smooth, compact, and biophysically interpretable transfer-function template required by later mesoscopic and whole-brain models. At the same time, they observed that cortical populations are functionally strongly inhomogeneous in young mouse visual cortex, which is partly in tension with the homogeneous-population assumptions used in earlier mean-field models. A natural extension would be to use the sensitivities to μV , σV ,and τV as coordinates for defining neuronal subpopulations beyond mere excitatory/inhibitory pop-ulations. 6.4. Discussion: Truncation to First-Order Dynamics 

A natural methodological question is why the article validates a second-order mean-field model at the local-network level but then truncates the spatial ring model to first-order dynamics. This is especially relevant because the master-equation formalism was introduced precisely to capture finite-size fluctuations and covariances in asynchronous irregular states. Moreover, the linear toy model of El Boustani and Destexhe shows that, near a critical instability, covariance terms can become large and the correction 12 ∂2Fμ cμν may no longer be negligible. The explanation given by the article is mainly practical: the first-order truncation is adopted for simplicity. A cautious interpretation is that, in the visually evoked regime considered by Zerlaut et al., the dominant spatiotemporal response is already captured by the first moments, whereas a full second-order spatial model would be substantially more complex and computationally expensive (more details in Sec. 9.1). The article does not explicitly prove that covariance terms are negligible in the ring setting. The truncation should therefore be presented as a pragmatic modeling choice, supported by the bounded and accurate behavior observed in the reported simulations, rather than as a formally demonstrated reduction. More generally, the strongest limitation of the approach is that adaptation is included only indi-rectly through the stationary transfer function. As a result, the model does not fully capture history-dependent aftereffects, such as the post-stimulus hyperpolarization following transient activation. This limitation directly motivates later work in which adaptation is promoted to an explicit mesoscopic dy-namical variable.

A central limitation of the previous model of Zerlaut et al. (2018) [4] was that spike-frequency adap-tation was included only through the stationary transfer function. As a result, the mean-field model could reproduce spontaneous activity and some transient responses, but it failed to capture long history-dependent aftereffects, such as the post-stimulus hyperpolarization following strong activation. 7.2. Up-Down dynamics and slow oscillations 

A major new regime explored in Di Volo et al. is the alternation of Up and Down states. The proposed mechanism combines bistability, adaptation, and noise. In the Down state, adaptation is low, so an active state remains accessible. Noise can therefore push the system into an Up state. During the Up state, adaptation gradually accumulates, progressively destabilizing the active state and eventually driving the system back into the Down state. 7.3. Validation against conductance-based spiking networks 

Di Volo et al. validate the adaptive mean-field model on the same family of conductance-based RS-FS AdEx networks used by Zerlaut et al. (2018). The model reproduces spontaneous asynchronous irregular activity well, including the dependence of firing rates and average membrane potential on adaptation strength. The improvement is especially clear under time-dependent stimulation. After a transient excitatory input, the accumulation of W produces a prolonged post-stimulus hyperpolariza-tion lasting hundreds of milliseconds, as W slowly decays. This response was missed by the previous stationary-adaptation approximation, but is reproduced by the adaptive mean-field model and matches the corresponding spiking-network simulations. The model also captures state-dependent responsiveness, or gain modulation. The response to the same perturbation depends on the baseline conductance state and on the pre-stimulus adaptation level. A highly active state, characterized by stronger conductance load and larger adaptation, responds less strongly to a new input than a low-activity state. This makes the adaptive mean-field model substantially more realistic as a local mesoscopic node.

8. Molecular-to-Macro Mapping: A whole-brain framework 

Sacha et al. (2025) [1] address a central difficulty in computational neuroscience: whole-brain models describe activity at the macroscopic scale, whereas many pharmacological interventions and patho-physiological mechanisms act at the molecular, synaptic, or cellular scale. The authors argue that a systematic framework is needed to propagate such microscopic perturbations up to whole-brain dy-namics. They therefore propose a bottom-up multiscale modeling strategy in which receptor-level mechanisms are mapped onto local circuit parameters, reduced to adaptive mean-field nodes, and then embedded into a connectome-based whole-brain model. Using this framework, they show how biologically interpretable perturbations of synaptic kinetics and adaptation can drive transitions from wake-like asynchronous activity to generalized slow-wave dynamics, as observed in anesthesia and NREM sleep. 

8.1. Multiscale architecture of the framework 

The article reuses several theoretical ingredients developed in the preceding literature: AdEx single-neuron models and RS-FS local cortical circuits [4], the second-order master-equation formalism of El Boustani and Destexhe [2], the semi-analytical transfer-function framework of Zerlaut et al. [3], the adaptive conductance-based mean-field model of Di Volo et al. [5], and the embedding of local mean-field circuits into spatially extended networks [4]. The main contribution of Sacha et al. is therefore not a new local mean-field derivation, but the integration of these components into a coherent receptor-to-whole-brain modeling framework. The framework is multiscale in four complementary senses. First, at the cellular scale, it relies on AdEx models of excitatory RS and inhibitory FS neurons. 8.2. Mapping molecular mechanisms to model parameters 

A major strength of the framework is that molecular actions are mapped onto explicit model parame-ters. GABAergic anesthetics such as propofol are represented mainly by an increase in the inhibitory synaptic decay time τi reflecting prolonged GABA A-mediated inhibition. NMDA antagonists such as ketamine and xenon are modeled mainly by a decrease in the excitatory synaptic decay time τe,reflecting shortened excitatory kinetics. NREM sleep is represented primarily by an increase in the spike-triggered adaptation parameter be, capturing the effect of reduced cholinergic tone on neuronal excitability. In this way, the framework allows microscopic pharmacological mechanisms to be propa-gated through local network dynamics up to whole-brain activity. Therefore, by adjusting the micro-scopic "knobs" within the framework that are not mere phenomenological fitting parameters, one can recreate the biological signatures of pharmacological agents and study their emergent impacts on both mesoscopic circuits and whole-brain activity. An important result of the article is that several distinct microscopic manipulations can generate slow-wave, unconscious-like macroscopic dynamics, but with different whole-brain signatures. Increasing be,increasing τi, and decreasing τe all affect the stability of the self-sustained active state. However, they do so through different mechanisms. Increasing be strengthens adaptation and destabilizes sustained activity, whereas changes in τi and τe shift the critical adaptation boundaries required to maintain or suppress active states. Using spiking-network survival-time maps and graphical fixed-point analysis of the mean-field model, Sacha et al. show that anesthesia-like slow waves arise from the interaction between synaptic kinetics and adaptation, rather than from a single control parameter. This is impor-tant conceptually. The framework does not simply generate a generic unconscious state. Instead, it can distinguish between different routes to altered consciousness, such as propofol-like, ketamine-like, xenon-like, and NREM-like conditions, by linking each of them to different microscopic mechanisms.

8.3. Whole-brain validation: structure-function coupling and PCI 

At the macroscale, Sacha et al. evaluate the model against two empirical signatures of unconscious states. The first is structure-function coupling. In both empirical BOLD data and simulated BOLD signals, functional connectivity becomes more constrained by anatomical structural connectivity during propofol anesthesia and NREM sleep. This increase in structure-function correlation is not observed in the ketamine condition. The model overestimates the magnitude of the increase, but qualitatively captures the structural entrapment of brain activity under propofol and NREM-like conditions. The second validation concerns perturbational complexity. By applying a localized perturbation to the whole-brain model, the authors compare the spatial and temporal propagation of activity across states. In the wake-like condition, perturbations produce broader and more sustained cortical propagation. In anesthesia and NREM-like states, the responses are shorter-lived and more spatially constrained. The model therefore captures not only spontaneous resting-state changes, but also state-dependent changes in responsiveness to external stimulation, consistent with the reduction of perturbational complexity index (PCI) observed in unconscious states. 

8.4. Conclusion 

Sacha et al. (2025) do not introduce a fundamentally new local mean-field theory. Their main con-tribution is the multiscale integration of receptor-aware adaptive conductance-based mean-field nodes into a connectome-driven whole-brain model. The resulting framework establishes a biologically inter-pretable chain from molecular synaptic mechanisms to local circuit dynamics, mesoscopic mean-field variables, whole-brain activity, and empirical observables. More broadly, the study shows that whole-brain models do not have to remain purely phenomenological or black-box descriptions. By preserving an explicit mechanistic link between receptors, local circuits, mean-field nodes, and connectome-level interactions, the framework makes it possible to investigate how specific microscopic perturbations reshape macroscopic phenomena such as slow waves, structure–function coupling, and perturbational complexity. 

9. Limitations and future directions for receptor-aware whole-brain models 

9.1. First-order truncation and finite-size fluctuations 

A first limitation of the Sacha et al. framework [1], and later colleagues [5, 33] is the truncation of the adaptive master-equation dynamics to first order. This choice removes the explicit propagation of finite-size covariances between population activities without explicitly proving that covariance terms are negligible in the framework’s target regimes. It therefore raises a natural question: why study the second-order master-equation formalism if the final whole-brain implementation only retains the first-order equations? The answer is twofold. First, the second-order formalism identifies what is lost in the first-order trunca-tion: not only a correction to the mean activity, but also a dynamical description of finite-size fluctua-tions and state-dependent covariances. These terms can matter near bifurcations, in fluctuation-driven regimes, or when quantitative variability rather than only mean activity is the target of the model. Sec-ond, the algorithmic-cost analysis in Sec. 10.2 shows why the truncation is computationally attractive. A first-order whole-brain model with K regions has a cost of the form W 1st 1s ∼ O (K) + O(ρK 2), where the O(K) term is node-local and the O(ρK 2) term comes from long-range connectome coupling. Asecond-order model with covariances kept only locally within each node increases the node-local prefac-tor ( O(P 3) against O(P 2)), but preserves the same whole-brain scaling, W 2nd ,local 1s ∼ O (K) + O(ρK 2).Therefore, second-order model with local covariances are computationally more expensive than first-order, especially when the number of population P increases, but they are asymptotically equal on the scaling of K nodes. By contrast, a full global covariance model, in which covariances are propagated between all P K population variables of the network, introduces a covariance matrix of size O(P 2K2)

and Jacobian–covariance products scaling as O(P 3K3). This global second-order closure is therefore generally prohibitive at whole-brain resolution. 28 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

In the Sacha et al. framework, externally added Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise partly compensates for the absence of explicit finite-size fluctuations by reintroducing stochastic variability at the level of the first-order dynamics. However, such noise is phenomenological: it does not reproduce the full covariance structure that would be generated by a second-order closure, nor its dependence on the current network state. The first-order approximation should therefore be understood as a pragmatic compromise. It is well suited for scalable whole-brain simulations targeting qualitative macroscopic phenomena, but it may be insufficient for applications requiring quantitative variability, state-dependent fluctuations, or accurate behavior close to dynamical instabilities. A natural future direction is therefore not necessarily a full global second-order whole-brain model, but rather an intermediate strategy: retaining local second-order covariance terms within each region while keeping inter-regional covariances truncated. Such a model would preserve most of the scalability of the first-order framework while restoring an explicit description of local finite-size fluctuations. 

9.2. Heterogeneity across cells and regions 9.2.1 Microscopic heterogeneity: Intra-node diversity 

Sacha et al. model each cortical node using two homogeneous neuronal populations: RS excitatory cells and FS inhibitory cells. This is a strong simplification, since cortical tissue contains substantial transcriptomic, morphological, and electrophysiological diversity. Such heterogeneity is not necessarily biological noise; it can shape the dynamical regime and computational properties of recurrent networks. Di Volo and Destexhe [34] showed that realistic neuronal diversity can improve responsiveness, facilitate propagation, and keep networks close to a dynamical transition. Similarly, Kim and Choi [35] studied heterogeneous inhibitory populations, including PV, SST, and VIP interneurons, and analytically showed with mean-field formalism that inhibitory diversity can stabilize spatially structured cortical networks in ways that homogeneous E-I models cannot. Their results also emphasize that conductance-based synapses may be necessary to reproduce certain cell-type-specific gain effects. However, introducing microscopic heterogeneity into whole-brain mean-field models is difficult. It increases the number of state variables and parameters, may require integration over distributions of cellular properties, and risks over-parameterization. Moreover, at the scale of EEG, MEG, or fMRI, some microscopic variability is averaged out. A useful compromise may therefore be to include only those forms of heterogeneity that change the dynamical regime of the node, while avoiding a fully detailed cell-type-resolved model unless the data can constrain it. 

9.2.2 Macroscopic heterogeneity: Regional Specialization 

A more tractable extension is to introduce heterogeneity at the level of brain regions. In the current Sacha et al. framework, all cortical nodes share the same local mean-field structure and parameters, apart from their connectome-based inputs. Yet real brain regions differ in cytoarchitecture, receptor density, local connectivity, and intrinsic dynamical regime. Region-specific mean-field nodes could improve the biological realism and quantitative accuracy of whole-brain simulations while remaining more scalable than full intra-node cellular heterogeneity. For example, Overwiening et al. [36] developed a biologically grounded mean-field model of the thalamus and showed that its functional role depends strongly on physiological state, such as wakefulness or sleep. Although building many region-specific nodes is labor-intensive, it is a natural direction for future whole-brain models that aim to move from qualitative reproduction toward direct quantitative comparison with empirical recordings. 

9.3. Beyond qualitative comparison: toward direct comparison with empirical signals 

A second major extension is the development of explicit observation models. The Sacha et al. frame-work mainly predicts latent mesoscopic variables such as firing rates, adaptation, and wave propaga-tion. These variables are biologically meaningful, but they are not directly EEG, MEG, LFP, VSDi, 29 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

or BOLD signals. Direct empirical validation therefore requires a forward model mapping mean-field variables to measured observables. For electrophysiological signals, Tesler et al. [37] proposed a mean-field-based framework for LFP and MEG forward modeling. Their approach combines unitary-LFP kernels with current-dipole and volume-conductor models, and was validated against conductance-based AdEx spiking networks. This makes it directly relevant to the Sacha et al. framework, where local mean-field nodes could be used to generate synthetic electrophysiological signals. For fMRI, Tesler et al. [38] proposed a mechanistic pathway from mean-field excitatory activity to BOLD through astrocytic calcium dynamics, vasomodulator release, arteriole tone, cerebral blood flow, and a Balloon-type hemodynamic model. This provides a biologically interpretable alternative to treating BOLD as a simple filtered version of firing rate. Such observation models would make the framework more testable and falsifiable across modalities. However, they also add a second layer of assumptions: tissue geometry and dipole orientation for electrophysiology, or neurovascular and astrocytic mechanisms for BOLD. They should therefore be viewed not as trivial readout layers, but as additional mechanistic models built on top of the neuronal mean-field dynamics. 

9.4. Plastic mean-field beyond fixed local transfer functions 

Another limitation of the Sacha et al. framework is that local transfer functions and effective couplings are fixed. Helson, Tanré, and Veltz [39] proposed a McKean–Vlasov mean-field formulation for spiking networks with stochastic non-symmetric STDP, in which the state of a typical neuron includes its activity, time since last spike, and a distribution over incoming synaptic states. Conceptually, this points toward a plastic mean-field theory in which local interactions evolve over time rather than being fixed parameters, and this reduces the computational burden relative to the original N 2 synaptic system. Such a direction would be important for extending receptor-aware whole-brain models toward learning, plasticity, and long-term adaptation. However, the current formulation is not yet a direct replacement for compact AdEx-based mean-field nodes: it relies on simpler neuron dynamics, remains computa-tionally heavier, and is not immediately connectome-ready. It is therefore best viewed as a promising theoretical direction rather than an immediately usable whole-brain node model. 

9.5. Data-driven and hybrid extensions 

Because the transfer function is a central bottleneck of the mean-field framework, data-driven surro-gates offer a natural extension. These strategies differ in how much of the mechanistic model they replace. One may replace only the single-neuron transfer function, learn the full local mesoscopic vector field, use scientific machine learning to train or augment the mechanistic equations, or bypass node-based modeling entirely with a whole-brain foundation model. 

9.5.1 Replacing only the transfer function 

The most conservative strategy is to replace only the transfer function F, while keeping the master-equation scaffold and population ODEs unchanged. Spaeth et al. [40] follow this logic by fitting a simple four-parameter phenomenological transfer function, the Refractory SoftPlus , directly from single-neuron simulations receiving stochastic input. This approach is attractive because it avoids some restrictive assumptions of classical mean-field deriva-tions, such as Gaussian membrane fluctuations, infinitesimal PSPs, or diffusion approximations. It is also model-agnostic and could be applied to complex neuron models for which semi-analytical fluctu-ation statistics are unavailable, such as complex multi-compartment Hodgkin-Huxley neurons. More-over, it is computationally lighter than the semi-analytical transfer function of Zerlaut et al. and can naturally absorb non-Gaussian effects. The cost is a loss of mechanistic transparency: the explicit 30 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

chain from synaptic conductances to membrane-potential moments and firing rate is compressed into a fitted input-output curve. Moreover, the fitted transfer function remains tied to the statistics of the input ensemble used during calibration. This makes receptor-level interpretation and extrapolation to strongly synchronized or out-of-distribution regimes more difficult. 

9.5.2 Learning the local mesoscopic vector field 

A more radical strategy is to replace the entire local mesoscopic vector field. Breyton et al. [41] learn a neural-mass model directly from spiking-network simulations, rather than deriving it analytically. Breyton et al. trained a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to reproduce the macroscopic phase flow of the average firing rate and membrane potential directly from data and compared with the exact mean-field MPR model. They further show that the learned vector field can still be studied with dynamical-systems tools such as bifurcation analysis, and that it can be embedded into a whole-brain framework for inversion against synthetic and empirical fMRI data. The advantage is flexibility: any parameter that can be varied in the simulator can in principle be included in the learned macroscopic model, such as connection probability that are difficult to include in standard analytical reductions which require strong assumptions and approximations. However, in addition of weaker interpretability, the method inherits the usual problem of domain dependence. If the training dataset does not sufficiently sample a regime of interest, the learned surrogate may interpolate well inside the training region but extrapolate poorly outside it. It may also lack of smoothness if no proper regularization is made during training. Nevertheless, encouraging extrapolation behavior was observed in practice, where Breyton et al. found chaotic regime similar to analytical solution, without explicit feeding chaotic data during training. The need for spiking network simulations is mitigated by the fact that mechanistic models also often require network simulations for calibration and validation, although in a much smaller quantity. This approach no longer answers the question "how can the mean-field equations be derived from the microscopic model?", but rather "how can a low-dimensional surrogate be trained to reproduce the microscopic simulator?" This is a legitimate and potentially powerful aim, where deep learning can excel, but epistemologically different from the mechanistic derivation followed in the present review. 

9.5.3 Scientific machine learning and hybrid equation learning 

Scientific machine learning (SciML) offers an intermediate route. Instead of replacing the mechanis-tic model, it makes the governing equations differentiable, trainable, or partially learnable. This is appealing because the problem is often not the absence of models, but the difficulty of fitting, correct-ing, or extending them. Neural ODEs [42] allow parameters or equation components to be optimized through an ODE solver; PINNs enforce differential-equation constraints through residual losses; and universal differential equations (UDEs) [43] keep the known mechanistic dynamics while learning only an unknown correction term: ˙X = fknown (X, θ ) + flearned (X, ϕ ). For receptor-aware mean-field models, this is especially appealing: the known terms could encode the adaptive conductance-based dynam-ics and connectome coupling, while the learned term could absorb missing heterogeneity, unmodeled nonlinearities, state-dependent gains, or phenomenological plasticity [44]. Other neural differential-equation variants may also be relevant. Neural SDEs [45] can represent stochastic dynamics, Neural CDEs [46] can handle irregularly sampled observations, and differentiable delay-equation solvers are relevant because whole-brain models contain propagation delays. Recent perspectives on functional whole-brain modeling also advocate differentiable mechanistic models refined through gradient-based optimization [47]. A closely related application is global parameter inference. In standard mechanistic modeling, tuning a whole-brain network to match empirical data is computationally prohibitive due to the curse of dimen-sionality. Tools such as TVB-Optim [48] use differentiable programming and advanced data-driven optimization to tune whole-brain models against empirical signals while preserving the mechanistic scaffold. Similar inverse-modeling logic can also be applied outside differential-equation models, as in recent personalized Generalized Ising approaches [49]. Nevertheless, this approach does not eliminate 31 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

all the core difficulties of the problem. First, the resulting optimization can be numerically delicate, especially for stiff, chaotic, delayed, or weakly identifiable systems. Second, the learned correction term in a UDE or related hybrid model may absorb several distinct modeling errors at once, thereby reducing interpretability if it is left unconstrained. In all cases, however, identifiability remains a major limitation: many parameter combinations may generate similar macroscopic signals, failing to recover a unique mechanistic explanation, so optimization must be constrained by strong biological priors. For these reasons, scientific machine learning should not be viewed as a replacement for mechanistic whole-brain modeling, but rather as a promising extension of it. 

9.5.4 Beyond node-based mean-field: data-driven whole-brain foundation models 

A more radical direction is represented by foundation models such as TRIBE v2 [50]. Here, the goal is not to derive local circuit equations and connect them through a structural connectome, but to learn a direct mapping from multimodal stimuli to whole-brain fMRI activity. The model takes video, audio, and text embeddings from pretrained AI systems and predicts high-resolution cortical and subcortical fMRI responses across many subjects and tasks. Such models can be highly valuable for prediction, subject generalization, in-silico stimulus exploration, protocol design, and possibly data-augmentation strategies and knowledge distillation with teacher-model [51] which could be valuable in Brain-Computer-Interface. However, these models should not be described as assumption-free nor fundamentally better. They replace mechanistic assumptions with statistical and architectural ones: pretrained feature extractors, transformer design, training objectives, temporal windows, subject-alignment procedures, and large supervised datasets. They also do not directly explain how receptor kinetics, adaptation, excitation-inhibition balance, or anatomical coupling generate brain activity. Moreover, because TRIBE v2 is based on fMRI, it inherits the temporal limitations and hemodynamic filtering of BOLD. For these reasons, foundation models are not direct competitors of receptor-aware mean-field models; they answer a different scientific question, centered on prediction and generalization rather than causal mechanistic explanation. 

9.5.5 Mechanistic transparency versus data-driven flexibility 

These extensions form a spectrum between mechanistic explanation and predictive flexibility. At one end, receptor-aware mean-field models preserve an explicit causal chain from synaptic kinetics, conductances, membrane fluctuations, adaptation, and connectome coupling to macroscopic activity. This transparency is essential when the goal is biological interpretation, pharmacological manipulation, or the construction of modality-specific observation models. Replacing only the transfer function increases flexibility while preserving most of this scaffold. Learning the full local vector field further expands the range of microscopic mechanisms that can be approximated, but weakens the link to an explicit derivation. Fully data-driven foundation models, such as TRIBE v2 [50], go further by learning a direct mapping from stimuli to brain activity, thereby maximizing prediction and generalization, valuable for large-scale encoding performance and in-silico experimental exploration, while largely abandoning the mechanistic bridge from microscopic physiology to macroscopic dynamics. These approaches should therefore not be compared through a simple "which is better?" criterion, because they optimize different objectives. A large data-driven model may define a strong predic-tive benchmark for the empirical observables included in its training objective, and may even set an empirical upper bound for predictive performance, but this advantage comes with weaker pa-rameter identifiability, reduced causal interpretability, high training cost, and limited ability to map receptor-level mechanisms onto model variables. Conversely, mechanistic whole-brain models may be less accurate predictors, but they retain explanatory structure: synaptic timescales, adaptation, excitation–inhibition balance, and conduction delays can still be manipulated and interpreted biologi-cally. The central question is therefore not whether one should choose black-box prediction or mechanis-tic modeling once and for all, but which level of explanation is required by the scientific question. 32 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

Reverse-engineering a successful data-driven model may reveal useful representational geometry or effective computational constraints, but it does not by itself recover a biophysical mechanism. Mech-anistic models remain more appropriate for causal interpretation, pharmacological intervention, and biologically grounded extrapolation. Hybrid scientific-machine-learning approaches may offer a use-ful compromise, because they preserve the causal structure of mechanistic differential equations while benefiting from data-driven calibration, correction, and optimization [47]. 

10. Toward benchmarks for mesoscopic and whole-brain surrogates 

As the repertoire of mesoscopic and whole-brain models expands, model choice increasingly depends on the scientific objective, yet turning that objective into an operational decision remains a methodological challenge. We posit that for the models considered in this review, predictive accuracy is necessary but not sufficient, and any benchmark reporting a single fidelity score is potentially misleading. The reason is empirical rather than rhetorical, and recurs at every scale below: model rankings reorder depending on the imaging modality [52], the summary statistic used to compare activity [53], the dynamical feature under evaluation [54], and even the optimization strategy used to fit the model [55]. A meaningful benchmark must therefore be explicitly multi-dimensional. Table 1 organizes the resulting axes; it deliberately assigns no scores, since a fixed grid of grades would misrepresent a state of the field in which the ranking itself is contingent on modality, statistic, feature, and fitting procedure. Each row instead states what an axis measures, why it does not collapse to a single number, and where the supporting evidence or derivation is found. It is useful to separate two questions that are often conflated because they rest on different notions of ground truth. Simulator-to-surrogate faithfulness asks whether a reduced model preserves the macro-scopic dynamics of the microscopic spiking network it emerges from; here the ground truth is the simulator. Empirical adequacy asks whether the full generative pipeline (such as node dynamics, con-nectivity, delays, noise, and observation model) reproduces measured signals; here the ground truth is experimental data, seen through an observation model. The two are genuinely distinct: a reduction faithful to its spiking parent may still mismatch BOLD or MEG, and a model tuned to empirical func-tional connectivity need not correspond to any well-controlled microscopic limit. To our knowledge, no unified benchmark yet exists for the surrogates discussed here, but several targeted studies begin to populate both regimes. 

10.1. Evidence from targeted studies, at both scales Simulator-to-surrogate (mesoscopic). At the mesoscopic scale, the natural reference is the mi-croscopic spiking network from which the reduced model is expected to emerge. Empirical resources such as MICrONS [56] provide valuable biological constraints, but they do not constitute complete ground truth, since not every cell, connection, state variable, or hidden physiological parameter is observed. Deschle et al. [57] compared Freeman-type neural masses with averaged noisy LIF networks across different E/I ratio and found agreement confined to specific regimes, notably near the onset of low-frequency synchronization, with substantial deviations in amplitude, frequency, or synchrony structure elsewhere. This shows that the validity must therefore be tested across regimes rather than inferred from the form of the equations alone. Baldy et al. [58] add the inverse perspective, bench-marking inference under dynamical noise, bistability, and partial observability, and identify parameter identifiability as a central axis: some parameters are intrinsically hard to recover from macroscopic observations, though simulation-based inference and Neural ODE methods can respectively improve parameter recovery and reconstruct macroscopic vector fields directly from microscopic simulations. Analytical toolboxes such as NNMT [59] may provide useful common baselines for mechanistic com-parisons.

Shared platforms such as Neurolib [60] and Virtual Brain Inference [61] are valuable because they allow node models, observation models, and inversion procedures to be compared under common simulation pipelines. Together, these studies show that the field already contains several ingredients of a benchmarking culture, but not yet a shared protocol for the class of mesoscopic and whole-brain surrogates considered here. Such a protocol should not collapse performance into a single accuracy score. Building one is what will ultimately allow the trade-off between mechanistic transparency and data-driven flexibility to be adjudicated case by case. 10.2. Algorithmic simulation cost and memory traffic as benchmark dimensions 

A benchmark for mesoscopic and macroscopic brain models should also quantify the computational burden of forward simulation. This is important when comparing spiking networks, semi-analytical neural masses, exact low-dimensional reductions, and learned surrogates, since these approaches can differ by orders of magnitude in the cost of simulating the same duration of biological activity. A useful, largely hardware-independent proxy for this burden is the algorithmic work required to simulate one biological second at a prescribed numerical resolution. Unlike wall-clock runtime, this quantity does not directly depend on programming language, compiler, memory layout, or hardware, and is therefore complementary to, rather than a substitute for, empirical runtime or energy mea-surements. We quantify it along two axes: the arithmetic cost per integration step W∆t, counted in FLOP-equivalents with special functions weighted by their relative expense, and the memory traffic per step M∆t, estimated as the number of bytes transferred. The second axis is essential because large-scale simulations can become memory-bound: accessing delayed states, structural-connectivity matrices, or heterogeneous regional parameters may dominate runtime even when the arithmetic itself is cheap. Their ratio, the operational intensity I = W1s /M 1s [62], distinguishes compute-bound from memory-bound models. 11. Conclusion 

Receptor-aware whole-brain modeling offers a promising route for linking microscopic physiology to macroscopic brain activity. The framework reviewed here shows how synaptic receptor kinetics, conduc-36 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

tance statistics, and spike-frequency adaptation can be propagated through a chain of reductions: from conductance-based spiking circuits, to adaptive mean-field nodes, and finally to connectome-coupled whole-brain dynamics. Its main strength is therefore mechanistic continuity: it preserves an inter-pretable causal path from receptor-level perturbations to population activity, large-scale propagation, and empirical observables. This strength depends on the assumptions that make the reduction tractable. These assumptions are valid in specific regimes, but weaker in others. Likewise, the first-order whole-brain implementation is computationally attractive, but discards explicit covariance dynamics and therefore cannot fully reproduce state-dependent finite-size fluctuations. The broader comparison with other reduced and surrogate models shows that there is no universally optimal level of description. Spiking networks retain microscopic detail at high cost; exact reductions provide mathematical clarity under restrictive assumptions; neural masses and adaptive mean fields offer scalable mechanistic approximations; and learned surrogates can maximize prediction while weakening causal interpretability. The algorithmic-cost analysis makes this trade-off explicit: local mean-field models remain scalable, whereas global covariance propagation changes the scaling class and rapidly becomes prohibitive. Thus, receptor-aware whole-brain mean-field models should be understood as structured approxi-mations, not exact replicas of brain dynamics. Their value lies in preserving the mechanistic vari-ables needed to ask causal physiological questions while remaining computationally tractable. Fu-ture progress will require better observation models, principled heterogeneity, improved treatment of fluctuations and plasticity, and systematic benchmarks combining predictive accuracy, identifiability, interpretability, and simulation cost.

A. Appendix 

A.1. Large-scale spiking network models 

It would be misleading to present detailed neural-network models as merely local toy systems. Recent years have seen impressive progress in large-scale mechanistic simulation. In C. elegans , Zhao et al. [14] introduced BAAIWorm, a recent OpenWorm-inspired integrative model [15] that couples a biophysically detailed multicompartment neural model to a 3D body-environment simulation. In contrast to earlier OpenWorm-style integrations, which remained largely open-loop, BAAIWorm implements closed-loop brain–body–environment interactions: sensory neurons receive input from the simulated environment, while motor neurons drive muscle activation and body defor-mation. This architecture produces worm-like zigzag locomotion toward an attractor. However, despite being an impressive organism-scale simulation, its current brain component models a restricted sensory-locomotor circuit of 136 neurons rather than the complete set of 302 neurons of the adult hermaphrodite nervous system. It should therefore be understood as an integrative brain–body–environment model of a specific behavioral regime, rather than as a complete whole-nervous-system digital twin of C. elegans .For Drosophila , the scale is even more striking. Shiu et al. [16] built a leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model of the adult fly brain using the FlyWire connectome, comprising 127,400 proofread neurons and more than 50 million synaptic connections. Implemented in Brian2, this model was used to gener-ate experimentally testable predictions about feeding and grooming circuits. The underlying FlyWire adult brain connectome [17] was produced through large-scale electron-microscopy reconstruction, AI-assisted segmentation, and extensive community proofreading and annotation. This therefore repre-sents a genuine whole-brain spiking model, although of a compact nervous system and with highly simplified neuronal dynamics. This modeling strategy contrasts with BAAIWorm, where it aims to build a biophysically detailed, embodied, organism-scale model of C. elegans , whereas Shiu et al. in-stead built a simple but brainwide connectome-based spiking model of the Drosophila brain. Despite omitting many biological details, such as neuronal morphology, receptor dynamics, gap junctions, neu-romodulation and internal state, the model made experimentally valid predictions about sensorimotor circuits: across 164 empirically tested predictions, 91% were consistent with experimental results. At the scale of mammalian cortical microcircuits, detailed circuit models have also become increasingly ambitious. Laquitaine et al. [19] used a biophysically detailed model of rat primary somatosensory cortex, based on the Blue Brain neocortical microcircuit framework [20]-[21], to generate synthetic Neuropixels-like extracellular recordings with known ground-truth spike times. The simulated subvol-ume contained 30,190 multi-compartment neurons distributed across all six cortical layers and con-nected by 36.7 million synaptic connections, with 60 morphological classes, 11 electrical firing classes, and realistic thalamocortical inputs. Extracellular signals were generated from the transmembrane currents of the detailed morphologies using NEURON/Neurodamus and the BlueRecording pipeline, then sorted with several modern spike sorters. This illustrates how large-scale biophysically detailed simulations can now serve as benchmarking environments for experimental methods. However, 10 min-utes of extracellular recording typically took 6 days on large clusters containing more than 120 CPUs, which clearly limits applications. In parallel, Pronold et al. [18] developed a multi-scale spiking network model of one human cortical hemisphere, illustrating that human-scale spiking simulations are becoming possible in highly struc-tured, reduced settings. The model covers the 34 areas of the Desikan-Killiany parcellation, with each area represented by a 1 mm ² layer-resolved cortical column. Layers 2/3, 4, 5, and 6 are mod-eled with excitatory and inhibitory LIF populations, yielding 3.47 million neurons and 42.8 billion synapses. Local connectivity relies on a rescaled cortical microcircuit blueprint, whereas long-range cortico-cortical connectivity is derived from human diffusion-imaging data and augmented with pre-dictive connectomics to determine laminar source and target patterns. The resulting framework links multiple scales, from single-neuron spiking statistics to area-level functional connectivity. The au-thors found good agreement with human medial-frontal spiking data and resting-state fMRI functional connectivity, though only after significantly strengthening inter-areal synapses, suggesting that robust 41 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

long-range coupling is critical for reproducing large-scale resting-state structures. While such simula-tions demonstrate that hemisphere-wide spiking models are now computationally tractable, they do not replace neural-mass or mean-field whole-brain models. Simulations performed in NEST on a 768-core supercomputer required approximately 200 core-hours to generate just 10 seconds of biological activity. Therefore, the model’s high dimensionality, extensive foundational assumptions, and massive computational cost make systematic parameter exploration, subject-specific fitting, and mechanistic interpretation substantially more difficult than in reduced whole-brain models. These examples show the scientific value of detailed spiking simulations. They can generate synthetic data, test mechanistic hypotheses, validate analysis pipelines, and reveal how structure shapes dynam-ics. Their main limitation, however, is often not conceptual but practical: even when feasible, they remain computationally heavy, difficult to invert, often empirical data-hungry, cumbersome to scan over large parameter spaces, and poorly suited to repeated whole-brain fitting. They are therefore very powerful for local circuit investigation and for selected large-scale demonstrations, but they are not yet the most convenient framework for receptor-to-whole-brain inference in humans. The challenge is therefore to find reduced descriptions that remain biologically meaningful without retaining the full cost of explicit spiking simulation.

A.3. Wong-Wang model 

The Wong–Wang model [8, 65] is a biophysically motivated reduced population model that occupies an intermediate position between detailed spiking-network simulations and purely phenomenological neural-mass models. Wong and Wang demonstrated how to reduce a highly nonlinear, 2,000-neuron spiking network (orig-inally developed by Wang [66]) down to a two-variable system while preserving its core biophysics. Unlike standard Wilson–Cowan-type descriptions, its state variables are not directly the firing rates themselves, but rather slow synaptic gating variables, specifically associated with NMDA-mediated excitation. The central idea is that fast spiking and membrane fluctuations can be adiabatically re-duced, while the slow synaptic dynamics dominate the collective behavior on the timescales relevant for decision-making, working memory, and resting-state whole-brain activity. The reduced system therefore evolves on the slow manifold defined by the synaptic gating variables Si. In that sense, the Wong–Wang formalism is not an exact reduction in the mathematical sense of Montbrió–Pazó–Roxin, but rather a biologically grounded mean-field approximation built around slow synaptic currents. This structure makes the Wong–Wang framework especially useful for cognitive and whole-brain appli-cations. Because the dynamics are governed by slow recurrent excitation, the model naturally accounts for persistent activity, attractor competition, and the low-frequency fluctuations relevant for fMRI-scale dynamics. In whole-brain implementations, each node is typically described by one or several Wong– Wang populations and coupled to other regions through long-range excitatory interactions derived from a structural connectome. Deco and colleagues [65] later scaled the Wong–Wang model up to the whole-brain level, connecting 66 local regions via a structural connectome to investigate resting-state functional connectivity. This provided a highly tractable mathematical framework to study how the resting brain’s functional networks are dynamically shaped by its anatomical structure. The main strength of the Wong–Wang framework is therefore that it preserves a clear biological interpretation while remaining computationally lightweight. Its main limitation is that the reduction is tied to specific assumptions: slow synaptic gating must dominate the collective timescale, and the transfer function H(x) is an effective fitted object rather than a fully explicit microscopic derivation. In addition, standard versions typically rely on the LIF model and current-based interactions, meaning the framework is less directly receptor-aware and less flexible than the model-free, conductance-based semi-analytical mean-field lineage studied in the present review.

A.5. Fokker-Planck formalism: from single neurons to population densities A.5.1 General principles of the Fokker-Planck formalism 

The Fokker-Planck Equation (FPE) is a partial differential equation widely used in statistical physics to describe the time evolution of the probability density function under the influence of both deterministic forces and random fluctuations. FPE can be very powerful in fields like computational neuroscience because it is a mathematical scale invariant framework, where the state variable x can represent either a microscopic quantity like a neuron’s membrane potential or a macroscopic quantity like average firing rate of an entire network. A.5.3 Fokker-Planck and population-density approaches at the population level 

At the population level, the role of the FP formalism becomes broader than in the single-neuron case. In the classical sparse-network work of Brunel (2000) [9], the FP equation is used within a self-consistent mean-field description of large excitatory-inhibitory networks. The central object is the population 48 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

density of membrane potentials, from which one can compute stationary firing rates and analyze the stability of asynchronous or oscillatory regimes by linear perturbation. This is therefore already a population-level theory, but it remains primarily a deterministic infinite-size description rather than a compact mesoscopic model of finite-size fluctuations. Several closely related approaches were developed, which also treated the density of neurons over membrane potential as the main dynamical object [70, 71]. The strength of this family of methods is its close connection to microscopic spiking dynamics; its drawback is that the model remains a partial differential equation, which is often costly to solve and difficult to reduce when additional biological variables are included. This difficulty becomes particularly evident when one introduces more realistic single-neuron mech-anisms such as spike-frequency adaptation. In that case, the FP description must be extended to a higher-dimensional state space, which greatly complicates both analysis and simulation. To overcome this limitation, Augustin et al. (2017) [13] derived low-dimensional spike-rate models for AdEx popu-lations directly from the FP framework. Using separately spectral decomposition and linear-nonlinear reduction methods, they obtained first- and second-order ODE approximations that preserve the de-pendence on underlying neuronal parameters while being much cheaper to simulate than the full population-density PDE. A second important limitation of classical FP formalisms is that they are naturally suited to the thermo-dynamic limit, where population activity becomes deterministic. Finite-size fluctuations are therefore not straightforward to include and usually require additional approximations or stochastic closures. In a different, though related, population-density approach, Schwalger et al. (2017) [10] introduced a mesoscopic theory based on refractory density, that is, the distribution of neurons with respect to their last spike time or age. By tracking the survival time of homogeneous populations of finite-size gen-eralized integrate-and-fire (GIF) neurons, they derived stochastic mesoscopic integral-equations that preserve normalization and reproduce collective effects such as finite-size-induced switching in bistable networks. Thus, Fokker–Planck and population-density approaches form a major alternative lineage of mech-anistic population modeling. They are especially powerful when one wishes to retain a close link with microscopic spiking dynamics, but they often remain mathematically heavier than reduced ODE formalisms. 

A.5.4 Synthesis: Master Equation and Fokker-Planck approaches 

Given that the Kramers–Moyal expansion shows how a master equation (ME) can, under suitable as-sumptions, be approximated by a Fokker–Planck equation, one may ask why El Boustani and Destexhe (2009) used a ME formalism rather than directly constructing a macroscopic FP theory. In fact, the two formalisms do not describe the same state space. The FP equation natively assumes continuous time. While this is mathematically sound for continuous variables like a single neuron’s membrane voltage, applying dt → 0 to the discrete spike counts of a finite-size population leads to mathematical singularities. The ME avoids this by tracking the stochastic evolution of discrete population activity states over a specific, coarse-grained mesoscopic time bin T . From this formalism, El Boustani and Destexhe [2] derived differential equations for the mean activity, the covariance matrix, and stationary lagged correlations. By contrast, the FPE is used only as a tool at the single-neuron level: it provides an approximate (semi)-analytical transfer function by describing the voltage density of a representative noisy neuron. In other words, the population-level ME is the mesoscopic formalism proper, whereas the FP formalism enters only through the closure problem. The ME needs a transition kernel, and this kernel depends on the probability that a neuron fires during the next bin T , that is, on the single-neuron transfer function Fμ. Fokker–Planck theory is one possible route to approximate this transfer function. Once Fμ is specified, the master equation formalism propagates it to the network level and yields explicit equations for finite-size activity fluctuations and covariances. This modularity is precisely the conceptual advantage of the El Boustani–Destexhe approach. Classical macroscopic population-density PDEs are immensely powerful, but they are naturally oriented toward 49 Bossard et al., preprint, July 2026 

deterministic, infinite-size limits and remain mathematically heavy when attempting to inject finite-size noise, unless further approximations and tricks. The ME formalism, by contrast, takes the single-neuron transfer function as a plug-and-play input and directly builds a compact mesoscopic description of finite-size population variance. In that sense, it does not replace the FP formalism; it rescues the FP stationary transfer function from the infinite-network assumption and embeds it into a fluctuating, mesoscopic stochastic framework. We stress that while this ME approach offers a highly intuitive bottom-up derivation, it is not in-herently superior to modern population-density theories. As previously discussed, exact macroscopic reductions (e.g., Montbrió et al., 2015 [11]) and stochastic refractory-density equations (e.g., Schwalger et al., 2017 [10]) have emerged as serious, highly efficient continuous competitors. At this stage, system-atic benchmarking of these diverse mesoscopic reduced models against large-scale spiking simulations remains the next logical step (cf. Sec. 10).

C.1.6 TRIBE v2 cost analysis 

The previous models are all dynamical systems integrated step by step. TRIBE v2 [50] is funda-mentally different: it is a tri-modal (video, audio, language) transformer that maps a window of naturalistic stimuli directly onto a window of fMRI responses, with no vector field and no numerical integration.
