# Computing Actual Causes for Neural Network Predictions under Structured Causal Inputs 

# Jannick Strobel, Muqsit Azeem, Stefan Leue 

University of Konstanz {jannick.strobel, muqsit.azeem, stefan.leue}@uni-konstanz.de 

Abstract 

Explaining the predictions of neural networks is a central challenge in trustworthy AI. Existing explanation methods, such as those based on feature attribution or minimal suffi-cient sets, typically treat input features as independent, which can yield misleading explanations when inputs exhibit struc-tured dependencies. We address this by formalizing explana-tions as Halpern–Pearl (HP) actual causes, modeling input de-pendencies using Boolean Structural Causal Models (SCMs). We compute HP causes by applying bound propagation and branch-and-bound techniques, while providing formal guaran-tees of completeness and minimality. Our experiments show that we substantially outperform brute-force and ILP base-lines in scalability, and outperform heuristic search as graph size grows, computing all minimal actual causes on instances with search spaces of up to 2.3 × 10 13 candidate (cause, con-tingency) pairs, on SCMs with up to 28 nodes, within a 180s per-instance budget. In a case study, we further show that ignoring input dependencies inflates the number of reported causes, 14.9% of which are spurious under our SCM. 

# 1 Introduction 

Explaining the predictions of neural networks (NNs) is a central challenge in trustworthy AI (Zhang et al. 2021). A useful explanation should identify why a particular predic-tion was made, and which feasible counterfactual changes would have altered it. Many existing explanation methods rely on attribution scores that quantify the importance of in-put features, often under an implicit feature-independence assumption (Salih et al. 2025). However, in practice, in-puts often exhibit structured causal dependencies: medical findings may constrain diagnoses, and high expenses may lead to negative cashflow while having an average income. Without accounting for these dependencies, a counterfac-tual explanation may vary negative cashflow directly instead of tracing it back to high expenses, so the root cause gets obscured among the other features it influences. More gener-ally, because changing one feature may constrain, determine, or invalidate changes to others, ignoring these dependencies can lead to misleading or even spurious explanations (Aas, Jullum, and Løland 2021; Slack et al. 2020). This motivates explanations with explicit structural causal semantics. In order to represent this semantics, we adopt

Halpern–Pearl (HP) actual causality (Halpern and Pearl 2005; Halpern 2015). In this view, explaining a prediction means identifying minimal sets of features/variables whose values, if changed under an appropriate contingency, would change the prediction outcome. To ensure that such counter-factual changes respect the structure of the input domain, we model feature dependencies using Structural Causal Models (SCMs) (Pearl 2009). We assume that the SCM faithfully models the causal dependencies of the real-world domain, and thereby the dependencies present in the data on which the network is trained. Such SCMs can either be manually de-rived from domain knowledge, or learned from observational data via structure learning methods (Zheng et al. 2018). We assume that such an SCM is given. Our goal is to com-pute HP actual causes, i.e., causal explanations, for a given NN prediction of an input whose feature dependencies are specified by a given SCM. Importantly, such explanations need not be unique (Chock-ler, Kelly, and Kroening 2025). Several minimal sets of vari-ables may each be an actual cause of the same prediction. In a loan-risk model, for instance, one high-risk prediction might be explained by income alone, another by high expenses. Returning only one cause can hide these independent rea-sons, and for auditing and debugging we want the full set of minimal causal explanations, not just a single witness. In our experiments, 68.7% of instances have more than one minimal cause. Computing HP actual causes requires searching over can-didate cause sets and contingencies via interventions . An intervention sets a candidate cause to an alternative value, producing the counterfactual that shows whether the predic-tion would have changed. A contingency holds some of the other features fixed at their actual values, ruling out con-founding from those features. This search is computationally hard in general (Eiter and Lukasiewicz 2006). Most existing work on actual-cause computation studies discrete causal or logical models without an NN predictor (Leitner-Fischer and Leue 2013; Coenen et al. 2022; Ibrahim and Pretschner 2020). Conversely, most NN explanation methods explain the predictor directly, without enforcing that counterfactual inputs respect an explicit SCM (Salih et al. 2025). The set-ting we study combines both: an SCM defines the structured input space, while an NN maps the resulting variables to a prediction. This combination raises a difficult search prob-arXiv:2608.03772v1 [cs.AI] 4 Aug 2026 lem, since one must reason simultaneously over candidate causes, contingencies, and the NN’s prediction. 

Our key idea is to compute HP actual causes by apply-ing bound propagation and branch-and-bound techniques. Instead of enumerating individual interventions and contin-gencies, we group them into regions of SCM-consistent in-puts. We then propagate sound bounds through the SCM and the NN via Interval Bound Propagation (IBP) (Gowal et al. 2019). During branch-and-bound (Bunel et al. 2020), regions that provably preserve the prediction are pruned, while regions that provably change it witness actual causal-ity. Inconclusive regions are refined by splitting and recursive invocation. We focus on Boolean SCMs. Their variables can repre-sent categorical, thresholded, or logical properties, such as income status in a loan application or the presence of a clin-ical finding, while interventions and contingencies have a clear finite semantics. This lets us isolate the core algorith-mic problem of exact HP-cause computation. At the same time, Boolean structural equations can be relaxed into mul-tilinear functions that agree with the original SCM on all Boolean assignments. As a result, we can reason soundly about whole regions of SCM-consistent inputs, rather than enumerating individual assignments one by one. 

Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 

• We formalize NN explanation under structured input de-pendencies as the computation of Halpern–Pearl actual causes over SCM-constrained inputs. 

• We introduce CausEx BaB , a sound and complete algo-rithm that combines multilinear relaxations of Boolean structural equations with bound propagation and branch-and-bound to return all minimal actual causes and minimum-cardinality witness contingencies. The search may be restricted to causes of size at most kmax , preserv-ing soundness and completeness within that bound. 

• We introduce synthetic SCM–NN benchmarks with trained NNs of varying capacity, and show that 

CausEx BaB substantially outperforms brute-force enu-meration and ILP-based causality computation, and out-performs heuristic search as graph size grows, scaling to SCMs with up to 28 nodes and worst-case search spaces of up to 2.3 × 10 13 candidate cause–contingency pairs, and a 66.7% majority at 30 nodes, with a 180s timeout. 

• In a case study for the U.S. Supplemental Nutrition As-sistance Program (SNAP), CausEx BaB outperforms all baselines and shows that ignoring input dependencies more than doubles the median number of reported causes, 14.9% of which are spurious under our SCM. 

# 2 Background 

We give only a brief overview here. Please see App. A and the cited references for a detailed background. 

Structural Causal Models (Pearl 2009). We use SCMs to represent causal relationships between variables. An SCM is a tuple M = ( ⃗U ,⃗ V , F) with exogenous variables ⃗ U =

{U1, . . . , U d}, endogenous variables ⃗ V = {X1, . . . , X d},and structural equations F = {f1, . . . , f d} such that Xj := 

fj (PA j , U j ), where PA j ⊆⃗ V \ { Xj } are the endogenous parents of Xj . A context ⃗u fixes ⃗ U and thereby determines all of ⃗ V . We consider binary SCMs, where Xj ∈ { 0, 1}

and each fj is a Boolean function. An intervention X ← x

represents externally forcing X to value x, overriding its usual causal mechanism, by replacing its structural equation with X := x.

Notation. We use ⃗ V ,⃗ X, and ⃗ W to denote vectors of vari-ables. When combined with set-theoretic operators such as 

⊆, \, and | · | , we identify these vectors, by abuse of notation, with their underlying sets. In Sec. 3, where candidate causes and contingencies are manipulated only as sets, we write C

and W without an arrow. 

Halpern and Pearl’s Actual Causality (Halpern 2015). 

SCMs do not by themselves specify what counts as the cause of an event. Halpern and Pearl’s actual causality fills this gap and has gone through several revisions. We adopt the lat-est, modified definition of actual causality (Halpern 2015), which avoids known problems with preemption and spuri-ous causes at lower complexity. According to this definition, given (M,⃗ u), a set of variables ⃗ X =⃗x is an actual cause of an outcome φ if ( AC1 ) (M,⃗ u) |=⃗ X =⃗x and (M,⃗ u) |= φ,(AC2 ) there exist ⃗ W ⊆⃗ V \⃗ X with actual value ⃗w and ⃗x ′̸ =⃗x

such that (M,⃗ u) |= [ ⃗ X ←⃗x ′,⃗ W ←⃗w] ¬φ, and ( AC3 )⃗ X

is minimal, i.e., no strict subset of ⃗ X satisfies AC1 and AC2. Intuitively, AC1 requires that the cause and the outcome both hold in the actual context, AC2 requires that changing ⃗ X un-der some contingency ⃗ W leads to ¬φ, and AC3 requires that no part of ⃗ X alone would suffice. A contingency ⃗ W is a set of other variables held fixed at their actual values ⃗w while ⃗ X

is changed, so other variables that also affect φ cannot mask or compensate for the change. 

Neural Networks (NNs). We consider NNs that map a vector of Boolean variables to a continuous value, formally represented as the mapping N : {0, 1}d → R, N (⃗x) = 

σL ◦ σL−1 ◦ · · · ◦ σ1(⃗x) where σ i(z) = max(0 , w i · z + bi)

is a ReLU-activated linear layer. 

Branch-and-Bound based Neural Network Analysis. 

NN verification decides whether a property of N ’s output holds over an input region: given bounds on the inputs, it computes sound bounds on N ’s output. IBP (Gowal et al. 2019) propagates an input box through each layer using in-terval arithmetic. When bounds are inconclusive, Branch-and-Bound (BaB) (Bunel et al. 2020) splits the region into sub-regions and recomputes bounds on each, until the prop-erty is certified, refuted, or all sub-regions are explored. 

# 3 Approach 

We compute actual causes of NN predictions under struc-tured input dependencies, modeled by an SCM M, under the modified HP definition of actual causality. We present our approach through a running example. We first show how exhaustive actual-cause computation searches over can-didate causes and contingencies, and then explain how CausEx BaB replaces this enumeration with bound propa-gation and branch-and-bound over regions. 

Problem Setup. The context ⃗u induces the actual assign-ment ⃗x and prediction y = N (⃗x) (Sec. 2). We say an assign-ment ⃗x ′ satisfies the outcome φ if N (⃗x ′) ∈ S, for some fixed 

acceptance set S ⊆ R that specifies what counts as the out-come. This set is a modeling choice, orthogonal to the rest of our approach. Throughout this section, including the running example, we take S = [ τ, ∞), where τ is a hard threshold on the output. Sec. 4 instead specifies φ via a distance metric. 

φ holds when N (⃗x ′) lies within a threshold distance of the actual output, i.e., S becomes an interval around it. Our goal is to find all sets of variables ⃗ X ⊆⃗ V that are actual causes 

(AC1–AC3) of φ, witnessed by an intervention ⃗ X ←⃗x ′ that, under some contingency, falsifies φ.

3.1 Running Example and Exhaustive Search 

We illustrate our algorithm using a concrete example. Con-sider a bank that uses an NN to predict the default risk of loan applicants. Given information about an applicant, the NN outputs a risk score y ∈ [0 , 1] indicating the proba-bility of loan default. The bank classifies an applicant as 

high risk if y ∈ S, for S = [ τ, ∞), with τ = 0 .5, and as low risk otherwise. We model five binary applicant fea-tures: X1 (high income), X2 (negative cashflow), X3 (high expenses), X4 (unsecured exposure), and X5 (has guaran-tor). These features are causally related: high income can cause high expenses; high expenses combined with no high income can cause negative cashflow; negative cashflow with-out a guarantor indicates unsecured exposure. We formalize this as a Structural Causal Model. Fig. 1 il-lustrates the SCM with its structural equations together with the NN classifier N . Each endogenous variable Xi is de-termined by its causal parents and an exogenous variable 

Ui via a structural equation. For root nodes (without par-ents), Xi = Ui. For non-root nodes, Xi is a node-specific Boolean function of its parents, combined with Ui via XOR. Concretely, X3 = X1 ⊕ U3, X2 = ( X3 ∧ ¬ X1) ⊕ U2, and 

X4 = ( X2∧¬ X5)⊕U4. The exogenous variables U1, . . . , U 5

encode all background factors not explicitly modeled. Their role is to capture uncertainty: even with high income, high expenses are not guaranteed, and negative cashflow without a guarantor does not necessarily lead to unsecured expo-sure. Given a fixed context ⃗u = ( u 1, . . . , u 5), all endoge-nous features are fully determined, and the predictor output 

y = N (X1, . . . , X 5) can be computed. We fix the following context for this example throughout the paper: ⃗u = (1 , 1, 0, 1, 0) . This induces the actual assign-ment ⃗x = (1 , 1, 1, 0, 0) 1: the applicant has high income, neg-ative cashflow, high expenses, no unsecured exposure, and no guarantor. The predictor outputs y = N (⃗x) = 0 .66 ≥ τ ,so the bank classifies the applicant as high risk . 3.2 The CausEx BaB Algorithm 

Rather than checking candidate cause and contingency pairs one at a time, we reason about regions that represent many possible role assignments at once. To this end, we mark a variable undecided when it has not been resolved to nat-ural , in which case it follows its structural equation, or to 

contingency , in which case it is fixed to its actual value. Fig. 2 presents an overview of our approach, which we refer to as CausEx BaB . We analyze each region using sound output bounds. Conclusive regions are pruned or confirmed without examining their assignments individually, while inconclusive regions are refined by resolving one undecided variable and recursively analyzing the two resulting subregions. Representing Contingencies as Regions. For a fixed can-didate cause set C, assigning every remaining variable either the natural or contingency role specifies one possible contingency. CausEx BaB compactly represents many such assignments using partial role assignments , in which vari-ables may remain undecided . A region B consists of a partial role assignment together with an interval [ℓX , h X ] for each variable X ∈⃗ V , representing the values that X may take across the contingencies captured by the region. For cause 

and contingency variables, this interval collapses to the cor-responding fixed value. For natural variables, it is induced by the structural equation, while for undecided variables it is the hull of the natural and contingency possibilities. We write B0(C) for the initial region of candidate cause set C, in which the variables in C take the cause role and all remaining variables are undecided . This region represents all possible contingencies for C. We next explain how its intervals are computed and propagated through the SCM. 

Propagating Bounds. To propagate intervals through the Boolean SCM, we use the continuous relaxations of Boolean functions as used in Petersen et al. (2022). 

• NOT (a) = 1 − a • OR (a, b ) = a + b − ab 

• AND (a, b ) = ab • XOR (a, b ) = a + b − 2ab 

These relaxations are exact on Boolean inputs. Each relax-ation is multilinear. As a result, the extrema of a single relaxed gate over its parents’ interval bounds are always attained at a corner of the corresponding interval box. This gives the tightest possible interval for that gate alone. On [0 , 1] , NOT, AND, and OR are additionally monotonic in each argument (NOT decreasing, and AND and OR increasing). For these gates, evaluating the appropriate interval endpoints suffices, whereas the non-monotonic XOR gate requires evaluating all four corners. The resulting map bound SCM processes the variables of M in topological order, applying the ap-propriate role rule using each variable’s already-computed parent bounds. bound NN then propagates the resulting fea-ture bounds through N via IBP (Gowal et al. 2019). Affine layers are bounded using interval arithmetic, and each ReLU maps an interval [ℓ, h ] to [max(0 , ℓ ), max(0 , h )] .

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate CausEx BaB on both synthetic benchmarks and a case study. The synthetic benchmarks allow us to systemati-cally vary graph size, causal structure, maximum cause size, and NN capacity, while the case study demonstrates applica-bility to a real decision-making setting. Because established benchmarks for HP-cause computation in NNs with struc-tured causal inputs are limited, we generate the synthetic benchmarks described below. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

We ran all experiments on an NVIDIA L40S GPU, an AMD EPYC 9375F CPU (32 cores), 192 GB RAM, and Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS. # 5 Related Work 

HP actual causality has been extensively studied in discrete, non-neural models (Leitner-Fischer and Leue 2013; Coenen et al. 2022; Ibrahim and Pretschner 2020), but computing HP causes for NN predictions under structured causal inputs remains underexplored. Closest to our setting, Ibrahim and Pretschner (2020) compute exact HP causes via an ILP en-coding that scales poorly with neural network components. Reyd, Diaconescu, and Dessalles (2025) search heuristically, without formal guarantees and possibly returning incomplete or non-minimal sets. Chockler, Kelly, and Kroening (2025) compute multiple actual causes for black-box image classi-fiers via repeated querying, but assume independent features. Aryan, Chockler, and Mousavi (2026) use an incomplete continuous gradient-based search. We instead provide for-mal guarantees along three dimensions: (i) minimality of the computed causes, (ii) minimality and sufficiency of the used contingencies, and (iii) computation of all valid HP actual causes, over an SCM-structured input space, while scaling to substantially larger instances than the baselines. Feature-attribution methods such as LIME (Ribeiro, Singh, and Guestrin 2016) and SHAP (Lundberg and Lee 2017), and causal variants that relax the independence as-sumption via a given causal graph (Heskes et al. 2020), re-turn importance scores rather than HP actual causes. Minimal abductive explanations (Bassan and Katz 2023) are a dual notion, where a fixed value alone guarantees the prediction rather than a change alone altering it. 

# 6 Discussion 

We keep the SCM separate from the NN because the two components serve different roles. The SCM provides explicit intervention semantics and domain knowledge, while the NN remains the fixed predictive model whose output is being ex-plained. Although the NN may learn statistical dependencies that overlap with those encoded by the SCM, these depen-dencies are not explicitly represented as causal mechanisms. The two components could instead be combined in a neural SCM, but this would require a model specifically learned as an SCM rather than an arbitrary pretrained predictor. 

Beyond Boolean SCMs. For continuous SCMs, verifica-tion methods can still reason soundly about continuous input regions, but exact enumeration of all minimal HP causes no longer reduces to a finite search over assignments. One must specify the admissible intervention space and the relevant no-tion of minimality. Extending our guarantees to that setting is therefore an important but separate problem. Towards this, we experimented with tighter bounds via β-CROWN (Wang et al. 2021). Each iteration’s bound computation took sub-stantially longer to run than IBP, and did not improve overall runtime in our Boolean setting. We conjecture it may help once inputs are continuous. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We formalized NN explanation under structured input depen-dencies as the computation of Halpern–Pearl actual causes over SCM-constrained inputs. We introduced CausEx BaB , a sound and complete algorithm that returns all minimal ac-tual causes together with minimum-cardinality witness con-tingencies. Across SCM–NN benchmarks, CausEx BaB sub-stantially outperforms brute-force, ILP-based, and heuristic search baselines as graph size grows. Future work includes an extension to continuous domains as well as bounding the maximum cause size automatically from the graph structure, an evaluation of our approach on larger networks and ad-ditional real-world datasets, and integrating gradient-based candidate generation with branch-and-bound refinement. A Detailed Preliminaries 

A.1 SCMs 

We use Structural Causal Models to represent and reason about the causal relationships between variables in a system. An SCM is a tuple M = ( ⃗ U ,⃗ V , F) (Pearl 2009) with exogenous variables ⃗ U = {U1, . . . , U d}, endogenous vari-ables ⃗ V = {X1, . . . , X d}, and structural equations F =

{f1, . . . , f d} such that 

Xj := fj (PA j , U j ), j = 1 , . . . , d, (1) where PA j ⊆⃗ V \ { Xj } denotes the set of endogenous parents (direct causes) of Xj .This formulation defines how each variable is generated from its direct causes and some exogenous influence. It cap-tures both the causal structure (via the dependencies among variables) and the functional relationships that determine how each variable is computed from its causes. Given a specific assignment ⃗u = ( u 1, . . . , u d) to the ex-ogenous variables, called a context , the values of the endoge-nous variables ⃗ V are determined by recursive application of the structural equations. Each SCM corresponds to a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each edge represents a direct causal influence. The nodes are represented by the variables Xj while the (directed) edges are represented by Xi → Xj ⇔ Xi ∈ PA j .

Binary SCMs. A binary SCM is a SCM in which all en-dogenous variables take values in the Boolean domain, i.e., 

Xj ∈ { 0, 1} for j = 1 , . . . , d . The exogenous variables Uj

need not be binary. Each structural equation fj is a Boolean function, typically expressed using the operators ∧, ∨, and ¬.

Interventions. An intervention refers to setting a variable to a specific value, independent of its usual causes. Given an endogenous variable X := fX (PA X , U X ), the intervention 

X ← x modifies the model by replacing this equation with the assignment X := x. This enables us to quantify the causal effect of a variable by analyzing how the system behaves when it is manipulated externally, regardless of its original causes. 

Counterfactuals. Interventions modify the SCM so that we can analyze how changes to one variable affect other variables at the population level. In contrast, counterfactuals explain "What would have happened if . . . ?", focusing on individual-level scenarios. They are computed by first deter-mining the values of the exogenous variables from the ob-served effect and then applying an intervention to the model. 

A.2 Halpern and Pearl’s Actual Causality 

SCMs allow us to compute the effects of interventions and simulate alternative scenarios but do not specify what counts as a cause in a particular instance, especially when multiple variables influence the effect. Halpern and Pearl’s definition of actual causality extends this framework by providing cri-teria to identify a minimal, explanatory cause of a specific observed event. This enables reasoning about responsibility and supports the generation of fine-grained causal explana-tions. We adopt the modified definition of actual causal-ity (Halpern 2015), which avoids known problems with pre-emption and spurious causes at lower complexity than earlier versions of the definition. 

Definition. We denote the set of endogenous variables by ⃗V = {X1, . . . , X d}. In the following, we use Halpern and Pearl’s notation, where subsets of ⃗ V are used to define can-didate causes and counterfactual contingencies. A context ⃗u is a specific assignment to all exogenous vari-ables in a SCM. Fixing a context determines the values of all endogenous variables via the structural equations, and there-fore defines the actual world in which causal relationships are evaluated. Let (M,⃗ u) be a causal model with context ⃗u. A set of variables ⃗ X taking values ⃗x is an actual cause of an outcome 

φ in (M,⃗ u) if the following conditions are satisfied: 

AC1 (M,⃗ u) |=⃗ X =⃗x and (M,⃗ u) |= φ. That is, ⃗ X =⃗x

is only considered a cause of φ if both the assignment and the outcome actually occur in the context ⃗u.

AC2 There exists a set ⃗ W ⊆⃗ V \⃗ X of endogenous vari-ables and alternative values ⃗x ′̸ =⃗x for ⃗ X, such that if ⃗w

denotes the actual values of the variables in ⃗ W in context 

(M,⃗ u), then 

(M,⃗ u) |= [ ⃗ X ←⃗x ′,⃗ W ←⃗w] ¬φ. 

This means that by holding a set of variables fixed at their actual values, changing ⃗ X alone is enough to prevent φ

from occurring. The triple (⃗ W ,⃗ w,⃗ x ′) serves as a witness showing that ⃗X ←⃗x ′ satisfies condition AC2 and thus qualifies as a cause of φ.

AC3 ⃗ X is minimal. No subset of ⃗ X satisfies both AC1 and AC2. The modified definition restricts contingencies to the vari-ables’ actual values in the given context ⃗u, rather than allow-ing arbitrary counterfactual settings. This prevents a variable from being designated a cause based only on a constructed contingency that never actually occurred, and instead focuses on whether the candidate cause made a difference under the conditions that actually held.

# C Additional Details on the Approach 

Remark 2. Halpern’s degree of responsibility of C as a cause of φ is defined as 1/(N + 1) , where N is the size of the smallest witnessing contingency (Halpern 2016). Since Step 2 searches for exactly this minimal N , deg( C) is ob-tained directly as a byproduct of the search. The smaller the contingency, the higher the responsibility.

Therefore Alg. 1 terminates.

Remark 3. The theoretical guarantees above assume ex-act arithmetic. In the implementation, every fully resolved witness is re-evaluated using high-precision arithmetic be-fore being returned. To avoid floating-point error causing an incorrect prune or confirmation, decisions within a numeri-cal tolerance of the acceptance-set boundary are treated as inconclusive and refined further. This adds negligible over-head, since the recheck only fires once per returned witness, and rules out large numerical errors, though errors within the tolerance margin itself are not eliminated. 

Remark 4. The algorithm can optionally restrict the outer search to candidate sizes k = 1 , . . . , k max instead of k =1, . . . , |⃗ V |, to terminate early. In that case, the guarantee above only holds up to kmax . A minimal cause larger than 

kmax is then never enumerated as a candidate. Bounding 

kmax is often desirable regardless of runtime, since smaller cause sets are more likely to be interpretable and actionable by a human, whereas a very large cause set may be less desirable as an explanation even when valid. 

Remark 5. Halpern and Pearl’s definition does not specify what counts as an outcome φ for continuous-valued models such as our NN predictor. The acceptance set S is how we close this gap. The soundness and completeness above hold for any choice of S: changing S changes which outcome φ

Alg. 1 decides and the concrete prune/confirm conditions it uses, but not the algorithm itself or its guarantees. A returned pair (C,⃗ w ∗) is thus guaranteed to be an actual cause of this specified outcome .

Dataset. Computing Causes. We flag a household for review when the NN’s output crosses the standard 0.5 threshold, so the question a cause should answer is: why was this house-hold flagged for review? We draw the held-out test instances flagged by the NN, deduplicated by eligibility profile so re-peated households do not inflate the sample: since the SCM has only 20 binary nodes, many real households share an identical profile, and only 11 distinct profiles exist among flagged households in the test split, so we use all 11 rather than a subsample of them. We run CausEx BaB , brute-force, 

ILP , and ACI on each, searching for witnesses that flip the prediction below 0.5, with kmax = |V | and a 180s timeout per method per instance. 

CausEx BaB completes all 11 instances within budget (me-dian 4.60 s, 0/11 timeouts), finding a median of 23 minimal causes, 33 .7% of them singletons. BF also completes every instance (median 43 .24 s). ILP times out on 3/11 , and ACI 

times out on 10 /11 and succeeds only once (Tab. 15). This reverses the ordering seen in Sec. 4, where ACI is the faster of the two at comparable scale. We conjecture this comes from the SNAP SCM’s structure being a poorer match for ACI ’s heuristic search than the random benchmark graphs, while 

ILP benefits here from a simpler acceptance set S (Rem. 5).

Limitations. The SCM is a simplified version of the real rule: it leaves out deductions, state waivers, and special-household cases, so eligible can disagree with what a caseworker actually decided for a given household. The households in our sample were also already certified for ben-efits, not drawn from all applicants, so our results speak to error risk among certified households rather than eligibility determination in general. Derived nodes such as eligible 

are themselves valid intervention targets under our formal-ism, so a returned cause may name one directly, a counter-factual a caseworker could not realize by acting on that node alone rather than on its determinants. We make no claim that a returned cause is the uniquely correct or most ac-tionable explanation, only that it is a provably minimal one under AC1–AC3. The 14.9% spurious-cause figure is spe-cific to this SCM and this predictor. A more complete SCM or a stronger predictor could yield a different number, so it should be read as evidence from this case study rather than a general estimate for SNAP QC targeting.
