Abstract. Single-cell RNA sequencing provides snapshots of cellular states, while RNA velocity offers directional information about their temporal evolution. A fundamental question is whether hidden gene regulatory networks governing cellular dynamics can be recovered from such incomplete observations. In this work, we develop a mathematical framework for regulatory network reconstruc-tion from RNA velocity data by formulating the problem as a sparse dynamical inverse problem arising from coupled transcription–splicing dynamics. We show that passive snapshot observations are generally insufficient to uniquely identify regulatory interactions, revealing an intrinsic limitation of unperturbed measurements. We then prove that controlled perturbations restore identifiability by generating informative regulatory trajectories and characterize conditions for unique recovery. To address noisy and imperfect observations, we introduce an integral reconstruction approach that avoids numerical differentiation and establish finite-sample guarantees for sparse network recovery. Our results provide a mathematical foundation for understanding when and how gene regulatory networks can be decoded from single-cell observations, linking perturbation design, inverse problem theory, and predictive modeling of cellular responses. 

1. Introduction. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides molecular snapshots of heterogeneous cellular populations and has become a fundamental tech-nology for studying dynamic biological processes, including development, regenera-tion, immune responses, and cellular reprogramming [1, 2, 3]. However, because each cell is typically measured only once and destroyed during sequencing, temporal evo-lution cannot be directly observed. RNA velocity was introduced by La Manno et al. [4] to infer local cellular dynamics from the relative abundance of unspliced and spliced messenger RNA, transforming static transcriptomic measurements into direc-tional information about cell-state transitions. Subsequent developments incorporated dynamical transcription models, latent-time inference, and gene-specific kinetic pa-rameters to improve velocity estimation under transient cellular states [5, 6, 7]. While RNA velocity provides information about local gene-expression dynamics, many biological questions concern a higher level of organization: the regulatory mech-anisms that generate these dynamics. Rather than estimating how individual genes change over time, one seeks to recover the underlying gene regulatory network (GRN) describing how transcription factors activate or repress downstream genes. Such reg-ulatory networks provide mechanistic explanations of cellular decision-making and enable prediction of cellular responses under genetic or pharmacological perturba-tions. Consequently, reconstructing GRNs from transcriptomic measurements has become a central problem in systems biology. Computational reconstruction of GRNs has been extensively studied using statis-tical inference, machine learning, causal discovery, and dynamical-system approaches [8, 9, 10]. Recent RNA-velocity-based approaches combine estimated velocities with sparse regression, ordinary differential equations, or causal inference techniques to infer regulatory interactions [11, 12, 13, 14]. In particular, it is unknown whether the regulatory network is uniquely determined by snapshot RNA-velocity observations, what experimental conditions are required to eliminate ambiguities, and how uncertainty in measure-ments and model parameters affects reconstruction accuracy. These questions are closely connected to classical problems in dynamical systems and inverse problems. Structural identifiability studies whether unknown parameters of a dynamical model can be uniquely determined from ideal observations and has been extensively investigated in mathematical biology and systems theory [15, 16]. Related notions of observability and distinguishability characterize whether internal system properties can be inferred from measured outputs [17]. In system identifica-tion, informative inputs and persistent excitation conditions play a fundamental role in determining whether unknown dynamics can be recovered from observed trajectories [18]. Despite these extensive developments, a systematic theory for identifying and recovering gene regulatory networks from RNA-velocity observations remains lacking. Recent perturbation-based single-cell technologies provide new opportunities to address this limitation. Techniques such as Perturb-seq enable systematic measure-ments of cellular responses under genetic interventions [19, 20]. From a mathematical perspective, such perturbations can be viewed as informative experimental inputs that modify system trajectories and potentially resolve ambiguities in inverse recon-struction. This connection motivates a fundamental question: when and how can perturbations make hidden regulatory mechanisms identifiable from single-cell obser-vations? In this work, we address this question by formulating gene-regulatory RNA veloc-ity as a sparse nonlinear inverse problem. Rather than proposing another empirical inference algorithm, we study the mathematical limits of regulatory reconstruction from snapshot observations collected under multiple experimental conditions. The unknown regulatory network appears implicitly in a coupled dynamical system govern-ing transcription, splicing, and degradation. The available measurements consist only of noisy observations of system trajectories together with condition labels, resulting in an inverse problem with partial observations, latent biological time, measurement uncertainty, and structural model error. The first objective of this work is to characterize the fundamental identifiability limits of regulatory inference. We show that under an unperturbed developmental trajectory, distinct sparse regulatory networks can generate identical observable RNA trajectories. The ambiguity is constructive rather than merely local: different regula-tory matrices produce the same transcriptional forcing along the observed trajectory while differing outside the observed state manifold. Consequently, no estimator can distinguish these networks using control observations alone. This establishes an in-trinsic impossibility result for regulatory inference from unperturbed snapshot RNA velocity data. The second objective is to determine how perturbations restore identifiability. We prove that controlled interventions enlarge the space of observable regulator trajec-tories and remove observational equivalence when an aggregated excitation condition is satisfied. For linear regulatory dynamics, this condition is characterized by the positive definiteness of a population information matrix, providing necessary and suf-ficient conditions for exact identifiability. For nonlinear regulation, we derive an anal-ogous local identifiability result through sensitivity analysis. These results establish a mathematical connection between perturbation design and structural identifiability DECODING GENE REGULATORY NETWORKS FROM SINGLE-CELL RNA VELOCITY 3in regulatory network reconstruction. The third objective is to develop a statistically stable reconstruction method. Di-rect estimation based on numerical differentiation is unstable because RNA velocity is inferred from noisy snapshot measurements with uncertain latent time. To over-come this limitation, we derive an equivalent integral formulation of the governing dynamics and formulate reconstruction as a sparse integral regression problem. This formulation is connected to broader approaches in sparse dynamical system identifica-tion [21, 22]. We establish finite-sample recovery guarantees that account for multiple uncertainty sources, including measurement noise, latent-time uncertainty, kinetic-parameter estimation error, numerical quadrature error, and model misspecification. The resulting analysis identifies persistent error sources that cannot be eliminated solely by increasing the number of observations. Finally, we connect the mathematical theory with predictive validation. Rather than evaluating inferred networks solely through edge recovery, we assess their mech-anistic validity by predicting cellular responses under perturbations excluded during model fitting. We further show that theoretical identifiability diagnostics are as-sociated with predictive performance on held-out perturbation conditions, linking inverse-problem theory with experimentally observable cellular responses. The principal contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. 1. We formulate gene-regulatory RNA velocity as a sparse nonlinear inverse problem arising from coupled transcription–splicing dynamics. 2. We prove constructive nonidentifiability of regulatory networks from un-perturbed snapshot trajectories and characterize the resulting observational equivalence. 3. We establish necessary and sufficient conditions under which controlled per-turbations restore identifiability through excitation of regulator trajectories. 4. We derive an integral sparse reconstruction method with finite-sample re-covery guarantees under measurement uncertainty, latent-time uncertainty, kinetic-parameter error, numerical approximation error, and model misspec-ification. 5. We validate the theory through held-out perturbation prediction and demon-strate that identifiability diagnostics provide information about predictive reliability. Section 5 derives the integral sparse estimator and finite-sample recovery guarantees. Section 6 presents numerical experiments verifying the theoretical predictions.

2.5. Inverse problem. The primary objective of this paper is to recover the sparse regulatory matrix A from the collection of noisy snapshot observations {Yi, q i}Ni=1 .This inverse problem differs from classical parameter estimation in ordinary dif-ferential equations in three important aspects. First, only snapshot observations are available, so the trajectories are not observed continuously. Second, biological time is latent and must be inferred jointly with the dynamics. 5. Integral sparse reconstruction. Sections 3 and 4 characterize when the regulatory inverse problem is identifiable. We now consider its numerical reconstruc-tion from noisy snapshot observations. A direct approach is to differentiate the estimated unspliced trajectory and recover the regulatory coefficients through the differential equation. Since both sources of error are unavoidable in snapshot single-cell data, derivative-based reconstruction is typically unstable. 6.5. Interpretation of the recovery bound. The recovery bound provides a quantitative connection between experimental design, statistical accuracy, and bio-logical model uncertainty. The stochastic term σ

q log KM decreases as the number of effective integral ob-servations increases. Here M denotes the number of integration intervals generated from the available trajectories, rather than the raw number of measured cells. There-fore, increasing the amount of informative trajectory data improves the statistical component of the reconstruction error. In contrast, the remaining terms 

δtime , δkin , δquad , δmodel 

represent systematic errors that cannot be removed simply by increasing the number of observations. These quantities arise from latent-time uncertainty, imperfect kinetic-parameter estimation, numerical approximation of trajectory integrals, and deviations between the true biological system and the assumed regulatory model. The restricted eigenvalue constant κ further reveals the role of perturbation de-sign. When perturbation experiments generate sufficiently diverse regulatory trajec-tories, the empirical design matrix contains well-separated regulatory directions and 

κ remains bounded away from zero. Conversely, poorly designed perturbations lead to nearly collinear regulatory features, causing unstable reconstruction even in the presence of large sample sizes. Therefore, the recovery guarantee identifies three fundamental requirements for accurate regulatory inference: (i) sufficient sampling of the underlying trajectories, (ii) accurate reconstruction of latent biological states, and (iii) informative perturbation experiments that excite distinct regulatory directions. This decomposition explains why increasing sequencing depth alone cannot elim-inate all reconstruction errors in snapshot single-cell regulatory inference. 18 L. MENG AND S.
