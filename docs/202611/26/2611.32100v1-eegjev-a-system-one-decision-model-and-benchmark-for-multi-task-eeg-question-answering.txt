## EEGJev: A System One Decision Model and Benchmark for Multi-Task EEG Question Answering 

Dengzhe Hou 1,2* and Kazunori D. Yamada 1,2 

GitHub Homepage Hugging Face Model · Bench 

1Graduate School of Information Sciences, Tohoku University, Sendai, Japan. 

2Unprecedented-scale Data Analytics Center, Tohoku University, Sendai, Japan. 

Abstract 

A single electroencephalographic (EEG) recording can support multiple decoding tasks. Fixed-label tests cannot show how EEG foundation models handle changing questions or options. We introduce EEGJev , a fast decision model that scores candidate answers in one pass. Our benchmark, EEGJev-Bench , covers six question types across 19 public EEG sources. We held the recording and options fixed while switching between questions about attributes paired during training, such as facial expression and the hand participants were asked to use. Across 13 test sources, EEGJev selected a label for the requested attribute with 99.70% accuracy (49.20% under uniform choice). Balanced accuracy for the specific answer was 62.04%. Its 95th-percentile response time was shorter than NeuroLM’s on matched validation questions answered individually. EEGJev lets users switch decoding targets by changing the question. With EEGJev-Bench, researchers can test whether new decoders follow the question more reliably, identify the correct answer more often, or both. 

Keywords: EEG, benchmark, question answering, refusal, foundation models

Fig. 1 EEGJev. CBraMod, fine-tuned on all sources in step 1 and then frozen, turns the window into C × L tokens, and per-source hidden layers add one spatial token. Frozen MPNet reads the question and the offered candidates, each candidate with a marker position. A two-layer bidirectional transformer reads all tokens together and scores each candidate at its marker, with a softmax over the offered candidates. A separately trained Act head decides whether to answer, and D3 is answered by a branch of the same head trained further on D3 questions. Direct attention from candidate tokens to EEG tokens is restricted to the candidate’s window in T1 and to blocks overlapping its half in T2. 

1arXiv:2611.32100v1 [eess.SP] 24 Nov 2026 Electroencephalographic (EEG) decoding tests which aspects of perception and action can be inferred from scalp recordings. An experimental trial can link a recording to several annotations, including the stimulus presented and the response that the participant was asked to make. For the same second of EEG in Figure 2, a decoder should answer “fearful” when asked about the facial expression and “left hand” when asked about the instructed response. Selecting a task-specific classifier fixes the prediction target in advance. A researcher who wants to predict a different attribute of the same trial must then select another classifier or output head. A decoder that accepts a question would let the researcher specify the target when analyzing the recording. Can one decoder follow a change in the requested attribute for the same recording, and how often does it choose the correct answer? LaBraM 1 and CBraMod 2 reuse pretrained EEG encoders across tasks, usually with a separate head for each prediction target. NeuroLM 3 generates answers from instructions. LEAF 4 uses the instruction to score candidate labels, and METIS 5

scores the offered options. Evaluating such models with a different choice set for each question can make the target apparent from the options. For example, choices that contain only expressions or only hand labels reveal which attribute is being asked about. Keeping the EEG and the ordered list of answer options fixed lets us test whether a change in the question changes the attribute of the selected answer. There is also a distinction between asking two questions and requiring two decisions. PhysioBench 6 asks multiple questions about one EEG window. For its paired seizure questions, identifying the seizure state also answers whether a seizure is present (released construction code). BrainBench 7 includes refusal cases without matched answerable controls. Pairing a refusal item with an answerable version tests whether a decoder also responds when a correct option is available. A shared CBraMod encoder represents the signal, and a frozen MPNet encoder 8

represents the question and candidate answers. Jev and Laya are System One models that return decisions directly 9. Following Laya’s design 10 , EEGJev’s decision head reads the EEG and text representations together and scores every offered answer in one pass. Changing the question can thus query a different attribute through the same scoring interface. A separately trained head decides whether to answer, and a branch of the decision head receives further training for ordered categories such as age groups. For two-window comparisons and long-window questions, the decision head uses information about which window or time block the signal tokens come from. The same head thus scores window and time choices as well as attribute labels. We also introduce EEGJev-Bench, which covers six question types across 19 public EEG sources. To build it, we checked the public releases against their original datasets and applied a common processing pipeline to obtain EEG windows. Within each scored source, different participants provide the training and test windows. To assess two predictions from one window, we pair questions whose answers do not determine one another in that source’s training recordings. The pair score requires a correct answer to each question, measuring whether the model can answer both on the same trial. To test refusal, we remove the correct option from one version and a distractor from the other. The two versions share the EEG and the question and offer the same number of choices, with the correct option available in one version. Results 

Question-guided selection of familiar attributes 

We first held both the EEG window and the ordered list of options fixed and changed only which attribute the question asked about. Each list contained four to ten options from two disjoint label sets. All tested attribute pairs appeared in training. Across 13 sources, EEGJev chose a label for the requested attribute with 99.70% test accuracy (49.20% for uniform choice). Balanced accuracy for the specific answer was 62.04% (Figure 3a). When both questions had to be answered correctly, the source-balanced score was 44.37%. We also tested how EEGJev responds when the question points to the wrong attribute. On D1, we replaced the question with one about another task from the same source, keeping the EEG window, candidates and reference answer fixed (Figure 3b). Test balanced accuracy falls by 12.6 percentage points (95% interval for the change, 

−14 .1 to −11 .1). An empty question lowers the score by 1.3 points, an untrained task code by 1.9 and the two held-out phrasings by 2.9 and 1.3. Scores are stable under candidate rotation. With letter codes in place of candidate names, test balanced accuracy is 43.5% with or without the mapping in the question, close to the controls without matching EEG. Training used the mapped format for 10% of D1 and D2 questions. Validation shows the same pattern. The question guides attribute selection, and candidate names help EEGJev identify the specific answer.

Refusal when the correct option is absent 

EEGJev’s D4-trained refusal head (Act) scored 59.2% on test and 58.6% on validation (Figure 5e). On matched test tasks it scores 4.8 points above the confidence threshold on its own predictions ( +4 .2 to +5 .4) and 6.2 above EEGNet’s ( +5 .1 to +7 .3,Figure 5f). Confidence rules for NeuroLM, METIS and LEAF score close to 50%, as does a rule that makes the same decision throughout each group. On their respective matched tasks, Act scores 7.8 points above NeuroLM’s rule ( +6 .0 to +9 .6) and 9.2 above METIS’s ( +7 .7 to +10 .8). NeuroLM’s threshold was calibrated on D4 validation after training on D1–D3. For tasks seen in training, the full-label check scores all answers of the task and refuses when the best one is not offered. On the same 31 D4 test tasks, this rule gives 65.4% for EEGJev and 66.7% for EEGNet (Supplementary Note 11). 7Inference rules for known tasks and ordered-bin scores 

For tasks seen in training, averaging the log-probabilities of EEGJev and the ordinary classifier with equal weights gives 65.2% on test D1 and 63.2% on test D2. This is 1.4 points above EEGJev on D1 ( +1 .0 to +1 .8) and 2.1 points on D2 ( +1 .4 to 

+2 .9), and it is not distinguishable from EEGNet on D1 ( +0 .7, −0.5 to +1 .9) or on D2 ( +0 .2, −1.3 to +1 .6). Averaging and the full-label check were fixed before v1.3 test evaluation and applied to the saved test outputs without additional fitting (Supplementary Note 11). Both apply only to tasks with known training labels. Figure 5c,d shows D3 accuracy by task and mean error in bins. The D3 branch of EEGJev reaches 41.5% on test and EEGNet 42.5%, a paired difference of −1.0

points ( −2.8 to +0 .8). The bin heads of the ordinary classifier reach 42.4%. On the 14 D3 tasks covered by LaBraM, the D3 branch scores 34.0% and LaBraM 39.7% (Figure 4c). The mean bin distance is 0.80 for the D3 branch, 0.83 for the ordinary bin heads and 0.75 for EEGNet. On validation the branch is at 41.3% and EEGNet at 42.7%. Without the branch, the decision head answers D3 at 35.3% on test, 7.2 points below EEGNet (Supplementary Note 12), so the binned questions are matched only with the further-trained branch. 

Questions over two windows and long windows 

Figure 6 compares direct T1 and T2 answers with fixed rules that combine D1 outputs (see “Baselines”). On T1, EEGJev reaches 77.7% on test and on validation. Composing the answer from its own D1 outputs gives 78.1%, so the native answer is not better than the composition here. The composition from EEGNet reaches 80.8% and is 3.1 points above EEGJev (interval 1.8 to 4.4), and the ordinary classifier is 1.1 points above it (0.3 to 2.0). LaBraM (77.5%) is not distinguishable from EEGJev on the 80 tasks it covers, and NeuroLM is 7.1 points lower. With the same early-versus-late training labels, EEGJev read the long window directly and scored 68.31% on 15 matched T2 test tasks from seven sources (Figure 6c). Trained readouts of the frozen one-second EEGNet and shared ordinary classifier both scored 58.62% using time-ordered class probabilities. Using mean probabilities over the window, they scored 53.21% and 52.63%, respectively. EEGJev led the ordered EEGNet readout by 9.69 points. The 95% interval was +7 .43 to +12 .22

Held-out tasks 

A question-reading model can in principle answer a question it was not trained on, which a classifier without an output layer for it cannot. We removed one task at a time from all training, including step 1 of the encoder, its D1 rows, the D2 rows paired with it, its D3 and D4 rows, its T1 pairs and its classifier layer. We then asked the retrained model the task’s validation questions, which no part of the new model had used. Eye state of vrnf is a concept that other sources also ask about. Face gender of asym appears in no other source (several sources ask the gender of the participant, which is a different attribute). Figure 3c,d shows the two held-out tasks. Supplementary Table 6 gives the individual training-seed scores. With vrnf eye state removed, EEGJev answers it at 78.9% balanced accuracy (seeds 76.7 to 80.8%, AUROC 0.86), against 48.6% with shuffled EEG and 83.1% for the model trained on the task. The per-source EEGJev reaches 81.2% (75.8 to 85.0%) against 87.2% in training. Eye state learned from 9other sources thus carries over to a source whose eye-state questions were never seen. Face gender does not transfer. EEGJev reaches 51.7% (46.5 to 60.3%, AUROC 0.53) and the per-source EEGJev 47.9%, at the level of shuffled EEG (49.7 and 50.1%), while the models trained on the task reach 82.1 and 82.5%. One seed of EEGJev is at 60.3% and the other two are below 50%. The evaluation is small (240 questions from five validation persons for vrnf, 3,909 from 14 persons for asym), and the ordinary classifier, EEGNet and LaBraM are not compared here because they have no output for a task removed from training. 

Datasets no model has seen 

On left-or-right-hand motor imagery in two datasets that no model had been trained or evaluated on, Weibo2014 11 with 10 persons and Dreyer2023A 12 with 60, EEGJev stays at chance (49.6% to 52.4%). The ordinary classifier (53.1% to 58.1%) is above it in all six conditions, by 2.2 to 7.0 points with intervals that exclude zero. Supplementary Note 9 describes the setup and few-shot calibration. 

Response time for individual questions 

EEGJev and NeuroLM-B answered the same 192 binary D1 validation questions (Exo walking transitions and LEMON eye state) one at a time, with checkpoints from three training seeds and 576 timed calls per model per platform. On one CPU core, the 95th-percentile response times were 394.97 ms for EEGJev and 3,677.34 ms for NeuroLM-B. On one GPU they were 9.60 and 24.99 ms, respectively. On each platform, EEGJev returned an offered option in all 576 calls and NeuroLM-B in 554. Timing started with preprocessed EEG in host memory, included text processing and answer return, and counted unparseable responses as infinite time to an offered option (Supplementary Note 6). 

Discussion 

EEGJev-Bench measures two decisions separately. Does the answer match the requested attribute, and is its value correct? On attribute pairs seen in training, EEGJev selected the requested attribute with 98.0% to 100% accuracy across 13 test sources. Balanced accuracy for the specific answer ranged from 40.2% to 85.0% (Figure 3a). Task-specific EEGNet achieved higher joint accuracy on paired questions, and EEGJev’s refusal head outperformed EEGNet’s confidence rule. EEGJev scored the offered answers in one pass and had a shorter 95th-percentile response time than NeuroLM-B on the same 192 validation questions answered individually, on both a single CPU core and a GPU. Golan et al. 13 used paired sentences to reveal failures that aggregate language-model scores could miss. Gu et al. 14 evaluated candidate selection in cardiac question answering, and EEGJev-Bench holds the EEG and answer choices fixed to test whether a decoder follows a change in the requested attribute. Most sources taken from UniEQA have one to three test participants. Bootstrap intervals over tasks condition on these participants. Validation is used for model selection or calibration, and the D1 gap between EEGJev and EEGNet is larger on validation than on test. The long-window result rests on 17 tasks from 8 sources, and the event times of T2 are instruction, cue or scoring times. The refusal head scores 59.2%, 9.2 percentage points above the 50% baseline for a rule that ignores 10 the EEG. Letter codes in place of the candidate names bring EEGJev down to the level of the controls without EEG, so it depends on the wording of the candidates. The held-out task results cover one concept seen in other sources and one attribute that no other source asks, and they show that transfer is possible, not how often it occurs. The motor-imagery transfer fills missing electrodes by interpolation, and the training data contain only two persons who imagine left and right hand movements. The decision-head stage of EEGJev trains 806.8 million parameters. Baselines use the benchmark’s splits and preprocessing. The fixed T1 and T2 rules combine D1 outputs. The supervised T2 readouts use the same T2 labels as EEGJev (Figure 6). METIS and LEAF use released weights. Comparisons with them involve differences in model design and training conditions. EEGJev lets users specify what to decode from an EEG recording through a question and returns a probability for each offered answer in one pass. EEGJev-Bench holds the recording fixed while varying the question or the availability of the correct option, so researchers can assess question following, answer accuracy and refusal separately. 

Methods 

EEGJev-Bench 

EEGJev-Bench asks multiple-choice questions about attributes such as the stimulus shown, the sleep stage or the participant’s diagnosis. All results use data version v1.3. 

> Sources and windows

We use recordings from the original releases of all 19 sources (Figure 2, Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Table 1). For 12 sources from Unified_EQA, the public EEG question-answering release in UniEQA 15 , we locate each of its segments in the original recording. The seven added sources are COG-BCI 16 , HBN-EEG 17,18 ,a verbal working memory dataset 19 , the Dortmund Vital Study 20 , ERP CORE 21 ,MPI-LEMON 22 and a resting-state depression dataset 23 . Seventeen sources have scored tasks. The bciciv and is sources have three training persons each and no test persons in this version, so they serve as training material only. Short windows last 1, 2 or 4 s. An event label gives one window from the event, and a state or person label gives at most eight non-overlapping windows per segment. Long windows of 8, 16 or 30 s are cut around an event for the T2 questions. All recordings come from public datasets, and no new data were recorded for this study. For 15 of the 19 sources, the original publications or dataset pages state an ethics approval and the consent of the participants. Those of BCI Competition IV, CHB-MIT and Sleep-EDF state neither, and those of the depression dataset state consent without naming a review board. 

> Question types

D1 asks one attribute of one window. Its options are all train values of that attribute for the same source and window length, so they depend on the cell (source, attribute, window length) and not on the window. Each attribute has three phrasings, and a D1 row uses one of them. Each attribute also has two phrasings that occur in no training text, and every D1 test row is repeated with both of them (D1h). 11 D2 asks two attributes of the same window, each in all three phrasings. The pair is chosen so that neither attribute determines the other over the train segments of the source. D2 is scored per attribute, and a joint score counts a window as correct only when both attributes are answered correctly. D3 asks the model to choose among ordered categories. The questions cover MMSE by clinical cut points ( ≥ 24 , 18–23, < 18 ), age in the Dortmund, HBN and LEMON sources, sleep stage by level (Wake, N1, N2, N3), n-back load, the level of the MATB task and the foreground contrast in HBN. During benchmark construction, we merged two LEMON age bins (55–65 and 65–70 years) after the test label counts showed that the younger bin was empty (Supplementary Note 2). D3 is scored by balanced accuracy and by the mean absolute distance between the chosen and the true bin. D4 tests refusal. For each D1 question with at least three options, D4 adds a refusal row, in which the correct option is removed, and a control row, in which one wrong option is removed and the correct answer remains. Both rows have one option fewer than the D1 question, so the number of options does not reveal the correct decision. T1 shows two windows of the same person and of the same length and asks which of them carries a given label of a D1 task. Each pair gives four linked views, with the two windows in either order and each of the two labels asked. A window enters at most one pair of a task. T2 asks whether an event lies in the first or the second half of a long window. The event is placed near one quarter or three quarters of the window, with a jitter of one sixteenth of its length, and the two placements of an event form one linked group. The events are changes of eye state, seizure onsets, sleep stage changes, cued transitions, changes of flicker frequency and targets in a stimulus stream. Their labels are instruction, cue or scoring times, not measured neural onsets. The train and validation persons have 940,604 short windows and 6,728,339 D1 to D4 questions. The test persons have 117,571 short windows and 878,827 questions, and D1h adds 488,786 rows. The scored T1 tasks have 557,933 train pairs, 94,924 validation pairs and 80,748 test pairs, and the scored T2 tasks have 64,465, 12,315 and 3,368 event groups. 

> Splits

In the 17 scored sources, participants belong to only one split. For the seven added sources, 15% of the persons form the test split and 15% of the rest the validation split, drawn with fixed seeds. For the sources from UniEQA, test and validation persons were drawn with fixed seeds in the numbers of the earlier data version, so most of these sources have few test persons (ten for dementia and photo, three for asym and sleep, two for chbmit, mom, surprise and vrnf, one for exo and gal). Photo and dementia record the same participants and are split jointly. The added sources have between 4 test persons (cogbci) and 91 (dortmund). Results are therefore reported per task and with the number of test persons in mind. 

> Data quality rules

In a study of six EEG datasets, changing preprocessing alone altered up to 42% of trial-level predictions 24 . We use one signal pipeline to construct the benchmark windows. We read each recording from its original file using a fixed channel list. For ERP CORE, we first subtract the mean across the 30 scalp channels at each time point. We apply a notch filter at the mains frequency where a peak is present, resample the whole recording to 200 Hz and standardize each channel within each 12 window. We exclude windows that start in the first 2 s, end in the last 0.5 s, lie within 0.1 s of a discontinuity marker or contain flat or dead channels. We correct labels against the original data, for example by restoring the official bciciv motor imagery labels and cutting each gal window from its own lift (Supplementary Note 2). 

> Evaluation protocol

Each row is scored with its own options in the given order. A task is a cell of one question type. The main metric of D1, D1h, D2 and D3 is balanced accuracy over answer labels, computed within each task. A task is scored only if every offered answer has at least 10 windows on validation and on test, and this task list was fixed on 15 November 2026 from the label counts alone, before any model was trained on this data version. It has 109 D1 tasks in 17 sources, 59 D2 tasks in 10 sources, 22 D3 tasks in 6 sources and 31 D4 tasks in 11 sources. Scores are averaged with equal weight for every source and equal weight for the tasks within a source (the source-balanced mean), so that a source with many tasks does not dominate. D3 also reports the mean absolute bin distance. The D2 joint score averages the nine combinations of phrasings of a window and is balanced over the combinations of the two answers. In the fixed-option experiment, we count a question pair as correct only when both answers are correct and average the scores within each pairing condition, across conditions within each source and then across sources. D4 is scored by a group-balanced criterion. A group holds the D4 rows that share source, attribute, window length and the unordered set of offered labels. Within a group, the criterion is the mean of the refusal recall on refusal rows and the answer rate on control rows, and a task’s score is the mean over its groups that contain both kinds of rows. A rule that does not look at the EEG cannot exploit which label is missing, since every decision that is constant within a group scores exactly 0.5. Every model can refuse by thresholding its calibrated maximum option probability. Per model, one temperature is fitted on validation D1 by negative log-likelihood and one threshold, shared across sources, is chosen on validation D4. Models with their own refusal output are also scored with it. Calibration is reported as the expected calibration error over 15 bins (ECE) of the D1 answers after temperature scaling. T1 and T2 are scored by accuracy. The linked views of a pair or an event group are averaged first, then persons, tasks and sources, so that the four views of a pair do not count as four samples. A T1 or T2 task is scored when validation and test each have at least ten pairs or event groups, which leaves 93 T1 tasks in 16 sources and 17 T2 tasks in 8 sources. Chance is 50% for both. 

> Controls without EEG

Three controls receive no information from the EEG of the window being asked about (an answer prior, a text-only Laya checkpoint 10 and shuffled EEG). On D1 they reach 43.5% to 43.8% on validation and test, the level of the answer prior (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Table 2). 

EEGJev 

EEGJev answers a multiple-choice question about EEG. It receives one window, or two windows for T1, the question text and the offered candidates. It returns one score per candidate and, from a separate output, whether it should refuse. The candidate set can change at run time, and nothing is generated or parsed (Figure 1). 13 EEG encoder. 

The encoder is CBraMod 2 with the official pretrained weights. It reads each source’s native channels at 200 Hz in patches of one second, so a window of C channels and 

L seconds gives C × L tokens of width 200. In a first step, one encoder is fine-tuned on the D1 labels of all 19 sources together. Each source and window length has its own hidden layers, namely the two-layer head that CBraMod places on the flattened tokens ( 200 CL to 800 to 200 units, with ELU and dropout 0.1), and each cell has its own linear output layer. The encoder is then frozen. The same step-1 model with its per-cell output layers is the ordinary classifier described in “Baselines”. 

> Decision head.

The decision head is a small bidirectional transformer that reads one sequence per question. The sequence holds the window’s C × L CBraMod tokens, one spatial token, and the text tokens of the question and of each candidate. Each CBraMod token is projected to width 256 and receives a channel and a patch embedding. The spatial token summarizes the whole window. New hidden layers of the same shape as the step-1 heads, one set per source and window length, map the flattened CBraMod tokens to 200 units, and a linear layer and a learned embedding turn the result into one more token. CBraMod has no vocabulary, so the text is read by a frozen MPNet encoder (all-mpnet-base-v2 8), whose 768-dimensional token outputs are projected to width 256. Segment and question-type embeddings mark which part of the sequence a position belongs to, and each candidate has a marker position. The head has two pre-norm layers with four attention heads, a feed-forward width of 1,024 and dropout 0.1. Every position attends to every other position, so each candidate is scored in the context of the others. A two-layer scorer reads each marker and returns the candidate’s logit, and a softmax over the offered candidates gives the answer probabilities. This joint scoring of candidates at markers follows the typed decision model Laya 10 . EEGJev uses none of Laya’s weights. 

> Two windows and long windows.

The same head answers T1 and T2. For T1, the tokens of both windows enter the sequence, and a learned embedding marks each token as belonging to window A or window B. The two candidates name the windows, and the tokens of a candidate attend to the EEG tokens of its own window only. For T2, the long window is cut into consecutive blocks of at most 4 s (1 s for erpcore, which has hidden layers for 1 s only), the frozen encoder reads each block separately with the hidden layers of its source and length, and a learned embedding gives the position of each block. The tokens of the candidate for the first half attend to the EEG tokens of the blocks in the first half only, and likewise for the second half. A block that spans the midpoint belongs to both halves. No new encoder layers are added for long windows. The decision-head stage trains 806.8 million parameters. Its 52 sets of spatial-token hidden layers contain 804.7 million (99.74%), and its two transformer layers together contain 1.58 million. With the frozen CBraMod and MPNet, EEGJev is therefore not a small model (Supplementary Note 6). 

> Refusal output.

A separate Act head decides whether to answer. It is a two-layer network (260 to 256 to 2 units) that reads the first output position of the decision head and four features of the answer distribution, namely the top probability, the gap between the top two 14 probabilities, the entropy divided by log K and the number of offered candidates K

divided by 255. EEGJev refuses when the Act head’s refusal output is the larger of its two outputs. 

> Training.

With the encoder frozen, one decision head is trained on all question types except D3 and D4. The 3,258,618 D1 and D2 questions of the training persons are used for 18,900 updates of 64 questions with soft cross-entropy over the offered candidates. Options are shuffled, 25% of the questions offer a random subset of their options, and 10% replace the option texts by letters whose mapping is stated in the question. For a D1 question whose source asks another attribute at the same window length, a quarter of the draws offer the labels of both attributes together, so that the question decides which attribute is asked. Mixed into the same schedule are 10,463 updates on T1 pairs and 1,913 updates on T2 event groups, 31,276 updates in all. The Act head is trained afterwards, with the decision head frozen, on pairs of D4 training rows, to refuse on refusal rows and to answer on control rows. The decision head sees no D3 questions, and its own D3 balanced accuracy is 34.1% on validation. A D3 branch therefore continues from the trained head on D3 training questions mixed with D1 and D2 questions and answers the D3 rows only. All D1, D2, D4, T1 and T2 results use the main head. Three seeds are trained, and each seed has its own step-1 encoder. Supplementary Note 4 summarizes the training schedule. 

Experimental setup 

> Baselines

Baselines use the benchmark’s windows, splits and scorer on the tasks they cover. Paired comparisons use rows that both models score. These scores describe perfor-mance under our benchmark protocol. EEGNet 25 and LaBraM 1 are trained for each D1 and D3 task. LaBraM has no models for chbmit, hbn and sleep, whose channels its channel table does not cover. The ordinary classifier is EEGJev’s step-1 model (see “EEGJev”). It does not read the question. These three answer a D2 row with the model or output layer for the requested attribute. The default D4 refusal rule uses a confidence threshold. We report the full-label check separately where available. Three baselines read the question. NeuroLM 3 is instruction-tuned on the D1 and D2 training questions of the benchmark without text-only batches, followed by a short stage on D3 questions, and has no input for hbn and sleep. METIS 5 and LEAF 4

are run from their released checkpoints without training on the benchmark. METIS scores the option letters in one forward pass. LEAF covers 12 of the 17 scored sources and is reported at its three instruction levels. As comparison rows, we also build the ordinary classifier and EEGJev on per-source encoders. Supplementary Note 5 describes the training and inference settings. The fixed comparisons in Figure 6 combine each baseline’s D1 outputs without fitting to T1 or T2 labels. For T1, the composed answer is the window to which the model’s D1 output gives the higher probability of the asked label. For T2, the model’s one-second D1 output is computed on every consecutive second of the long window. For a change of state, the answer is the half that holds the second at which the mean output before and after differs most, and for a target in a stream it is the half that holds the second with the highest target probability. The photo events have no matching D1 attribute, so 15 of the 17 T2 tasks can be composed. These 15 rules were fixed before any model output existed. The same compositions applied to EEGJev’s own D1 answers serve as a control. For the supervised T2 comparison, we kept the one-second EEGNet and shared ordinary classifiers frozen and fitted a logistic regression for each task and seed. The input was either the class probabilities concatenated in time order or their mean across the window. We standardized each feature using the training split and selected the inverse regularization strength ( C = 0 .01 , 0.1, 1 or 10 ) on validation, choosing the smaller value in a tie. The selected readouts were then applied to the test split. 

> Protocol

The tables average three seeds for each trained model. We use validation for checkpoint selection where applicable and to fit temperatures and D4 thresholds. NeuroLM uses the checkpoint after its final 300-step stage (Supplementary Note 5). The model, training recipe, task list and primary metrics were frozen before the v1.3 test. Further v1.3 analyses include fixed-option question switching, participant and source resampling, supervised T2 readouts and full-label refusal rescoring. Supplementary Notes 12, 11 and 14 describe D3 head scores, averaging and the paired D4 comparisons. Most of the earlier sources have few test participants (see “Splits”). Differences between models are computed per task on the rows that every model in the comparison scored. They are given as the source-balanced mean of the paired task differences with a 95% interval from a bootstrap over tasks within sources (10,000 resamples). These task-based intervals condition on the participants evaluated. For the D1 comparison of EEGJev and EEGNet, we also computed a paired 95% percentile interval from 10,000 resamples of participants within sources, keeping each participant’s windows together across both models and all three seeds. The same draw was used for participants shared by dementia and photo. For matched T2 readouts, we also used 10,000 paired resamples to estimate intervals. One analysis resampled participants with matched T2 data within fixed sources. The other resampled sources first and then drew participants from each selected source’s test pool. Both placements of an event stayed with their participant. The scores in the tables are each model’s own source-balanced means over the tasks it covers, so they can differ slightly from the paired differences. Supplementary Note 6 reports model sizes and response times.

Windows follow what a label refers to in time. Event labels describe a stimulus, a cue or a trial condition that starts with the segment. Such a segment gives one window from its start. State and person labels, such as eye state, sleep stage, seizure or diagnosis, give non-overlapping windows, at most eight per segment, so that long recordings do not dominate. Windows last 1, 2 or 4 s (1 s only for erpcore, whose trials are shorter than 2 s, and for surprise, and 1 and 2 s for mom), stay within their 22 segment and are never padded with zeros. In chbmit, pre-seizure windows are cut backward from the seizure onset, so all of them lie within 32 s of it. The OpenNeuro metadata archived for our source checks identify mom v1.1.0, photo v1.0.6 and dementia v1.0.9. The dementia recordings and participant table are identical in v1.0.9 and the cited v1.0.8. The UniEQA release does not specify which OpenNeuro snapshots it used for these three sources. 

Supplementary Note 2. Data quality rules 

> Signal pipeline.

We use a fixed channel list and order for each source and exclude recordings with missing channels. For 50, 60 or 100 Hz, we apply a zero-phase notch filter to the whole recording if the median power ratio relative to frequencies 3–8 Hz away is at least 3 in sampled training recordings. This is the only filter. We drop windows with all channels flat for at least 0.1 s or any channel flat throughout. Only ERP CORE is re-referenced, by subtracting the mean of its 30 scalp channels 21 . We retain 120 of HBN’s channels E1–E128 after dropping eight that are often constant, 62 channels excluding Cz for COG-BCI, the 17 common channels for LEMON and 62 channels for the depression rest dataset. 

> Source corrections.

• In bciciv, we exclude three artificial recordings 31 . Matching windows to the official 100 Hz calibration cues revealed reversed UniEQA labels. Lateralized band power supported the official mapping, which we restored. • We label the two vrnf rest periods in recording order (open eyes, then closed eyes). Posterior 8–13 Hz relative power was higher in the second period for 33 of 34 checked participants. We omit eye-state questions for the participant whose signal showed the reverse pattern. • In photo, the original flash markers distinguish stimulation from rest after the flashes stop. • In gal, we align each window to its own lift in the original WS files. The recorded surface codes and grip-to-load ratios support exchanging the labels silk and sandpaper and retaining suede. • In exo, we read the 320 affected segments at 100 Hz to correct early starts caused by truncation to 99 Hz. • For one chbmit participant, we derive 18 bipolar channels by taking electrode differences in 26 common-reference segments. Their amplitudes were similar to those of the participant’s native bipolar segments. 

> Question eligibility and participant attributes.

We omit training cells with a single answer class because window length would reveal the answer. Scored tasks have at least 10 windows per answer in each of validation and test. We merged the LEMON age bins of 55–65 and 65–70 years after checking test label counts and finding no test participant in the younger bin. We hold age and gender constant across each participant’s windows.

Supplementary Note 4. Training details of EEGJev 

Step 1. We fine-tuned for 50 epochs and kept the checkpoint with the highest mean validation balanced accuracy over cells. 

Decision head. Over 50 epochs, we sampled sources in proportion to the square root of their question counts and window lengths in proportion to their counts. We weighted questions by inverse answer frequency within each cell and kept both questions from each D2 window together. D1 draws offering two attributes used up to 12 options and omitted the subset and letter augmentations. We used 557,933 T1 training pairs and 64,465 T2 training event groups. Each sampled pair or group contributed one linked view under the same sampling weights. We selected the checkpoint on D1 and D2 validation questions, with up to 300 per task. 

Act and D3 heads. The frozen decision head supplied features to the Act head, which used up to 2,000 D4 pairs per cell for 11 epochs of 788 updates with 64 rows each. Pairs from 15% of training windows were held out for epoch selection. The D3 branch continued from the trained head for six epochs of 400 updates, with each batch split evenly between D3 questions and a pool of D1 and D2 questions. 

Supplementary Note 5. Baselines 

EEGNet and LaBraM. 

EEGNet 25 uses the official architecture (revision 4a512e5) in PyTorch. Across three seeds, 134 tasks including three unscored bciciv tasks yielded 402 models, each trained for 500 epochs and selected by validation cross-entropy. LaBraM 1 uses the official 50-epoch TUAB recipe and an effective batch of 512, or the full training set for four smaller tasks. Selection used validation accuracy. The 113 covered tasks yielded 339 models across three seeds. Validation and test inference used deterministic GPU settings. 

Ordinary classifier. 

Its D3 bin heads use frozen step-1 features and the same D3 training questions as EEGJev’s D3 branch. The per-source variants fine-tune one encoder per source on its own D1 labels and follow the same recipe thereafter. 

NeuroLM, METIS and LEAF. 

NeuroLM 3 used five epochs on D1 and D2 without text-only batches, then 300 updates with half D3 questions. All tracks use the final checkpoint. Its three seeds used benchmark windows with the official filter. METIS 5 received EEG in volts. LEAF 4 used its released v1.0 checkpoint in three modes, with no instruction, the question alone, or the question and candidate prototypes. For cognitive EEG analysis, CogEEGAgent maps natural-language questions to registered analysis workflows 63 . EEGJev uses questions to select candidate answers for supplied EEG windows. 24 Supplementary Note 6. Compute and parameters 

The decision-head stage of EEGJev trains 806.8 million parameters, and the model with its frozen CBraMod encoder has 811.7 million (899.6 million with the 19 per-source encoders), to which the frozen MPNet text encoder (109.5 million) is added. The 52 sets of spatial-token hidden layers account for 804,692,000 of the 806,770,979 trainable parameters (99.74%). Their first layer grows with the number of channels and the window length. The hidden layers of the ordinary classifier are large for the same reason. Offline evaluation of EEGJev on one CPU thread at 32-bit precision took 6.8 to 7.6 hours per seed for the same 649,834 D1 and D2 test questions. Across the three runs, the average was about 0.04 s per question, including scoring and writing the results. 

> Single-question response time.

We timed EEGJev and NeuroLM-B on the same 192 binary D1 validation questions about walking transitions (Exo) and eye state (LEMON). For each source, we used 32 questions at each window length (1, 2 and 4 s). We tested both models on one Intel Xeon Gold 6126 CPU core (2.60 GHz), then repeated the comparison on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Max-Q GPU. Both models used 32-bit floating point. TensorFloat-32 was disabled on the GPU. On each platform, for each of three training seeds, the models answered 12 separate warm-up questions followed by the 192 timed questions one at a time. We alternated which model answered first. Timing began with the preprocessed EEG window in host memory and the question and options available as text. It included question and option encoding and scoring for EEGJev, and tokenization, EEG input assembly, generation of five tokens and answer parsing for NeuroLM. For the GPU run, we included the input transfer and synchronized the device at both ends of each timed call. Model loading, signal acquisition and offline preprocessing took place before timing. For each model and platform, we pooled 576 timed calls. The 95th percentile was the shortest time by which at least 95% of calls had returned an offered option. We assigned infinite time to a response that could not be parsed as an offered option. On each platform, EEGJev returned an offered option on all 576 calls and NeuroLM on 554. The NeuroLM-to-EEGJev P95 time ratios were 9.31 on the CPU and 2.60 on the GPU. Counting parsing failures at their observed return times gave NeuroLM a 95th-percentile response time of 3,664.11 ms on the CPU and 24.92 ms on the GPU.

Supplementary Note 9. Datasets no model has seen 

We also asked the frozen models left-or-right-hand motor imagery on two datasets that no model had been trained or evaluated on, Weibo2014 11 with 10 persons and Dreyer2023A 12 with 60. The task itself occurs in bciciv, so EEGJev, the ordinary classifier, EEGNet and LaBraM use their bciciv parts. The new recordings are laid out on the 59 bciciv electrodes. Electrodes that a dataset has are copied, and each missing one is the mean of its two nearest recorded neighbours on the official bciciv coordinates (18 missing in Weibo2014, 32 in Dreyer2023A). LEAF maps the native electrodes with its own table. Windows start at the cue. EEGJev stays at chance on both datasets (49.6% to 52.4%) and is below the ordinary classifier in all six conditions, by 2.2 to 7.0 points with intervals that exclude zero. EEGNet reaches 52.5% to 57.5%. LEAF does best on Weibo2014 (63.6% at level 2 and 2 s) but is at chance on Dreyer2023A, and LaBraM’s bciciv models output almost a single class. No model does well. In the bciciv training data only two persons imagine left and right hand movements, so this task has little training signal to begin with. We tested whether a few labelled windows from a new person recover the task. For each person and window length, k labelled windows per class calibrate a logistic regression on the frozen features of the model, and the person’s other windows are scored, with 20 random draws of the labelled windows. With 20 windows per class, EEGJev reaches 51.9% to 60.8% and the ordinary classifier 55.4% to 69.9%. We average scores over participants, draws and three seeds. 

Supplementary Note 10. Fixed options and text controls 

> Fixed EEG and options.

We asked two questions about the same window, one for each of two attributes. Both offered the same four to ten labels in the same order, with disjoint label sets for the two attributes. All tested pairs were available during training. Attribute match required selecting a label of the requested attribute. Answer balanced accuracy required selecting the correct label. Uniform choice uses the fraction of the offered labels that belong to the requested attribute. Each split had 190 eligible combinations of source, window length, attribute pair and requested attribute, with 409,461 validation questions and 380,570 test questions. The source-balanced means across three seeds were 99.71% attribute match and 63.25% answer balanced accuracy on validation, and 99.70% and 62.04% on test (Figure 3a). For the paired score, both attributes had to meet the benchmark’s eligibility criteria. The 90 eligible pairing conditions yielded 201,963 validation pairs and 187,770 test pairs. A window could contribute to several attribute pairs. Both answers were 26 correct on 45.19% of validation pairs and 44.37% of test pairs after averaging within each condition, within each source and then across sources.

Supplementary Note 11. Inference rules for known tasks 

Both rules use only saved outputs of the frozen models and apply to tasks whose full answer set was seen in training. Both rules were fixed before v1.3 test evaluation and applied to the saved v1.3 validation and test outputs without additional fitting. 

Full-label check for D4. 

For a D4 question, the model first scores the complete answer set of the task, that is, the options of the matching D1 question of the same window. If the best of these answers is among the offered options, the model answers with it, and otherwise it refuses. Across the same 31 test tasks, the full-label check gives group-balanced scores of 65.4% for EEGJev, 66.7% for EEGNet and 65.5% for the ordinary classifier. The paired difference (EEGJev minus EEGNet) is −1.3 percentage points (95% interval 

−2.0 to −0.7 from resampling tasks within sources). EEGJev’s Act head scores 59.2%. 

Averaging with the ordinary classifier. 

For D1 and D2 questions, the log-softmax over the offered options of EEGJev and of the ordinary classifier of the same seed are averaged with weight 0.5 each. The weight was fixed at 0.5 before v1.3 test evaluation. Both models share the step-1 encoder, so part of the gain is the effect of combining two output layers. The averaged D2 answers had a joint score of 42.1% on the test set.

Supplementary Note 13. External comparison with CBraMod 

EEGJev and CBraMod 2 used the same splits from the released CBraMod preprocessing, with three seeds and model-specific validation selection. On PhysioNet-MI 34,64,65 , their test balanced accuracies were 59.3% and 61.7%, respectively. On Mumtaz2016 66,67 , they were 93.6% and 89.3%. The CBraMod paper reports 10 Mum-taz2016 test participants, while our split had 11. EEGJev’s gain came mainly from one participant with depression. The CBraMod reruns scored below the published five-seed values of 64.2% and 95.6%. Mumtaz2016 reports ethics approval and written consent 67 . The PhysioNet-MI dataset page does not report ethics approval.
