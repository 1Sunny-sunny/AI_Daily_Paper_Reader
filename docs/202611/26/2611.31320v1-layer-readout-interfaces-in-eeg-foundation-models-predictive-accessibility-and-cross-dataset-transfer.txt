# Layer–Readout Interfaces in EEG Foundation Models: Predictive Accessibility and Cross-Dataset Transfer 

Yanlin Fu Wei Liu Liming Zhao 

QMindTech, Shanghai, China 

## Abstract 

Intermediate layers often outperform final layers when foundation models are used as frozen feature extractors, but the source of this advantage is unclear. We study this question in EEG foundation models by separating three factors: how representations are read out, whether intermediate layers provide predictive utility beyond the final-layer interface, and whether useful depth structure transfers across datasets. Across five models and three tasks, improving the final-layer readout consistently recovers substantial predictive performance, yet intermediate layers remain advantageous within frozen extraction. We also find that layers selected on one dataset remain useful on another across five dataset pairs and four task families, and that a compact set of source-derived task directions retains predictive value after transfer in sleep staging. Together, these results show that predictive utility varies across depth, and its usefulness depends on how representations are read out and transferred. More crucially, source-selected layers provide a reusable feature interface across datasets, reducing the need for target-side layer search while outperforming final-layer baselines on target datasets. 

## 1 Introduction 

Electroencephalography foundation models (EEG-FMs) have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for neural decoding, leveraging large-scale pre-training to learn rich, transferable brain representations across diverse paradigms and recording configurations. However, when evaluating these frozen encoders for downstream tasks, probes on intermediate layers frequently outperform those on the final layer. This observation raises a fundamental question: how much of the apparent final-layer disadvantage reflects differences in predictive representations, and how much arises from how hidden tokens are aggregated? In the context of EEG FMs, the answer is tightly coupled with the readout mechanism. Standard global averaging over spatial channels and temporal steps deliberately discards structured dynamics, whereas channel- or dispersion-preserving summaries retain them. Consequently, a weak final-layer baseline may be less a limitation of the representation itself than a symptom of a suboptimal readout. While recent work highlights that layer selection and pooling strategies profoundly impact frozen representation decoding across vision and biosignal domains (1, 2), downstream evaluations remain acutely sensitive to choices in probing protocols and classification heads (3–5). (1) selects inter-mediate features using target-task data, whereas (2) examines pooling and block-wise probing in EEG-FMs. Neither directly tests whether a layer-readout interface selected on one downstream EEG dataset remains useful on another without repeating target-side layer selection. These insights leave two critical questions unresolved: Do intermediate layers still offer additional predictive utility once the final-layer readout is selected? And can a source-selected layer-readout interface be reused across other datasets? We structure our investigation around three hypotheses, and our findings to the hypotheses are as illustrated in Figure 1. First, we hypothesize that readout mismatch accounts for a substantial 

Preprint. arXiv:2611.31320v1 [cs.LG] 23 Nov 2026 portion of the final layer’s apparent performance gap ( H1 ). Second, intermediate layers may provide additional predictive utility after fixing the final-layer readout ( H2 ). Third, we test whether a source-selected layer-readout interface remains useful on another dataset of the same task, and whether source-derived task directions retain predictive utility after transfer ( H3 ). We systematically test these hypotheses by isolating readout selection from depth selection, assessing dimensionality-dependent residual utility, and validating cross-dataset transfer through explicit layer controls and subspace interventions. By evaluating five representative foundation models across motor imagery and two event-related po-tential (ERP) paradigms, we first reproduce the widespread empirical finding that intermediate layers often outperform final-layer representations. Selecting the readout improves final-layer prediction in every condition, and selecting the layer yields further gains in most conditions. These interface choices also alter the relative ranking of foundation models. We further use cross-fitted residual augmentation to assess incremental predictive utility and its sensitivity to feature dimensionality. Finally, source-selected layers remain useful in within-task cross-dataset transfer for sleep staging and motor imagery, as well as SSVEP and P300, outperforming final-layer baselines in all ten CBraMod/REVE comparisons across five dataset pairs. Source residual directions also improve sleep transfer across three model families. In sleep staging, removing these source task directions drops target classifier performance significantly more than matched controls, though refitting the classification head recovers most of the accuracy. Together, these results separate readout artifacts from depth-dependent predictive utility and support selecting the final-layer readout before evaluating depth, then reusing the source-selected interface across datasets.

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 EEG foundation models and their evaluations 

Early work on EEG pretraining laid the foundation with two main approaches: BENDR introduced contrastive self-supervised learning (6), while MAEEG focused on reconstructing masked EEG features (7). Later models expanded on these ideas. BIOT introduced support for varying channel setups (8), and both EEGPT and EEG2Rep trained on masked signals by using learning objectives directly in the representation space (9, 10). The five models we examine vary in architecture, tokenization, and pretraining objectives. LaBraM uses spectrum prediction to learn discrete codes across different datasets (11). CBraMod separates spatial and temporal information using a cross-attention mechanism paired with masked patch reconstruction (12). REVE combines masked autoencoding with positional embeddings to handle flexible electrode setups and varying signal lengths (13). LUNA uses learned queries to compress channels into a fixed set of hidden tokens, decoupling feature representations from the physical layout of the electrodes (14). Finally, CSBrain divides signals across temporal windows and brain regions using structured sparse attention (15). Because these designs preserve different aspects of the input signal, both the choice of intermediate layer and how its outputs are summarized directly affect what information reaches the downstream classifier. Standard evaluations usually test these models either by training a simple linear classifier on top of frozen features or by fine-tuning the whole network on a new task. Recent benchmarks have standardized probing on frozen encoders (3) and compared how models handle different adaptation 2strategies and signal perturbations (4). Classifier architecture choice also introduces variability, though most prior comparisons focus on full fine-tuning scenarios (5). In our primary analysis, we keep the encoder fixed and focus on how feature extraction varies across both layer depth and readout design, benchmarked against the standard final-layer baseline. 

2.2 Extracting information from intermediate layers 

It is well established that feature usefulness varies with model depth and depends heavily on how closely the pretraining task aligns with the downstream target (16). Training independent linear classifiers at each layer offers a straightforward way to trace how class separability evolves across a model (17). For EEG models specifically, preserving the underlying token layout often improves downstream accuracy: simply flattening tokens frequently works better than averaging them, and intermediate blocks can contain rich, useful representations (2). Combining features from multiple layers can similarly boost performance on new tasks (1), a trend also observed in time-series and audio models (18, 19). These insights highlight why we need to evaluate not just which layer to extract features from, but also how those features are summarized. Building on this, we examine how readout and depth jointly affect predictive utility, and whether source-selected layer-readout interfaces remain useful across datasets. Separating a practical feature summary from a genuinely rich representation is also a key challenge in language modeling. For instance, pooling attention across layers can improve sentence embeddings (20). Conversely, certain dominant feature dimensions can artificially skew similarity metrics without reflecting actual model behavior (21), often driven by frequency artifacts in the inputs (22, 23). These observations demonstrate that conclusions about representation geometry or feature quality are highly sensitive to the choice of readout. 

2.3 Evaluating feature utility and representations 

A classifier’s accuracy reflects not only the quality of the underlying representation, but also the classifier’s own capacity to learn. Prior studies use synthetic control tasks or measure the learning effort required to extract information to avoid overestimating representation quality (24, 25). Merely being able to decode a feature does not mean the model actually relies on it to make predictions (26). To test whether features are functionally important, we can selectively remove specific information from the representation and measure the impact on downstream task performance (27–29). In EEG research, feature removal has typically focused on explicit, predefined physiological properties (30). Here, we adopt a different approach: we isolate residual directions under a specified final-to-intermediate mapping and feature dimension, and test their predictive utility across datasets using matched augmentation and deletion controls.

3.4 Cross-dataset Transfer and Subspace Interventions 

To evaluate the transferability of layer depth and residual dynamics, we freeze the source-selected configuration (ℓs, r s) and evaluate it on target datasets, re-fitting only downstream classification heads and standardizations using target training data. Layer controls use the same readout: Final, First (the first encoder block, H01), the mean score over all non-final layers (Random), and a layer selected on target development participants (Target). The source-selected layer and readout are determined using source development data and held fixed during target evaluation, whereas Target selects the layer using target development participants under the same readout. To assess the transferability of source-derived predictive directions, we construct a task-relevant subspace from source residuals and evaluate both coordinate augmentation of the target final-layer features and direction removal from the target intermediate representation. 3.5 Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocols 

We evaluate five representative EEG foundation models (CBraMod, REVE, LaBraM, LUNA, and CSBrain) on motor imagery (OpenBMI; 31), two ERP tasks (ERP CORE N170 and N400; 32), and emotion recognition (SEED-V; 33). Within-task cross-dataset transfer uses HMC (34) to ISRUC-1 (35) for sleep staging, and OpenBMI to BCI Competition IV-2a (36) for motor imagery. Additional pairs are OpenBMI–WBCIC-SHU, Benchmark–BETA (SSVEP), and ERP CORE P3–Won2022 (P300). Evaluation strictly follows participant-disjoint outer folds to prevent selection bias (37, 38). Per-formance is measured using participant-balanced accuracy (BA) for motor imagery and SEED-V, participant-balanced AUROC for ERP paradigms, and macro-F1 for sleep staging. For signal-derived physiological probing, we additionally report the participant-averaged coefficient of determination (R2). Statistical significance is assessed via paired 95% bootstrap confidence intervals resampled at the participant level across all conditions. All implementation and pre-processing details are provided in Appendices A–F. For signal-derived probing, we compare raw EEG, three independently initialized CBraMod encoders, and all 12 layers of pretrained CBraMod using Ridge regression. PCA200 is used for the primary comparison, with native-dimensional controls for raw EEG, H01, and H12. For parameter-efficient adaptation, rank-8 LoRA adapters ( α = 16 ) are applied to all 12 CBraMod blocks on N170, while 5the original backbone weights remain frozen. Both experiments use five outer participant folds and three seeds; seed-level scores are averaged within participants before bootstrap estimation. Adapted representations are evaluated using freshly fitted native-dimensional diagnostic probes, separately from the adaptation classifier. We evaluate both the pretrained-validation-selected layer after adaptation and a layer independently reselected using inner validation, without accessing outer-test labels.

4.2 Residual predictive utility depends on feature dimensionality 

We next examine whether intermediate-layer residuals provide additional predictive utility under a compressed feature interface. In the PCA200 analysis, cross-fitted residual augmentation improves final-layer prediction in 13 of 15 conditions. Relative to shuffled residuals matched in distribution, covariance, and scale, the residual contribution Eresid also has a positive paired confidence interval in 13 of 15 conditions (Figure 8A; Table 7). Nonlinear mappings and task heads also retain positive residual gains in the diagnostic conditions (Appendix C). The utility of residual representations depends strongly on dimensionality. In our dimensionality scan, expanding the final-layer representation from PCA200 to its native dimension while keeping the residual features at PCA200 reduces augmentation gains for CBraMod and REVE from 9.57% and 8.96% to 1.22% and 0.48% on N170, and from 6.11% and 2.12% to 0.71% and −0.01% 

on N400. When both representations are kept at their native dimensions, these differences drop to −0.16% and +0 .25% on N170, and +0 .14% and −0.99% on N400, showing no consistent improvement over the baseline (Table 10). Likewise, under a matched 200-feature budget, neither residual augmentation nor fusion outperforms the stronger single-layer baseline. Together, these findings indicate that the incremental predictive value of residual features is interface-dependent, rather than reflecting information intrinsically absent from the full final-layer representation. Detailed controls for dimensionality, model capacity, and fusion strategies are provided in Appendix C.

4.4 Source residual directions remain useful after transfer 

We next test whether a compact subspace defined from source residuals retains predictive utility on an unseen target dataset. Source residuals are used to define a low-dimensional task subspace, which is then applied to target representations. Appending four source-derived residual coordinates to final-layer features improves target macro-F1 by 1.17, 0.67, and 0.97% for CBraMod, REVE, and LaBraM (Figure 4A). Gains over ordinary supervised-coordinate augmentation, using the same four-dimensional feature budget, are 0.54, 0.44, and 0.58%, all with positive paired intervals. Removing these directions with the target scaler and classifier frozen produces a pronounced per-formance drop for all three models (Appendix E.1, Figure 9). When the scaler and classifier are refit after removal, performance largely recovers, but deletion of the source-defined directions still incurs an additional loss of 1.70, 0.78, and 2.17% relative to equal-rank random deletion (Figure 4B; Tables 19–20). Together, augmentation and post-refit deletion support transferable predictive utility beyond equal-dimensional controls in sleep staging. Frozen-head degradation reflects the target-fitted classifier’s dependence on these directions, while alternative predictive structure remains available after refitting. 

4.5 Signal-derived Feature Accessibility and Adaptation 

To clarify why intermediate layers excel when frozen, we first probe signal-derived measurements across depth using PCA200 Ridge probes, with additional native-dimensional controls for raw EEG, H01, and H12 (Appendix F.1). Pretraining affects the accessibility of linear and nonlinear signal-derived measurements differently: in native features, it preserves time-domain N170 PO8 amplitude (raw ceiling R2 = 0 .999 ) at H01 ( 0.876 vs. 0.574 random) before attenuating it at H12 ( 0.181 vs. 0.462 ). Thus, depth-wise gains track the linear accessibility of signal-derived measurements rather than high-level physiological representations. This depth profile generalizes across pretext objectives, yet remains contingent on keeping the encoder frozen. ## 5 Discussion 

Our findings show that the apparent underperformance of final-layer EEG representations stems from two distinct factors. First, much of this deficit disappears when the final layer is paired with an appropriate readout. Second, intermediate layers frequently offer higher predictive utility even with the readout held fixed, though this advantage is tightly coupled to the feature interface: residual gains diminish sharply as final-layer dimensionality increases, yielding no reliable improvement once both representations retain their native dimensions. Together, these results indicate that readout design and layer selection govern complementary facets of representation accessibility—how effectively task information is extracted within a layer, and where along the encoder hierarchy it is most readily decodable. This distinction directly affects how frozen foundation models should be benchmarked. A single pooling rule confounds layer depth with readout mismatch, making layer comparisons meaningful only after an appropriate readout is established. Even under matched readouts, intermediate layers improve performance across most conditions and substantially shift model rankings, lifting average rank across the three tasks from 1.67 to 1.00 for REVE and from 4.00 to 2.67 for CBraMod. Crucially, this depth preference is specific to frozen extraction. Under all-block LoRA adaptation on N170, the final layer reaches 79.57% AUROC—surpassing the fixed intermediate layer (78.59%) and matching validation reselection (79.54%). Layer utility therefore depends on the adaptation regime: depth selection is a primary lever when probing frozen representations, whereas parameter-efficient adaptation realigns task-relevant information with the model output. Beyond single-dataset probing, our transfer experiments show that useful depth structure generalizes across datasets. Across five dataset pairs and four task families, source-selected layer–readout interfaces outperform Final in all ten CBraMod/REVE comparisons, Random in eight, and First with positive paired confidence intervals in seven, showing that source-side selection identifies transferable interfaces beyond default final-layer extraction or heuristic layer choices. Intervention analyses sharpen this geometric picture: a compact set of task-relevant directions from source residuals transfers directly to the target representation, where ablating them degrades a frozen classifier and appending them to final-layer features improves prediction. Although refitting the target classifier 9recovers most of this drop, ablating source-derived directions still incurs greater loss than equal-rank random deletion, confirming that residual subspaces capture reusable predictive coordinates in sleep staging rather than arbitrary variance. Collectively, these findings argue against treating the final hidden state as the canonical representation of an EEG foundation model. Instead, depth, readout, and downstream adaptation jointly govern which predictive structures are accessible. Rigorous frozen-model benchmarking therefore requires reporting not only final-layer scores, but also the readout interface and the task-dependent predictive utility distributed across depth. 

Limitations. The current experiments use lightweight mappings and focus the intervention analysis on a subset of the transfer settings. Residual analysis depends on the tested feature dimensions and mapping/probe families; it does not establish information-theoretic absence from final-layer representations. On SEED-V, readout helps some models, but depth gains remain inconclusive under video-level probability averaging. Our all-block LoRA experiment is limited to one model (CBraMod) and one dataset (N170). 

## 6 Conclusion 

We show that intermediate-layer advantages in frozen EEG foundation models depend on both readout design and downstream task. Better readouts recover substantial performance at the final layer, while selecting depth at a fixed readout provides additional predictive utility in most conditions. These depth-dependent features can also remain useful across datasets, with source-derived predictive directions retaining value after transfer in sleep staging. These effects depend on representation dimensionality and adaptation: residual gains diminish with native-dimensional features, while the intermediate-layer advantage does not persist under the tested all-block LoRA setting. Overall, predictive utility varies across depth and depends on how representations are read out, transferred, and adapted. Evaluating EEG foundation models should therefore consider not only which layer performs best, but also how representations are read out and reused across datasets and downstream adaptation settings.

A.1 Datasets, readouts, and preprocessing 

All data splits are participant-disjoint, and all sessions from the same participant remain in the same split. ERP CORE N170 and N400 share the same participant cohort. SEED-V probabilities are averaged within video before participant-equal scoring. For HMC-to-ISRUC transfer, we use the 103-participant HMC development subset as the source and 98 ISRUC participants as the target after excluding S008 and S040 for channel/reference incompatibility. Readouts operate directly on frozen encoder tokens. For H ∈ RC×T ×d, global mean averages channels and time, while global mean+std additionally summarizes token dispersion. Channel mean averages over time while preserving channel identity, and channel mean+std additionally retains temporal dispersion within each channel. The structure readout concatenates channel summaries from four fixed temporal bins when the tokenization permits this. LUNA has no physical channel axis and therefore uses the applicable global and temporal summaries. Each readout is standardized using training-fold statistics before fitting the downstream classifier. The sleep interface uses four mastoid-referenced leads. Each 30-s epoch is represented by six 5-s encoder outputs, with temporal dispersion computed within segment before segment averaging. 

A.2 Model checkpoints and evaluation protocol 

We evaluate CBraMod, REVE-base, LaBraM-base, LUNA-base, and CSBrain with frozen encoders in the primary experiments. REVE’s released pretraining inventory includes OpenBMI. All layer–readout candidates use the same outer and development folds. Regularization is selected on development data for each candidate, and standardization and dimensionality reduction are fitted only on the corresponding training data. Participant-level contrasts use paired participant bootstrap intervals. ISRUC macro-F1 is computed from pooled participant confusion counts within each bootstrap resample. Seeds are averaged within participant before bootstrap resampling. Intervals are unadjusted for multiple comparisons. ## E Source-direction transfer 

E.1 Source-residual intervention 

For HMC-to-ISRUC transfer, the selected/final layers are H06/H12 for CBraMod and H14/H22 for REVE, and H07/H12 for LaBraM, using channel means and temporal standard deviations. Source standardization and 200-dimensional PCA are fitted on HMC and applied unchanged to ISRUC. A ridge map from final to selected-layer features is fitted on HMC, with regularization selected by participant-grouped validation. Participant-equal class means of source residuals form five sleep-stage prototypes. Centering them and taking their right singular vectors gives four task directions. Augmentation appends four projected target residual coordinates to the 200 final-layer features. Random, residual-PCA, shuffled-label, and ordinary supervised directions use the same coordinate count and fitting procedure. Ordinary directions come from the unresidualized source class means; both bases are also compared on the same residual representation. Deletion removes four directions from the selected-layer features in source-fitted PCA200 coordinates at unit strength (Equation 7). Random and within-participant label-shuffled controls average 20 independent bases. The frozen condition holds the target scaler and classifier fixed; refitting updates both, including regularization. 29 F.2 All-block LoRA adaptation 

On N170, rank-8 LoRA adapters ( α = 16 ) are inserted into spatial/temporal attention QKV and output projections and both feedforward matrices in all twelve CBraMod blocks (307,200 trainable encoder parameters). Original weights, normalization parameters, and patch embedding remain frozen. AdamW uses adapter/head learning rates 10 −4/10 −3, weight decay 0.01, batch size 64, and gradient clipping at 1. Five outer folds and three seeds each use three independently trained inner encoders, excluding validation participants from gradient updates. Inner validation selects training duration (maximum 30 epochs, patience five); the outer encoder trains for the median inner duration, giving 60 fitted checkpoints. Adapted native channel-mean features receive freshly fitted balanced L2 logistic probes with C ∈ { 10 −5, 10 −4, . . . , 10 3}. Layer and C selection use inner-checkpoint validation features only. Fixed selected retains the pretrained-validation-selected layer; Reselected searches all twelve adapted blocks. 30 F.3 Controlled pretraining 

MAE and JEPA share an eight-block encoder, tokenizer, 25-channel HBN input, sampling stream, temporal masking, optimizer schedule, and nominal exposure. MAE reconstructs masked waveform patches; JEPA predicts normalized teacher representations. Three paired seeds train for 50,000 updates with global batch 256 (microbatch 32, accumulation eight). Teacher computation and output heads differ, so this compares pretraining schemes. Pretraining excludes windows with RMS above 100 μV or maximum channel peak-to-peak amplitude above 500 μV, retaining 12,792 windows (17.77 hours).
