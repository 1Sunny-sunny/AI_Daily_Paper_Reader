# Is 4 Bits Enough for EEG Sleep Staging? A Subject-Level Benchmark of MXFP4, NVFP4 and FP8 

Emese Batizi Hossein Valavi 

Princeton University, Princeton, NJ, USA 

{eb1870, hvalavi}@princeton.edu 

Abstract 

Staging sleep on a wearable device keeps the raw electroencephalogram (EEG) private and saves the energy needed to transmit it. It also requires small, energy-efficient neural networks. We benchmark 13 post-training quantization (PTQ) configurations, from FP16 to the block-scaled 4-bit formats MXFP4 and NVFP4, for five-stage sleep staging from single-channel Fpz–Cz EEG. We use Sleep-EDF-78 (78 subjects), subject-wise cross-validation, three training seeds, value-exact emulation, and per-subject paired statistics. All 8- and 16-bit formats stay within 0.45 percentage points (pp) of FP32 Macro-F1. With 4-bit weights and activations, an epoch-wise CNN loses 1.98–3.04 pp, and a temporal-context network loses at most 1.19 pp. A same-encoder ablation shows that temporal context explains little of this gap. At equal block size, NVFP4 outperforms MXFP4. The first layer, which quantizes the raw EEG, is the most sensitive layer in both networks. Keeping the first and last layers in FP8 brings both tested 4-bit formats within 0.48 pp of FP32. This recipe adds at most 2.1% weight storage, needs no retraining, and is equivalent to quantization-aware training (QAT) within an exploratory 1-pp margin. The 4-bit losses fall on the same subjects across architectures (Spearman ρ = 0.69–0 .79), and they reduce N3 recall the most. On the nested Sleep-EDF-20 subset, NVFP4 with FP8 first and last layers again stays within 0.25 pp of FP32. Under MXFP4, however, the ranking of the architectures reverses, and FP8 first and last layers do not fully recover two of the three tested models. In our emulation, NVFP4 with FP8 first and last layers thus loses little accuracy without QAT in both cohorts. 

Keywords: sleep staging, EEG, quantization, microscaling, MXFP4, NVFP4, FP8, edge AI, wearable health 

# 1 Introduction 

Sleep staging assigns each 30-second epoch of a polysomnogram to one of five stages: wake (W), N1, N2, N3, or rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep [1, 2]. It is the basis of most sleep diagnostics. Manual scoring is slow, and agreement between scorers is only moderate. In the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) inter-scorer reliability program, overall epoch agreement was 82.6% [3]. Among expert scorers, Cohen’s κ

was 0.68 under the Rechtschaffen and Kales (R&K) rules and 0.76 under the AASM rules [4]. Deep networks that read a single electroencephalography (EEG) channel now reach comparable agreement [5–10]. This makes sleep monitoring at home and with wearable devices feasible [11]. Dry-electrode EEG headbands, for example, already stage sleep with an accuracy comparable to that of polysomnography scoring [12]. There are three main reasons to run the staging model on the device instead of streaming the signal to a phone or a server. Privacy: EEG is health data, and neural recordings raise privacy concerns of their own [13, 14]. A device that stages sleep locally needs to send only a hypnogram. For an 8-hour night (960 epochs), the hypnogram takes about 1 kB, whereas the raw signal takes about 5.8 MB (100 Hz, 16 bit). The raw EEG never has to leave the device. Energy: In wearable EEG systems, the radio can dominate the power budget. Processing the EEG on the chip instead of transmitting it has reduced system power 14-fold [15]. 1arXiv:2611.31436v1 [cs.LG] 23 Nov 2026 Latency: Closed-loop interventions must act on the signal in real time and cannot depend on a network connection. One example is auditory stimulation timed to the slow oscillations of slow-wave (N3) sleep, which has been shown to enhance memory retention [16]. In all three cases, the model must fit within the memory and energy budget of a battery-powered device. Low-precision arithmetic is one of the most effective ways to meet such a budget. INT8 post-training quantization (PTQ) is mature [17–19], and 8-bit floating point (FP8) is standard on recent accelerators [20]. The Open Compute Project (OCP) Microscaling (MX) specification [21, 22] and NVIDIA’s NVFP4 [23] go further. They define 4-bit floating-point formats in which small blocks of values share one scale factor. These block-scaled formats were developed and tested mainly on large language and vision models. Small biosignal networks differ in three ways. Their input is a raw physiological signal with a high dynamic range. They have only 10 4–10 5 parameters. Finally, the clinical question is whether every patient is staged reliably, not only whether the average is acceptable. Compression is known to affect some inputs much more than others [24], and mean accuracy can stay unchanged while many individual predictions change [25]. We are not aware of a subject-level evaluation of block-scaled 4-bit formats for EEG sleep staging. This paper provides one. Its contributions are as follows: 1. A controlled benchmark. We evaluate 13 quantization configurations (Table 1) on two architectures: an epoch-wise CNN and a temporal-context network. A third model, used as an ablation, has the same encoder as the temporal-context network but no temporal layers. We train each model with three seeds in 10-fold subject-wise cross-validation on all 78 subjects of Sleep-EDF-78. Every format is emulated value-exactly. Calibration uses only training subjects, so no information leaks from the test subjects. We compare the formats with per-subject paired tests, Holm correction, and non-inferiority bounds. 2. Temporal context explains little of the 4-bit robustness. With 4-bit weights and activations (W4A4), PTQ costs the epoch-wise CNN 1.98–3.04 percentage points (pp) of Macro-F1, the unweighted mean of the per-stage F1 scores. The temporal-context network loses at most 1.19 pp. On Sleep-EDF-78, removing the temporal layers, while keeping the encoder and its training regime, preserves most of this robustness. At equal block size, the scaling recipe of NVFP4 (E4M3 block scales plus an FP32 tensor scale) outperforms the power-of-two block scales of MXFP4. Block size has smaller effects, and their sign varies. 3. A training-free recipe. The first layer, which quantizes the raw EEG, is the most sensitive layer in both networks. For the CNN, it accounts for most of the loss. On Sleep-EDF-78, keeping only the first and last layers in FP8 brings Macro-F1 within 0.48 pp of FP32 and adds at most 2.1% weight storage. In a direct paired comparison, this recipe is equivalent to quantization-aware training (QAT) within 

±1 pp. QAT beats PTQ applied after the same fine-tuning (significantly so for the CNN). However, QAT does not beat an FP32 model that received the same fine-tuning. 4. A subject-level analysis. We compare per-subject losses with a retraining-noise reference. The 4-bit losses fall on the same subjects across architectures. Among the five stages, N3 loses the most recall. We also repeat the study on the nested Sleep-EDF-20 subset to see which findings hold in a smaller cohort. 5. Native measurements. We report latency, throughput, and dynamic energy on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU for the natively supported 16- and 32-bit formats. We add micro-benchmarks of FP8 general matrix multiplications (GEMMs). 22 Background and Related Work 

2.1 Automatic sleep staging 

DeepSleepNet [5] established sleep staging from raw single-channel EEG. It combines a CNN front end with recurrent sequence modeling. TinySleepNet [6] kept this design and reduced the parameter count. Sequence-to-sequence models such as SeqSleepNet [7], U-Time [8], and SleepTransformer [10] also use dependencies across neighboring epochs, much as human scorers do. AttnSleep [9], in contrast, applies attention within individual epochs. Sleep-EDF [26, 27] is the most widely used public benchmark. Its 20-subject and 78-subject Sleep Cassette subsets (SC-20 and SC-78; here, Sleep-EDF-20 and Sleep-EDF-78) are standard evaluation cohorts, alongside datasets such as MASS-SS3 [28]. All of these works report FP32 accuracy. 

2.2 Efficient and quantized sleep staging 

DistillSleep [29] distills a large teacher into a single-channel student with 109k parameters. The student runs in under 10 ms per epoch on three embedded platforms. Hardware-oriented studies instead use integer or fixed-point arithmetic. SleepViT [30] is a vision-transformer accelerator synthesized in 65-nm CMOS. Its model has 31.6k weights and uses layer-dependent fixed-point quantization. Chen et al. [31] present a CNN-LSTM accelerator fabricated in 65-nm CMOS. It assigns INT2–INT16 precision layer by layer and keeps 5-class accuracy within 0.15% of the floating-point baseline on MASS-SS3. A related system-on-chip paper with overlapping authors [32] reports a 5.01% accuracy drop for static 8-bit quantization of its CNN-LSTM and uses runtime dynamic quantization instead. These studies report cohort-level accuracy for a single deployment configuration of their own design. None of them evaluates block-scaled floating-point formats such as MXFP4 and NVFP4. None compares formats under a common protocol or analyzes how quantization affects individual subjects. This paper addresses these gaps. 

2.3 Low-precision formats 

Integer quantization maps real values to b-bit integer codes. A scale is shared across a tensor, a channel, or a group of values. Asymmetric quantization adds a zero point, the integer code that represents zero [17–19]. We write E xMy for a signed floating-point format with one sign bit, x exponent bits, and y fraction (mantissa) bits. FP32, FP16, and BF16 (bfloat16) are E8M23, E5M10, and E8M7, respectively. The two common FP8 formats are E4M3 and E5M2. Their largest finite values are 448 and 57,344. E4M3 offers more precision, and E5M2 offers a wider range [20]. Microscaling formats give each block of elements a shared scale [21, 22]. MXFP4 uses blocks of 32 four-bit E2M1 elements and one 8-bit E8M0 scale per block. E8M0 has no sign or fraction bits, so its finite values are positive powers of two. Including the shared scale, a full block needs 4.25 bits per element. NVFP4 uses blocks of 16 E2M1 elements and one FP8 E4M3 scale per block. It adds one FP32 scale for the whole tensor [23]. A full block needs 4.5 bits per element, not counting the tensor scale and any padding (Fig. 1). The OCP rule rounds the scale exponent down, so scaled values can exceed the element range and saturate. Mishra et al. [33] report that this rule harms MXFP8 training, and they round the scale up instead. Chhugani et al. [34] propose overflow-aware scaling and macro-block scaling to reduce the quantization error of MXFP4. Low-bit convolutional neural networks (CNNs) often keep the first and last layers at higher precision [35, 36]. More generally, the quantization sensitivity of each layer can guide its precision [37]. Quantization-aware training (QAT) simulates quantization during training. 2.4 Beyond average accuracy 

Compressed networks can keep their overall accuracy while performing much worse on a subset of inputs [24]. In compressed language models, many answers switch between correct and incorrect while overall accuracy barely changes [25]. Such switches are called flips .For sleep staging, we care about how quantization affects individual subjects. We therefore use the subject as the statistical unit. We also compare quantization-induced changes with a retraining reference. This reference is the variation between FP32 models of the same architecture, trained with different random seeds on the same data splits. 

# 3 Methods 

3.1 Data 

We use the Sleep Cassette (SC) subset of the Sleep-EDF Expanded database [26,27]. It contains 153 overnight recordings from 78 healthy subjects aged 25–101 years. (Subject numbers 39, 68, 69, 78, and 79 do not exist in the SC numbering.) Figure 2 shows an example night. We use the Fpz–Cz EEG channel. It measures the voltage between the midline of the forehead (Fpz) and the vertex, the top of the head (Cz). Both positions follow the international 10–20 system [39] (Appendix A). The signal is sampled at 100 Hz, and we apply no further filtering. We cut each recording into non-overlapping 30-second epochs of 3 ,000 samples and align them with the expert labels. The labels follow the R&K rules [2]. These rules split deep sleep into S3 and S4 by the share of the epoch that contains high-amplitude slow waves. For five-class staging, we merge S3 and S4 into N3, following the AASM grouping [1]. This step relabels the original scores; it does not rescore the recordings under AASM rules. We remove epochs labeled as movement time or unscored. We keep each night from 30 minutes before the first sleep epoch to 30 minutes after the last one, a common convention for Sleep-EDF [5, 10]. This limits the long stretches of wake at the start and end of each recording. Appendix A summarizes the EEG features of each stage and shows their spectra in our data. 40 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

Such gaps occur in 47 of the 153 recordings, with 140 gaps in total. The median gap is 1 epoch long, and the longest is 97 epochs. For each night, we place the retained epochs in one sequence, in their original order, without placeholders. The last epoch before a gap is therefore followed directly by the first epoch after it. The temporal model predicts each epoch from a window of 31 retained epochs (Section 3.3). For 3,601 target epochs (1.84%), this window crosses at least one gap, so the model treats epochs separated by missing data as neighbors. The epoch-wise model sees only the target epoch and is not affected. Section 3.7 tests a gap-aware variant. 

3.2 Evaluation protocol 

We split the data by subject. Within each fold, all nights of a subject stay in the same subset. Recordings of one person therefore never appear in both the training data and the held-out data. We divide the 78 subjects into 10 disjoint test folds of 7–8 subjects. For each fold, we randomly draw seven validation subjects from the 70 or 71 remaining subjects. The validation subjects are used for early stopping and model selection. The other 63 or 64 subjects form the fitting set, which we call FIT. Only FIT subjects update the network weights. All data-dependent preprocessing uses FIT only. For each fold, we compute one EEG mean and standard deviation (SD) from FIT. We use them to z-score the FIT, validation, and test signals. We then clip the normalized signal to [−20 , 20 ], that is, to 20 FIT SDs around the FIT mean. Clipping limits the effect of extreme amplitudes, such as movement or electrode artifacts, on the quantization ranges. The cross-entropy 5class weights are wc = N/(5 nc), where N is the number of FIT epochs and nc is the number of FIT epochs in class c. The PTQ calibration set contains 1,020 FIT epochs, 204 per stage. As a result, no information from validation or test subjects enters normalization, class weighting, or calibration. Test subjects are used only for evaluation. SleepCNN is calibrated on the 1,020 selected epochs directly. For ContextSleepNet, each selected epoch is the center of a 31-epoch window from the same night. (Near the start or end of a night, the window is shifted so that it stays inside the night.) Per fold, the windows contain 31,620 epoch positions. Because windows can overlap, these positions cover 27,828–28,535 distinct FIT epochs. The center epochs are balanced across stages, but the full windows are not. For example, N2 makes up 30.5% of the window positions and N1 only 13.1%. Activation statistics are collected from the full windows. Section 3.5 describes how the search for the clipping threshold subsamples them. We train each model with three random seeds per fold. For each fold and seed, all quantized variants derive from the same trained FP32 model. That FP32 model and its quantized variants are evaluated on the same test epochs. 3.3 Models 

SleepCNN classifies each 30-second epoch on its own. It has 55,381 parameters and needs 1.04 million multiply–accumulate operations (MACs) per epoch. The first convolution has 16 filters with a kernel of 50 samples and a stride of 5. The second has 32 filters with a kernel of 25 samples and a stride of 3. Each convolution is followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) and max-pooling by a factor of 4. The resulting 320 features pass through two fully connected layers (320 → 128 → 5), which give one output per stage. 

ContextSleepNet also uses neighboring epochs. It has 381,701 parameters and needs 5.39 million MACs per epoch. A per-epoch encoder, inspired by TinySleepNet [6], first processes each epoch. The encoder also uses batch normalization (BN), max-pooling by factors of 8 and 4, and dropout. A linear layer maps the resulting 960 features to a 128-dimensional embedding. Four residual temporal convolution layers then process the sequence of embeddings. Each layer has 128 channels and a kernel size of 3. Their dilations are 1, 2, 4, and 8, which set the spacing between the epochs that each convolution combines. The receptive field therefore grows from 3 to 7, 15, and finally 31 epochs. A linear classifier outputs five stage scores per epoch. The final receptive field covers the target epoch and up to 15 retained epochs on each side. For contiguous epochs, this is 7.5 min of context on each side. Across a gap, the window spans more time. The model is fully convolutional along the epoch axis, so it handles sequences of any length. We train it on 32-epoch windows and evaluate each night in one forward pass. Because it uses future epochs, the model is non-causal. It is meant for offline scoring of a whole night, not for online staging. Figure 10 in Appendix A shows SleepCNN, ContextSleepNet, and the ablation model EncoderNet (Section 3.7). It gives their tensor shapes and shows how the receptive field grows (3 → 7 → 15 → 31 epochs). 

3.4 Training 

Both models use Adam [41] with an initial learning rate of 10 −3, a weight decay of 10 −4, and a cosine learning-rate schedule. After each training epoch, we measure validation Macro-F1 and keep the checkpoint with the highest value. Training stops after eight training epochs without improvement or at the budget of 30 training epochs (SleepCNN) or 40 (ContextSleepNet). SleepCNN uses mini-batches of 64 EEG epochs. ContextSleepNet uses dropout with a probability of 0.5 and mini-batches of 16 windows, each of 32 consecutive retained EEG epochs. The windows are drawn at random within nights. Each training epoch draws enough windows to cover the FIT epochs once on average. Across runs, the median training epoch of the selected checkpoint was 11 for SleepCNN and 18.5 for ContextSleepNet. The full budget, without early stopping, was used by 0 of the 30 SleepCNN runs and by 3 of the 30 ContextSleepNet runs. We disable TensorFloat-32 (TF32) matrix arithmetic, so the FP32 reference is true FP32. The main study covered both models, 10 test folds, three seeds per fold, and all evaluated conditions. All experiments ran on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition GPU with PyTorch 2.11 and CUDA 13.0. 73.5 Number formats and quantized inference

A block therefore never mixes values from different output positions. Neighboring patches of a convolution overlap, so an input value that belongs to several patches is quantized separately in each one, with the block scale of that patch. In the first layer, each EEG sample enters up to 10 patches in SleepCNN (kernel 50, stride 5) and up to 9 in the larger encoder (stride 6). The emulation thus models the quantization of an unfolded matrix-multiplication operand. It does not model an activation tensor that is stored once in 4 bits. On the tested inputs, both 4-bit quantizers match the TorchAO reference quantizers [42] bit for bit (Section 3.7). We emulate all formats, including FP16 and BF16, by fake quantization. Weights and activations are rounded to the values of the format and then stored in FP32. All later arithmetic, including accumulation, is FP32. Our accuracy results therefore capture the effect of the number format alone. They do not model the accumulator precision, operation order, intermediate rounding, or operator fusion of a specific low-precision kernel. For FP16 and BF16, we also check the emulation against native GPU execution (Section 3.7). We measure speed only for formats with native kernels. 

3.6 Recovering 4-bit accuracy 

We test two mixed-precision variants that need no retraining. Both start from W4A4. The first runs the weights and input activations of the first layer in FP8-E4M3. The second does the same for both the first and the last layer. These layers thus use W8A8, and all other layers stay at W4A4. We call the second variant 

FP8 first/last .For quantization-aware training (QAT), we start from the trained FP32 model with BN folded into the convolutions. We fine-tune it for five training epochs at a learning rate of 10 −4. Fine-tuning uses the same training loop as above (Adam, cosine schedule, and the best-validation checkpoint). The forward pass simulates 4-bit quantization, and a straight-through estimator passes gradients through the quantizer [38]. After fine-tuning, we convert the model with the same procedure as PTQ. During NVFP4 fine-tuning, the activation tensor scale is computed on the fly from the largest magnitude of each activation tensor. After conversion, the model uses a fixed activation tensor scale calibrated on FIT subjects, as in PTQ. The tensor-scale estimate thus differs between fine-tuning and evaluation. QAT also adds training. To separate the two effects, we include an FP32 fine-tuning control, FP32+FT . It receives the same five-epoch fine-tuning without simulated quantization. We also apply the same PTQ to the FP32+FT model, which gives the condition FT →PTQ . Comparing QAT with FT →PTQ separates the benefit of simulating quantization during fine-tuning from the benefit of extra training. We run QAT, FP32+FT, and FT →PTQ for all three seeds. 93.7 Control experiments 

Same-encoder ablation. EncoderNet is ContextSleepNet without its four temporal convolution layers. It has 184,581 parameters. It keeps the same encoder, classifier, BN, and dropout. It also keeps the training windows, optimizer settings, training budget, early-stopping rule, and calibration windows. At inference, it classifies each epoch from that epoch’s 30-second signal alone. We train it with the same folds and seeds and evaluate it under the same PTQ conditions. For each model, ∆MF1 is the change in Macro-F1 relative to that model’s own FP32 baseline. We compute it per subject and compare it between models on the same subjects. The temporal-layer effect is ContextSleepNet’s ∆MF1 minus EncoderNet’s ∆MF1. It captures everything that the temporal layers add: their use of neighboring epochs and their effect on capacity and optimization. The non-temporal effect is EncoderNet’s ∆MF1 minus SleepCNN’s ∆MF1. It combines the differences between the two encoders and between the two training procedures. (EncoderNet uses mini-batches of 16 windows of 32 epochs and a budget of 40 training epochs. SleepCNN uses mini-batches of 64 epochs and a budget of 30; see Section 3.4.) This ablation cannot separate the encoder from its training procedure. We Holm-correct the eight W4A4 contrasts (two effects for each of four formats). 

First-layer probes. We quantize only the first layer, in one of three ways: weights only, input activations only, or both. All other layers stay in FP32. These probes separate the effect of quantizing the first-layer weights from that of quantizing the raw EEG input. 

Recording gaps. We split each night at every internal gap. We then retrain and evaluate ContextSleepNet on the resulting segments. Training windows stay within a segment, and predictions never use epochs from across a gap. We call this variant the gap-aware model. The change applies only to training and evaluation. PTQ calibration reuses the windows of the main model, which are drawn from unsplit nights and may cross gaps. 

Emulation checks. We run the BN-folded models natively in FP16 and BF16 on the GPU and compare them with the emulated formats. We also compare our MXFP4 and NVFP4 quantizers with the TorchAO [42] reference implementations. The inputs are heavy-tailed random tensors and every trained weight matrix of fold 0, seed 0 of both models. TorchAO is pinned to version 0.18.0 and runs inside the notebook. The check fails, rather than being skipped, if any pair cannot be computed or differs. Finally, reloaded checkpoints, the rerun FP32+FT control, and the rerun single-layer probes must reproduce the stored predictions exactly. 

Sleep-EDF-20. We train and evaluate all three models with the same pipeline on subjects 0–19 (the SC-20 subset). We use 10 folds of two test subjects and three seeds. 

3.8 Statistics 

Our primary metric is Macro-F1 (MF1), the unweighted mean of the five per-stage F1 scores. It gives each stage equal weight. This matters here because W and N2 make up most epochs. We also report accuracy, per-stage F1, and Cohen’s kappa [43]: 

κ = po − pe

1 − pe

. (9) Here, po is the observed agreement between predictions and expert labels. The quantity pe is the agreement expected by chance, computed from the stage frequencies of the predictions and the labels. 

Pooled metrics combine all test epochs of the cohort for each seed. We then average them over the three seeds. For statistical inference, the subject is the unit of analysis. Per-subject MF1 always averages five stages. If a stage does not occur in a subject’s expert labels, its F1 is set to zero. For each subject and seed, we subtract the FP32 MF1 from the MF1 under the tested condition. We average these paired differences over the three seeds, which gives one ∆MF1 value per subject. Negative values mean a loss. We report the mean 10 over the 78 subjects with a 95% percentile bootstrap confidence interval (CI). The CI uses 5 ,000 resamples of subjects drawn with replacement [44]. We also test the per-subject differences with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test [45]. We adjust its p-values with Holm’s method across the 20 conditions of each model [46]. The two analyses answer different questions. The bootstrap CI describes the uncertainty of the mean. The Wilcoxon test uses the signs and ranks of the differences, and its usual reading as a location shift assumes symmetric differences. Its associated Hodges–Lehmann pseudomedian is a location measure, not the mean. The CI and the test can therefore disagree. We report both and read the rank test with caution when the differences are skewed. The 95% bootstrap CIs are pointwise: each one describes a single condition without a multiplicity adjustment. We handle multiplicity separately, through the Holm-adjusted tests and the adjusted bounds for non-inferiority and equivalence. A nonsignificant test does not show that no meaningful loss exists. We therefore also test non-inferiority 

with a margin of 1 pp. Let m = 20 be the size of the Bonferroni family for each model. From 20 ,000 bootstrap resamples of subjects, we take the 0 .05 /m quantile of the resampled means as a one-sided lower bound. A condition is non-inferior if this bound exceeds −1 pp. This procedure targets a familywise error rate of 0.05, to the extent that the bootstrap approximation holds. We chose the margin after seeing the main results, so the non-inferiority analysis is exploratory. The margin is of the same order as the SD of the FP32 pooled MF1 across training seeds. This SD ranges from 0.26 to 0.81 pp across the three models. We also test equivalence between two conditions from their paired per-subject MF1 differences. Two conditions are equivalent if the adjusted lower bound exceeds −1 pp and the adjusted upper bound is below 

+1 pp. This test uses the same exploratory margin. For the comparison of FP8 first/last with QAT, the Bonferroni family contains the four model–format pairs. We also check how sensitive the non-inferiority decisions are to the interval method. For this check, we recompute the lower bounds with a bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap and with a t interval. Both use the same multiplicity adjustment. The flip rate is the share of test epochs whose predicted stage differs from the FP32 prediction. It also counts changes from one wrong stage to another. We compute it over all test epochs for each seed and then average it over seeds. To put per-subject changes in context, we build a retraining-noise reference from FP32 models trained with different seeds on the same folds. Three seeds give three seed pairs. We include each pair in both directions, so the reference is symmetric around zero by construction. This reference shows how much results vary between training runs. Reversing a pair does not create an independent observation. The subject-level correlation analyses in Section 4.6 were not planned in advance and are exploratory.

# 5 Discussion 

5.1 Implications for edge accelerators 

Four findings bear directly on hardware–software co-design for sleep-monitoring devices. 

First, 8 bits were enough for these models. FP16, BF16, INT8, and FP8-E4M3 change Macro-F1 by at most 0.13 pp for either model. For an EEG front end, we prefer INT8 or E4M3 to E5M2. Per-channel weight scales and calibrated activation ranges already cover the dynamic range, so the wider range of E5M2 is not needed. Its coarser mantissa, in contrast, costs SleepCNN a significant 0.45 pp. This finding is limited to our two convolutional models and our quantizer. Xu et al. [32] report a 5.01% accuracy drop for static 8-bit quantization of a CNN-LSTM, and we did not test recurrent models. 

Second, the scaling recipe matters at 4 bits. NVFP4 (E4M3 block scales plus an FP32 tensor scale) beats MXFP4 (power-of-two block scales) in all four matched comparisons, three of them significantly. Halving the block size, in contrast, does not help MXFP4 and costs 0.25 extra bits per element. 

Third, a 4-bit datapath should come with an 8-bit path for the first and last layers (FP8-E4M3 in our tests). The first layer performs 29.2–45.5% of all MACs, so its precision limits the achievable savings. Relative to pure MXFP4-32 W4A4, FP8 first/last layers raise BOPs by a factor of 1.9–2.4, but they add at most 2.1% weight storage. Among the tested layers on our GPU, the first layer is also the only one where 22 native FP8 GEMMs ran faster than BF16. The 8-bit path was sufficient in both cohorts only with NVFP4. With MXFP4-32, it left a significant loss for two of the three models on Sleep-EDF-20 (Section 4.3). 

Fourth, the architecture can matter more than the format. MXFP4-32 ContextSleepNet needs 201.2 KiB of weights, less than FP32 SleepCNN (216.3 KiB). It needs 0.086 GBOPs per epoch instead of 1.064 GBOPs. Yet it reaches a higher pooled Macro-F1 (75.3% vs. 69.9%). In this comparison, a fixed weight-storage budget was better spent on a larger model than on higher precision. The comparison counts weights only, not activation memory. The two models also differ in temporal context, and ContextSleepNet needs future epochs (Section 3.3). Our ablation suggests that the larger encoder or its training regime lowers the sensitivity to 4-bit error. On Sleep-EDF-20, however, this advantage held only for NVFP4. For closed-loop applications, per-stage performance matters as much as Macro-F1. Slow-oscillation stimulation, for example, targets slow-wave (N3) sleep [16]. Under MXFP4-32 W4A4, N3 recall drops by 9.7 pp for SleepCNN and 9.6 pp for ContextSleepNet. Devices built for closed-loop use should therefore be validated on N3 recall, not only on Macro-F1. 

5.2 What makes a model robust to 4-bit error? 

On Sleep-EDF-78, the same-encoder ablation shows that the temporal layers contribute little to the robustness of ContextSleepNet. Most of this robustness comes from non-temporal differences. One of them is the training regime. ContextSleepNet trains on mini-batches of 32-epoch windows with a budget of 40 training epochs. SleepCNN trains on mini-batches of 64 independent epochs with a budget of 30. Several architectural differences could also matter. The larger encoder has 64 first-layer filters instead of 16, it is deeper, and it uses BN, which is folded into the weights before quantization. The first-layer probes point to a difference in the first layer. In SleepCNN, rounding either the weights or the input of conv1 costs 0.5–1 pp, and rounding both costs more than the sum. In the larger encoder, neither operand alone has a detectable effect. One possible explanation is that 64 filters represent the raw EEG redundantly. Later layers could then average out the rounding errors of individual filters. We have not tested this idea, which would require varying only the width of the first layer. Moreover, on Sleep-EDF-20, the larger encoder was more sensitive to MXFP4 than SleepCNN. Each model was also trained on about one-quarter as many subjects there, so we cannot tell whether the cohort or the amount of training data causes this reversal. Either way, quantization robustness is not a fixed property of an architecture, and claims about it must be tested on independent cohorts. 

5.3 Reporting quantized clinical models 

The mean ∆MF1 hides two patterns that our per-subject analysis reveals. First, the 4-bit loss concentrates in a subset of subjects. Second, the same subjects are affected in all tested architectures. A small mean loss therefore does not show that every patient is unaffected. We recommend that quantization studies on clinical signals report four items: (i) per-subject paired statistics with multiplicity correction; (ii) the subject-level tail, next to a retraining-noise reference (for example, the median, the lower percentiles, and the share of subjects with a large loss); (iii) a matched fine-tuning control and PTQ applied after the same fine-tuning, whenever QAT is evaluated; (iv) ablations that change one architectural factor at a time, before robustness is attributed to that factor. Without (iii), ContextSleepNet’s NVFP4 QAT result could have been read as an improvement over FP32. Without (iv), we would have credited its robustness to temporal context. 23 5.4 An unexpected block-size effect 

For ContextSleepNet, MXFP4 with 16-element blocks performs slightly worse than with 32-element blocks (−0.29 pp, pH = 0.02). Smaller blocks usually reduce quantization error, so this direction is unexpected. The round-down scale rule of MXFP4 offers one possible explanation, in line with the saturation reported for MX formats [33]. After scaling, any block maximum that falls in (6, 8) saturates at the E2M1 limit of 6. Halving the block size doubles the number of blocks, and thus roughly doubles the number of block maxima that can saturate. The E4M3 block scales of NVFP4 place the block maximum near 6 and so largely avoid this failure mode. The evidence for this effect is thin, however. It rests on one contrast: for SleepCNN, the same change has no detectable effect (0 .02 pp, pH = 0.87). We also did not test the explanation, for example by rounding the scale exponent up. 

5.5 Limitations 

(i) We emulate all formats with FP32 arithmetic. The value grids are exact, but the emulation does not model accumulator precision or kernel-level rounding. Latency and energy for MXFP4, NVFP4, and INT4 must come from native kernels or a hardware model. (ii) We study one public cohort of healthy subjects scored with the R&K rules. Clinical populations, such as patients with sleep apnea, and AASM-scored datasets may behave differently. Sleep-EDF-20 is a subset of this cohort, not an independent replication. Future work should test an external dataset and a published sleep-staging backbone. (iii) We evaluated two architectures, one ablation, three seeds, one calibration-set size, and a QAT schedule of five training epochs. The ablation isolates the temporal layers. It cannot separate the encoder from its training regime or isolate individual encoder factors (width, depth, and BN). Under MXFP4, the architecture ranking also differed between Sleep-EDF-78 and the smaller Sleep-EDF-20 subset. That comparison confounds the cohort with the amount of training data. (iv) ContextSleepNet is non-causal. A causal variant for real-time staging may be less robust. (v) Our class-weighted loss favors Macro-F1 over accuracy, and we did not train an unweighted control. Each quantized model, however, is compared with its own FP32 model, which was trained with the same loss. (vi) The subject-level correlation analyses and the 1-pp non-inferiority and equivalence margin are exploratory. (vii) NVFP4 QAT uses dynamic activation tensor scales during fine-tuning and static scales after conversion. We did not evaluate a fully static QAT variant. (viii) Three implementation choices limit how closely the emulation matches a device. Activation blocks are formed on unfolded input patches, so a value shared by overlapping patches is quantized once per patch. The skip path of the temporal layers stays in FP32, which may add to the low sensitivity of these layers. For EncoderNet and ContextSleepNet, the threshold search for INT8 and FP8 activations sees only part of the calibration data. (ix) All runs share one assignment of subjects to folds; only the training seed varies. Per-subject Macro-F1 assigns zero to a stage that a subject lacks, and it is unstable for subjects with few epochs of a stage. 

# 6 Conclusion 

For single-channel EEG sleep staging, 8- and 16-bit formats cost at most 0.45 pp of Macro-F1. They are also non-inferior to FP32 at an exploratory 1-pp margin. Pure W4A4 PTQ, in contrast, did not establish non-inferiority. Its cost depends on both the format and the model, and on Sleep-EDF-78 it depends little on temporal context. Two training-free choices close most of the gap. NVFP4 scaling loses less than MXFP4 scaling, and keeping the first and last layers in FP8 recovers most of the remaining loss. Combined, NVFP4-16 with FP8 first and last layers keeps every model within 0.25 pp of FP32 in both cohorts. QAT also restores Macro-F1 to near the FP32 level, but it does not beat an equally fine-tuned FP32 model. It is also equivalent within 1 pp to FP8 first and last layers without retraining. The 4-bit PTQ error is not spread evenly. It recurs in the same subjects across architectures, and it reduces N3 recall the most. These results, obtained 24 in emulation, support pairing 4-bit compute with FP8 for the first and last layers in hardware for wearable sleep monitoring. Finally, evaluations of quantized clinical models should report per-subject effects against a retraining-noise reference and should test architectural explanations with ablations.

# A Background: EEG, sleep stages, and the models 

EEG rhythms. The EEG measures voltage differences between scalp electrodes. These differences reflect the summed activity of cortical neurons. EEG rhythms are grouped into five frequency bands: delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), sigma (about 11–16 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz). The slow waves of deep sleep lie at 0.5–2 Hz, within the delta band. Sleep spindles lie in the sigma band. Faster (gamma) activity is not used in visual sleep scoring. 

Electrode position. Sleep-EDF records two EEG derivations: Fpz–Cz and Pz–Oz [26]. We use Fpz–Cz, the voltage between the forehead midline (Fpz) and the vertex (Cz) in the international 10–20 system [39] (Fig. 9a). Because Fpz–Cz is a frontal derivation, it picks up the posterior alpha rhythm of relaxed wakefulness only weakly. In addition, human scorers use eye-movement and chin-muscle channels to recognize REM sleep, but these channels are not part of our input. Eye movements appear in Fpz–Cz only as artifacts. Both factors make single-channel staging harder than expert scoring with a full montage.
