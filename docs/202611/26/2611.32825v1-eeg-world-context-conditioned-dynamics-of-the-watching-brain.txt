## Abstract 

Brain encoding models predict neural responses from au-diovisual input, but typically overlook how ongoing brain activity shapes an individual’s response. We introduce 

EEG-World , a recurrent world model that forecasts 10 seconds of EEG band-power dynamics from 6 seconds of preceding EEG and the subsequent audiovisual stimulus. Pretrained on continuous EEG, EEG-World learns shared neural dynamics and adapts to unseen individuals through lightweight subject-specific readouts. Using 2,584 hours of EEG from 78 participants, we show that nonlinear re-current forecasting surpasses strong history-aware linear baselines at scale. EEG-World achieves a forecasting cor-relation of 0.261 versus 0.233 for the strongest history-and-video linear model, with a 3.3-second forecasting-horizon advantage. Its learned dynamics transfer to unseen partic-ipants, outperforming the linear baseline in 23 of 24 indi-viduals after calibration. While ongoing EEG history ac-counts for most predictive performance, controlled stimulus analyses reveal an additional video-dependent component. This component also enables evaluation of visual represen-tations, with V-JEPA 2 improving clip-locked prediction by 35% over combined CLIP and DINOv2 features. Our re-sults demonstrate the value of transferable neural dynam-ics for individual EEG forecasting and highlight the impor-tance of separating state-driven from stimulus-driven pre-dictability.

Predicting the neural response evoked by a stimulus, however, is not the same as forecasting how a particular viewer’s brain activ-ity will unfold while watching it. Even for the same video, different viewers can follow different neural trajectories be-cause they start from different brain states (Fig. 1). Fore-casting these trajectories therefore requires modeling not only what is on screen but also the brain’s ongoing dynam-ics. Conventional stimulus-to-brain encoding models predict neural responses from visual or audiovisual features [23, 26, 34, 35]. Although effective at characterizing stimulus-related responses, they typically do not condition on an in-dividual’s preceding neural activity. This omission matters particularly for EEG, where ongoing rhythms and tempo-ral persistence make recent activity highly predictive of the next few seconds. As a result, a model can forecast EEG ac-curately while learning little about the stimulus itself. This 1arXiv:2611.32825v1 [cs.CV] 24 Nov 2026 raises two questions: can nonlinear models capture neu-ral dynamics beyond what strong history-aware linear mod-els already explain, and can the resulting state-driven pre-dictability be separated from the contribution of the video? We approach this problem through the lens of world models , which predict how a system evolves from its current state under external inputs [15, 16, 18]. In our setting, the brain is the dynamical system, EEG provides partial obser-vations of its state, and the video acts as an exogenous input. We introduce EEG-World, which infers a latent brain state from preceding EEG and rolls it forward under audiovisual conditioning to forecast an individual’s neural activity. Arecurrent dynamics core is pretrained on continuous EEG, and lightweight subject-specific readouts adapt the shared dynamics to new individuals. Because neural history alone explains much of the future activity, we compare against strong linear forecasters that receive the same EEG history and video, rather than against stimulus-only encoding mod-els. Learning and evaluating such dynamics requires data that existing EEG resources rarely combine: extensive recordings to learn temporal regularities, the same videos shown to many viewers to separate stimulus-driven from state-driven activity, and enough participants to hold some out for testing transfer. To meet these requirements, we collect VAEEG, a dataset of 2,584 hours of EEG from 78 participants watching naturalistic audiovisual clips. On this dataset, nonlinear recurrent forecasting overtakes strong lin-ear baselines once hundreds of hours are pooled and keeps improving as linear models saturate. Its dynamics trans-fer to unseen participants, where 33 minutes of calibra-tion match a linear model fitted on ten times more data. Although ongoing EEG dynamics dominate the predictive performance, controlled stimulus analyses reveal an addi-tional video-dependent component whose in-silico tuning matches recorded EEG, enabling more informative compar-isons between visual representations and human neural re-sponses. 

Contributions. 

• We introduce EEG-World, a recurrent world model for in-dividual EEG forecasting that combines pretrained neu-ral dynamics, audiovisual conditioning, and lightweight subject-specific adaptation. • We demonstrate that nonlinear recurrent forecasting sur-passes strong history-aware linear baselines at scale, with learned dynamics transferring to unseen participants, us-ing 2,584 hours of naturalistic EEG from 78 participants. • We establish history-aware baselines and persistence-controlled evaluations that distinguish neural-state pre-dictability from stimulus-driven responses, enabling more targeted comparisons of visual representations with the brain. 

## 2. Related work 

Brain encoding models. Encoding models predict fMRI, MEG or EEG responses from stimulus features, usually with regularized linear maps [2, 23, 26, 34, 35], and how well a vision model’s features predict the brain is used to compare it with human vision [41, 49]. Recent challenges and models predict whole-brain fMRI responses to movies from multimodal features [8, 13]; for EEG and video, most work decodes the stimulus from the response [30] or com-pares the response with the representations of vision mod-els [40]. All of these treat the response as a function of the stimulus alone. They cannot say what a particular brain will do next, because the ongoing state that shapes the next sec-onds (arousal, attention, endogenous rhythms) differs be-tween viewers and moments and is treated as noise. 

Brain world models. World models learn how an envi-ronment evolves under an agent’s actions [4, 15–18, 33]; in their usual settings the observation is rich and the ac-tions strongly drive what comes next. For the brain, latent dynamical models infer neural states from spiking activ-ity [38, 50], and NeuroWorld [9] rolls out fMRI with the stimulus as the action, starting from a single observed vol-ume. Neither transfers directly to EEG. First, the scalp signal is a noisy mixture driven mostly by ongoing activ-ity, so the state must be inferred from seconds of history, not one snapshot, and the video is a weak input to strong internal dynamics. Second, the phase of ongoing oscilla-tions is unpredictable seconds ahead, so reconstructing the raw signal, as pixel world models reconstruct frames, is ill-posed; band power is predictable. Third, channel and band scales differ between subjects, so one readout cannot serve all, while a new subject offers only minutes of data. Fourth, a slow, persistent state rewards copying the starting point, so gains must be measured against baselines with the same history; NeuroWorld’s only linear baseline is stimulus-only and its identification metric leaves the candidate pool un-specified, choices we show inflate gains (Secs. 5 and 6.5). EEG foundation models [10, 24, 47] and fMRI founda-tion models [48] are evaluated mostly on classification, not forecasting, and EEG representations align with video and language models only modestly above a time-shifted con-trol [46]. EEG-World follows from these properties. It infers a latent state from seconds of EEG, forecasts band power, adds the video as a zero-initialized action to dynam-ics pretrained on continuous EEG, and reads out through shared and subject-specific linear heads.

Video pathway. Each second of the clip is described by frozen features, namely OpenCLIP and DINOv2 frame em-beddings, loudness and spectral flux (Sec. 3); other en-coders are compared in Sec. 6.4. The video pathway g is an MLP whose last layer is initialized at zero, so a pretrained model is unchanged at the start of fine-tuning. Its output is added to the GRU input at every step, so the clip acts as a control input that moves the latent state. 

Training. Stage 1 pretrains the core, readout and heads on continuous EEG with the mean squared error of the free rollout (AdamW [32], early stopping on the selection error). The number of steps per epoch grows in proportion to the data, so the model sees the data equally often at every scale. Stage 2 adds the video pathway and fine-tunes on video tri-als, with half of each batch drawn from continuous windows to keep the pretrained dynamics; the pretrained parameters use a smaller learning rate than the new ones. Hyperparam-eters are in Sec. A. 

Unseen subjects. For a subject not seen in training, the trained model stays frozen. Because f (ht) is then fixed given the context and the clip, the new head (Ui, c i) in Eq. (2) is a ridge regression on the subject’s own training trials, shrunk toward the shared readout with the penalty chosen on their own selection trials. 

Linear baselines. Every result is compared with the strongest linear model available for each subject. History ridge maps the Tc ×D context directly to the Tf ×D future, fitted per subject, pooled over subjects, or hierarchically with per-subject weights shrunk toward the pooled ones, the linear analog of a shared core plus heads. Adding a block of clip features gives the history + video encoding models. For each subject, the best of the three on their own selection data is the best linear model. We also train a linear world model with EEG-World’s architecture and training but ev-ery nonlinearity removed. 

Stateless nonlinear baselines. To ask whether the gain needs the recurrent state or only a nonlinear network, two direct forecasters, an MLP and an iTransformer [31] that embeds each feature’s history as one token, map the context, plus the clip features on trials, to all Tf steps at once, with EEG-World’s inputs, per-subject heads and step budget. 45. Evaluation protocol 

Our benchmark has five tasks, namely multi-step forecast-ing, use of the video through clip swapping and clip-locked 

r, clip identification, transfer to unseen subjects and data scaling, and fixes three choices that decide the conclusions. 

Baselines with EEG history. Every task reports the con-text mean, history-only ridge and history + video ridge, each per subject, pooled and hierarchical; linear models are hard to beat on brain data [20, 42] and time series [51]. A stimulus-only linear model, the usual encoding base-line [9, 13], reaches a per-step correlation of only 0.04 on our trials, against 0.23 once EEG history is added; com-paring with it would overstate any forecaster’s gain several-fold. On trials, hierarchical ridge beats per-subject ridge by 0.008 skill, so comparing with per-subject ridge alone would double the apparent gain of the world model. 

Metrics. The headline metric is the forecast correlation r,as in brain encoding [9, 13]. It is the Pearson r across test windows for each forecast step and each feature, averaged over features and steps. 6.1. Forecasting continuous EEG 

As in scaling studies [21, 25], we pretrain EEG-World with-out video on nested pools of continuous EEG and com-pare it at every size with the best linear history model fit-ted on the same data (Fig. 4). The ranking reverses with scale. With tens of hours the linear model is clearly better; the world model overtakes it between 227 and 685 pooled hours and from then on wins in every subject. The non-linear part of EEG dynamics thus becomes learnable only once hundreds of hours are pooled across people, which explains why linear forecasters are hard to beat on typical EEG datasets of a few hours per person. 

The linear model saturates; the world model does not. 

Beyond the crossover the two models diverge. The best linear model levels off after a few hundred hours, so more data add nothing that a linear map from the recent past can use, while the world model keeps improving up to 2,012 h. What matters is a state carried through time. The same architecture with every nonlinearity removed falls below closed-form ridge, so the gain is not an artifact of training. An MLP and an iTransformer [31] that map the same con-text directly to the future, with the same heads and budget, barely improve on the linear model, so nonlinearity alone is not enough. LSTM and causal-Transformer cores, which like the GRU unroll a latent sequence step by step, gain al-most as much as the GRU (Fig. 4, right). What the linear models miss is therefore how the brain’s state evolves, not a richer map from past to future. The GRU is both the best and the smallest core, so at this scale a compact recurrent state matters more than capacity. 

6.2. Forecasting with the video as action 

On held-out clips EEG-World improves on the strongest history + video encoding model by 12% in r (Tab. 2). The advantage is small in the first second and grows with the horizon. To be as accurate at its last step as EEG-World, the linear model would have to stop 3.3 s earlier. The first sec-ond after the context is largely fixed by the immediate past, which any forecaster can copy; the world model’s value lies in carrying the state further ahead. Stateless nonlinear fore-casters help more on trials than on continuous EEG but stay well below EEG-World, so the recurrent state matters with video as it does without. 

Individual trajectories versus the average response. 

More video-paired data help the world model more than the linear model, and its clip-locked response grows with them (Fig. 5). Yet the linear encoding model follows the mean response across viewers more closely (Fig. 5b), while the world model forecasts each viewer more accurately (Fig. 5a). The two targets differ, because averaging over viewers removes the state-dependent part of the signal that the world model predicts. A benchmark that scores models only against averaged responses therefore favors encoding models and cannot see what a forecaster of individual brains adds. 

6.3. Transfer to unseen subjects 

We tested 24 subjects never used during development, once, with the analysis fixed in advance; both the subjects and the clips are new to the model. With the video model frozen and only a linear head fitted to each subject, EEG-World beats the best history + video linear model in 23 of the 24 (Fig. 6a); a model retrained on the other 41 subjects repli-cates the result (Fig. 6b). 

What transfers is the dynamics. A world model that never saw video transfers almost as well as the video model, although the video model does use the clip (Fig. 6c). What carries over to a new brain is mainly the dynamics learned from continuous EEG; the stimulus mapping learned on the training cohort adds little for someone new. Averag-ing world-model and linear forecasts beats both, so the two make partly independent errors. On two other EEG devices a frozen model with only a new head merely matches the linear models (Sec. I), so the dynamics transfer across peo-ple within a recording setup but not yet across devices.

Without calibration it already correlates better than the pooled linear model but is not more accurate in squared error, because the shared readout gets the shape of the trajectory right and the scale wrong. 6.4. What the world model learns 

The gain covers the whole scalp, most in α. It is pos-itive at every electrode and band, on continuous EEG and on video trials (Fig. 7a). A model that had learned a local response, such as occipital α to visual input, would give a localized map; a gain spread over the whole scalp points to a global brain state. On video trials it is largest in α (+20% ), the rhythm most closely tied to visual attention and arousal, and about three times as large as on continuous EEG. 

The gain is largest far from the brain’s usual state. Alinear forecaster can only let each feature relax toward its mean at a fixed rate. If the brain’s return depends on where it starts, a nonlinear model should gain most when the start-ing state is unusual. Indeed, the gain grows steadily with the distance of the starting state from the subject’s mean, and the farthest fifth of starting states gains 1.6× as much as the nearest in r (+5 .2% against +3 .2% ; Fig. 7b) and 3.8× as much in skill. The advantage is concentrated where linear relaxation is least adequate, in states far from equilibrium. The forecasts are smooth but not shrunk toward the mean (Sec. G). 

In-silico tuning matches recorded EEG. We use EEG-World as a virtual subject. Keeping the real context, we raise one video or audio feature for the whole clip and record the change in the forecast. On held-out validation clips we measure the same tuning in recorded EEG from the mean response of all viewers of each clip. The two agree across features and channels (Fig. 7c); for the strongest fea-ture, the first CLIP component, which separates plucked-string instruments from vehicles, the θ-band maps nearly coincide. The model was trained only to forecast single viewings and never saw an averaged response, so this agree-ment is not built in. Ranking vision encoders. Because the clip enters EEG-World only through its features, swapping the encoder turns the model into a test of vision models (Tab. 3). The fore-cast correlation hardly changes across encoders, since it is dominated by the brain’s own dynamics; the clip-locked re-sponse, which isolates the clip-driven part of the forecast, separates them clearly. V-JEPA 2 [3], trained to predict masked parts of videos in its latent space, drives this re-sponse best, 35% above our CLIP + DINOv2 input. Tem-poral input alone does not explain this, because VideoMAE, also a video model but trained to reconstruct pixels, ranks with the image models. The objective of predicting video in a latent space thus appears to matter more than seeing motion. The main results keep the pre-registered CLIP + DINOv2 input. 

6.5. Stimulus identification 

Under NeuroWorld’s trajectory-matching protocol [9], EEG-World finds the right trial among the subject’s test viewings far above chance and slightly more often than the linear model. But a “model” that ignores the video and re-peats the context mean already reaches 12 .5× chance, and widening the candidate pool to all viewings moves the same forecasts from 16 × to 145 × chance. The protocol mainly measures how persistent the starting state is and how the pool is drawn. Under our persistence-controlled protocol, where ignoring the video scores exactly chance, both EEG-World and the linear model identify the clip above chance but are not separated, because a single viewing carries little clip-specific information in band power. This information adds up across viewers (Fig. 8). Averaging each candidate’s score over all viewings of a test clip, each forecast from its own context, raises top-1 identification from 0.8% to 3.8% of the 394 clips (chance 0.25%; linear model 3.3%), and the top candidate shares the true clip’s category in 13% of clips (chance 4%). This pooled analysis is post hoc. 

## 7. Discussion and limitations 

The brain is the world. In every analysis the strongest predictor of the next seconds of EEG was the brain’s own state, and what transferred to unseen subjects was the dynamics learned from continuous EEG. A brain-based benchmark for vision that ignores these dynamics attributes to the stimulus what belongs to the brain; history-aware baselines and persistence-controlled metrics are cheap and should be standard. Because the advantage grows with training EEG, forecasting is a natural pretraining objective for EEG foundation models. 

Limitations. We forecast one representation (per-second log band power); finer time scales and source-space targets remain open. Video helped the trained cohort but added no net gain on unseen subjects. The core subjects’ test sets were used in several pre-registered analyses, all listed in Sec. K; the confirmatory transfer result uses 24 subjects tested once. Clips were shown in a largely fixed order, so carry-over from the previous clip is controlled only through the swap analyses. 

Ethics. The study was approved by the Science and Tech-nology Ethics Committee of the Southern University of Sci-8ence and Technology (approval no. 20260399). All par-ticipants gave written informed consent and were compen-sated.

11 A. Implementation details 

Sizes. Context Tc = 6 and rollout Tf = 10 steps of 1 s (16-s windows); D = 155 features (31 channels × 5 bands of log power); H = 512 . The encoder e is linear, Lay-erNorm, GELU and dropout 0.1; the trunk f is linear and GELU; the video pathway g is a two-layer MLP whose last layer is initialized at zero. The linear world model replaces the GRU by a linear recurrence and drops every GELU. The GRU core has 1.6M parameters and the shared model 2.0M (2.3M with the video pathway); each subject head adds 80k, so the 2,012-h model with its 70 heads has 7.6M. 

Optimization. Both stages minimize the mean squared error over the Tf rollout steps only; gradients flow back through all 16 GRU steps, including the context, and no ob-served EEG enters during the rollout (no teacher forcing). Stage 1: AdamW [32], learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 0.01, batch 64, warm-up and cosine decay over 80 epochs, early stopping on the selection error; 250 steps per epoch at 908 h and 555 at 2,012 h. Stage 2: video trials of 65 subjects (226,979 viewings, 630 h), half of each batch from continu-ous windows (chosen on validation); learning rate 3×10 −4

for the pretrained parameters (selected from four candidate rates on validation clips) and 10 −3 for the video pathway and new heads. 

Baselines. Selection data are 10% of each subject’s recordings for continuous EEG and the viewings of the 391 validation clips for video trials. All models, linear or not, choose their hyperparameters on these data, and the trained ones also their stopping epoch; none is refitted on them. Ridge penalties are chosen per subject from 10 −4

to 10 5 times the mean eigenvalue of the design matrix, ex-tended to 10 7 for the shrinkage of hierarchical ridge toward the pooled weights; for history + video ridge, the scale of the video block is chosen from 1, 0.3, 0.1, 0.03 and 0.01. The stateless baselines, a three-layer MLP and an iTrans-former [31], choose width, depth and learning rate in the same way. 

Models for unseen subjects. The 2,012-h continuous pool and the 65-subject video set include the training data of the 24 unseen subjects, so the models of Sec. 6.3 are trained without them. Figure 6a uses the frozen video model fine-tuned on 54,133 trials before these subjects were used; Fig. 6b and the zero-shot test use a model fine-tuned from the same pretrained core on the other 41 subjects of the video set. The pooled and hierarchical linear baselines are refitted on the same subjects as each model, so no com-parison involves data of the 24 subjects beyond their own calibration data. 

## B. Data 

Public assets. Of the 2,584 h of EEG, 2,122 h were recorded on four public video datasets: AVE [44] (4,097 clips of 10 s in 28 event categories; 1,016 h), MUSIC-AVQA [29] (536 h), the Perception Test [39] (510 h) and the EEG Moments Dataset [14] (60 h). The remaining 462 h, mostly from an earlier recording phase, were recorded on other short videos. Only AVE clips enter the video experi-ments; the other three public datasets contribute continuous EEG. The ancillary file ave_clip_split.csv lists the public identifier of every AVE clip and whether it is a train-ing (3,312), validation (391) or test (394) clip. All stimulus features come from frozen public encoders. 

In-house assets. The EEG recordings are human-subject data collected under an ethics-approved protocol with writ-ten informed consent. All analyses identify participants by pseudonymous codes only. 

EEG preprocessing. The cap samples at 250 Hz with the reference at FCz; the corpus preprocessing band-passes at 0.1–96 Hz, applies 50 and 60 Hz notch filters and resamples to 256 Hz. Clip onsets are mapped to the EEG clock as de-scribed below. Each recording is cut into 1-s bins on the cor-rected EEG time axis, starting 6 s before its first clip onset. For every bin and channel, the power spectrum is a mul-titaper estimate with discrete prolate spheroidal tapers of time–half-bandwidth product 2, keeping tapers with spec-tral concentration above 0.9, after removing the mean and weighting the tapers by their eigenvalues. Band power is the mean spectral power over the frequency bins of δ 1–4, θ

4–8, α 8–13, β 13–30 and γ 30–45 Hz, and the feature is its base-10 logarithm, giving 31 ×5 = 155 features per second. A bin is marked bad when any channel’s peak-to-peak am-plitude exceeds 500 μV; features are z-scored per recording over its good bins, and windows that contain a bad bin are skipped. 

Stimulus features. For each second of a clip, Open-CLIP [6] and DINOv2-L [37] image embeddings are av-eraged over four frames; each is reduced to 24 principal components fitted on the training clips and scaled to unit variance there. The sound track, converted to 16-kHz mono, gives the loudness of each second as its RMS level in dB, floored at 16-bit silence, and its spectral flux as the mean positive change of log short-time power with a 10-ms hop; both are z-scored on the training clips. A flag marks the sec-onds in which a clip is on screen, and all features are zero before onset. The encoders compared in Tab. 3 are used in the same way, with 16 frames per second for the video models. 12 Clock correction. The device clock differs from the presentation computer’s by a median of −980 ppm (IQR 

−1,430 to −760 ). Left uncorrected, onsets at the end of a recording (median 31 min) would be off by a median 1.5 s, 4.5 s at the 90th percentile and up to 10 s. The clock offset and drift rate of each recording are fitted with a matched fil-ter on 1–30 Hz EEG around clip onsets and offsets, on odd clips, and tested on even clips. Where the acquisition soft-ware also logged device sample counts, the two estimates agree (median difference 1 ppm, r = 0 .994 , 501 record-ings). The data-driven timing check in Sec. 3 (posterior α

at the 2-s blank, 1,765 of 1,820 recordings in the expected seconds) uses the training clips of recordings with at least 40 viewings. Without the correction the video gain of a lin-ear model nearly halves. The constant offset (median 51 ms) includes the mean display and transmission delay; the abso-lute latency was not measured with a photodiode. 

## C. Viewers’ states persist through a clip 

Figure 1 rests on the observation that viewers begin the same clip in different states and that the difference outlasts the clip’s onset. Figure S1 tests this on every test view-ing. We split the viewings of each clip by a feature’s mean over the 6 s before onset and follow the top and bottom 10% through the clip. For 141 of the 155 features the two groups still differ over seconds 2–10 of the clip in all 15 subjects, and for all 155 in at least 13 (Fig. S1c). The prediction from the video alone, the mean response of the other sub-jects to the same clip, carries almost none of this difference (median 0.3% of the measured one), so a stimulus-only en-coding model cannot express it. The most persistent direction keeps a correlation of λ =0.81 between its pre-clip and in-clip means. Its weights lie on β and γ power at the temporal electrodes (T7, T8; Fig. S1a), a pattern typical of scalp muscle activity, so part of what persists is likely muscle tone rather than neu-ral activity. The first principal component weights all fea-tures about equally and keeps a correlation of 0.38; poste-rior α keeps only 0.25. Along all three directions, EEG-World and the linear model with EEG history and video keep the groups as far apart as they are measured (2.3 and 2.4 SD against 2.4 SD for the persistent component), while the video-only prediction does not (0.01 SD; Fig. S1d). Two conclusions follow. A large part of each viewer’s response is carried over from before the clip, which is why our baselines see the same EEG history rather than the stim-ulus alone. And carrying a state forward does not by itself separate the world model from the linear model, since any history-aware forecaster does it; the world model’s advan-tage lies in how the state evolves, most of all when it starts far from the subject’s usual state (Fig. 7b).

The world model’s advantage lies in the dynamics that the stimulus perturbs, not in a better stimulus encoding. 

## E. Where the clip’s features act

## G. Calibration: smooth forecasts are not a col-lapse to the mean 

The forecasts are smoother than the EEG, as forecasts of an uncertain future must be. To check that they are not simply shrunk toward the mean, we regressed the measured EEG on the forecast, per subject, step and feature. On continu-ous EEG the slope is 0.93–0.99 at every step, and the pre-registered criterion (slope within [0 .8, 1.25] at all 10 steps in at least 13 of 15 subjects, on validation and test data) holds (14 /15 and 15 /15 ). On video trials the slope falls from 0.93 at the first second to 0.81 at the tenth, so the criterion holds in only 6/15 : late forecasts slightly overshoot , the opposite of a collapse, and the best linear model does the same (0.95 to 0.79). In both settings the forecast variance is close to r2

times the measured variance, as for a calibrated forecast.

## K. All pre-registered tests 

Tables S5 and S6 list every pre-registered test run on the data of this paper, H1 to H60, including those the main text does not discuss. Before each round we wrote down its hy-potheses, models, selection procedure, decision rules and the test data it would score, and scored the test data only af-terwards. Unless noted, a test is decided on skill by the rule of Sec. 5; for H12–H14 the subject criterion was more than half of the 24 (the outcomes are the same under 16/24), and on the lab cap it was 9/12. Non-inferiority tests require the lower bound of the 95% interval above the stated margin, and second attempts after a failed test use p < 0.025 . The identifiers H7, H39 and H40 were never registered. The 15 core subjects’ test data were scored in many rounds, so later results on them are not independent replications; the 24 unseen subjects were first scored in the confirmatory test (H12–H14) and later in H24–H26, H53 and H53b. Before this corpus existed, pilot models trained only on the lab-cap data and on the EEG Moments Dataset [14], a few hours per subject, did not beat linear forecasters; H41–H45 repeat the lab-cap comparison with the pretrained model. Of the 60 tests, 36 were met and 23 failed; H35 was two-sided. Several failures shaped the paper. The first video model, trained from scratch, lost to the linear model (H4); pretraining on continuous EEG helped (H9) but needed a second attempt to beat the best linear model (H8, H11). The linear model’s video term matched the mean response across viewers better than the world model’s (H18, H20), although the world model’s clip-locked response grew with more data (H19). On unseen subjects’ continuous EEG the frozen model beat the best linear model only nominally (H12), zero-shot transfer lost (H24, H25), and multi-device pretraining transferred slightly worse to new people (H53, H53b), so the main models are trained on one device. Fine-tuning the video pathway on the 2,012-h core with the 908-h recipe was worse (H32, H33), as early stopping ended 7 of 8 runs at epoch 8 before the video pathway converged. On the lab cap, pretraining turned the pilots’ loss into parity but not into a win (H41–H45). Identification did not separate EEG-World from the linear model (H23, H34; Sec.
