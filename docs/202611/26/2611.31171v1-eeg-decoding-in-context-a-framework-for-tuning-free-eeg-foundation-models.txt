## ABSTRACT 

Electroencephalography (EEG) decoding is a central route toward brain–computer interfaces for communication, rehabilitation, cognitive assessment, and neurological monitoring. Recent EEG foundation models have substantially advanced reusable signal representations, but their downstream use still typically requires a task-specific decoder, gradient-based adaptation, and validation-driven model selection. We investigate a complementary foundation-model paradigm based on in-context learning: labeled EEG recordings define the decoding task as context, and new recordings are predicted without updating model parameters. We first pair raw EEG features with a pretrained general-purpose in-context predictor. This direct formulation provides a promising starting point, but its performance remains limited and unstable and does not generalize reliably across EEG paradigms. We find that suitable context construction can address these limitations by improving the effective signal-to-noise ratio and exposing temporal and spatial structure. Building on this insight, we introduce a general framework that combines EEG-specific context construction with a frozen in-context predictor and a metric-based context adaptation mechanism, which matches each downstream task to an appropriate context representation without relying on validation data. Across ten datasets spanning seven EEG decoding paradigms, our method achieves the highest mean BAcc among eleven specialized and fine-tuned EEG baselines, without task-specific parameter updates or validation-based model selection. This framework provides a practical path toward tuning-free EEG foundation models in which adaptation is expressed through context rather than parameter updates.

Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a portable and comparatively low-cost means of recording brain electrical activity at high temporal resolution. Decoding EEG has consequently become central to brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) and related neurotechnology applications, including assistive communication and control, neurorehabilitation, cognitive-state assessment, sleep analysis, affective computing, and neurological monitoring [1, 2, 3]. Progress in signal processing and deep learning has enabled increasingly capable decoders across mo-tor imagery, event-related potentials, steady-state responses, sleep, seizure, and clinical paradigms [4, 5, 6]. These advances make reusable EEG de-coding models scientifically and practically impor-tant: a useful model should transfer accumulated knowledge to a new decoding problem rather than require training anew for every task. arXiv:2611.31171v1 [cs.LG] 23 Nov 2026 Deployment remains challenging because EEG is low-amplitude, artifact-prone, and nonstationary, while its distribution varies across subjects, sessions, devices, and recording conditions [7, 8]. Across paradigms, class-discriminative information appears in distinct temporal, spectral, and spatial structures [9, 10, 5, 11]. A practical decoder must therefore adapt from limited labeled recordings without assuming that one representation or decision rule transfers unchanged. Calibration can itself be lengthy and user dependent [12], and collecting more trials from the same small participant pool does not necessarily substitute for participant diversity [13]. This creates a recurring requirement: use the labeled EEG that is available for a task to predict held-out recordings quickly, reliably, and with little target-time optimization. Recent EEG foundation models pursue reusability primarily through pretrained encoders. BIOT introduces frequency-aware tokenization for heterogeneous biosignals [14]. LaBraM scales masked pretraining over large EEG corpora [15]. CSBrain explicitly models cross-scale temporal and regional dependencies [11]. Although their downstream protocols differ, applying these models to a new EEG task generally requires learning at least a task-specific prediction component; many methods further optimize the backbone or lightweight modules and use validation data to select checkpoints or protocols, incurring additional computation and tuning expertise [16]. Pretraining therefore provides reusable representations, but does not usually yield a complete decoder that can be deployed unchanged. As illustrated in Figure 1, specialized training and representation transfer both adapt to a new task through parameter optimization; we investigate a third route in which labeled context specifies the task .In-context learning (ICL) offers a complementary adaptation interface: a predictor receives labeled examples and a query together and produces a prediction while its pretrained parameters remain fixed [17]. General-purpose ICL predictors such as TabPFN [18, 19] and TabICL [20] demonstrate this capability across heterogeneous structured prediction problems. As a first attempt, we vectorize each EEG trial and provide the raw features directly to a frozen ICL predictor. This simple formulation is promising on several tasks, but its performance is uneven across paradigms and not consistently competitive. Our results point to context construction as a central design challenge: raw vectorization preserves the waveform, yet may foreground nuisance variation and obscure the spectral, spatial, and local temporal relationships on which different EEG tasks depend. We therefore ask how EEG-specific context construction can reduce nuisance variation and expose task-relevant structure to a frozen predictor. To answer this question, we introduce a general framework that combines EEG-specific context construction, a frozen ICL predictor, a support-only context adaptation rule, and optional post-hoc calibration. Our contributions are: • We introduce a general framework for constructing tuning-free EEG foundation models from frozen general-purpose in-context predictors, in which labeled EEG context replaces task-specific parameter adaptation at deployment. • We identify context construction as the key interface between EEG and a frozen predictor, and develop complementary waveform, spectral, covariance, regional, and local-temporal contexts together with a metric-based adaptation rule that selects a candidate context using labeled support data alone, without validation performance or query information. • Across ten datasets spanning seven EEG decoding paradigms, our support-only Metric route achieves the highest mean BAcc among eleven specialized and fine-tuned EEG baselines, improves over raw context on every dataset, without downstream gradient optimization or validation-based selection. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Specialized EEG Decoding 

EEG decoding has traditionally been developed through task-specific signal-processing and prediction pipelines. Clas-sical approaches combine temporal or frequency filtering with spatial filters and shallow classifiers [1]. Filter-bank com-mon spatial patterns expose band-specific discriminative activity for motor imagery [9], while covariance-based methods represent cross-channel relationships on the symmetric positive-definite manifold [21, 10]. These approaches encode explicit assumptions about where class information lies, yet large-scale evaluations show that their relative performance depends on the dataset and paradigm, with covariance-based pipelines remaining competitive in many BCI settings [3]. Deep specialized decoders replace parts of this handcrafted pipeline with learned temporal and spatial operations. Convolutional architectures learn directly from EEG waveforms [4], and EEGNet uses compact temporal and depthwise spatial filters across several BCI paradigms [5]. Subsequent architectures explore convolutional, multibranch, and transformer-based temporal–spatial modeling [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Transfer learning, alignment, semi-supervised learning, and calibration-reduction methods further address subject and session shift, but still fit or select task-specific components using target data [8, 12]. Collectively, these methods show that useful EEG evidence can be captured by waveform, spectral, covariance, spatial, or local temporal representations. These established signal axes motivate both our complementary context families and the support-set descriptors used to adapt context construction across tasks. 22.2 EEG Foundation Models 

EEG foundation models seek to replace task-by-task representation learning with reusable pretrained encoders. Models such as BIOT, LaBraM, EEGPT, and CBraMod explore cross-dataset or large-scale self-supervised pretraining over heterogeneous EEG signals [14, 15, 28, 29]. Subsequent work extends reusable representation learning across modalities, acquisition setups, anatomical regions, temporal scales, and electrode topologies [30, 31, 32, 11, 33]. Across these designs, the pretrained encoder remains the principal reusable object. Downstream deployment, however, still commonly specifies a new task through optimized parameters. Depending on the model and protocol, this may involve full fine-tuning, parameter-efficient adaptation, or learning a prediction head or aggregation module. Comparative evidence shows that the selected adaptation protocol materially changes both accuracy and resource use [16]. NeuralBench further motivates evaluation across heterogeneous tasks rather than conclusions drawn from a single paradigm [6]. EEG foundation models therefore make representations reusable, but generally retain parameter optimization as the interface for task adaptation. In-context learning offers a complementary route in which labeled examples specify the task directly. 

2.3 In-Context Learning 

In-context learning conditions a pretrained predictor on labeled examples and a query, producing a task-specific prediction without updating the predictor’s parameters [17]. General-purpose ICL predictors provide a concrete instance of this interface. TabPFN pretrains a transformer over synthetic prediction problems and conditions predictions on labeled rows from a new dataset [18, 19]; TabICL extends this principle to larger structured datasets [20]. Because these predictors are pretrained independently of EEG, they provide a useful basis for asking whether EEG task adaptation can be expressed through a context interface rather than EEG-specific parameter updates. ECHO provides an EEG-specific approach to contextual decoding [34]. It learns contextual behavior through a meta-learning-like support–query objective and autoregressively predicts task and label tokens without inference-time parameter updates. Its held-out evaluations cover new datasets only within EEG task families represented during training. Despite avoiding parameter updates, ECHO remains computationally inefficient at inference because each target trial requires a separate support-conditioned autoregressive decoding pass. Our framework instead reuses a general-purpose ICL predictor pretrained independently of EEG and makes EEG-specific context construction the task-adaptation interface.

4.2 Temporal and Spatial Structure 

Feature choice alone may still hide localized or anatomically organized evidence. We therefore define information operators which act before τfeat :

Tinfo = {Id , τ REG , τ MV }. (9) 

Regional pooling. We augment each trial with pseudo-channels obtained by fixed pooling statistics over audited anatomical channel groups, and then apply the Riemannian feature operator. This deterministic augmentation exposes coarse regional organization without learning additional weights [11, 33]. 

Local temporal views. We split each trial into m temporal windows and treat each window as a context instance. Support labels are repeated across views, while query probabilities are averaged back to the trial level. This exposes localized evidence without learning a temporal aggregation module [28, 11]. 

4.3 Inference-Time Strategies 

Inference-time strategies modify the labeled context or class decomposition without changing the frozen predictor. We define 

Tinfer = {Id , τ CB , τ OVR }. (10) 

Counterbalanced support. We use seeded subsampling to retain the same number of support trials from every class. Context budgets are then enforced over complete trials after accounting for any trial-to-row expansion introduced by the selected context operator. 

One-versus-rest prediction. For a K-class task, we construct K binary labeled contexts and obtain one positive-class probability from each frozen contextual prediction. Normalizing these scores yields the multiclass distribution. This decomposition can simplify a large native label space, but requires K prediction passes.

## 6 Conclusion 

We introduce a general framework for constructing tuning-free EEG foundation models by coupling EEG-specific context construction with a frozen general-purpose ICL predictor, making labeled context the interface for target-task adaptation. Having identified context construction as the central challenge, we develop a family of complementary contexts and a support-only metric-based adaptation rule that selects an appropriate context for each target task without validation performance or query information. Across ten datasets spanning seven EEG decoding paradigms, our Metric route achieves the highest mean BAcc among eleven specialized and fine-tuned EEG baselines, improves over 9raw context on every dataset, and closely approaches the inspection Oracle. These results demonstrate that EEG-specific context construction and support-derived adaptation enable a frozen general-purpose ICL predictor to serve as an effective EEG decoder without task-specific parameter updates or validation-based model selection. More broadly, our framework provides a practical path toward EEG foundation models in which labeled context, rather than downstream optimization, specifies the target decoding task.

A.5 Support-Only Metric Route Neurophysiological motivation. The four routing groups reflect complementary organizations routinely examined in EEG analysis. Task-related oscillatory modulation motivates band-power descriptors; distributed scalp activity motivates cross-channel correlation and covariance summaries; transient, rhythmic, and nonstationary events motivate autocorrelation and line-length descriptors; and large deflections or burst-like morphology motivate amplitude and higher-order statistics [9, 21, 10, 38, 2]. The route uses these established signal organizations as low-dimensional probes of class-conditioned structure in the target support set. A dominant score indicates where discriminative organization is most visible under these probes; it does not identify a unique physiological generator or establish a causal neural mechanism. 

Diagnostic support subset. The Metric route first counterbalances the labeled support trials. Descriptor extraction uses at most 256 trials, sampled with the registered seed through a balanced-first rule: it allocates an equal per-class quota and then fills any remaining capacity from unused support trials. This bounds routing cost while retaining minority classes. The diagnostic subset contains support EEG and labels only; it is never formed from validation or query data. 15 Signal descriptors. ## B Experimental Details and Reproducibility 

This section records the experimental protocol corresponding to the main text. The primary evaluation contains ten EEG datasets spanning seven decoding paradigms. The training split supplies labeled support, the held-out test split supplies queries, and our frozen-ICL method does not use a target validation split for context selection, predictor fitting, or model selection. 

B.1 Datasets and Stored Trial Arrays 

The suite contains two motor-imagery datasets, one imagined-speech dataset, one emotion-recognition dataset, one mental-arithmetic/stress dataset, two seizure-detection datasets, two sleep-staging datasets, and one depression-diagnosis dataset [39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 35, 36, 44, 45, 46]. We use the dataset releases and task labels supplied by their original repositories and do not collect new human-participant data. ISRUC uses subject-disjoint stored partitions: subjects 1–80, 81–90, and 91–100 form the training, validation, and test partitions, respectively. For the remaining datasets, we retain the split keys or manifests supplied by the corresponding wrappers and do not claim subject-disjoint evaluation unless the source metadata establishes it. 

B.2 Preprocessing and Partition Roles 

Following the main text, raw recordings are preprocessed with band-pass filtering, notch filtering, resampling, and amplitude normalization before context construction [29, 11]. The stored arrays consumed by the evaluator are sampled at 200 Hz. Most datasets contain 30 one-second windows per trial; BCIC2020-3 contains three one-second windows. The waveform panels in Figure 4 of the main text display a centered three-second excerpt for visualization, whereas routing and prediction use the complete stored trial. Non-finite values are replaced by zero, the registered channel order is applied, and the stored temporal shape is preserved. Context operators act only after these common preprocessing steps. In particular, dataset-level amplitude normalization is not the per-trial τNORM operator defined in Equation (S7). For the frozen predictor, the stored training partition provides labeled support and the held-out test partition provides query trials. The validation partition is not read by context construction, Metric routing, frozen contextual prediction, or threshold selection. Query labels are exposed only after prediction to compute evaluation metrics. Trainable baselines use their registered training protocol and may use the validation partition for early stopping or checkpoint selection; this difference reflects their standard downstream adaptation setting. The deployment boundary is therefore: • the target support EEG, support labels, class count, and valid participant or recording groups may be used; • target-validation performance, query labels, and query metrics may not be used by context construction or the Metric route; • the target-wise inspection Oracle is reported only as an undeployable evaluation upper bound. 17 B.3 Context Budgets and Frozen Predictor 

We evaluate full support and numeric budgets of 2,048, 4,096, 8,192, 16,384, 32,768, and 65,536 effective support rows. As defined in Equation (S16), the budget is applied after trial-to-row expansion and retains complete trials. A full-support result can represent a smaller numeric budget only when its recorded effective support rows already fit that budget; a smaller-context result is never promoted to a full-support result. The main-text comparison instantiates ficl with the official TabPFN-2.5 checkpoint [47]. Its registered configuration uses random state 42, eight ensemble estimators, disabled automatic estimator scaling, and enabled pretraining-limit override for wide EEG contexts. The evaluator uses one preprocessing worker, four PyTorch CPU threads, one interoperation thread, low-memory fitting, and query chunking for wide or multiview contexts. No target-validation prelude, target-task gradient update, or decision calibration is used in the canonical context-comparison results. The supplementary predictor-variant analysis replaces only the frozen backend with the official TabPFN-3 checkpoint [49]. It retains the same seed, eight-estimator setting, disabled automatic estimator scaling, pretraining-limit override, support–query partitions, and context operators. TabPFN-3 is therefore reported as a backend robustness analysis; it does not replace TabPFN-2.5 in the main-text comparison. 

Parameter-development protocol. The context family, the seven effective-row budgets, the four descriptor groups, the Metric score, the deterministic operator mapping, and its tie/fallback rules are fixed parts of the method rather than target-task hyperparameters selected by validation performance. The Metric route matches a context to a downstream task using labeled support statistics alone. Optional calibration uses prespecified recall targets and is evaluated separately from the canonical comparison. Consequently, the proposed method performs no target-task hyperparameter search. 

B.4 Baselines and Training Protocols 

We compare with seven specialized decoders—EEGNet, ST-Transformer, ContraWR, CNN-Transformer, FFCL, SPaRCNet, and EEGConformer—and four pretrained EEG models with downstream adaptation—LaBraM, BIOT, CBraMod, and CSBrain [5, 22, 27, 23, 24, 26, 25, 15, 14, 29, 11]. All baselines receive the same stored training, validation, and test partitions as applicable to their registered protocols. The specialized decoders are trained for at most 100 epochs with Adam, learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −5, batch size 512, and early-stopping patience five. BIOT, LaBraM, and CBraMod use 50-epoch AdamW fine-tuning with learning rate 10 −4, weight decay 5 × 10 −2, batch size 32, label smoothing 0.1, gradient-norm clipping at one, and validation-BAcc checkpoint selection. CSBrain uses 50 epochs and its registered dataset-specific downstream recipe. These trainable methods optimize task-specific parameters, whereas our predictor and context rule remain fixed.

Scientific interpretation. The context families are motivated by established EEG practice. Spectral representations ex-pose oscillatory composition, covariance and Riemannian representations expose cross-channel second-order geometry, regional pseudo-channels expose coarse anatomical organization, and local views expose transient temporal structure [9, 21, 10, 5, 11]. These context families provide signal-informed inductive structure for decoding. Future frequency-band ablations, topographic perturbations, and window-level attribution could further characterize the physiological information they capture. 

Deployment cost. Deployment cost depends on the selected context and frozen predictor. Wide waveform features increase memory, multiview contexts expand the number of support and query instances, and OVR requires K contextual prediction passes. Riemannian and regional operators add deterministic preprocessing. 22
