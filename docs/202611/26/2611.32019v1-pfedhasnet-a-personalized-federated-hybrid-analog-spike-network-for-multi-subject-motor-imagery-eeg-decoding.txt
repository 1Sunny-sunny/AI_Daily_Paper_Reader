# PFedHASNet: A Personalized Federated Hybrid Analog–Spike Network for Multi-Subject Motor Imagery EEG Decoding 

Peijia Li, Ruidong Ma, Xianghong Lin, Xiangwen Wang, Guoyong Shi, Shuai Huang, and Hongqi Li 

Abstract — Motor imagery electroencephalography (MI-EEG) data are often collected and stored separately across subjects or institutions, and EEG acquisition and anno-tation remain costly. Training models solely on individ-ual subject data limits the use of information from other subjects, whereas centralizing raw EEG recordings raises privacy and data governance concerns. Federated learn-ing (FL) enables collaborative model training across dis-tributed clients without sharing raw EEG data, providing a practical approach to leveraging information from mul-tiple subjects. Accordingly, we propose PFedHASNet, a personalized federated hybrid analog–spike network for MI-EEG decoding. The shared encoder integrates continuous-valued temporal features, leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) spike responses, and channel-wise log-variance statistics through learnable fusion weights. Client-private residual adapters and classifiers perform subject-specific feature refinement and personalized prediction, respectively. Ex-periments on held-out trials from participating subjects across four public MI-EEG datasets show that PFedHASNet achieves the highest mean client macro-F1 among 18 model configurations in cross-model comparisons, while main-taining competitive decoding performance with a compact client-side model in comparisons using the same back-bone. Ablation studies further examine the contributions of hybrid representations and personalization components to decoding performance. 

Index Terms — Motor Imagery, Electroencephalography, Personalized Federated Learning, Hybrid Analog–Spike Representation, Client Heterogeneity

To this end, we propose PFedHASNet, a Personalized Fed-erated Hybrid Analog–Spike Network for multi-subject MI-EEG decoding. > •

We propose a lightweight, personalized federated framework for EEG decoding of motor imagery across multiple subjects. PFedHASNet combines shared representation learning with subject-specific adaptation; it supports collaborative learning among clients via a shared encoder and utilizes client-private residual adapters and classifiers to achieve feature refinement and personalized prediction, respectively.  

> •

We designed a hybrid analog–spike representation module that fuses temporal features with channel statistics. HASR fuses continuous-valued temporal sum-maries extracted from spatio-temporal features, LIF pulse firing rates, and channel-level logarithmic variance statis-tics extracted directly from EEG inputs to construct a shared representation for personalized decoding via learnable fusion weights.  

> •

We conducted multi-dataset evaluations to analyze the roles of representation learning and personalized design. On four public MI-EEG datasets, we evaluated the framework’s decoding performance through cross-model comparisons, comparisons with backbone fed-erated strategies, component ablation, and client-level analysis. We characterized the contributions of each com-ponent and the differences in benefits across participants, and examined the trade-offs between performance and client-side model size. 

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Motor Imagery EEG Decoding 

Deep MI-EEG models employ compact spatiotemporal con-volutions, spectral–spatial statistics, and temporal convolution or attention for longer-range dependencies [1]–[3], [24], [25]. Recent work has explored multiscale spatial–temporal fusion, lightweight residual convolution, and fine-grained spatial– frequency–time features for MI decoding [26]–[28]. SST-DPN combines spatial–spectral attention, multiscale variance pooling, and dual prototype learning [29]. Context-based adap-tation has been explored to address cross-session variabil-ity [30]. Those methods motivate richer EEG representations, but do not determine how shared and client-specific parameters should be organized during federated training.

However, a unified model may have difficulty accommodating differences in signal distributions and decision boundaries across subjects, motivating PFL. PFL methods address client heterogeneity through dif-ferent parameter-sharing and optimization strategies. FedPer separates shared feature extraction layers from client-private prediction layers and jointly updates them during local train-ing [12]. For heterogeneous EEG datasets, the Sandwich frame-work combines federated and transfer learning with dataset-specific input networks, a shared network, and individual classifiers [31]. For multi-subject MI-EEG decoding, PFedHASNet extends shared–private personalization with an EEG-specific hybrid analog–spike encoder and a private residual adapter for fea-ture correction. Local training jointly updates the encoder and private modules; the server aggregates shared parameters according to sample counts. This design combines hybrid EEG representation learning with subject-specific prediction. Supervised SNN algorithms and evaluation practices have been reviewed by Wang et al. [32]. Subsequent studies have investigated one-pass online learning using gradient descent for multilayer SNNs [33] and joint synaptic weight–delay learning [34]. For EEG applications, spiking networks have been studied for MI decoding [19] and epilepsy detection [35]; federated SNN research has also examined decentralized train-ing [20]. In PFedHASNet, average LIF firing rates serve as one feature pathway alongside continuous-valued temporal features and channel-wise LogVar statistics.

B. Multi-Scale Spatiotemporal Feature Extraction 

As illustrated in Figure 2(a), the shared spatiotemporal backbone transforms each preprocessed EEG trial X ∈ RC×T

into a temporally ordered feature sequence. The input is processed by parallel temporal convolution branches with different receptive fields, capturing short-term signal fluctu-ations and rhythmic patterns over multiple temporal scales. Their outputs are concatenated to integrate temporal informa-tion. Subsequent spatial convolution models the relationships among EEG channels, allowing the network to extract spatially discriminative patterns from the multi-scale features. The resulting features are further processed by a residual temporal convolutional network (TCN) comprising two di-lated blocks with dilation factors of 1 and 2, respectively. Dilated convolutions expand the temporal receptive field to capture contextual dependencies beyond the initial convolu-tional stages, while residual connections preserve preceding representations and facilitate feature propagation. Temporal pooling then reduces the sequence length, followed by linear projection and GELU activation to obtain a compact feature representation. The backbone produces a feature sequence H =[h1, . . . , h T ′ ] ∈ RT ′×d, where T ′ denotes the reduced tem-poral length and d = 64 is the feature dimension. This sequence provides the shared input to the analog and spiking branches of HASR, as shown in Figure 2(b). In parallel, the preprocessed EEG input is directly routed to the channel-wise LogVar branch, preserving signal-level variance infor-mation independently of the learned convolutional features. This design enables HASR to combine learned spatiotemporal representations with complementary channel-wise statistics.

IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Datasets and Experimental Settings 

We evaluate all methods on four public motor imagery EEG (MI-EEG) datasets: BCI Competition IV Dataset 2b (BNCI 2014-004) [36], BCI Competition IV Dataset 1 [37], BCI Competition IV Dataset 2a (BNCI 2014-001) [38], and Weibo2014 [39]. Each subject constitutes one federated client, with training on local training trials and evaluation on held-out test trials from the same subject. Signals are linearly detrended, band-pass filtered at 8–30 Hz, and resampled to 250 Hz; each 4-s trial gives an input x ∈ RCd×1000 , with Cd channels for dataset d (Table I). Training, validation, and test trials are stored in sepa-rate, prepartitioned per-subject arrays. The recorded total training/validation/test trial counts are 3,058/622/2,840 for M1, 1,162/238/1,125 for M2, 2,160/432/2,592 for M3, and 1,956/414/2,370 for M4. The same predefined arrays are selected for all three random seeds; no new seed-specific 80/20 split is created. These recorded counts do not independently authenticate trial-level split identities or session separation. Federated configurations run for 100 rounds with full client participation and two local epochs per round by default; FedRep and Ditto follow the method-specific schedules in Table III. The recorded optimization settings are AdamW, learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −4, batch size 64, and maximum gradient norm 1.0. The default dropout is 0.2, with module-specific rates stated in Section III. Endpoint results use the prespecified round 100, without early stopping or test-based checkpoint selection. Round-wise test measurements are used for retrospective training-dynamics analysis. Central and Local are evaluated at epoch 200. For each dataset and model configuration, the endpoint performance is summarized across three independent random seeds (42, 2026, and 3407) and reported as the mean ±6 IEEE JOURNAL OF BIOMEDICAL AND HEALTH INFORMATICS 

sample standard deviation (SD). For each seed, the primary metric is calculated by first computing macro-F1 over the classes of each participating client and then averaging the client-level scores with equal weight, as defined in Eq. (10). The resulting dataset-level scores are subsequently averaged across seeds. The sample SD is calculated from the three seed-level scores using a denominator of 3 − 1 and reflects variation across random seeds, rather than variation among clients or a confidence interval. Accuracy, macro-precision, macro-recall, and test cross-entropy are summarized using the same client-then-seed averaging procedure. Cross-entropy loss is reported on its original numerical scale and is excluded from performance ranking. To summarize performance across datasets, Average is calculated as the arithmetic mean of the four dataset-level mean scores (M1–M4), assigning equal weight to each dataset regardless of its number of clients or test trials. Mean Rank is obtained by ranking the compared model configurations according to their mean macro-F1 within each dataset and averaging these ranks across the four datasets, with rank 1 indicating the highest score. These complementary summaries report the overall predictive performance and the relative ranking of each configuration across the evaluated datasets. On M2, PFedHASNet achieves a macro-F1 of 66.10% and an accuracy of 69.05%, compared with 65.70% and 69.64% for ADFCNN, respectively. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of PFedHASNet in multi-class MI-EEG decoding, with notable macro-F1 improvements on the four-class M3 and six-class M4 datasets. The 18 configurations combine different model families and training strategies. V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

PFedHASNet integrates shared hybrid representation learn-ing with client-private adaptation to enable collaborative MI-EEG decoding while keeping raw data local. Experiments on four public datasets demonstrate that the framework achieves competitive decoding performance on held-out trials from participating subjects while maintaining a compact client-side model. Ablation analyses indicate that personalization and channel-wise log-variance statistics are major contributors to performance improvements, whereas the benefits of the spike branch and residual adapters vary across datasets and con-figurations. These findings support retaining subject-specific feature refinement and prediction modules alongside shared EEG representations, providing a feasible approach to person-alized brain–computer interface modeling with decentralized data. Future work will investigate adaptation across recording sessions and explore personalized decoding for previously unseen subjects using limited calibration data.
