## Abstract 

In associative memories storing correlated patterns, retrieval of individual memories can compete with symmetric mixed states. A dynamical description is needed to determine how the initial state selects between these outcomes. We extend the generating-functional dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) for independent patterns to independent blocks of correlated binary patterns of fixed finite size. The resulting effective single-neuron process has a self-consistent cross-talk noise covariance and self-feedback coefficients that depend on the block-covariance eigenvalues. For hierarchically correlated patterns with nonmonotonic transfer, increasing the loading can permit retrieval from an imperfect initial state before further loading prevents it. DMFT reproduces the retrieval trajectories and basin geometry found in direct simulations. Near the lower boundary of this re-entrant region, direct networks reveal stationary saddles separating coexisting mixed and target-retrieval states. Parameter sensitivities of DMFT trajectories predict nearby shifts of this boundary. At the tested upper boundary, retrieval remains accessible from initial states with larger target overlap, indicating a change in basin access. Under fixed-point convergence assumptions, the theory recovers the equations of generalized self-consistent signal-to-noise analysis. 

1arXiv:2610.40060v1 [cond-mat.dis-nn] 29 Oct 2026 I. INTRODUCTION 

Associative memory models describe how recurrent neural networks retrieve stored pat-terns from corrupted initial states. In the Hopfield model, random binary patterns are embedded in the synaptic couplings by Hebb’s rule [1]. The statistical mechanics of this model is well understood when the patterns are independent [2, 3]. Memories with common structure require correlated patterns. Hierarchical and ultramet-ric ensembles have therefore been studied since the early development of associative memory [4–7]. These models support retrieval at different levels of a hierarchy; in particular, Gut-freund described retrieval of a descendant preceded and assisted by retrieval of its ancestors [5]. Correlated-pattern models have also been studied in relation to storage capacity [8] and symmetric and asymmetric mixed states, which overlap with several stored patterns [9]. Ref. [10] studied independent clusters whose descendants are generated from a latent ancestor and whose descendants alone enter the coupling. The generalized self-consistent signal-to-noise analysis (SCSNA) describes the stationary states and predicts two coexist-ing symmetric mixed states, each with equal overlaps with the descendants of a cluster. Simulations show that retrieval can approach a mixed state before selecting one memory pattern. The remaining question concerns the dynamics that selects among these states. A station-ary analysis identifies possible endpoints but does not determine the finite-time trajectory or its dependence on the initial state. Retrieval can also occur transiently when stable retrieval states are absent [11]. This distinction is especially important for nonmonotonic transfer functions. Such functions can increase storage capacity and alter the attractor structure [12–14]. The resulting dynamics does not generally follow an energy function. Ref. [15] analyzed this dynamics using the dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) for independent patterns. The analysis relates the basin of attraction to the cross-talk noise and the self-feedback coefficients. It also shows how the finite-time trajectory selects a branch when the stationary fixed-point condition has several roots. This independent-pattern theory provides the starting point for the present work. We extend this analysis to correlated patterns in independent blocks of fixed finite size.

> IV. RESULTS

We solved Eq. (34) with the kernels in Eqs. (31) and (32) and the self-consistency equa-tions (35)-(37). Direct simulations provide a finite-size comparison with the thermodynamic-limit dynamics. Unless stated otherwise, s = 3, b = 0 .61, N = 16000 and the observation time is T = 100. The figures use 1 .25 × 10 5 effective DMFT trajectories. Boundary re-finements use 10 6 trajectories and, for direct simulations, 64 disorder realizations with eight initial states per realization. A large target overlap alone does not distinguish individual retrieval from a symmetric mixed state. We therefore count the retrieval pattern ξ1,1 as retrieved when 

M1(T ) − max ν> 1 Mν (T )1 − b2 ≥ 12, (41) for s > 1 and b < 1. For s = 1, the criterion is M1(T ) ≥ 1/2. Non-target retrieval satisfies the same contrast between the largest and second-largest descendant overlaps. A symmetric endpoint is labelled η when its overlaps agree within three pairwise standard 10 errors, p2(1 − b2)/n for n effective trajectories, and their mean is within 0 .01 of (1 + b2)/2. The remaining endpoints form the failure class. These finite-time criteria do not require microscopic convergence. The fixed-point analysis below additionally requires the stationary assumptions in Appendix B.

15 C. Basin selection and reentrance 

The lower retrieval boundary marks a change from the symmetric mixed state to target retrieval. Both attractors remain accessible on either side of this boundary in the directly studied networks. To identify the separating dynamics, we used edge tracking, developed to follow the “edge of chaos” in shear flows [19]. Potential fields leading to different attractors are repeatedly bisected and the bracket is advanced with the original dynamics. At b = 0 .61 and M 0 = 0 .20, this procedure finds stationary saddles with one unstable direction in three networks. The two unstable branches lead to mixed and target retrieval. We increased the loading by adding uncondensed blocks while keeping the condensed patterns and binary initial state fixed in each network. The resulting trajectories approach different sides of the saddle’s stable manifold (Fig. 6a). At closest approach, the projection of their displacement onto the left unstable eigenvector identifies the eventual attractor. Reversing the corresponding component along the right unstable eigenvector switches the outcome, whereas equally large perturbations within the stable eigenspace preserve it. The lower boundary therefore reflects a change in basin access while both attractors coexist. Nearby parameter values also show competition organized by saddles, although the con-nections can change: in two of the investigated cases, a local mixed-state trapping region disappears and a non-target descendant becomes the competing attractor. 24 V. SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION 

We derived the DMFT for an associative memory whose patterns occur in independent binary blocks of fixed size. The derivation extends the independent-pattern generating-functional analysis of Ref. [15]. Diagonalizing the block covariance separates the uncon-densed disorder into covariance eigenmodes. Each covariance eigenvalue remains inside the temporal response resolvents that determine the cross-talk noise covariance and the self-feedback coefficients. At fixed block size and fixed finite time, the leading uncondensed contribution depends on the block distribution through its covariance. The condensed sig-nal and one-site averages retain the complete binary distribution. For the hierarchically correlated patterns, the two covariance modes have distinct roles in the initial motion. Under the linear approximation for weak initial overlaps, the λ+

mode amplifies the symmetric block direction relative to the λ− modes that distinguish one descendant from the others. Increasing correlation strengthens this initial preference. In the full dynamics, the target-retrieval boundaries cross as the correlation increases. At the highest correlation examined and at low loading, the condensed block remains active while descendant discrimination is lost over the observation time. Direct finite-network simulations reproduce the basin geometry and retrieval trajectories over the tested conditions. Near the re-entrant lower boundary, increasing the loading can restore retrieval from an imperfect initial state while both the target and symmetric mixed states remain accessi-ble. Direct networks reveal stationary saddles separating the two basins. DMFT shows an increasing delay before mixed or target retrieval is selected as the lower boundary is ap-proached. The response of these trajectories to parameter changes predicts nearby boundary shifts. At the upper boundary, target retrieval remains accessible from other initial states and stable under the tested perturbations. The sampled direct boundary trajectories remain time dependent over the measured interval. Their asymptotic dynamics remains unresolved. The response also changes how the covariance modes contribute to cross-talk. In retrieval, the negative integrated response suppresses the cross-talk noise variance through the mode-dependent stationary denominators. The suppression is strongest in the λ+ mode. Under the stationary assumptions of Appendix B, the fixed-point equations reproduce generalized SCSNA. When several fixed-point roots coexist, their occupation depends on the preceding 25 dynamics, as in Ref. [15]. A single effective transfer function g(y) is sufficient only when histories reaching the same field y select the same output. The generalized SCSNA of Ref. [10] establishes the stationary mixed states of the hierar-chically correlated ensemble and their coexistence under sign dynamics. Retrieval through a mixed state and dependence on the initial state were already observed in that study. Here, DMFT determines the basins of the coexisting mixed states in the thermodynamic limit. Their selection depends nonmonotonically on the initial overlap: increasing the initial target overlap can lead to a mixed state with a smaller final overlap. Several directions remain for future investigation. A central challenge is to character-ize the dynamical mechanisms governing the boundaries of retrieval basins directly within DMFT. In particular, the stationary saddles observed in finite networks near the lower boundary suggest the possibility of an effective stability analysis of the corresponding macro-scopic dynamics, while the asymptotic behavior of trajectories on the upper boundary re-mains to be clarified. Such an analysis could establish a more systematic connection between the temporal response, basin geometry, and re-entrant retrieval. Another direction is to ex-amine the extent to which these phenomena depend on the nonmonotonic transfer function and the specific hierarchical correlation structure considered here. Applying the present framework to more general correlated-pattern ensembles, including larger blocks and mul-tiple levels of hierarchical correlations, may reveal which aspects of the retrieval dynamics are universal and which depend on the detailed organization of stored memories.
