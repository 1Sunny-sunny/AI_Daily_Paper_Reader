Energy consumption is a major concern for modern artificial intelligence (AI) applications. The brain is thought to achieve energy-efficient computation through sparse coding schemes. By emu-lating the spiking dynamics of the brain, neuromorphic chips hold the potential to realize energy-efficient AI. In this work, we use birdsong to demonstrate that auditory object recognition can be achieved using ultra-sparse coding. Feature-detecting neurons tuned to short temporal features ( ∼37 ms), sampled from exemplar syllables, transform continuous auditory signals into streams of ultra-sparse spikes that mark the onsets and offsets of features within the signal. Spike patterns elicited by the exemplars are stored as templates. During recognition, template matching is performed using axonal delays between feature-detecting and template-detecting neurons. Recognition is further en-hanced by an additional group of specialized binary neurons trained to discriminate between syllable categories with subtle differences. When tested on Bengalese finch songs, our approach achieved error rates below 5% using 10 to 40 exemplars per syllable type. We then implemented this recog-nition algorithm using spiking neural networks on Intel’s neuromorphic chip, Loihi, and found that response latencies remain constant (approximately 17 ms per syllable), regardless of the number of templates used. Our results demonstrate the potential of neuromorphic chips for low-latency, low-energy recognition of complex sounds, including speech. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is a key technol-ogy for human–machine interfaces and speech transcrip-tion. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly improved ASR performance [1–6]. Large models such as Whisper from OpenAI [2], trained on 680,000 hours of labeled speech collected from the In-ternet, demonstrate robustness against noise corruption in speech signals [7]. Nonetheless, the deep learning paradigm on which these systems are built requires a substantial amount of energy for training [8, 9]. For instance, Canary, an ASR model from NVIDIA, was trained on 86,000 hours (∼9.8 years) of audio data using 128 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs over a period of 48 hours [5]. We estimate that the energy consumption for this process is on the order of 2 MWh, which corresponds to approximately 20% of the annual energy consumption of a typical U.S. household. Because hyperparameter tuning requires multiple rounds of training, the energy costs associated with optimizing these models are considerable [8]. Beyond training, the energy cost of inference also increases with model size, which correlates with accuracy [10]. The human brain is remarkably energy-efficient, con-suming on the order of 20 W [11]. Neuronal activity, in-cluding signal transmission, accounts for more than half of the brain’s energy consumption [12]. This suggests that the brain’s energy efficiency may be due to the use of sparse spike coding [13, 14]. Indeed, neural population

responses to natural stimuli are highly sparse in both the visual cortex [15] and high-level auditory areas [16, 17]. Neuromorphic computing emulates the spiking dynam-ics of the brain on hardware to achieve energy-efficient, low-latency computation [18–21]. With millions of spik-ing neurons implemented on processing chips that can be stacked to scale up network size, neuromorphic platforms such as Loihi 2 have the potential to provide energy-efficient, real-time solutions for a range of image and auditory processing tasks [22]. The Loihi platform has supported the development of useful algorithms, such as LASSO regression implemented using spiking dynamics [23]. Loihi chips are also employed in practical applica-tions, including image and speech recognition [22, 24]. A common approach to signal decoding is to use spiking versions of deep neural networks trained via backpropa-gation. However, this approach typically yields less ac-curate results compared to conventional deep neural net-works [23]. Key to the limited practical use of neuro-morphic computing is the lack of algorithms that take advantage of the temporal dynamics of spiking neural networks for computation. In this paper, we show that ultra-sparse coding of au-ditory signals can be used for the recognition of auditory objects from continuous signals. In particular, we ad-dress the task of labeling syllables in continuous birdsong recordings. Automatic labeling of birdsong syllables is important for studying the neural basis of complex vocal-izations [25, 26], and various machine learning techniques have been developed for this purpose [27–29]. Birdsong syllables are stereotyped and can be categorized into a small number of types, but still show many features in common with speech [25]. Therefore, this task provides an ideal testbed for developing novel speech recognition arXiv:2610.38564v1 [q-bio.NC] 28 Oct 2026 2techniques without confronting the full complexity of hu-man speech. We train feature-detecting neurons (feature neurons) that respond to complex spatiotemporal acoustic pat-terns [30]. A collection of feature neurons transforms the continuous auditory signal into a stream of ultra-sparse spikes. By matching the spatiotemporal spike patterns to templates learned for auditory objects, a sequence of labels is produced. We implement the template matching process on both a CPU and Loihi and show that the re-sponse latency on Loihi remains constant as the number of templates increases, while on the CPU it increases su-perlinearly. Our results demonstrate that neuromorphic chips such as Loihi can use ultra-sparse spike coding for pattern recognition tasks and hold potential for achieving low latency and low energy consumption in applications such as speech recognition.   

> II. RESULTS A. Encoding syllables into ultra-sparse spike sequences

Song recordings were manually annotated and con-verted into spectrograms using fourth-order gammatone filters that simulate the spectral analysis performed by the cochlea [31] (Appendix A 3). Spectrogram seg-ments were then mapped into vectors in a feature space [Fig. 1(a)]. Specifically, feature vectors were constructed by concatenating 11 spectrogram frames within a ∼37 ms temporal window, each frame representing 3 .3 ms and spanning a frequency range of 1–10 kHz in 32 bands, resulting in a 352-dimensional feature space. As the win-dow advanced in time, syllables were mapped into con-tinuous trajectories in this space [Fig. 1(a)]. We trained feature-detecting neurons (feature neurons) to respond to acoustic features shared by syllables of the same type. For each type, we first drew at random a ref-erence syllable. Next, for each feature vector in the refer-ence, we calculated from the other syllables of the same type the vectors that correlated the highest with the ref-erence vector. This produced a cluster of vectors repre-senting the same part of the syllable across renditions (Appendix A 4). Using support vector machines [32] (SVMs), we trained feature neurons to discriminate the vectors of each cluster against other acoustic events, in-cluding other syllable types, silence, and noise [Fig. 1(b); Appendix A 4]. It reliably crosses the thresh-old when the neuron’s positive feature appears in the in-put and eventually returns to baseline after the positive feature passes. Two examples are shown in Fig. 1(c). Thus, feature neurons can respond to sustained features of variable du-ration. By definition, a syllable consists of a sequence of fea-ture vectors. If a collection of decision boundaries is trained to cover this sequence, these feature vectors will enter and leave the boundaries orderly [Fig. 1(d)], effec-tively creating an ultra-sparse sequence of onset and off-set spikes. Extending this idea to all types enables each syllable to elicit a distinctive spike sequence in a subset of feature neurons that also identifies that syllable’s type. Thus, syllable types are encoded by ultra-sparse spike patterns produced by the population of feature neurons [Fig. 1(e)]. Collectively, feature neurons transform continuous au-dio inputs into ultra-sparse onset and offset spikes [Fig. 1(f)]. As feature neurons spike only in response to their positive features, this sparseness reflects the preva-lence of that feature in the input sound. These spike patterns are highly stable across renditions of the same syllable [Fig. 1(g)], although missing or erroneous spikes can occasionally occur due to variability in syllable acous-tics [feature neuron 5 in Fig. 1(g)].

C. Decoding syllables from continuous ultra-sparse spike sequences 

To recognize (detect and classify) syllable types from continuous spike streams, we employed a template matching scheme based on the expected relative spike times of feature neurons. In this scheme, neuronal con-nections were assigned delays that compensated for the typical arrival time of spikes [Fig. 2(a)], ensuring that the target neuron (the neuron receiving the connections) re-ceived all feature neuron spikes synchronously [Fig. 2(a)]. Two template-decoding target neurons (template neu-rons for short) were assigned per template, one for on-sets and another for offsets [Fig. 2(b)], each receiving the spikes of the feature neurons in that template. Thus, templates were directly encoded in the delay lines be-tween feature neurons and template neurons. To accommodate variability in spike timing, we smeared spikes with a Gaussian kernel of standard de-viation 5 ms and passed the results through a set of trained linear filters (Appendix A 7). This effectively weighted the contribution of each neuron so that those that fired more reliably had higher weights [Fig. 2(c); Ap-pendix A 5]. When the sum of these responses exceeded a threshold, the template neuron fired, indicating recog-nition of the input spike pattern [Fig. 2(d)]. From here on, we refer to template neurons as ON and OFF neurons (depending on whether their inputs are onsets or offsets, respectively) and to the onset and offset spikes they pro-duce as ON and OFF spikes. Thresholds and filters were optimized to maximize the response of the neuron to its assigned syllable type while minimizing its responses to other types [Fig. 2(d); Appendix A 5]. A pair of ON and OFF spikes indicates a positive match to a template [Fig. 2(e)]. Since there are mul-tiple templates per syllable type, multiple detections can be made simultaneously, conveying redundant informa-tion [Fig. 2(f)]. For any group of overlapping detection, we kept the one with the highest combined ON and OFF responses (Appendix A 7). D. Binary discrimination 

Some syllable types can exhibit subtle differences. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), syllables A and B appear very similar, but the harmonic stack in A has a slight pitch downshift, whereas B has a constant pitch. Due to their high similarity, feature neurons of both types tend to spike simultaneously in response to either syllable. In addition, a syllable may sometimes be a segment of a longer one. In this case, feature neurons tuned for the shorter syllable will respond within the duration of the longer one, leading to conflicting recognition labels. We resolved this issue by training binary neurons to discriminate features of type B from features of type A [Figs. 3(a)–3(b)]. Each binary neuron examined a small spectrotemporal patch sampled at a random position in the spectrogram, lasting between 13 and 33 ms and span-ning at least 8 of the 32 frequency bands. Binary neurons were linear classifiers trained to distinguish the patch in syllables of type B from the same patch in syllables of type A. We used AdaBoost [33] to select binary neurons one at a time and weight their contributions, producing an ensemble of 10 binary neurons for per ordered pair of syllable types (Appendix A 6). To speed up training and recognition, ensembles were trained only for pairs of syllable types that produced detection collisions during recognition (Appendix A 7). Binary neurons are informative only when conditioned on one or both of the syllable types to which they were trained to discriminate. The same binary neurons are in-effective when applied to other categories or when used in isolation. In Fig. 3(c), we show the responses of both feature neurons and binary neurons to a sequence of sylla-bles. A large fraction of the feature neurons spike during both syllables A and B, making it difficult to distinguish between the two. In contrast, binary neurons trained to distinguish B from A spike vigorously during syllable B but only weakly during syllable A. Thus, binary neurons provide additional discriminative power for distinguish-ing syllable type B from type A.

> F. Continuous recognition on the neuromorphic device Loihi

If all templates operate in parallel, the response latency should remain constant despite increases in the number of templates. Such extreme parallelism can be achieved on neuromorphic hardware. To demonstrate this, we im-plemented our recognition algorithm on the Loihi chip [19, 23]. The implementation consisted of three modules: template matching, binary decision, and recognition out-put [Fig. 5]. The first two modules were feed-forward spiking neural networks (SNNs) of leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons. These networks received onset and offset spikes from feature neurons in the CPU [Fig. 1]. The recognition module received input from the other two modules. As in the CPU-based version, each template was em-bedded in the connections delays leaving feature neurons [Fig. 5(a), left]. These connections targeted a pair of LIF units representing template neurons. These neurons (here also referred to as ON and OFF neurons) integrate synchronized inputs and spike when at least 60% of the template’s spikes arrive aligned. For each syllable type, a two-compartment LIF neuron (plateau neuron) consist-ing of a dendrite and a soma was added to function as a conditional detector of OFF spikes given the prior arrival of ON spikes [Fig. 5(a), right]. The ON and OFF neurons of all templates of a type were connected to the dendritic and somatic compartments of a plateau neuron, respec-tively. When ON neurons spiked, the dendritic voltage rose above threshold and the neuron entered an UP state, which lasted while the dendritic voltage decayed back to threshold. If OFF neurons spiked during this interval, the soma spiked, signaling a detection. In its resting, or DOWN, state, the soma could not be driven above threshold by OFF inputs alone. A plateau neuron thus detected onset matches followed by offset matches, with the interval between them variable up to ∼217 ms when 60% of the templates match [35], which exceeds the du-ration of Bengalese finch syllables. Each somatic spike inhibited the dendrite and returned the neuron to the DOWN state, so that a syllable elicited a single detec-tion (Appendix A 8). The binary decision module for a pair of syllables A and B consisted of a collision detection neuron (collision neuron for short) and two two-compartment neurons— one for each syllable [Fig. 5(b)]. The collision neuron received inputs from the ON units in the template mod-ules of the two syllables. The input strengths were set such that the collision neuron spiked when both ON units spiked within ∼217 ms. A self-inhibitory mechanism pre-vented the collision neuron from spiking more than once. > G. Energy consumption

We measured the energy consumption of the network with Loihi 1’s energy probe [19]. The network with 40 templates per syllable type processed the spikes com-puted on the CPU from 80 s of audio in 11.63 s, about 7 times faster than real time, and used 8.71 J. The dynamic power consumption was therefore 0.75 W. These figures cover the Loihi network only; they exclude the CPU com-putation of the feature-neuron and binary-neuron spikes.  

> III. DISCUSSION

Ultra-sparse spike representations of birdsong have been observed in higher auditory areas of the songbird brain [17]. Such representations are also robust to noise. Feature-detecting neurons tuned to short snippets within auditory objects—such as birdsong syllables or words— transform a continuous auditory signal into a stream of ultra-sparse spikes [30]. We have shown that a template-matching method based on connection delays can detect syllable types from this spike stream. Detection is further enhanced by binary feature-detecting neurons, whose spikes are highly discriminative when conditioned on con-flicting simultaneous detections of multiple syllables by the template-matching process. We implemented the al-gorithm on Intel’s neuromorphic chip, Loihi, achieving low latency that remained constant despite an increasing number of templates. The success of our approach high-lights the potential for low-latency and energy-efficient audio processing using ultra-sparse coding on neuromor-phic devices. A previous study applied the same coding scheme to the task of recognizing isolated spoken digits from the AURORA-2 database, and showed that performance was robust against challenging noise types, such as exhibition hall noise and babble noise, down to a 0 dB signal-to-noise ratio [30]. Isolated recognition involves only substi-tution errors (i.e., incorrect labels), whereas continuous recognition introduces additional challenges due to inser-tion and deletion errors. We demonstrated continuous recognition using ultra-sparse spike coding for birdsong syllable recognition. Compared to speech, birdsong is more manageable in some respects, as the syllables are stereotyped and the number of syllable types is limited. However, the task remains non-trivial, as automatic bird-song recognition continues to pose significant challenges in birdsong research [28, 29]. Whether our approach can be successfully applied to continuous speech recognition remains an open question. It is important to note that we have not incorporated syntactical information from bird-song syllables [29, 36]. A complementary system that integrates syntactical structure could be valuable for ex-tending this approach to speech recognition. The binary feature-detecting neurons are an important component of our system. These specialized neurons are sensitive to differences between specific syllable types. As a result, they are highly informative for distinguish-ing those particular syllable pairs. For other syllables, however, their activity may appear random or uninfor-mative. It would be interesting to investigate whether such neurons exist in the auditory system of the brain. The spikes of the feature-detecting neurons and binary neurons were generated on CPUs. To achieve a fully neu-romorphic system, it is important to implement these neurons as spiking neural networks. Spectral analysis on Loihi chips can already be performed using resonate-and-fire neurons [22]. It is plausible that the feature-detecting neurons could also be implemented using delay lines and synaptic weights driven by these spectral-analysis neu-rons. Additionally, it would be interesting to explore whether such feature neurons could be tuned through un-supervised learning schemes, such as the Kohonen map [37]. Our template-matching scheme using axonal delays is closely related to a pioneering study that employed ax-onal delays to detect digits from continuous speech [38]. In that earlier work, auditory signals were transformed into continuous neural activity representing high energy in specific frequency bands. These analog signals were di-rectly summed after being delayed, and the weights were learned accordingly. In contrast, our feature-detecting neurons utilize the full frequency information and emit spikes, which simplifies the template learning process. Additionally, transmitting spikes is inherently simpler than transmitting analog signals. Spike-based outputs are less susceptible to noisy fluctuations that can cor-rupt analog signals. Biologically, axons transmit spikes rather than analog signals, making our approach more plausible from a neurobiological standpoint. Due to its simplicity, template matching was used in early automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems dur-ing the 1960s and 1970s [39]. However, it soon became evident that this approach could easily exhaust compu-tational resources as the number of templates increased, since the time required to search for matches grew ac-cordingly. Beginning in the 1980s, hidden Markov mod-els (HMMs) took over and made ASR more practical [39]. With advances in computing power, template matching has again become viable, although more sophisticated methods are now required to improve the speed of search-ing for matching templates [40]. More recent approaches embed word templates into high-dimensional spaces us-ing deep neural networks, enabling better discrimination through learning and more efficient search for matches [41]. We have demonstrated that neuromorphic devices enable the simultaneous evaluation of all templates, al-lowing fast responses even as the number of templates increases. This requires that the neuromorphic hardware 9can be scaled to accommodate the necessary number of templates. Given the rapid progress in neuromorphic technology [21], it is conceivable that our approach could scale to support large-vocabulary speech recognition. We have used onset and offset templates to detect the beginnings and ends of syllables. In the songs of some species, such as the canary, syllable durations can vary substantially due to features like harmonic stacks, which can exhibit large variability in duration [28]. The sep-aration of onset and offset templates enables success-ful detection of such syllables without requiring explicit dynamic warping techniques [42]. In the neuromorphic implementation, onsets and offsets are bridged by long-lasting plateau potentials in dendritic compartments. In-deed, neurons with dendritic compartments capable of generating plateau potentials can be wired to decode complex spike sequence patterns, even when the intervals between spikes vary over a wide range [35]. For speech recognition, it may be beneficial to use such neurons in combination with axonal delays to accommodate vari-ability in speech tempo. In conclusion, we have demonstrated that auditory objects, such as birdsong syllables, can be detected from ultra-sparse spike patterns generated by feature-detecting neurons driven by continuous auditory signals. We successfully implemented this process on Loihi chips, highlighting the potential of neuromorphic devices for low-latency and low-energy processing of complex signals such as speech.

> 2. Song annotation

We designed a semi-automated pipeline to annotate the recordings of 8 Bengalese finches [see Table I]. We initially manually annotated a subset of the recordings from each bird, ensuring that all syllable types were iden-tified and labeled. We then trained our recognition sys-tem and used it to automatically annotate approximately 10 additional recordings, each roughly 30 seconds long. We manually curated the results by correcting mislabeled syllables and adjusting syllable boundaries. We repeated this process iteratively, each time using a larger training set, until all recordings were annotated.  

> 3. Feature processing

We devised a preprocessing pipeline inspired by mod-els of the auditory periphery. Recordings were first band-pass filtered between 1 and 10 kHz with a fifth-order But-terworth filter, applied forward and backward to avoid phase distortion. They were then passed through a bank of 32 fourth-order gammatone filters, which simulate the spectral analysis performed by the cochlea [31]. The im-pulse response of a fourth-order gammatone filter with center frequency f is 

g(f, t ) = g0t3 exp {− 2πb (f )t} cos (2 πf t ), (A1) 10 where g0 sets the gain and b(f ) the bandwidth. Our im-plementation followed Ref. [43], but used an exact calcu-lation of the filter coefficients, with the gain normalized to unity at the center frequency. Bandwidths were set by an equivalent rectangular bandwidth (ERB) function [44]. Although songbirds can hear frequencies up to 10 kHz, their hearing is most sensitive between 2 and 5 kHz [45]. To emphasize this range, we replaced the human ERB function with a lin-ear function fitted by least squares to the starling data of Ref. [46]: ERB( f ) = f /Q + fmBW , (A2) where Q = 10 .49 is the (dimensionless) quality factor and 

fmBW = 88 .29 Hz is the minimum bandwidth. These val-ues are similar to those suggested in Ref. [47] for human hearing at low to moderate frequencies and levels. Fol-lowing Ref. [48], we set b(f ) = 1 .019 × ERB( f ). The 32 center frequencies were equally spaced between 1 and 10 kHz on the ERB-number scale, making filters denser at lower frequencies where they are also narrower. The filter outputs were converted into spectrograms in five steps. (i) Each channel was advanced in time by 3/(2 πb (f )), rounded down to a whole number of samples, to compensate for the longer delays of the low-frequency filters. (ii) The outputs were squared to obtain the in-stantaneous power in each band, half-wave rectified, and compressed with a power of 0.01 to reduce their dynamic range. (iii) The envelope of each channel was extracted by locating the maxima of the compressed power within consecutive 5 ms windows and interpolating linearly be-tween them. (iv) The envelopes were resampled to 300 Hz. (v) The envelope of each channel was standardized by ( ei − μi)/σ i, where i indexes the channel, ei is the envelope and μi and σi are the mean and standard devi-ation of channel i computed using all the recordings. Feature vectors were formed by stacking 11 consecutive spectrogram frames, giving ∼37 ms segments represented as 352-dimensional vectors (11 frames × 32 channels). Each vector was divided by its Euclidean norm, with a small constant (10 −8) added to the norm to avoid division by zero. Feature vectors are therefore dimensionless and insensitive to the overall sound level. Sliding the window forward one frame at a time produced the trajectories in the feature space, as shown in Fig. 1(a). To speed up decoding, we discarded low-power audio segments before computing the responses of feature neu-rons. The instantaneous power P (t) was computed di-rectly from the waveform with a 20 ms sliding Kaiser window ( πα = 14) advanced by one spectrogram frame (3.3 ms). In each window, the samples were squared and the maximum was taken. The result was smoothed with a Gaussian filter with a standard deviation of one frame, half-wave rectified, and compressed with a power of 0.01. Segments were discarded where P (t) fell below the threshold 

Pmin = MED( P ) − MAD( P ), (A3) where MED and MAD are the median and the median absolute deviation of P , computed separately for each recording. To avoid cutting syllables apart, we merged adjacent segments separated by less than 100 ms. We also discarded isolated segments shorter than 20 ms, which is shorter than any syllable we analyzed.  

> 4. Feature neurons

Feature neurons were trained by nonlinear (Gaussian kernel) support vector machines (SVMs) [32] using the Python interface to LIBSVM in Scikit-Learn [49, 50]. We effectively used hard margins by setting the soft-margin penalty to C = 10 20 . The response of a neuron to the time-dependent input x(t) is the SVM decision function evaluated at time t [Eq. (1)]. Because feature vectors were normalized (Appendix A 3), V (t) in Eq. (1) is di-mensionless. A feature neuron detected a feature whenever V (t) rose above the decision boundary ( V (t) = 0). Spike times were defined as the time when V (t) = −1 + δ, with δ =0.2, just above the SVM lower margin V = −1. The onset spike was assigned to the nearest preceding time at which V (t) crossed this level from below, and the offset spike to the nearest following time at which it crossed from above. Onset and offset spikes therefore mark the times when the input trajectory enters and leaves the neighborhood of the neuron’s decision region. Feature neurons were trained one time bin at a time from reference syllables, with a K-fold scheme. We par-titioned the training syllables at random into K groups of equal size, each containing the same number of sylla-bles per type. Each group in turn served as the held-out set, and the remaining K − 1 groups as the training set. We used K = N/ 5, i.e., K = 2 , 4, 8 for N = 10 , 20 , 40 training syllables per type; for N = 5, all syllables were used for training. In each fold, we randomly selected one reference syllable Sr per type and, for each time bin τ of 

Sr , trained one feature neuron as follows. 1. The positive training set (target y = 1) consisted of the reference feature vector Sr (τ ) and, from each of the other training syllables of the same type, the feature vector with the highest cross-correlation with Sr (τ ); 2. The negative training set (target y = −1) consisted of all the feature vectors of 5 syllables sampled at random from each of the other syllable types. To make neurons robust to background events and reduce false alarm rates, we added feature vec-tors from audio segments that did not contain an-notated syllables (silence, calls, and background noise); 3. Because hard-margin SVMs can overfit when forced to separate similar vectors, we removed from the negative set any vector that cross-correlated by 11 more than 0.97 with any positive vector. If SVM optimization failed to converge, this threshold was lowered in steps of 0.05, up to 10 times. Lower-ing the threshold smoothed V (t) and reduced the number of false positive spikes; 4. The kernel parameter γ in Eq. (1), which sets the width of the decision region around each support vector, was set to γ = 2 /d 2min , where dmin is the smallest Euclidean distance between Sr (τ ) and the other positive vectors. Because neighboring time bins can have similar acous-tics, this procedure can produce several redundant neu-rons. To prevent the system from scaling unnecessarily, we removed poorly performing or highly correlated neu-rons. Each neuron was scored on its held-out set as an isolated-syllable detector: its error rate was the fraction of held-out syllables of its own type to which it did not respond, plus those of other types to which it did. For 

N = 20 and N = 40, we then clustered the neurons of each syllable type. For M neurons of a given type, we computed the correlation matrix of their responses V (t)to the training syllables, using M/ 2 clusters for N = 20 and M/ 4 for N = 40, and performed spectral clustering on this matrix. In each cluster we kept the 4 neurons with the lowest error rates and discarded the rest.  

> 5. Template training and matching on the CPU

A template consisted of the onset and offset spike times produced by the feature neurons in response to a sylla-ble. A feature neuron was included in a template only if it spiked during the template’s syllable, i.e. if V (t)reached the decision boundary ( V (t) = 0) at least once. Neurons included in this way were kept in fixed order, guaranteeing that templates stored the identity of each neuron as well as its spike times. Spike times were stored as delays relative to a reference neuron: onsets relative to the onset of the first feature neuron, and offsets rela-tive to the offset of the last. ON template neurons were therefore anchored to the beginning of the syllable and OFF template neurons to the end. Templates were then used to train the ON and OFF neurons for onsets and offsets, respectively. For each tem-plate T1 of a syllable type, we shifted the spikes of every template T2 of the same type, including T1 itself, by the delays in T1. When T2 resembles T1, all spikes become temporally aligned. The aligned spikes were converted into binary matrices (rows representing feature neurons, columns representing time, and ones representing spikes) and convolved in time with a Gaussian kernel of standard deviation 5 ms to tolerate jitter in spike timing. From each aligned template we extracted one temporal window

We used a model inspired by dendritic plateau neu-rons [35] to detect a template onset match followed by an offset match. Each plateau neuron was a Loihi 1 13 multi-compartment neuron with two compartments, a dendrite and a soma [19]. Each compartment followed Loihi’s discrete-time leaky integrate-and-fire dynamics,

There was one plateau neuron per syllable type. Its dendrite received the ON spikes of all templates of that type, and its soma received their OFF spikes. The order of the two events was enforced by the so-matic decay. The soma was given a strong voltage decay, much stronger than that of the dendrite, so that its volt-age remained above threshold only briefly after a burst of OFF spikes. > 9. Template recognition on a neuromorphic device

The complete network is shown in Fig. 6. It received three spike streams computed on the CPU: the onset and offset spikes of the feature neurons and the spikes of the binary neurons. One network timestep corresponded to one spectrogram frame (3.3 ms). Unless stated otherwise, all neurons were single-compartment LIF neurons with instant current decay (Appendix A 8). 

Template matching. Each template was encoded by one ON and one OFF template neuron. Onset spikes of the feature neurons in the template were sent to the ON neuron, and offset spikes to the OFF neuron, through connections whose delays aligned the spikes as in the CPU implementation [Fig. 5(a)]. Loihi 1 stores synaptic delays in 6 bits, limiting them to 62 timesteps. For sys-tems with longer template delays, we compressed time by multiplying all delays and input spike times by 62 /D max ,where Dmax is the largest onset template delay, and rescaled the output spike times accordingly. Because templates differ widely in the number of spikes they con-tain, the threshold of each template neuron was normal-ized to its template size: θ = max[1 , ⌊fth nF w⌋], where nF

is the number of feature neurons in the template, w the synaptic weight, and fth = 0 .6 the fraction of spikes that must arrive aligned. The voltage decay of the template neurons, which sets how much timing jitter is tolerated, was tuned by trial and error. A refractory period en-sured that each template neuron spiked at most once per syllable. 

Syllable detection. The ON and OFF neurons of all templates of a syllable type were connected to the den-drite and soma, respectively, of a single plateau neuron for that type (Appendix A 8). A somatic spike signaled detection of the syllable. Since the delays align the onset spikes to the end of the template, offset spikes were given an additional delay equal to the largest onset template delay, which kept the ON and OFF streams synchronized. 

Collision detection. Because the dendrite of a Loihi plateau neuron cannot spike, the onset of a detec-tion is not directly available. We therefore added one coincidence-detection neuron per syllable type. It re-ceived the same ON spikes as the dendrite of the plateau neuron and had the same parameters, except that it spiked and reset when its threshold was crossed. Its re-fractory period ensured a single spike per syllable onset. For each pair of syllable types with binary neurons, a collision neuron received input from the two coincidence-detection neurons, through a small uniform delay. Its weights, threshold, and decay were set so that it spiked when both inputs arrived within a short window, but not when two spikes of the same type arrived one refractory period apart. An inhibitory self-connection prevented each collision neuron from spiking more than once per collision. Two further inhibitory pathways kept collision neurons from firing falsely. Binary decision. When a collision neuron spiked, it inhibited the dendrites of the plateau neurons of both syllable types, returning them to the DOWN state so that neither could report a detection. A refractory period of 20 timesteps ( ∼67 ms) prevented double reports when both pathways responded to the same syllable. Each recognition spike marks the end of a syllable. The total delay of the network is the largest onset template delay plus two timesteps, inde-pendent of the number of templates; output spike times were shifted back by this amount. Because the network reports only syllable ends, syllable onsets were estimated by subtracting the mean duration of the recognized type, computed from the training set.  

> 10. Benchmarks

For each bird, the recordings were split at the file level: 60% of the files, selected at random with a fixed seed, formed the test set, and the remaining 40% formed the training pool. The same split was used for every condi-tion and for both the CPU and neuromorphic systems. For each condition, N = 5 , 10 , 20, or 40 syllables per type, we drew 10 training sets independently from the training pool by sampling N syllables of each type at random without replacement. Syllables of the training pool that were not sampled were discarded. Each train-ing set also included approximately 60 s of unannotated audio from the training recordings, representing silence and background noise, which was used to build the neg-ative sets of the feature neurons (Appendix A 4). One system was trained on each training set, and all systems were tested on the full test set. CPU and neuromorphic 15 systems were trained and tested on the same sets (data from this paper). Table I summarizes the sizes of the benchmarks. Predictions were scored against the manual annota-tions with the word error rate (WER) [Eq. (2)]. For each test recording, the predicted and annotated sylla-bles were ordered by time into label sequences, which were aligned by minimum edit distance; each alignment step counts as a correct label, a substitution, a deletion, or an insertion. The counts were summed over all test recordings, so that K in Eq. (2) is the total number of annotated test syllables. Annotations of calls and noise, which the systems were not trained to recognize, were excluded from scoring, together with any prediction over-lapping such an annotation by at least 10% of the shorter of the two. Because the alignment uses only the order of the labels, the WER does not penalize errors in the pre-dicted syllable boundaries.
