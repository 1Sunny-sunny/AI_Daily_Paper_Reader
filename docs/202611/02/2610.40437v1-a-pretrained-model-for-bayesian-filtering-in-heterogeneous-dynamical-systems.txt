ABSTRACT 

Filtering has not yet had its foundation-model moment: state estimation is still dominated either by online Bayesian filtering algorithms that require known dy-namics as inputs, or by learning-based neural filters trained from scratch for a single system or parametric family. The core challenge is structural heterogene-ity: dynamical systems differ in their governing dynamics, physical scales, and noise levels, precluding a shared representation space for a reusable filter. To bridge this gap, we introduce FilterFM , the first pretrained model for Bayesian filtering that estimates latent states across heterogeneous dynamical systems with a single checkpoint. FilterFM employs a hierarchical two-level observation se-quence encoder and a prior-to-posterior state-reconstruction module that together handle varying observation and state dimensions across heterogeneous systems, while modeling state uncertainty via quantile prediction. Trained on a mixture of heterogeneous filtering tasks, FilterFM exhibits superior in-domain performance, delivering significant gains in estimation accuracy, runtime efficiency, and noise robustness compared to existing baselines. Beyond in-domain performance, it marks the first model to successfully enable zero-shot filtering on unseen dynamics, bridging the generalization gap in state estimation. Furthermore, we uncover clear scaling behaviors in foundation-model-based Bayesian filtering, which combined with comprehensive component ablation studies, validate the structural optimality of our design and broaden the generalization scope for this research field. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Bayesian filtering serves as a basic tool for state estimation in robotics (Barfoot, 2024), navigation (Ko & Fox, 2009), atmospheric science (Hickman et al., 2025), chemical process monitoring (Wang & Chiang, 2019), and many other scientific domains (Zhang et al., 2024; Course & Nair, 2023). Although foundation models have reshaped multi-modal modeling and time-series forecasting, filtering still lacks a foundation-model paradigm shift. Classical methods from Gaussian filters (Li et al., 2015; Ito & Xiong, 2000; Cao et al., 2026), particle filters (Godsill, 2019) to PDE-based filters (Wang et al., 2025; Yau & Niu, 2025) instantiate a predict-then-update recursion, their strong performances usually depend on exactly known system-specific transition models, observation mappings, and noise assumptions. Learning-based filters reduce this modeling burden by training neural estimators from trajectories (Revach et al., 2022; Dahal et al., 2024), but they are still typically tied to one specific dynamical system (Ji et al., 2022), or to a parametric family sharing the same underlying dynamics (Holtmann et al., 2025). This raises a natural foundation-model question: 

Can one model learn to filter across heterogeneous dynamical systems? 

The challenges lie in structural heterogeneity rather than data scale alone: dynamical systems differ in their governing equations (Brunton et al., 2016), observation processes, physical units, and noise levels (Zheng et al., 2025), so a reusable filter cannot rely on a fixed state-observation interface. To fill this research gap, we introduce FilterFM, a transformer-based pretrained model for Bayesian filtering. Unlike LLMFilter and FM-UKF, FilterFM is pretrained across heterogeneous systems with varying dynamics and dimensions and is able to generalize to unseen systems with a single checkpoint. Figure 1 compares the three approaches’ training and generalization scopes across datasets, system parameters, and state dimensions. Our model consists of a hierarchical two-level causal observation sequence encoder coupled with a prior-to-posterior state-reconstruction module. The encoder’s design is motivated by two requirements: high-dimensional systems make dense attention over all time-variable pairs inefficient, and Bayesian filtering needs the current observation to update posterior rather than just coarse patch summaries. Accordingly, patch-level blocks compress completed patches into causal history memory , while intra-patch-level blocks preserve local evidence at each time step through cross-scale conditioning . This design retains the per-step resolution that Bayesian filtering requires for real-time correction based on the current observation. Within each scale, causal temporal attention first models each variable’s temporal dependence. The resulting features are summarized into compact prototype memory and exchanged across variables through a gated reassembly router (Liu et al., 2026c). The state-reconstruction module then performs the filtering update: it first reads the shifted local memory to form a prior , then incorporates current observation to obtain a posterior, and decodes the posterior into the latent state estimate. Also to the best of our knowledge, FilterFM is the first model to support uncertainty estimation for Bayesian filtering, decoding multiple posterior quantiles to provide both a median state estimate and predictive uncertainty intervals. To train FilterFM, we build a training dataset containing approximately 15K training trajectories, with about 45% from filtering numerical examples commonly used in previous literatures (Liu et al., 2025), 20% from synthetic low-dimensional chaotic systems (Lai et al., 2026), and 35% from high-dimensional composites formed by concatenating and orthogonally mixing low-dimensional trajectories. The resulting corpus spans system dimensions from 2 to 100, exposing the model to both standard filtering regimes and broad dimensional variation. On in-domain evaluations, FilterFM delivers strong gains along three practical axes: it achieves the lowest average raw-space RMSE for state-estimation accuracy, runs substantially faster than all compared baselines, and remains consistently more robust than LLMFilter and robust online Bayesian filters under observation-noise misspecification. Beyond in-domain performance, FilterFM demonstrates zero-shot filtering on unseen heterogeneous systems . FilterFM exhibits foundation-model-style scaling behavior: broader system coverage at an approximately matched training-data budget, more trajectories per system at fixed system diversity, and greater model capacity each improve generalization to unseen dynamics. 2Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose FilterFM, a pretrained model for Bayesian filtering built on a hierarchical two-level causal encoder and a prior-to-posterior state-reconstruction module. The model enables a single checkpoint to filter across heterogeneous dynamical systems with varying dimensions and, to our knowledge, is the first Bayesian filtering model to introduce quantile prediction, providing both point estimates and predictive uncertainty intervals. • We curate a heterogeneous training corpus spanning diverse dynamics and dimensions, and establish a comprehensive evaluation showing FilterFM’s advantages in state-estimation accuracy, runtime efficiency, and robustness to observation-noise misspecification, together with the first demonstration of zero-shot filtering on unseen heterogeneous systems. • We investigate whether scaling pretraining can improve generalization to unseen dynamical systems. This is the first systematic study of scaling behavior in Bayesian filtering across training-system diversity, training-trajectory coverage, and model capacity. Our results provide evidence that expanding pretraining can improve a single model’s ability to estimate states across heterogeneous dynamics. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Online Bayesian filtering. Classical Bayesian filters such as Gaussian filters or particle filters (Li et al., 2015; Ito & Xiong, 2000; Cao et al., 2026; Godsill, 2019) provide the predict-then-update 

paradigm for state estimation, which first predicts a prior distribution over the estimated state from the past observations and then updates it to a posterior via the transition model and observation map, but these methods require known transition models, observation maps, and noise statistics. In real-world applications, however, when explicit mathematical models are unavailable or noise statistics are misspecified, the feasibility of these approaches drops considerably. 

Learning-based filtering. Neural filters reduce the need for explicit mathematical models by learning estimators from the trajectories. KalmanNet learns a neural Kalman gain correction (Revach et al., 2022) while other methods use different neural networks (Ji et al., 2022; Dahal et al., 2024) to map observation sequences to state estimates. LLMFilter (Liu et al., 2025) adopts a frozen LLM but suffers from patch-boundary inconsistencies across varying dimensions. Yet all these methods are system-specific and demand retraining for new dynamics, highlighting the need to broaden the generalization scope of Bayesian filtering toward reusable foundation-model-like frameworks. 

Foundation models. Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) such as Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2025) and Moirai 2.0 (Liu et al., 2026b) demonstrate strong zero-shot forecasting capabilities, while Panda (Lai et al., 2026) and ChaosNexus (Liu et al., 2026a) target chaotic-system forecasting. In the area of state estimation, FM-UKF (Holtmann et al., 2025) inserts a causal transformer into an unscented Kalman filter but still requires an explicit observation map and operates within a single parametric family. Critically, forecasting and filtering follow different information structures :forecasting predicts a future window from an observed one, while online Bayesian filtering causally updates the state from one new observation at a time . FilterFM is designed for filtering: it learns the observation-to-state mapping from data, preserves strict per-step causality, and is the first pretrained model for Bayesian filtering across heterogeneous systems.

4 METHOD 

In this section, we introduce the architecture of FilterFM, which comprises a hierarchical two-level causal observation sequence encoder and a prior-to-posterior state-reconstruction module, as shown in Figure 2. We first describe how the encoder represents observation history and current evidence, then explain how these representations support prior prediction and posterior update. 4.1 HIERARCHICAL TWO -L EVEL OBSERVATION SEQUENCE ENCODER 

Our encoder is designed to preserve current observations for filtering while efficiently summarizing longer histories. We therefore represent observations at two temporal levels: patch-level embeddings summarize consecutive groups of observations, while intra-patch-level embeddings retain individual timesteps within each group. Causal temporal and prototype-based variable interaction operate at both levels, with cross-scale conditioning providing historical context to intra-patch embeddings.

5 EXPERIMENT 

Benchmarks. We curate 14 established in-domain filtering tasks spanning low-dimensional nonlinear systems such as Selkov (Sel’kov, 1968) and Oscillator (Brunton et al., 2016), and high-dimensional chaotic systems such as Lorenz96 (Lorenz, 1995), VL20 (Vissio & Lucarini, 2020), and Cubic-Sensoring100 (Hazewinkel et al., 1983). We augment them with auxiliary systems from skew-product chaotic trajectories in Lai et al. (2026), using clean latent-state windows and per-channel Gaussian observation noise with a log-uniform scale. Because these source trajectories have at most 10 dimensions, we also form systems up to dimension 100 by normalizing, concatenating, and orthogonally mixing low-dimensional windows. In-domain evaluation uses 20 held-out trajectories per system, while OOS evaluation uses held-out systems with the same noise protocol. Full system definitions and generation details are in Appendix B. 

Baselines and Metrics. We compare FilterFM with four baseline groups. Learning-based filters include MEstimator (Ji et al., 2022), RStateNet (Dahal et al., 2024), ProTran (Tang & Matteson, 2021), KalmanNet (Revach et al., 2022), and RNNYYF (Chen et al., 2025), each trained separately for each system. LLMFilter (Liu et al., 2025) uses the System-as-Prompt setting, whereas FM-UKF (Holtmann et al., 2025) couples a time-series foundation model with an unscented Kalman filter (Wan & Van Der Merwe, 2000). Online Bayesian baselines comprise EnKF, its robust variants EnKFI, EnKFS, and HubEnKF (Evensen, 1994), together with a particle filter (Godsill, 2019). We measure state-estimation accuracy by root mean square error (RMSE), defined in Appendix C, and runtime by wall-clock latency per filtering step (ms/step), excluding model loading. 5.1 IN-D OMAIN PERFORMANCE IN TERMS OF ACCURACY , R UNTIME , AND ROBUSTNESS

FilterFM runs at 0.145 ms/step on average, substantially faster than all compared baselines. FM-UKF is slower because each step requires both neural prediction and explicit UKF posterior correction , incurring the computational cost of both stages. In contrast, FilterFM produces state estimates end-to-end in a single forward pass , eliminating prompt overhead and symbolic filtering.

We study how training-system diversity, training-trajectory coverage, and model capacity affect Bayesian filtering on unseen dynamics. Improvements along all three axes suggest that a shared model can learn more general state-estimation capabilities from larger-scale pretraining. 

Scaling system diversity. Increasing the nominal system count from 164 to 20,980 lowers mean OOS normalized RMSE from 0.3086 to 0.2366 and the median from 0.2624 to 0.1996 in Figure 7B. With approximately matched total training-trajectory counts, greater dynamical diversity improves OOS generalization, with diminishing gains as the number of training systems increases. 

Scaling model parameters. We train six FilterFM models, ranging from 2.9M to 51 .2M parameters, on the same expanded corpus of 301,873 long trajectories with a fixed number of training epochs. 6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We presented FilterFM, the first pretrained model for Bayesian filtering across heterogeneous dynam-ical systems with a single checkpoint. FilterFM combines a hierarchical two-level causal encoder with a prior-to-posterior state-reconstruction module. It delivers superior in-domain performance in state-estimation accuracy, runtime efficiency, and robustness to observation-noise misspecification, alongside zero-shot generalization to unseen dynamics. Ablation and scaling studies support the architectural design and the benefits of greater system diversity, trajectory coverage, and model capacity. Future work should expand training data to cover broader observation models, sensor modalities, and noise structures, advancing general-purpose filtering models. AI USE STATEMENT

In the mismatch experiments of Appendix C.2, we increase the actual observation covariance while keeping the assumed Q fixed. A.2 LEARNING -B ASED FILTERS 

Following the learning-based baseline selection in LLMFilter (Liu et al., 2025), we evaluate MEsti-mator, RStateNet, ProTran, and KalmanNet, and additionally include RNNYYF. These task-specific models are trained separately on each system, with adaptations to their inputs, output dimensions, and supervised objectives described below. Each model predicts states from observations without receiving target states in its forward pass. • MEstimator. The estimator of Ji et al. (2022) was developed alongside a control policy for legged locomotion. We adopt its multilayer-perceptron estimator and replace robot-specific inputs with causal observation history. At time t, we concatenate the standardized observation vectors from 

t, t − 2, t − 4, t − 6 into a 4M -dimensional input. This input passes through two fully connected hidden layers with 256 and 128 units, respectively, each followed by an ELU activation and dropout 0.1. An N -unit linear layer produces ˆxt, and training uses only state-estimation MSE. • RStateNet. RobustStateNet (Dahal et al., 2024) combines learned motion prediction with a recurrent measurement update for ego-vehicle state estimation. We adapt it to our benchmark by replacing the vehicle-specific sensor branches with a single LSTM branch that processes the full 

M -dimensional observation vector together with the previous N -dimensional state estimate. We resize the output heads to predict an N -dimensional state increment and an N × M correction matrix, matching each system’s state and observation dimensions. The current estimate is obtained by adding the predicted increment and a correction based on the observation residual to the previous estimate, and we train the model on each system with state-estimation MSE. • ProTran. The probabilistic Transformer of Tang & Matteson (2021) models temporal structure through a recursively constructed latent representation. Our supervised filtering adaptation main-tains a hidden-state history and lets the current query attend both to earlier hidden states and to observations up to the current time. We use representation width 256, eight attention heads, a 16-dimensional latent representation, and dropout 0.1. A latent projection updates the recurrent representation before state decoding. Training uses state-estimation MSE rather than the original variational objective, so this baseline evaluates an adaptation of ProTran’s temporal architecture rather than its full probabilistic generative model. • KalmanNet. KalmanNet (Revach et al., 2022) retains known transition and observation functions while learning the Kalman gain with recurrent networks. We adapt the gain-network architecture from the official KalmanNet repository 1, including its three GRUs for process, state, and innovation covariance features, and provide system-specific wrappers for the benchmark dynamics and observation maps. Our runs use input and output expansion factors of 5 and 1, respectively, and batch size 1. LLMFilter (Liu et al., 2025) reports training divergence with the official implementation on high-dimensional systems. Table 1 reports KalmanNet results for the five low-dimensional benchmark systems. • RNNYYF. We adapt the recurrent Yau–Yau filtering framework of Chen et al. (2025) to learn state estimates directly, without explicitly representing the conditional density. Starting from a learned initial vector, the model updates a 512-dimensional hidden representation at each time step 

> 1https://github.com/KalmanNet/KalmanNet_TSP

17 through a feed-forward network with tanh activations, using the previous representation and the observation increment yt − yt−1 as inputs. A linear layer then maps the updated representation to the N -dimensional state estimate ˆxt. We train the model on complete 200-step windows with unnormalized observations and target states, minimizing state-estimation MSE in the original state coordinates. A.3 OTHER BASELINES 

These baselines use sequence models to reduce the amount of manually specified dynamics, but their interfaces and original generalization settings differ. We adapt both to the same benchmark trajectories and report their in-domain filtering performance, with the original model design and our changes distinguished below. • LLMFilter. LLMFilter (Liu et al., 2025) maps observations into a frozen language model’s embedding space and decodes its representations into state estimates, using System-as-Prompt to describe the filtering task. We build on the official repository 2, retain the frozen LLaMA-7B backbone 3, and train the observation encoder and state decoder separately for each system, using hidden width 512 and tanh activations in both adapters. The public implementation flattens time and observation channels before splitting them into fixed-length scalar segments, so patch boundaries need not coincide with time-step boundaries. Our adaptation preserves the time– channel layout by dividing each 40-step window into two contiguous 20-step patches, each containing all M observation channels and decoded into the corresponding N state channels. The encoder input and decoder output dimensions are therefore 20 M and 20 N , respectively. Following the official preprocessing, we standardize observations and target states using training-set statistics. The public evaluation script reports RMSE in this standardized space, so we invert the state standardization before evaluation to compare all methods in the original state coordinates. Appendix F provides the System-as-Prompt inputs for all fourteen benchmark systems. • FM-UKF. FM-UKF (Holtmann et al., 2025) combines a learned causal dynamics model with an unscented Kalman filter for posterior correction. Its official implementation 4 was developed for container-ship models with a 10-dimensional state space and 70 dynamics parameters, evaluating transfer to held-out parameterizations with a shared state layout. To adapt the predictor to our benchmark systems, we use a state-only causal Transformer and train one model per state-dimension group, pooling the systems within each group for in-domain evaluation. The adapted architecture uses four layers, width 256, eight attention heads, feed-forward width 1,024, and dropout 0.05, with input and output dimensions matched to each group. We train on standardized 80-step state windows with a next-step MSE loss at every position except the last under causal attention. During filtering, recent posterior state estimates provide temporal context for the learned dynamics prediction, and the UKF incorporates the current observation through the known observation map to obtain the posterior estimate. Table 5 reports the per-system runtime values used in Figure 4. All measurements are obtained on a single NVIDIA A800 GPU, exclude model loading time, and are averaged over the 20 held-out testing trajectories for each benchmark system. LLMFilter is timed with the full System-as-Prompt input protocol, including instruction and example tokens, to retain the input configuration used for its accuracy evaluation. The runtime gap widens as the state dimension increases. EnKF and PF propagate 1,000 samples at every time step, so higher-dimensional systems increase the work required to evolve the samples and evaluate their observations. EnKF additionally forms covariance matrices and solves for the observation update, while PF evaluates particle likelihoods and resamples the population. These repeated operations make high-dimensional inference substantially more expensive: EnKF takes 0.4834–0.8383 ms per step on the five low-dimensional systems, compared with 7.9090–15.9068 ms on the nine high-dimensional systems. Sampling-based inference thus incurs a cost at every update even though no neural network is involved. LLMFilter processes observation embeddings together with its System-as-Prompt tokens through the frozen LLaMA-7B backbone. Freezing the backbone reduces training requirements but does not 

remove this computation at inference. FM-UKF uses its neural dynamics model for prediction and still performs recursive UKF covariance propagation and posterior correction. This combination of neural prediction and recursive filtering makes FM-UKF the slowest method on average, and its cost grows with the number of sigma points in higher dimensions. FilterFM instead reconstructs states directly from learned observation representations without propagating an ensemble or solving a UKF update, giving the lowest measured runtime on all fourteen systems in Table 5. C.2 MODEL MISMATCH EVALUATION DETAILS
