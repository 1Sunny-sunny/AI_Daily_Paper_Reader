Abstract 

Experience can guide an ambiguous choice or bias a choice despite strong current evidence. We asked how a thalamocortical circuit carries historical content forward and regulates its influence across successive decisions. In published data from adult male mice, suppressing posterior parietal cortex (PPC) or pulvinar (PUL) improved current judgments most when sensory history conflicted with the stimulus, reflecting re-duced historical weighting. In trained thalamocortical circuit models, replaying recorded PUL input to PPC reconstructed a historical trace after equalizing complete recipient states across histories. Replaying the opposite history redirected the later choice, even after the circuit correctly judged an intervening strong stimulus. This historical message occupied a low-dimensional direction in thalamic output that remained broadly reachable under firing-rate bounds; redistributing the arriving current at matched energy showed that fine spatial organization, beyond total input strength, selectively shapes later history use without dis-rupting current choices. Strong sensory evidence suppressed historical output while preserving internal state differences, whereas ambiguity recruited neuronal rectification thresholds and readout alignment to express history. Small shifts in the cortical operating point modulated later historical influence, and partial attenuation of recurrent drive reduced it across all circuits while preserving current commitments; correspondingly, recurrent dynamics were essential for predicting post-stimulus trajectories in held-out histories. Together, these findings distinguish three operations in history-guided choice: returning thala-mocortical input determines which past is reconstructed, recurrent dynamics carry its influence forward across intervening demands, and current sensory evidence gates how strongly it enters the decision. 

Significance Statement 

Neural circuits must carry prior experience across decisions while limiting its influence when current evidence is clear. In mouse behavioral data, suppressing posterior parietal cortex or pulvinar selectively reduced the accuracy cost of sensory history when it conflicted with the current stimulus. In trained thalamocortical circuit models, replacing pulvinar input to parietal cortex determined which past guided a later choice, even after a correct judgment about a new stimulus. Strong current evidence suppressed the immediate behavioral impact of the retained trace without erasing it, whereas ambiguity recruited the same readout to express history. Introduction 

The same recent experience can help resolve an ambiguous stimulus yet bias a choice when current evi-dence points in the opposite direction. Recent stimuli can bias perception and choice in opposite directions (Fritsche et al., 2017). In rodents, perturbing posterior parietal cortex (PPC) alters the influence of recent sensory inputs and actions on subsequent decisions (Akrami et al., 2018; Hwang et al., 2017). This dual role poses a central circuit question: how does a specific historical distinction remain available across successive judgments, and what determines when it shapes behavioral output? Thalamocortical feedback can sustain cortical preparation (Guo et al., 2017), amplify functional connec-tivity, and switch between task representations (Schmitt et al., 2017; Rikhye et al., 2018). The pulvinar coordinates communication between cortical areas as attentional demands change (Saalmann et al., 2012). Mouse studies show thalamic control of sensory selection and phase-specific contributions to working memory (Wimmer et al., 2015; Bolkan et al., 2017). Hosford et al. (2026) linked this loop to sensory history, showing that transient suppression of PPC or pulvinar (PUL) impaired history-guided decisions while improving judgments of the current stimulus through reduced sensory-history bias. However, sup-pression experiments establish necessity without distinguishing whether returning thalamic feedback sup-plies a nonspecific permissive drive or carries specific historical content that instructs which past governs later choice. Resolving this distinction requires replacing—rather than merely silencing—the arriving message along a defined pathway, and testing whether the substituted history persists even after the cir-cuit accurately processes a new sensory stimulus. Maintaining history across an intervening decision also raises a complementary question of readout con-trol. Work on latent working memory shows that retained neural distinctions can persist without imme-diately dictating behavior and later be reactivated to guide action (Rose et al., 2016; Wolff et al., 2017). In recurrent circuits, persistent and activity-silent processes interact to generate serial biases (Masse et al., 2019; Barbosa et al., 2020), while population geometry can decouple internal representations from immediate motor or decision output (Kaufman et al., 2014; Muhle-Karbe et al., 2021). A stable popula-tion code can coexist with heterogeneous single-neuron dynamics, and population rotations can separate memories from new sensory inputs (Murray et al., 2017; Libby and Buschman, 2021). Yet how a content-bearing thalamocortical input interacts with cortical operating points, nonlinear thresholds, and recurrent dynamics to gate historical expression across changing evidence remains poorly understood. Here we combine reanalysis of the mouse behavioral data with causal circuit interventions in the trained thalamocortical models of Hosford et al. (2026). First, we show in mouse behavior that suppressing PPC or PUL yields its greatest accuracy benefit precisely when sensory history conflicts with the current stimu-lus, pinpointing when historical weighting becomes costly. Second, by equalizing recipient network states and exchanging recorded PUL-to-PPC inputs between opposing histories, we demonstrate that returning thalamic content alone reconstructs and redirects later choices—even across a correct intervening sensory judgment. Third, by manipulating the dimensionality, spatial allocation, and biologically bounded reacha-bility of the arriving current, we show that fine input geometry selectively shapes later history use without disrupting current decisions. Finally, by crossing stimulus strength with trial position and applying cal-ibrated perturbations to cortical state and recurrence, we establish how current evidence gates historical expression through neuronal rectification thresholds, readout alignment, and recurrent propagation.

Results 

Which history is carried forward? Historical evidence contributes benefit and bias according to present evidence. Suppressing either PPC or PUL improved current sensory decisions at the expense of history-guided decisions (Figure 1c). PPC suppression increased current accuracy by +4.9 percentage points (95% mouse-bootstrap interval [1.5, 8.0]) while reducing history-probe accuracy by -14.0 percentage points [-21.9, -6.2]. PUL suppres-sion produced a similar tradeoff (+4.4 [1.9, 6.3] versus -20.9 [-35.0, -7.6] percentage points), consistent with the reduced sensory-history bias reported by Hosford et al. (2026). Crucially, this accuracy gain was concentrated on trials where sensory history conflicted with the current stimulus (Figure 1d). PPC suppression improved accuracy by 10.3 percentage points under conflict versus 1.0 point under agreement—a conflict advantage of 9.3 percentage points [5.5, 13.0] observed in all nine mice (Holm-adjusted signed-rank 𝑝 = 0.012 ). PUL suppression showed a parallel conflict advantage of 6.1 percentage points [1.8, 11.2] across seven of ten mice (Holm-adjusted 𝑝 = 0.055 ), whereas TRN-to-PUL activation produced no reliable conflict effect (-0.8 percentage points [-5.9, 4.0]). Mouse-specific choice models confirmed that this conflict benefit arose specifically from weaker historical weighting rather than altered sensory sensitivity. In PPC, fitted history terms accounted for 8.4 [5.1, 11.5] percentage points of the conflict advantage, compared with -0.04 points for sensory terms—an association that held under flexible spline sensory models and across both feedback-coherent and all binary-choice trials (Figure 1-2). After jointly adjusting for prior intervention exposure, preceding choice and outcome, and within-session drift (Figure 1-1), PPC suppression still substantially reduced historical weighting (𝛽 ℎ𝐿 = −0.70 [-1.11, -0.27], negative in eight of nine mice), whereas PUL suppression showed a weaker negative mean (-0.47 [-1.07, 0.22], negative in seven of ten mice) and TRN-to-PUL activation remained near zero (-0.08 [-0.50, 0.32]; Figure 1e; evidence-magnitude analyses in Figure 1-3). Together, the behavioral data pinpoint when historical weighting becomes costly—specifically under history–stimulus conflict—and establish PPC-linked historical drive as a primary locus of this tradeoff. 

Returning feedback selects which past is reconstructed. We next asked whether the returning PUL-to-PPC signal merely sustains existing cortical activity or actively specifies which past guides a later choice. For pairs of opposing five-trial histories, we averaged the complete 500-unit recipient state at replay onset—eliminating any internal state difference between the two trajectories—and replaced the PUL-to-PPC input with recorded feedback from the own, pair-mean, or opposite history before allowing endogenous recurrence to resume under identical future stimuli (Figure 2a). Neural population separa-tion re-emerged during replay and persisted after endogenous connectivity resumed, with the sign of the final paired output contrast ( 𝐻 𝑅 ) following the delivered message, whereas pair-mean replay kept both trajectories indistinguishable (Figure 2b–d). Across all nine checkpoints, one second of opposite-history replay completely reversed the later histor-ical contrast under common future input (96 new contexts per checkpoint; natural contrasts +0.51 to +0.65 versus opposite-source contrasts -0.64 to -0.38, with every within-checkpoint 95% interval below zero). Conversely, replaying own-history input restored positive historical contrasts after either PPC-only or complete-state averaging. During 500-ms exchanges, amplitude substitution exerted a stronger historical effect than pattern substitution in three checkpoints (G22, G23, and S1), spatial pattern domi-nated in three others (G24, S2, and S3; all six directional differences had 95% intervals excluding zero), and the remaining circuits showed balanced contributions (for example, S0 difference +0.036 [-0.025, +0.095]). 

A reconstructed past survives an intervening successful judgment. For a historical signal to serve sequential behavior, it must survive the processing of a new stimulus without corrupting the current choice. We therefore delivered a shared strong fifth stimulus followed by a balanced sixth probe after replacing the PUL-to-PPC message (Figure 3a; 48 new base histories per checkpoint). Following one second of opposite-source replay, every circuit judged the intervening fifth stimulus with 100% mean-sign accuracy and 99.5–100% native commitment (reaching the correct ±0.75 decision threshold without crossing the opposite bound; Figure 3b). Yet on the subsequent ambiguous sixth trial, historical contrasts reversed in all nine checkpoints, shifting from +0.52 to +0.67 under natural evolution to -0.66 to -0.35 under opposite replay. Median accuracy relative to the original history dropped from 93.5% to 16.2%, while median native commitment to the substituted history reached 64.6% (Figure 3e). Comparing output trajectories across the two decisions highlighted a sharp functional separation: replac-ing the returning message altered mean output by only 0.03–0.13 units during the strong current judgment, but by 0.91–1.33 units during the later historical choice (Figure 3c). This redirection was also graded by input duration: shortening replay to 500 ms still reversed eight of nine checkpoints, leaving only a near-zero residual contrast in G20 (+0.030). A substituted thalamocortical message can therefore pass latently through a correct sensory judgment and redirect a later choice. 

A low-dimensional historical message is shaped by source and target geometry. We next ex-amined the representational structure of the returning historical signal. An independently estimated leading axis captured 77.7–98.8% of held-out source-difference energy across circuits. With a modest common-current uplift (0.2–15.9% of the natural mean-current norm) to preserve nonnegativity, this one-dimensional difference alone rebuilt positive historical contrasts under own replay and reversed them under opposite replay in every checkpoint, showing that historical identity is carried by a compact low-dimensional signal embedded within a higher-dimensional background drive. Dimensional reduction also separated historical direction from current-decision stability. Projecting the message onto the leading axis modestly reduced intervening sensory accuracy in two circuits (84.4% in G23 and 97.9% in S2), but simply restoring the reduced message’s differential-current magnitude— without restoring the discarded residual dimensions—recovered 100% current mean-sign accuracy and nearly complete native commitment (192/192 trials in G23; 190/192 in S2) while maintaining strong historical reversals (-0.49 and -0.57; Figure 4-1). Current sensory competence therefore depended on input magnitude rather than high-dimensional residual structure. To test whether the receiving cortex is sensitive to the direction of this low-dimensional signal beyond its total energy, we compared the learned axis against 16 orthogonal random directions matched in pair mean and differential-current norm (24 base histories per checkpoint). The learned direction consistently produced stronger historical reconstruction (+0.50 to +0.69) than random alternatives (mean absolute contrast 0.02–0.35), yielding a positive learned advantage of 0.32–0.65 across all nine checkpoints (every 95% interval above zero; Figure 4-4). Only 3 of 144 sampled alternatives reached or exceeded the learned magnitude (two in G23 and one in G24), and random directions frequently degraded intervening current commitment (dropping as low as 19.3%, versus 99.5–100% under the learned direction; Figure 4-2). Thus, the trained thalamocortical projection naturally privileges the learned historical direction while sharply restricting alternative high-energy messages in most circuits. 

Fine target allocation modifies history use beyond aggregate input. Because random directions can alter total excitatory and inhibitory drive, we next asked whether fine-scale spatial allocation within native cortical cell classes affects history use when aggregate class input and total differential energy are strictly conserved. We redistributed arriving currents within the native PPC excitatory (280 units) and inhibitory (120 units) populations using energy- and sum-preserving rotations (four maps across 24 base histories per checkpoint; Figure 5a,c). Even under these strict aggregate constraints, fine spatial reallocation strongly and selectively weakened later history use in three checkpoints—reducing historical contrast by 0.51 [0.44, 0.57] in G20, 0.29 [0.22, 0.36] in G24, and 0.24 [0.16, 0.33] in S0—while leaving intervening sensory judgments virtually un-touched (100% mean-sign accuracy and 99.5–100% native commitment). In S2, the same redistribution disrupted both later history (loss 0.56 [0.47, 0.64]) and current commitment (91.2–93.8%). By contrast, the remaining five circuits showed modest or negligible sensitivity despite 100% current accuracy: his-torical losses were small in G23 (0.012 [0.006, 0.018]) and spanned zero in G22, G25, and S1, while S3 tolerated large target displacements (relative displacement 0.90) with a modest mean loss of 0.073 [0.049, 0.098] (Figures 5-1 and 5-2). Fine synaptic targeting within excitatory and inhibitory pools can therefore selectively govern historical efficacy beyond aggregate drive, though sensitivity varies across trained solutions. 

Biologically attainable source reallocations separate reachability from functional sensitivity. We next asked whether comparable energy- and E/I-preserving reallocations remain functionally potent when restricted to messages physically realizable by the 70 bounded PUL rates. Using reflection maps in source space that preserved paired PUL means, rate bounds, receiving-current energy, and aggregate E/I dif-ferences, we observed sharp across-circuit differences in both attainable displacement and functional impact (initial 12-base assay in Figure 4-3; expanded 48-base confirmation with 64- and 256-proposal searches in Figure 4d). Expanding source exploration enlarged receiving displacements and produced re-liable, selective historical losses in G20 (0.044 [0.034, 0.054]), S0 (0.016 [0.009, 0.025]), and S3 (0.012 [0.008, 0.016]), while leaving intervening sensory responses tightly preserved (99.5–100% commitment; all pooled and stimulus-specific sensory-output shifts within ±0.02 units; Figure 5c and Figure 5-3). Because pointwise reflections can introduce rapid high-frequency fluctuations, we further restricted source reallocations to fixed spatial transformations across the entire replay whose temporal roughness remained within 1.25 times the natural signal (48 independent bases per checkpoint; Figure 4-5). Under this smoothness constraint, seven checkpoints admitted no nontrivial alternative and G20 admitted only 0.23% reallocation. Notably, S3 still supported a substantial 14.2% temporally coherent reallocation with virtually zero historical loss (-0.0001 [-0.0025, 0.0024]) and 100% current commitment. Together, these source-level assays demonstrate that physical reachability and functional impact are dissociable properties of the thalamocortical projection: in some circuits, bounded thalamic rates admit smooth, large-scale coding alternatives that leave historical behavior invariant.

Having es-tablished how returning feedback injects historical identity, we next asked why the retained trace barely 11 History-bearing thalamocortical signals Research preprint

Decomposing out-put contrast into population activity separation, readout alignment, and temporal consistency (Equation 11) revealed that strong sensory evidence did not erase the neural trace—rather, it altered how that trace projected onto the decision readout. In G22, for instance, the population rate-difference RMS norm re-mained high across both windows (27.1 during the current judgment versus 24.7 during the later historical judgment), whereas RMS alignment with the fixed readout rose more than sixfold (from 0.0017 to 0.011). Across all nine checkpoints, later-to-current readout alignment increased 6.2- to 17.9-fold (Figure 6a,b). Moreover, once injected at the PUL-to-PPC synapse, the historical distinction propagated throughout the recurrent loop rather than remaining localized to cortex. Decomposing the natural-to-opposite historical output shift by population showed that PPC accounted for 16.5–98.9% of the readout change across cir-cuits; in S3, for example, 83.6% of the final behavioral shift exited via PUL, 16.5% via PPC, and -0.07% via TRN (Figure 6-2). Thus, the entry pathway specifies the historical content, while recurrent population dynamics determine how and where that distinction projects onto choice. 

Current evidence modulates historical expression within the same sequence position. To separate sensory evidence strength from trial position, we varied the fifth and sixth stimuli independently while holding the preceding network state identical across stimulus conditions (Figure 7a,b; 12 new bases per checkpoint). All nine circuits expressed markedly stronger history under ambiguity at both the fifth and sixth positions. Receiver thresholds and recurrent propagation gate continued historical use. Inspecting single-unit activation regimes in the trained circuits revealed how current evidence controls readout alignment. Under ambiguity, units whose paired trajectories straddled the lower firing-rate rectification threshold accounted for 69.9% of the absolute PPC readout difference on average, compared with only 25.1% under strong neg-ative evidence and 33.8% under strong positive evidence, whereas upper-threshold crossings contributed less than 1.0% across all conditions (Figure 6c). Discussion 

A causal bridge from maintenance to use 

In the trained thalamocortical circuits, returning PUL feedback does more than sustain ongoing cortical activity: it specifies which past guides a later decision. Own-history replay reconstructs the historical distinction from identical recipient states, whereas opposite-history replay reverses it—and this reversal survives a correct judgment about an intervening stimulus. Together, these interventions establish a direct causal link between the content carried by a defined thalamocortical pathway and the identity of a subsequent choice. Carrying a historical trace across competing decisions also depends on how the receiving circuit gates its expression. Distinct preparatory and movement subspaces in motor cortex illustrate how one population can support different stages of behavior (Elsayed et al., 2016). A large internal activity difference can remain nearly silent while current sensory evidence is strong, yet drive substantial behavioral contrast once evidence becomes ambiguous. Our exact trajectory decomposition (Equation 11) shows that this gating reflects coordinated changes in rate-difference magni-tude, alignment with the fixed readout, and temporal consistency across the response window. Thus, the returning message sets which past is carried forward, while receiving-circuit dynamics determine when and how strongly that past enters behavior. The mouse behavioral analyses ground this circuit mechanism in functional utility. Suppressing PPC produced its largest accuracy benefit precisely when sensory history opposed the current stimulus, driven by reduced historical weighting that persisted after controlling for prior intervention exposure, behavioral carryover, and within-session drift. PUL suppression showed a consistent mean reduction in historical weighting, albeit with greater across-animal variability after sequential adjustment. By pinpointing when historical drive imposes a behavioral cost in vivo, the mouse data frame the circuit replay experiments, which isolate how pathway-specific content alters that cost-relevant influence. 

Source content, physical reachability and receiving computation 

Circuit models have shown how retained sensory history can generate recency and central-tendency biases in work-ing memory (Boboeva et al., 2024). In the replay experiments, both the scalar amplitude and normalized spatial pattern of arriving thalamic currents carry historical information, with individual circuits differing in how strongly they rely on each coordinate. Although a single leading axis retains sufficient structure to reconstruct or reverse historical choices, dimensional reduction can weaken intervening sensory performance in some circuits—a deficit rescued by restoring differential current magnitude alone. The behavioral impact of a returning message therefore jointly reflects its magnitude, spatial organization, duration, and the tuning of its receiving circuit. Low-dimensional descriptions link recurrent trajectories to memory and timing in both trained networks and recorded populations (Sussillo and Barak, 2013; Cueva et al., 2020). The arrival-current experiments test a complementary question about the incoming message by separating spatial allocation from the amount of delivered activity. At matched differential energy, the learned direction generally reconstructs history more strongly than sampled alternatives. Redistributing the current at fixed E/I totals produces substantial historical losses in three circuits with preserved current judgments. When the same question is posed through the bounded PUL rates, the trained projection limits which changes are attainable. The learned source direction generally reaches the natural current norm, while random directions have markedly different reachability across circuits. S3 supports both a large pointwise reallocation that reduces history use and a temporally coherent reallocation that largely preserves it. Reachability and historical effect are therefore distinct properties of the same source projection. At the receiving end, lower-threshold crossings and readout alignment link retained population activity to behav-ioral expression, echoing principles of context-dependent recurrent computation (Mante et al., 2013), output-null subspaces (Kaufman et al., 2014), and attractor-gated information flow (Finkelstein et al., 2021). Nudging the com-mon cortical state alters later historical output by shifting paired trajectories relative to rectification thresholds, while partially attenuating recurrent-drive differences selectively weakens later history use without disturbing intervening sensory choices. Likewise, held-out forecasting separates two complementary components of post-stimulus dynam-ics: stereotyped evidence-driven trajectories shared across histories, and recurrently propagated history-specific deviations that become decisive under ambiguity. 21 History-bearing thalamocortical signals Research preprint 

From neural maintenance to sequential cognitive availability 

The replay experiments connect earlier accounts of thalamocortical maintenance and latent-memory reactivation to the identity of the history used in a sequence of choices (Guo et al., 2017; Rose et al., 2016; Wolff et al., 2017; Masse et al., 2019; Barbosa et al., 2020; Muhle-Karbe et al., 2021). The incoming current enters PPC, then propa-gates through the recurrent circuit. The signed regional readout components show that a change delivered through one connection can be expressed through several populations. In this account, the entering pathway identifies the manipulated message, and the resulting population trajectory determines where its output contribution appears. Work in frontal cortex connects recovery after perturbation with redundancy across coupled modules and discrete attractor dynamics (Li et al., 2016; Inagaki et al., 2019). Complementing these experiments, our theoretical con-struction (Extended Theory; Figure 2-1) demonstrates that circuits with identical stability spectra and isolated decay curves can nonetheless diverge in recovery after erasure depending on how erased partitions and the readout weight underlying modes. Active content replacement therefore probes functional circuit organization in a way that passive perturbation or decay measurements alone cannot resolve. More broadly, maintaining a specific historical distinction across an intervening task demand—without allowing it to corrupt current sensory processing—provides a concrete circuit primitive for flexible cognitive availability (Dehaene and Changeux, 2011). Extending this framework to tasks that explicitly probe flexible rule switching or subjective report will clarify how content-bearing thalamocortical loops support higher-order cognitive control. 

Scope and testable predictions across circuit and behavioral scales 

While fresh histories rigorously tested each of the nine released checkpoints, those checkpoints derive from a finite set of training runs whose exact provenance is only partially documented in the released archive (Hosford et al., 2026). Training networks across multiple tasks can reveal representations that generalize across related computations (Yang et al., 2019). New circuit ensembles and sequential assays spanning intermediate evidence strengths can test how the three operations identified here vary across tasks and training regimes. Crucially, our circuit findings yield concrete, falsifiable predictions for pathway-specific neural interventions in vivo. First, following transient suppression that reduces cortical historical differences, patterned stimulation of PUL-to-PPC terminals mimicking congruent versus opposite historical states should selectively steer later ambiguous choices even after stimulation ceases and an intervening stimulus is judged correctly. Second, at a fixed trial position and matched pre-stimulus state, increasing current sensory evidence should attenuate behavioral historical bias far more strongly than it shrinks the underlying population state difference in PPC and PUL. Third, manipulating the spatial pattern of thalamic input at matched total drive should alter subsequent historical bias whenever the receiving cortical population exhibits fine-scale spatial selectivity. 

Conclusion 

In trained thalamocortical circuits, returning pulvinar feedback can reconstruct a specific historical trace and redi-rect later decisions across a correct intervening sensory judgment. The trained thalamocortical projection constrains which messages can physically reach cortex, while rectification thresholds, recurrent propagation, and readout ge-ometry govern when that reconstructed past enters behavior. 22 History-bearing thalamocortical signals Research preprint

## Figure 2-1. Extended Theory 

Overview 

The same historical distinction can persist in a circuit, be reconstructed by arriving input, and have different effects on successive decisions. The theory relates these phenomena to the initial state, the arriving message, and the projection from population activity to choice. ET1–ET3 derive the consequences of replacing a source message and characterize the currents available to that source. ET4–ET5 connect persistent activity differences to readout geometry and saturation. ET6 shows how cir-cuits with identical stability spectra and conditional decay can still reconstruct history differently after erasure. ET7 applies the same distinction to a neuronal recovery variable. ET8–ET9 extend these geometric and expression prin-ciples to bounded source transformations, calibrated receiver perturbations, and cross-history trajectory prediction.
