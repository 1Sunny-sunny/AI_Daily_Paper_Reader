ABSTRACT 

Test-time structure-aware inference can improve cross-subject EEG-to-image re-trieval by exploiting relations among unlabeled test queries, but it also makes predictions depend on query-set composition. Existing evaluations typically re-port accuracy under a single full-query context, leaving unclear whether gains and comparative conclusions remain reliable when the available context changes. We introduce Context-Marginalized Retrieval Evaluation (CMRE), which repeatedly evaluates each target query within sampled subsets of companion queries, apply-ing each inference rule to the corresponding rows of a fixed query–gallery similar-ity matrix. CMRE distinguishes structural exploitability, quantified by gains over independent retrieval, from context robustness, measured by same-query Top-1 context consistency. Under leave-one-subject-out evaluation on THINGS-EEG2 across six frozen representations and six structure-aware inference rules, structure-aware inference improves full-context Top-1 accuracy by up to 20.40 percentage points over independent retrieval. However, the rule selected under full-context evaluation is no longer optimal in 17 of 24 representation–context-size conditions (70.8%). Selecting the full-context winner incurs an average loss of 2.70 percent-age points and a maximum loss of 8.29 percentage points under reduced contexts. Representation rankings are likewise inference- and context-dependent: the order-ing under independent retrieval is not consistently preserved under structure-aware inference. Moreover, larger retrieval gains do not necessarily imply greater con-text robustness, showing that retrieval effectiveness and prediction stability pro-vide complementary views of structure-aware inference. Together, these results show that full-context accuracy represents only one operating point of context-dependent inference. CMRE complements conventional evaluation by character-izing structural gain, context consistency, and the context dependence of conclu-sions about inference rules and frozen EEG-to-image representations. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

EEG-to-image retrieval seeks to identify visually corresponding images in a candidate gallery by aligning EEG representations with visual embedding spaces. Recent advances in neural represen-tation learning and cross-modal alignment have substantially improved retrieval performance (Song et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2026; Jo et al., 2026). Despite this progress, cross-subject generalization remains challenging because of pronounced inter-subject variability in EEG signals. This challenge has motivated leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) evaluation, in which models must generalize to previously unseen subjects without subject-specific supervised retraining. Beyond representation learning, recent work has begun to exploit information from unlabeled target-subject queries at test time to improve cross-subject retrieval without labeled calibration (Huang & Zhu, 2026; Cui et al., 2026). However, once test-time inference exploits re-lations among multiple target queries, retrieval becomes context-dependent: the prediction for the same EEG query may change with the composition of the other queries processed alongside it. 1arXiv:2611.28648v1 [cs.CV] 21 Nov 2026 This coupling creates an evaluation problem. For a fixed gallery, independent retrieval predicts each query from its own similarity vector and is therefore unaffected by the other test queries. By contrast, inference rules based on set-level statistics or global assignment constraints couple each prediction to the remaining test queries. Their measured performance therefore reflects both the quality of the frozen representation and the particular query context used for evaluation. A single full-context operating point can consequently conceal sensitivity to query-set composition. Existing cross-subject EEG-to-image studies that exploit unlabeled target-side information at test time do not systematically examine how retrieval performance and same-query prediction stability vary with query context (Huang & Zhu, 2026; Cui et al., 2026). Related sensitivity to the composition and size of unlabeled test sets has also been reported in transductive learning (Veilleux et al., 2021; Martin et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2023). These observations motivate two complementary questions. First, how much additional retrieval performance can structure-aware inference extract from a fixed sim-ilarity matrix? Second, how stable are the predictions for the same EEG query when the available companion-query context changes? We refer to these properties as structural exploitability and 

context robustness , respectively. Beyond these two properties, we further examine whether com-parative conclusions about inference rules and frozen EEG-to-image representations remain stable across test-time inference conditions. To address these questions, we introduce Context-Marginalized Retrieval Evaluation (CMRE). CMRE repeatedly evaluates each target query across sampled query contexts at predefined sizes while ensuring that every target is evaluated equally often. For each frozen representation, all infer-ence rules operate on context-specific rows drawn from the same full query–gallery similarity ma-trix. CMRE reports context-marginalized retrieval performance, quantifies structural exploitability through gains over independent retrieval, and measures context robustness through the consistency of same-query Top-1 predictions across contexts. Under LOSO cross-subject protocols on THINGS-EEG2 (Gifford et al., 2022), we apply CMRE to six frozen EEG-to-image representations and six structure-aware inference rules spanning set-level and assignment-based inference, with indepen-dent retrieval serving as the context-invariant reference. Within each frozen representation, this controlled design isolates differences attributable to test-time inference by providing all inference rules with identical query–gallery similarity evidence. Our results reveal several limitations of relying on a single full-context evaluation. Across six frozen EEG-to-image representations and six structure-aware inference rules, test-time structure improves full-context Top-1 accuracy by up to 20.40 percentage points over independent retrieval. How-ever, the inference rule ranked first under full-context evaluation is no longer optimal in 17 of 24 representation–context-size conditions (70.8%). Selecting the full-context winner incurs an average performance loss of 2.70 percentage points and a maximum loss of 8.29 percentage points under reduced context. The apparent ranking of frozen representations is also inference- and context-dependent: the ordering observed under independent retrieval is not consistently preserved under structure-aware inference. Moreover, larger gains over independent retrieval do not necessarily co-incide with greater same-query context consistency, showing that retrieval effectiveness and context robustness capture distinct properties of structure-aware inference. Together, these findings indicate that full-context accuracy represents only one operating point of context-dependent inference and can provide an incomplete basis for comparing both inference strategies and frozen EEG-to-image representations. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We formalize test-time EEG-to-image retrieval under fixed query–gallery similarity scores and distinguish three inference regimes: independent retrieval, set-level inference, and assignment-based inference. This formulation enables controlled comparisons of inference rules while holding the underlying representation fixed. • We identify structural exploitability and context robustness as two complementary prop-erties of test-time structure-aware inference and introduce Context-Marginalized Re-trieval Evaluation (CMRE) to quantify both. CMRE repeatedly evaluates each target query under randomly sampled query contexts of predefined sizes while balancing target-query evaluation frequency. It measures gains over independent retrieval and same-query Top-1 consistency across contexts. • Through LOSO cross-subject evaluation across six frozen EEG-to-image representations and six structure-aware inference rules, we show that larger structural gains do not neces-sarily imply greater context robustness. We therefore introduce Context-Marginalized Retrieval Evaluation (CMRE) , which marginalizes evaluation over repeatedly constructed query contexts while keeping the full query–gallery similar-ity matrix fixed (Fig. 2). Each inference rule is applied independently to every context, and the resulting per-query predictions and ranks are aggregated across context evaluations. 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Dataset and Retrieval Protocol. We evaluate THINGS-EEG2 (Gifford et al., 2022) under the standard 200-way EEG-to-image retrieval setting using a leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-subject protocol, with results averaged across ten held-out subjects. We additionally perform cross-modal validation on THINGS-MEG (Hebart et al., 2023) under a LOSO protocol across four held-out subjects. 

Representations. We evaluate six frozen EEG-to-image representations: NICE (Song et al., 2024), ATM (Li et al., 2024), UBP (Wu et al., 2025), BLUR (Liu et al., 2026), HyFI (Jo et al., 2026), and BrainHIVE (Zheng et al., 2026). These methods cover diverse neural–visual alignment strategies and representation strengths, allowing us to assess whether test-time structure-aware in-ference behaves consistently across different EEG–image similarity spaces. 

Compared Inference Methods. We compare independent retrieval with three set-level methods: CSLS (Lample et al., 2018), IS (Smith et al., 2017), and GC (Dinu et al., 2015) and three assignment-based methods: Sinkhorn (Cuturi, 2013), Hungarian matching (Kuhn, 1955), and Partial OT (Chapel et al., 2020). For each representation, all inference methods operate on the same frozen similarity matrix, with independent retrieval serving as the context-invariant reference. The fixed configura-tions and test-time complexity of all inference methods are provided in Appendix B.3. 

CMRE Evaluation. We apply CMRE following the protocol in Section 3, using the same con-structed contexts for all inference methods. The conventional full-context setting ( m = 200 ) is evaluated separately. We report CM-Top1 and CM-Top5 for all methods. SE is measured by the cor-responding Top-K gain over independent retrieval, whereas CC is reported only for reduced contexts with Rm > 1. We use T = 10 construction rounds in the main experiments. We first evaluate structural exploitability at the conventional full-context operating point ( m = Nq =200 ), where all test queries are processed jointly. For each frozen representation, every inference rule receives the same full query–gallery similarity matrix. The gain over independent retrieval therefore measures how much additional retrieval performance each rule obtains by exploiting cross-query structure at test time. All six structure-aware inference methods improve accuracy over indepen-dent retrieval for every representation, although the gains vary substantially across representations. Averaged across represen-tations, Hungarian achieves the highest Mean SE (+12.96 pp), followed by Sinkhorn (+10.71 pp) and Partial OT (+8.68 pp). The preferred inference rule is representation-dependent. Among the set-level methods, IS performs best on five representations, whereas GC performs best on ATM. We therefore next evaluate structure-aware inference under reduced query contexts using CMRE. 4.3 STRUCTURAL EXPLOITABILITY UNDER REDUCED QUERY CONTEXTS 

The full-context results quantify structural exploitability at a single operating point, but do not show whether retrieval performance, prediction stability, and comparative rankings remain stable as query context changes. We therefore apply CMRE at reduced query-context sizes m ∈ { 25 , 50 , 100 , 150 }.Across these evaluations, the image gallery and frozen full query–gallery similarity matrix remain fixed, while only the size and composition of the constructed query contexts vary. Table 1 identifies Hungarian as the full-context winner on BLUR, achieving 40.15% Top-1 accuracy at m = 200 . However, Fig. 3(a) shows that Sinkhorn ranks first at every reduced context size, whereas Hungarian declines from 33.36% at m = 150 to 22.02% at m = 25 . Retaining the full-context winner therefore incurs losses of 1.30, 5.71, 8.29, and 8.11 pp at m = 150 , 100, 50, and 25, respectively. Independent retrieval remains context-invariant at 20.05%. Thus, on BLUR, reducing the query context reverses the preference between Hungarian and Sinkhorn. Table 2 summarizes mean CM-Top1 structural exploitability across the four reduced context sizes. All 36 representation–rule combinations yield positive mean gains over independent retrieval, rang-ing from 0.02 pp for GC on NICE to 12.82 pp for Sinkhorn on BLUR. Thus, useful cross-query structure remains exploitable even when inference operates on only a subset of the test queries. To assess whether the observed gains were consistent across subjects, we averaged each subject’s gain across the six representations and four reduced context sizes. All six structure-aware inference rules yielded positive mean subject-level gains, and the corresponding subject-level gains differed signifi-cantly from zero under two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction across methods (Appendix D.2). However, both the magnitude of the gain and the ranking of inference rules depend strongly on the frozen representation. Sinkhorn performs best on NICE (+1.32 pp), BLUR (+12.82 pp), and BrainHIVE (+10.02 pp), whereas CSLS ranks first on ATM (+2.51 pp) and IS ranks first on UBP (+10.34 pp) and HyFI (+12.69 pp). No single inference rule is therefore uniformly optimal across representations. We next examine whether same-query predictions remain consistent across contexts. 4.4 CONTEXT ROBUSTNESS UNDER REDUCED QUERY CONTEXTS 

Retrieval performance alone does not reveal whether the same EEG query retains its Top-1 pre-diction as the companion-query composition changes. We therefore quantify context robustness using Context Consistency (CC), which measures pairwise agreement among the Top-1 predictions produced for the same query across its constructed context evaluations at a given context size. Figure 3(b) reports the CC profiles on BLUR across the four reduced context sizes. Independent re-trieval maintains CC = 100% because its predictions are context-invariant by construction. Among the structure-aware inference rules, CSLS is comparatively stable, with CC increasing from 76.92% at m = 25 to 90.07% at m = 150 . By contrast, Hungarian’s CM-Top1 increases from 22.02% to 33.36% over the same range, while its CC decreases from 76.69% to 65.12%, reaching a min-imum of 63.44% at m = 100 . This divergence shows that higher retrieval performance does not necessarily imply greater context robustness. Table 3 shows that the pattern extends beyond BLUR. CSLS achieves the highest mean CC on each of the six frozen representations and the highest overall CC (85.04%). The other inference rules attain lower overall CC values, ranging from 61.70% to 69.44%, and vary more strongly across representations. 5 CONCLUSION 

We studied how test-time structure-aware inference in EEG-to-image retrieval depends on query-context composition. Using fixed EEG–image similarity matrices, we introduced CMRE to measure structural exploitability and Context Consistency across sampled contexts. The preferred inference rule varied across representations and context sizes, while representation rankings were themselves inference- and context-dependent. Larger structural gains did not necessarily imply greater context robustness, and this separation also persisted on THINGS-MEG. These findings show that struc-tural exploitability and context robustness are complementary properties and that full-context ac-curacy alone can provide an incomplete evaluation of context-dependent test-time inference. Ac-cordingly, CMRE evaluates inference across sampled query contexts by jointly considering context-marginalized retrieval performance, structural gains, and same-query prediction consistency. 9AI USE STATEMENT

A RELATED WORK 

A.1 EEG-TO -I MAGE RETRIEVAL 

Most EEG-to-image retrieval methods align EEG embeddings with pretrained visual spaces. Early studies established zero-shot retrieval through contrastive neural–visual learning (Song et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024; Chen & Wei, 2024). Subsequent work added language-guided supervision, multimodal semantic bridging, and richer cross-modal objectives (Song et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Wang & Chen, 2026). More recent methods explored prompt tuning, hierarchical visual representations, pretrained vision encoders, and subject-aware alignment (Wang et al., 2025a; Zhou et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026). Perceptual and neuroscientific priors have also been introduced through uncertainty-aware supervi-sion, multiscale targets, hyperbolic modeling, neuromimetic simulation, and masked cross-modal fusion (Wu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026; Jo et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Shao et al., 2026; Xu & Lin, 2026). Cross-subject retrieval remains challenging because EEG representations vary 13 across individuals. Recent methods address this problem through subject-invariant learning, label-free calibration, similarity-geometry adaptation, and target-subject coordinate recovery (Choi et al., 2026; Huang & Zhu, 2026; Cui et al., 2026). A recent diagnostic study further showed that stan-dard retrieval performance may not transfer to controlled evaluation settings (Zhang et al., 2026a). However, prior work generally evaluates each target under a fixed test context, leaving sensitivity to companion-query composition insufficiently characterized. A.2 TEST -T IME STRUCTURAL INFERENCE FOR RETRIEVAL 

Post-hoc structural inference refines retrieval scores without changing the underlying representa-tion. One line of work addresses hubness, where a small number of gallery items become nearest neighbors of many queries (Dinu et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2017; Lample et al., 2018; Bogolin et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025). Global Correction (GC) re-ranks can-didates using query-population preferences (Dinu et al., 2015); Inverted Softmax (IS) suppresses popular targets through reverse normalization (Smith et al., 2017); and Cross-domain Similarity Local Scaling (CSLS) corrects for local embedding density (Lample et al., 2018). Related matrix-normalization methods have also been applied to cross-modal retrieval (Cheng et al., 2021; Bogolin et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). We refer to these methods as set-level inference because predictions remain query-wise while using statistics from other queries or gallery items. Joint matching introduces stronger structural coupling. Optimal transport provides soft global as-signments (Cuturi, 2013; Courty et al., 2017; Chizat et al., 2018; Chapel et al., 2020), with entropic regularization enabling efficient Sinkhorn iterations (Cuturi, 2013). Unbalanced transport relaxes mass conservation (Chizat et al., 2018), while partial transport relaxes full matching (Chapel et al., 2020); the Hungarian algorithm instead imposes hard one-to-one assignment (Kuhn, 1955). Related global matching methods have also been used in vision and cross-modal correspondence (Sarlin et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2022). These approaches span score recalibration, soft global coupling, and hard assignment. The effectiveness of post-hoc normalization can also depend on available query and gallery statistics. Querybank Normalisation uses an external query bank (Bogolin et al., 2022), whereas Dual Bank Normalization uses both query and gallery banks (Wang et al., 2023). Target-set structure has also been used for label-free cross-subject EEG-to-image calibration (Huang & Zhu, 2026; Cui et al., 2026). However, these studies optimize retrieval under prescribed test con-figurations. We instead hold the representation and inference rule fixed and vary only the available query context. A.3 EVALUATION OF TRANSDUCTIVE INFERENCE 

Transductive inference uses statistics from unlabeled test samples, making its performance sensitive to assumptions about the query set. Transductive few-shot methods benefit from joint reasoning over queries (Boudiaf et al., 2020), but evaluations show that class balance, class composition, and query-set structure can substantially alter measured performance (Veilleux et al., 2021; Martin et al., 2022). Test-set composition therefore forms part of the information available to an inference procedure rather than merely a benchmark detail. Related concerns arise in test-time adaptation. Early methods adapt models using statistics or en-tropy objectives from unlabeled batches (Wang et al., 2021), while continual and source-free vari-ants address changing test distributions (Wang et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2022). Test-time behavior is also affected by temporal correlation, batch size, class imbalance, and distribution shifts (Gong et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023). Benchmarks further show sensitivity to evalua-tion protocols, model selection, backbone quality, and shift type (Zhao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025b; Hagen et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026). However, these studies primarily vary distributions or adaptation settings rather than repeatedly evaluating the same target under controlled changes to its companion-query context. 

B IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

This section provides additional implementation details for the six EEG-to-image retrieval represen-tations and the six test-time structural inference methods used in our experiments. For each repre-sentation, we preserve its original EEG–image representation pipeline and obtain a frozen query– 14 gallery similarity matrix before applying any structural inference. The inference methods therefore modify only the test-time similarity structure and do not update the representation parameters or embeddings. B.1 REPRESENTATION IMPLEMENTATIONS 

NICE. NICE (Song et al., 2024) represents the standard contrastive EEG–vision alignment paradigm. It learns an EEG encoder whose outputs are directly aligned with pretrained visual repre-sentations through a contrastive objective, providing a relatively direct neural-to-visual embedding space. 

ATM. ATM (Li et al., 2024) introduces an Adaptive Thinking Mapper to transform EEG rep-resentations toward the pretrained visual embedding space. Compared with the direct contrastive alignment of NICE, ATM explicitly learns a mapping between the neural and visual representations, providing a different similarity geometry for retrieval. We use the retrieval branch of the original framework without involving its reconstruction components. 

UBP. UBP (Wu et al., 2025) incorporates uncertainty-aware visual supervision into EEG–image alignment. Rather than treating every EEG–image pair as an equally reliable training target, it modu-lates visual supervision according to the estimated ambiguity of neural–visual correspondence. This produces a similarity space shaped by uncertainty-aware alignment rather than purely deterministic contrastive matching. 

BLUR. BLUR (Liu et al., 2026) constructs a perceptually diversified visual space using multiple levels of image blur and learns to select informative visual evidence for EEG alignment. Its retrieval representation therefore reflects multi-view perceptual supervision rather than a single visual target, making it particularly useful for examining whether test-time structural inference behaves differently on a perceptually enriched similarity space. 

HyFI. HyFI (Jo et al., 2026) models EEG–visual relations in a hyperbolic feature space, aiming to capture hierarchical semantic structure that may be difficult to represent in a purely Euclidean embedding. It therefore provides a geometrically distinct retrieval representation from the Euclidean contrastive and perceptual-alignment backbones considered above. 

BrainHIVE. BrainHIVE (Zheng et al., 2026) employs hierarchical and multiscale visual supervi-sion to align EEG representations with visual information at multiple semantic granularities. Unlike approaches based on a single visual embedding or a single perceptual transformation, BrainHIVE in-tegrates complementary visual representations before retrieval, yielding a richer hierarchical EEG– image similarity structure. The six representations are selected to span different representation families rather than merely different model capacities. NICE and ATM provide relatively direct neural–visual alignment, UBP and BLUR modify visual supervision through perceptual degradation, HyFI introduces a different embedding geometry, and BrainHIVE incorporates hierarchical multi-source visual representations. This diversity allows us to evaluate whether structural inference behavior persists across different frozen EEG–image similarity geometries. B.2 TEST -T IME INFERENCE METHODS

F CMRE C ONVERGENCE AND SAMPLING ROBUSTNESS 

We conduct the convergence analysis on BLUR using three representative inference rules with dis-tinct context-response characteristics: CSLS, which exhibits consistently high Context Consistency; Sinkhorn, which achieves the largest average reduced-context retrieval gain; and Hungarian, which shows pronounced context-dependent variation in both retrieval performance and prediction consis-tency. For each reduced context size m ∈ { 25 , 50 , 100 , 150 }, we generate a fixed schedule of 50 random partition rounds and evaluate progressively larger prefixes with T ∈ { 2, 5, 10 , 20 , 30 , 50 },where T denotes the number of partition rounds. The estimate obtained with T = 50 is treated as a high-budget reference. For inference method Φ, we measure the average absolute deviation of CM-Top1 from the reference as 

DΦTop1 (T ) = 14

X CM -Top1 Φ,T (m) − CM -Top1 Φ,50 (m) , (23) and analogously define the deviation of Context Consistency as 

DΦCC (T ) = 14

X |CC Φ,T (m) − CC Φ,50 (m)| . (24) Figure 8 shows that both estimates rapidly approach the high-budget reference as the number of par-tition rounds increases. At the T = 10 setting used in the main experiments, the average CM-Top1 deviations are only 0.204, 0.322, and 0.263 pp for CSLS, Sinkhorn, and Hungarian, respectively. The corresponding CC deviations are 0.549, 0.769, and 0.415 pp. Thus, even for inference rules with substantially different context-response behaviors, the estimates obtained with 10 partition rounds are already close to those obtained with 50 rounds. These results support the partition-round budget used in the main experiments. Increasing the number of rounds beyond T = 10 produces only small changes in the reported CMRE estimates. This analysis evaluates prefix convergence within a shared 50-round partition schedule, rather than variability across independently resampled schedules. G ROBUSTNESS TO GALLERY EXPANSION AND QUERY -CONTEXT 

VARIATION 

The standard THINGS-EEG2 retrieval protocol uses a closed gallery with Nq = Ng = 200 , con-taining one ground-truth image for each held-out EEG query. To examine whether structural gains depend on this setting, we keep the 200 queries and their ground-truth images fixed while expanding the gallery to Ng ∈ { 200 , 500 , 1000 , 2000 }. We construct a fixed pool of 1,800 distractors from un-used images in the original THINGS collection. We exclude all images used in the THINGS-EEG2 training and test sets and all images belonging to the 200 test concepts, ensuring that the distrac-tors neither duplicate evaluated concepts nor have corresponding EEG queries. Expanded galleries are formed by adding subsets of this pool; at Ng = 2000 , all 1,800 distractors are included. The EEG and image representations remain frozen throughout, so only the test-time gallery composition changes. Figure 9 summarizes the BLUR results. As expected, absolute Top-1 accuracy decreases for every method as Ng increases, reflecting greater retrieval difficulty (Figure 9(a)). Structure-aware in-ference nevertheless remains superior to independent retrieval across all expanded-gallery settings, although its gains decrease substantially with Ng . Hungarian achieves the largest gain in the original closed gallery, improving Top-1 accuracy by 20.10 percentage points at Ng = 200 . Its gain falls to 5.11, 2.19, and 0.90 percentage points at Ng = 500 , 1000 , and 2000 , respectively. By contrast, Partial OT retains the largest gain after expansion, achieving 6.02, 4.04, and 2.25 percentage points at the three larger gallery sizes, while Sinkhorn and CSLS show intermediate behavior. These results indicate that the effectiveness of structural inference depends not only on the frozen similarity representation but also on the structural assumptions induced by the test gallery. H DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

CMRE characterizes how a fixed inference rule behaves when the query context available at test time changes. This perspective complements conventional full-context evaluation, but it does not model every plausible deployment setting. In particular, our contexts are generated through balanced random constructions. Real query streams may instead be temporally correlated, non-uniform, or task dependent. Although the balanced construction controls the number of appearances of each query, sampled contexts overlap and therefore do not constitute independent observations. We intentionally keep each inference rule and its hyperparameters fixed across frozen representa-tions and context sizes. This isolates the effect of available query context from context-specific retuning. Consequently, our results describe the behavior of the evaluated rules at shared fixed op-erating points, rather than their optimally tuned performance under every representation or context size. Moreover, Context Consistency measures agreement among predictions across contexts, not their correctness. A method can therefore be highly consistent while making consistently incorrect predictions. Our empirical study focuses primarily on THINGS-EEG2. The complementary THINGS-MEG evaluation includes four participants and two frozen representations, and should therefore be inter-preted as supporting evidence rather than a comprehensive cross-modal benchmark. The gallery-expansion analysis further shows that the gains of hard one-to-one assignment depend strongly on the closed, bijective gallery assumption. Its behavior is less favorable when unmatched distrac-tors are introduced. Extending CMRE to larger cohorts, broader neural recording modalities, more realistic query streams, and non-bijective retrieval settings is an important direction for future work. 28
