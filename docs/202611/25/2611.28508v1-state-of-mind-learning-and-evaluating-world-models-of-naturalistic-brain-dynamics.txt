## Abstract 

Understanding naturalistic brain activity requires going be-yond the stimulus alone, as external inputs continuously in-teract with an individual’s evolving brain state. A world model captures such dynamics in a latent state by inferring it from neural observations, predicting stimulus-driven evo-lution, and updating with new observations. However, ex-isting brain world models fail to maintain a persistent la-tent state over time, and standard metrics of neural pre-diction accuracy cannot tell whether a model tracks an in-dividual’s brain state or relies solely on the stimulus. To directly test state inference, prediction, and updating, we introduce BrainStateBench , which evaluates a model’s la-tent state through a frozen linear probe on whole-cortex, movie-driven fMRI. Its State task tests whether a model cap-tures trial-to-trial variability in an individual’s responses to repeated stimuli, while Forecast and Updating evaluate fu-ture activity predictions without and with new observations, respectively. To track individual brain state across time, we propose Spectator , a linear latent world model trained with a joint-embedding predictive objective. Spectator en-codes short- and long-horizon observation histories into a two-timescale latent state and predicts state transitions lin-early from the current state and stimulus. ## 1. Introduction 

Brain activity during a naturalistic stimulus arises from a continuous interaction between the incoming stimulus and the individual’s evolving brain state [11, 19]. Because of these ongoing internal dynamics, even an identical stimu-lus evokes different responses across individuals and across repeated trials within the same individual, which the stimu-lus alone cannot explain: an individual watching the same movie twice is in a different state of mind each time. Cap-turing naturalistic brain dynamics therefore requires infer-ring this unobserved brain state from past neural activity and tracking how it evolves under incoming stimuli. Doing so makes it possible both to predict an individual’s future responses and to study how internal dynamics and external inputs jointly shape brain activity. World models provide a principled formulation for this goal: they infer a latent state from observations, predict how it evolves under actions, and update it as new observations arrive [17, 18, 22, 24]. In neural modeling, the stimulus acts as the action, fMRI recordings provide the observations, and the latent state represents the individual’s brain state. Yet existing approaches fall short on both modeling and evaluation (Fig. 1, left). On the modeling side , stimulus-only encoding models predict shared stimulus-driven activ-ity without observing the individual’s brain and maintain no latent state [7, 14]. Medical world models learn im-age representations, guide imaging, and simulate treatment outcomes [46, 50, 51]. Existing brain world models [10] and fMRI forecasters [12, 49] either forecast directly in ob-servation space or encode only brief observation windows without maintaining a persistent, updatable latent state. On the evaluation side , standard metrics score fMRI prediction accuracy, mixing shared stimulus-driven responses with in-ternal dynamics so one cannot tell whether a model tracks an individual’s brain state or simply predicts the stimulus response. To directly test state inference, prediction, and updating, we introduce BrainStateBench on whole-cortex, movie-driven fMRI (Fig. 1, right). Rather than scoring end-to-end fMRI predictions alone, BrainStateBench maps each model’s latent state to fMRI via a linear probe fitted on training data and then frozen, evaluating diverse architec-tures on equal footing without retraining. Its State task pairs two trials of the same stimulus from the same individual and tests whether the probed state predicts the response differ-ence between them, where any stimulus-only model scores zero by construction. Forecast evaluates one-minute pre-dictions of future activity without new observations, while 

Updating tests how effectively a model revises its predic-tions as new observations arrive. To maintain and update a persistent latent state over time, we propose Spectator , a linear latent world model of nat-uralistic brain dynamics (Fig. 1, right; Fig. 2). Its encoder compresses observation history into a two-timescale latent state capturing fast dynamics over seconds and slow context over minutes, while its predictor advances this state using incoming stimulus features. When new observations arrive, they pass through the encoder to update the latent state. Fol-lowing JEPA [1, 24], we train the encoder and predictor by aligning multi-step predicted latent states with target states inferred from future observations. Crucially, because its dy-namics are linear, every forecast decomposes exactly into additive latent-state and stimulus terms, making their con-tributions directly interpretable. Experiments show why brain world models should be evaluated on their latent state (Secs. 5.2 and 5.3). Stimulus-only encoders reach 0.320 in fMRI prediction correlation without neural input (0% on State ), while prior brain world models reach 0.239 yet capture almost no trial-specific state (at most 0.4% on State ). Across multi-minute horizons, Spectator outperforms all six baselines across every met-ric, achieving 0.349 correlation alongside 5.5% on State ,16.0% on Forecast , and 20.3% on Updating , more than dou-ble the best baseline. Ablations show that its learned slow state drives long-horizon gains, while linear dynamics beat Transformer variants and remain stable across five-minute rollouts (Sec. 5.4). Moreover, Spectator forecasts over 

20 × faster than prior forecasting models, matches baselines trained on full data using 10–25% of recordings, and gen-eralizes across scanners to unseen individuals on a second dataset without calibration (Sec. 5.2). Finally, decompos-ing Spectator’s forecasts reveals a clear cortical hierarchy: inferred latent state drives early predictions, especially in prefrontal and parietal cortex, whereas the stimulus domi-nates later predictions in sensory and narrative-processing regions (Sec. 5.5). Our contributions are: • State-centric evaluation of brain world models. We in-troduce BrainStateBench , moving beyond prediction ac-curacy to directly probe latent state inference, prediction, and updating. • A simple and effective linear brain world model. We propose Spectator , showing that linear latent dynamics over multi-minute horizons outperform complex nonlin-ear and stimulus-only models across tasks and datasets at over 20 × the speed. • Insights into individualized brain dynamics. We show that Spectator naturally disentangles shared stimulus-2driven responses from an individual’s trial-specific inter-nal brain state, revealing where and when each governs naturalistic brain activity. 

## 2. Related work 

World models and their evaluation. World models learn dynamics for prediction and planning [5, 17, 18, 45]. Latent world models predict in representation space, with JEPA learning encoders and predictors by matching predicted and target representations [1, 4, 24]. Linear state-space mod-els provide a classical formulation of latent dynamics [22], including input-driven neural activity [41]. Spectator com-bines linear dynamics with a JEPA objective to learn a two-timescale brain state. Evaluation is a separate challenge: output quality does not test a model’s grasp of the underly-ing dynamics [37, 52]. Existing work probes learned rep-resentations [2, 25] or compares predicted trajectories with known system states [40]. For the brain, BrainStateBench tests latent-state capabilities through neural measurements because the underlying state is unobserved. 

World models of brain dynamics. Medical world mod-els span diagnosis, imaging, and treatment simulation [46, 50, 51]. For brain dynamics, NeuroWorld learns stimulus-conditioned latent transitions and decodes rollouts into fMRI [10]. BrainVista predicts brain activity autoregres-sively from network-wise tokens and multimodal stim-uli [49]. Other approaches generate whole-brain fMRI conditioned on anatomy [43], forecast from neural his-tory [12, 33], or learn representations for classification [9]. Brain forecasting and generation models are evaluated by how well their outputs match measured activity. Specta-tor integrates multi-minute neural history into a latent state that evolves under stimuli and incorporates new observa-tions, while BrainStateBench evaluates state inference, pre-diction, and updating. Section F discusses broader medical applications and world-model evaluation. 

Brain encoding models and benchmarks. Encoding models predict brain activity from stimulus features [21, 31]. Recent models trained on movie-fMRI datasets accu-rately predict stimulus-driven activity using video, audio, and language features [6, 7, 14]. Because they receive no neural observations at test time, they do not infer the in-dividual’s ongoing brain state and serve as stimulus-only references in BrainStateBench. Brain-Score [35] and Algo-nauts [13] assess alignment between model predictions and measured brain responses. BrainStateBench asks what a la-tent state contributes beyond the stimulus, including differ-ences between repeated trials and the benefit of new neural observations. fMRI history · 3 min

## 3. Spectator: A Linear Latent Brain World Model 

Spectator is a latent world model of naturalistic brain dy-namics (Fig. 2). An encoder compresses recent neural ob-servations into a two-timescale latent state, and a predictor evolves this state forward under incoming stimulus features. Rather than forecasting noisy fMRI directly, Spectator rolls out dynamics entirely in latent space, aligning predicted tra-jectories with future states inferred by a target encoder. 

3.1. Problem formulation 

Consider an individual experiencing a continuous naturalis-tic stimulus. At each discrete time step t, we observe neu-ral activity xt ∈ RD (whole-cortex fMRI parcellated into 

D = 1 ,000 regions [34]) alongside stimulus features at ex-tracted from the movie’s video, audio, and transcript by a pretrained multimodal encoder [7]. Cortical responses to naturalistic stimuli unfold across a hi-erarchy of timescales: sensory areas track immediate inputs within seconds, whereas higher-order regions integrate con-text over tens of seconds to minutes [20, 48]. To capture both regimes, the encoder constructs a two-timescale latent state st = [ ft; ut] comprising a fast state ft and a slow state 

ut. A fast network gϕ extracts ft from the m most recent observations, 

ft = gϕ(xt−m+1 , . . . , x t), (3) while a slow network hϕ summarizes fast-state history over a longer horizon of L steps: 

ut = hϕ(ft−L+1 , . . . , f t). (4) 

3.3. Residual latent predictor 

The predictor models latent state transitions via a residual update pθ conditioned on the current state and stimulus: 

ˆst+1 = ˆ st + pθ (ˆ st, ¯at+1 ), (5) where ¯at+1 concatenates stimulus features from recent steps up to t + 1 , allowing the predictor to account for the delay between stimulus onset and the recorded neural re-sponse (e.g., the hemodynamic lag in fMRI). During a roll-out, pθ advances the fast and slow states together, and hϕ is applied only inside the encoder. 

3.4. Two-stage training 

Training proceeds in two stages: observation-only initial-ization followed by joint-embedding latent prediction. 

Encoder pretraining. We first pretrain the encoder on neural observations alone to initialize informative state rep-resentations. Specifically, gϕ is trained alongside an aux-iliary predictor and observation readout—both discarded after pretraining—to forecast upcoming observations from the preceding m steps. We then fit hϕ so that the slow state 

ut predicts the mean of future fast states. 

Latent prediction. Next, we jointly train Eϕ and Pθ un-der a multi-step JEPA objective [1, 24], requiring open-loop state rollouts to match future inferred states. Starting from 

ˆst = st, the predictor unrolls K steps via Eq. (5) to produce 

ˆst+1 , . . . , ˆst+K , minimizing the multi-step squared error:

## 4. BrainStateBench: Probing Latent Brain State 

BrainStateBench evaluates brain world models across three core capabilities: inferring a latent state from neural obser-vations, predicting its stimulus-driven evolution, and updat-ing it as new observations arrive. All scores are computed on the model’s latent state against measured observations. 

4.1. Evaluation protocol 

Each evaluation window begins at an anchor step t0 of a recording. Given the individual’s neural observations up to 

t0 and the stimulus stream, the model rolls out predicted latent states over the next T = 40 steps (60 s in our fMRI setup). To compare architectures with distinct latent spaces on a common neural target [1, 2], BrainStateBench evaluates predicted latent states by linearly probing how much ob-served neural activity (fMRI) they explain. For each model and individual, we fit a linear probe from latent space to observation space on the training split and freeze it for all evaluations. ## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Setup 

Baselines. We compare Spectator against six baselines across four families: (i) TRIBE v2 [7] and MIRAGE [14], stimulus-only encoders without fMRI input; (ii) a per-individual Linear forecaster fitting independent ridge re-gressions per lead step from stimulus features and fMRI history; (iii) IPSID [41], a stimulus-driven linear state-space model with Kalman filtering; and (iv) prior brain world and forecasting models: NeuroWorld [10] (adapted with latent history tokens for multi-minute context) and 

BrainVista [49] (trained per individual using official code). Latent-state models (Spectator, NeuroWorld, IPSID) are read out via frozen per-individual linear probes (Sec. 4.1), while observation-space models are scored directly on their predicted fMRI (Sec. A.2). 

Implementation. Spectator trains a single shared encoder and predictor across individuals, using 180 s of fMRI his-tory by default, a 7.5 s ( m = 5 ) fast-state window, 1,024 -dimensional fast and slow states ( st ∈ R2,048 ), a 7.5 s stimulus window, and K = 40 -step (60 s) training rollouts (Tab. 5). In Updating , unobserved intermediate steps are imputed with the model’s own forecast before encoding. 

5.2. Main results 

Across multi-minute contexts, Spectator outperforms all baselines on every metric (Tab. 2), for every individual (Sec. B.4), and across all forecast lead times and observa-tion budgets (Sec. B.2). At 180 s, it reaches 16 .0% on Fore-cast and 20 .3% on Updating (+4 .3 and +6 .9 points over the strongest baseline; Sec. B.3), while more than doubling 

State (5.5% vs. 2.6% ), a lead that also holds for the cor-relation of trial-to-trial differences (Sec. B.5). Extending history from 7.5 s to 180 s boosts Spectator’s State fivefold (1.1% → 5.5% ), whereas baselines plateau or decline be-yond 120 s (Sec. B.1). Spectator also transfers under distribution shift to the HCP data (184 individuals), with a distinct scanner, sam-pling rate, and stimuli (Sec. B.6). Freezing the Algonauts fast encoder gϕ and fitting only the slow weights and linear predictor on 148 HCP training individuals, Spectator out-performs all baselines trained on HCP from scratch across all three tasks ( 6.6% , 6.2% , and 15 .7% ; Tab. 11) and on standard metrics (Tab. 12), with significant gains in most comparisons. Moreover, Spectator generalizes zero-shot to the remaining 36 unseen individuals: reading out its latent state with a single probe shared across the 148 training indi-viduals reaches 7.3% on Forecast and 16 .7% on Updating 

on their test recording without any calibration, matching ac-curacy on the training individuals (Sec. B.7). 

Inference efficiency. With 30 M parameters, Spectator rolls out 60 s in 5.4 ms, over 20 × faster than Transformer-based NeuroWorld and BrainVista (Sec. A.3). 

Data efficiency. Retrained end-to-end on nested data sub-sets, with just 10% of the episodes ( ≈ 4.7 h per individ-ual), Spectator already surpasses the full-data ( 46 .6 h) linear forecaster on State (3.6% vs. 2.6% ) and Updating (15 .5% 

vs. 12 .7% ) while matching its Forecast accuracy ( 11 .5% vs. 

11 .6% ). With 25% ( ≈ 11 .7 h), Spectator surpasses the full-data forecaster across all three tasks and continues scaling up to 100% (Sec. E.1). Section E.2 shows that Spectator’s forecasts also improve other models when used as training targets.

5.4. Ablations 

In Tab. 4, the learned slow state ut drives long-context performance: removing it drops Forecast by 4.6 points and State from 5.5% to 1.2% , reverting to 7.5 s accuracy (Tab. 2), while uniform 180 s averaging still loses 2.8 points. ## 6. Discussion 

Evaluating world models beyond prediction accuracy. 

World models are increasingly used in vision, medicine, and neuroscience for systems shaped by both internal state and external inputs, yet standard evaluations still rely on output prediction accuracy [37, 52]. When external inputs strongly drive the signal, a model can score well simply by predicting the shared response to the input without tracking internal state at all (Sec. 5.3). BrainStateBench addresses this with two simple tests that apply beyond neuroimaging: comparing repeated trials of the same input to isolate what the latent state captures ( State ), and providing new observa-tions during a rollout to test whether the model updates its predictions ( Updating ). 

Two-timescale latent prediction for interpretable world models. Spectator’s gains come from how it represents and evolves state over time rather than from complex non-linear architectures (Secs. 5.2 and 5.4). First, predicting in a shared latent space with a multi-step joint-embedding objective avoids fitting separate regressions at each future step to noisy fMRI, allowing strong generalization from un-der 5 hours instead of 47 hours of recordings per individ-ual (Secs. E.1 and 5.2). Second, splitting the state into fast and slow channels with learned multi-minute weights captures slow internal context where simply feeding longer histories into a Transformer plateaus. Third, combining rich pretrained stimulus features with linear latent dynam-ics outperforms deeper Transformer encoders and predic-tors (Sec. 5.4), keeps five-minute rollouts stable (Sec. C.2), and cleanly separates each forecast into exact latent-state and stimulus terms (Secs. 3.5 and 5.5). 

Individual brain states in a shared latent space. 

Stimulus-only encoding models predict an individual’s re-sponse from the stimulus, without inferring their ongoing state from neural observations [7, 13, 14]. Our results re-veal a predictable, multi-minute brain state that improves prediction in every cortical parcel, especially in prefrontal and parietal cortex, and is anchored in default-mode and control regions (Sec. 5.5). Default-mode regions integrate incoming narratives with an individual’s prior context [48]. Two individuals’ inferred states cannot be swapped at the same movie moment (Secs. 5.3 and 5.5), yet a single shared encoder and predictor track each individual’s own state tra-jectory, and the fast encoder trained on four Algonauts in-dividuals transfers across scanners and stimuli to 184 HCP individuals (Secs. B.6, B.7, 5.1 and 5.2). Different indi-viduals thus follow personal state trajectories that can be 8modeled within a common latent space. 

## 7. Conclusion 

Calling a model a world model makes a functional claim about its latent state: that it infers internal context from observations, evolves it under incoming inputs, and re-vises it as new measurements arrive. BrainStateBench tests these capabilities directly on whole-cortex fMRI, and Spectator shows that two-timescale linear dynamics over multi-minute histories track individual brain state accurately and efficiently across datasets. By separat-ing trial-specific state in association cortex from shared stimulus responses, this framework provides a founda-tion for linking inferred brain state to behavior and closed-loop stimulation.

Latent state and stimulus. At each lead step, we compute the variance of the state and stimulus components across anchor steps, sum across parcels, and normalize by their combined variance (excluding covariance). The initial-state share drops from 95% at 1.5 s to 13% at 60 s (Fig. 9a, matching Fig. 3a) as unforced state modes decay while incoming stimuli continually drive the rollout. 

Response to a brief input. We propagate a single-step stimulus perturbation along the principal input direction (maxi-mizing response norm under the summed five-lag input-to-parcel maps for one individual) through the input map, transition 

M , and linear probe. Averaging the 50 parcels with the largest peak absolute responses after peak-sign alignment and peak normalization yields a response that peaks near 3 s, crosses zero near 13 s, and shows a small negative undershoot (Fig. 9b), reflecting both the 5-step stimulus window and recurrent propagation. 

State decay times. Each eigenvalue λi of M defines a latent eigenmode with 1/e decay time τi = −∆t/ log |λi| (∆t =1.49 s). All 2,048 eigenvalues satisfy |λi| < 1 (maximum 0.9951 ), ensuring asymptotic stability of unforced rollouts, while 444 modes ( 21 .7% ) persist beyond 30 s (Fig. 9c) to sustain multi-minute context alongside stimulus-driven updates.

## F. Additional related work 

Medical world models. Medical imaging world models learn diagnostic representations [26, 51], guide ultrasound [50], simulate tumor progression [46], synthesize contrast MRI [23], and generate CT reports [39], evaluated via task accuracy, guidance error, Turing tests, image fidelity, or report metrics. For biosignals, Bao et al. [3] further call for explicit interven-tion conditioning and causal verification; complementing clinical tasks, we model and evaluate ongoing brain state under naturalistic stimulation. 

Latent prediction and world-model evaluation. Joint-embedding world models plan in latent space [2, 29, 38, 53]. High visual quality need not imply effective planning [37, 52], physical consistency [30], or accurate tracking of out-of-view state [27]. Unlike synthetic domains with ground-truth states [25, 40], brain states are unobserved, so BrainStateBench tests state inference through forecasting, repeated stimuli, and mid-rollout state updating. 24
