# PhyMind: Physics-Informed Masked Autoencoding for EEG Foundation Models

Abstract 

EEG foundation models are pre-trained almost exclusively with masked reconstruction through a free-form decoder, sometimes with hand-crafted spectral targets added, as in MANAS-2. We study what happens when that decoder is replaced by a generative forward model of EEG . PhyMind keeps a standard masked transformer encoder, but its decoder must explain the masked signal through 68 cortical regions, each driven by damped stochastic oscillators and an aperiodic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, projected to the recording’s own montage by a fixed boundary-element lead field evaluated per derivation. Training combines masked reconstruction with a Whittle spectral likelihood, an oscillator-dynamics residual, a minimum-norm source prior and a penalised neural residual. In a controlled comparison (same encoder, data, steps and seed), the physics decoder produces encoder tokens whose spectral content is far more linearly decodable than with a plain masked-autoencoder decoder or with the MANAS-2 objective: mean R2 on a token-level spectral probe with amplitude-normalised targets is 0.67/0.77/0.77 on three datasets, against 0.24/0.50/0.50 (MAE) and 0.48/0.67/0.66 (MANAS-2 objective), approaching the released 92.8M-parameter MANAS-2 (0.71/0.81/0.80). Downstream gains in the controlled setting are small (four-task mean balanced accuracy 56.6 vs. 53.8 for MAE frozen, 63.8 vs. 62.7 fine-tuned). Region-level features from the physics decoder raise frozen four-class motor-imagery accuracy by 7–14 points, so a 7.2M-parameter model pre-trained on 3k hours of open EEG is comparable to the released MANAS-2 with frozen features (63.0 vs. 61.6). After full fine-tuning, MANAS-2 remains better (66.8 vs. 65.4 for our best model). The inferred sources do not reproduce the lateralisation that a classical inverse solution finds in motor imagery, so we do not claim they are anatomically interpretable. Code and weights are released. 

## 1 Introduction 

Self-supervised pre-training has produced a family of EEG foundation models (BENDR [14], BIOT [30], LaBraM [11], CBraMod [27], REVE [20], MANAS-2 [15]). Most follow the masked-autoencoder recipe of vision [9]: hide some patches, encode the rest, and decode the hidden waveform with an unconstrained network. MANAS-2 adds six log band-power targets per patch (Raw-Band Hybrid, RBH) and a physics-motivated RMS continuity penalty at patch boundaries (ConRec). The generative process of scalp EEG is unusually well understood. Cortical current sources oscillate in characteristic bands on top of an aperiodic background [3], and they reach the electrodes through volume conduction, which is linear, instantaneous and computable from a head model [7, 8]. Reconstruction-trained embeddings have been reported to be dominated by the aperiodic component

We ask a narrow question: what does replacing the free-form decoder with a physical forward model change in the encoder? PhyMind’s decoder is a parametric, differentiable generative model of EEG (Fig. 1; a detailed architecture diagram is given in Fig. 2). The encoder must produce tokens from which a small network can infer cortical source dynamics that, after a fixed lead field, explain the masked channels and their spectra. We answer the question with a controlled comparison against a plain MAE decoder and the MANAS-2 objective, and we report a comparison with the released MANAS-2 for context, including where it is better. 

Contributions. 

• A physics-informed masked-autoencoding objective for EEG: a fixed BEM lead field evaluated per derivation (any montage or reference), oscillator and aperiodic source dynamics, a Whittle spectral likelihood, and a constrained neural residual. • A controlled comparison of MAE, the MANAS-2 objective (RBH + ConRec) and PhyMind with identical encoder, data, sampler, steps and seed, under frozen probing, full fine-tuning and a token-level spectral probe whose targets are normalised so that models with per-channel-normalised input are not penalised. • The main empirical finding: the physics decoder makes spectral structure markedly more linearly accessible in encoder tokens (mean R2 +0.27 to +0.43 over MAE, +0.10 to +0.19 over the MANAS-2 objective, without any band-power target), while downstream accuracy gains are small. • Region-level source features that improve frozen motor-imagery decoding, together with a negative result: the sources do not localise motor imagery the way a classical minimum-norm inverse with the same lead field does. • An exact, padding-aware re-implementation of the released MANAS-2 encoder that loads its weights strictly (bit-identical outputs), used for evaluation and for a retrofit experiment (PhyMind-M2), which is negative for classification. 

## 2 Related work 

EEG foundation models. BENDR [14] used contrastive learning; BIOT [30] tokenises channels independently; LaBraM [11] predicts neural codes from a learned spectral tokenizer; CBraMod [27] uses criss-cross spatial/temporal attention; REVE [20] introduced a 4-D Fourier positional encoding of electrode position and time and pre-trains a masked autoencoder on more than 60k hours. MANAS-2 [15] adopts this positional encoding and adds the RBH and ConRec targets. DeeperBrain [26] uses distance-based channel embeddings and neuro-statistical targets. BrainOmni [29] learns latent “sources” shared by EEG and MEG, but they are not tied to a lead field. To our knowledge none of these decodes through a biophysical forward model. 

Physics and EEG. Neural-mass models [10] combined with lead fields underpin supervised source imaging (DeepSIF [24]). NeuroDyn-EEG [1] couples a Jansen–Rit model with a lead field, but trains on simulations. PINR-ESI [32] fits a lead-field-constrained implicit representation per recording. ## 3 Method 

3.1 Tokenisation and encoder 

Recordings are band-passed (0.5–45 Hz), resampled to 200 Hz, average-referenced when unipolar, and cut into 10 s windows. Each window is divided by a single robust scale (median absolute deviation) shared across channels . Per-channel z-scoring, used by MANAS-2 and most other models, would destroy the relative channel amplitudes the lead field predicts. A channel is represented by its derivation (a, b ): an anode and either a cathode or “average reference”, both indexed into the 343-electrode 10-05 system. Each 1 s patch is linearly embedded. A Fourier embedding of the electrode coordinates (the anode–cathode midpoint and difference for bipolar channels) and a learned time embedding are added, and a pre-norm transformer encodes all channel ×patch tokens jointly with a key-padding mask, so channel counts can vary within a batch. 

Masking. We mask 50% of valid tokens. Selection is block-structured: random tokens, whole time patches (25% probability) and whole channels (20%). Masking whole channels forces the model to predict a channel from its neighbours, which is exactly the problem that volume conduction, and hence the lead field, solves.

Source space. We use the 68 Desikan–Killiany regions [2] on the FreeSurfer fsaverage tem-plate [4]. A two-layer transformer trunk processes the full token grid (visible tokens plus mask 3tokens). Learned region queries, augmented with a Fourier embedding of each region’s centroid, then cross-attend to the trunk output and produce one token per (region, patch).

3.4 PhyMind-M2: physics decoder on the MANAS-2 encoder 

We re-implemented the MANAS-2 encoder (22 layers, d=512, 8 heads, 200-sample patches with stride 180, 4-D Fourier positional encoding, and the GEGLU variant used in the release) with a key-padding mask. It loads the released weights strictly; on unpadded input its outputs match the release exactly (max. abs. difference 0.0), and padding changes outputs by less than 10 −5. MANAS-2 

z-scores each channel, so we feed it z-scored visible samples and add a zero-initialised projection of each channel’s log standard deviation. This is intended to restore the amplitude information that the lead field needs. The 11 stride-180 tokens are interpolated to the decoder’s 10 one-second patches. Pre-training data. All data are openly available. Phase 1 uses 621 h: CHB-MIT [6, 23] (bipolar, 23 channels), a subset of Sleep-EDF cassette [12] (2 bipolar channels), and four OpenNeuro [17] resting-state and oddball datasets (ds002778, ds003478, ds003490, ds004584; 32–64 channels). The final runs (lite, large, M2) use 2,959 h from the same sources with all of Sleep-EDF cassette. The three dataset groups (CHB-MIT, Sleep-EDF, OpenNeuro) are sampled with equal total mass, so the small OpenNeuro group is heavily over-sampled relative to its size. At most 32 channels are drawn per window, and channels are randomly dropped (probability 0.2). 

Models. Pre-norm transformers with drop-path 0.1. lite: d=256, 6 layers (4.9M encoder parame-ters, 7.2M with decoder); base: d=384, 12 layers; large: d=512, 16 layers (50.8M / 59.6M). AdamW (lr 3 × 10 −4, weight decay 0.05, cosine schedule), bf16, batch 128, one GPU. Phase 1 base models (MAE, RBH, v1, v2, v2-H) train for 15k steps with seed 0; lite and large train for 20k and 30k steps. The MAE baseline uses the same encoder with a transformer decoder that regresses masked patches. RBH adds MANAS-2’s six log band-power targets (weight 0.5) and ConRec ( q=32, λ=3). There is one pre-training seed per model. 

Downstream tasks. 7 Limitations 

• Scale and seeds. One pre-training seed per model, a corpus of 0.6–3k h, and test splits of 7 (EEGMAT) and 18 (ADFTD) subjects. Differences of a few points on these tasks are within noise; we do not have per-window predictions for a paired subject-level bootstrap. 

• Selection. PhyMind variants were iterated on the evaluation tasks (Sec. 4). The controlled baseline comparison predates this, but the lite/large results may be optimistically biased. 

• Scaling. Base, lite and large differ in corpus, steps and objective version, so they do not form a clean scaling study. 

• Physics. The lead field is a template head, the inverse problem is under-determined ( ≤64 channels, 68 regions), regional spectra are assumed independent, and the inferred sources do not localise motor imagery (Sec. 6.1). 

• Comparison with MANAS-2. We evaluate the released weights through our verified re-implementation with our own preprocessing (0.5–45 Hz, bipolar channels placed at the anode), which may differ from its training pipeline. ## 5 Results 

5.1 Spectral content of encoder tokens 

Table 3 and Fig. 3 (right) show the clearest effect of the physics decoder. With encoder, data, steps and seed fixed, PhyMind-base v2 reaches mean R2 0.67/0.77/0.77 on EEGMAT/MI/ADFTD, against 0.24/0.50/0.50 for MAE and 0.48/0.67/0.66 for the MANAS-2 objective (RBH), although RBH receives band power as an explicit target and v2 does not. The gain is broad: on EEGMAT, 

β and γ power, the aperiodic exponent and periodic alpha all improve by 0.2–0.8 over MAE. The strength of the spectral likelihood matters: v1, which applies the Whittle term only on masked tokens with weight 0.2, lies between MAE and RBH (0.40/0.59/0.60). The released MANAS-2 remains slightly ahead of v2 (0.71/0.81/0.80) with a much larger model and corpus. Lite and large exceed it (0.80–0.88), but they were trained with a band-power head on encoder tokens, so their probe numbers are largely a consequence of direct supervision and are reported for completeness only.

5.3 Source features 

The pre-declared “+ src” features help most on four-class motor imagery with frozen encoders: from 33.2 to 47.0 (lite) and from 36.1 to 45.2 (large), against 40.5 for the released MANAS-2; these gains exceed the split sd ( ≤1 point) by a wide margin. On the other tasks the effect is within noise, and for the phase-1 base models it is small or absent. After full fine-tuning the source features add at most 1.6 points (lite, MI), because the encoder itself can adapt. 

5.4 Comparison with the released MANAS-2

5.5 Physics decoder on MANAS-2 (PhyMind-M2) 

Continuing pre-training of the released MANAS-2 encoder on our 2,959 h for 20k steps is negative for classification. With the RBH objective (control), the frozen four-task mean rises from 61.6 to 62.2; with the physics decoder it falls to 60.0 (60.5 + src), mainly on MI (37.9). We hypothesise that this is a normalisation mismatch: MANAS-2’s encoder consumes per-channel z-scored input, which removes the relative channel amplitudes the lead field explains, and our log-scale side channel restores them only partially. We have not tested this hypothesis directly. 

## 6 Analysis 

6.1 Do the sources localise? 

As a sanity check we contrasted right- and left-fist imagery on PhysioNet MI (Fig. 4). At the sensors the expected contralateral alpha/beta desynchronisation is present (C3 t=−5.5, C4 t=+6 .5), and a classical wMNE inverse with the same lead field recovers it in source space (precentral t=−6.5left / +4 .4 right; postcentral −7.3 / +6 .4). PhyMind-lite’s inferred sources do not (precentral 

−0.03 / −1.1; postcentral −2.1 / −0.05). The decoder’s 68 “regions” therefore behave as a learned, lead-field-constrained basis that carries task information (Sec. 5.3), not as anatomically faithful source estimates. We do not claim anatomical interpretability.

6.3 Learned oscillators 

In v1 the oscillator slots separate into roughly delta, theta, beta and gamma frequencies without supervision (Fig. 5). With the stronger Whittle weight and the band head (lite, large) they collapse to 1.7–6 Hz, so the “four oscillators per region” of the final models are not band-specific; the encoder and band head carry the higher-frequency information. A per-slot frequency prior would be needed to keep the slots separated.

## 8 Conclusion 

Replacing the free-form decoder of an EEG masked autoencoder with a lead-field forward model driven by oscillator and aperiodic source dynamics substantially increases how much spectral struc-ture is linearly decodable from encoder tokens, compared with plain masked reconstruction and with the MANAS-2 objective under identical conditions. This does not translate into large downstream gains: in controlled comparisons the physics objective is on par or slightly better, its region-level features help frozen motor-imagery decoding, and small PhyMind models are competitive with the released MANAS-2 when frozen but behind it after fine-tuning. The inferred sources are useful features but not valid localisations. Larger corpora, multiple seeds, and a mechanism that keeps sources anatomically faithful are the natural next steps. 11 Figure 4: Right- minus left-fist motor imagery, PhysioNet MI (paired t over subjects). Left: classical depth-weighted minimum-norm estimate (wMNE) with the same lead field. Middle: PhyMind-lite source power. Right: hemispheric contrast for sensorimotor regions. wMNE shows the expected contralateral pattern; PhyMind’s sources do not. • Selection. PhyMind variants were iterated on the evaluation tasks (Sec. 4). The controlled baseline comparison predates this, but the lite/large results may be optimistically biased. • Scaling. Base, lite and large differ in corpus, steps and objective version, so they do not form a clean scaling study. • Physics. The lead field is a template head, the inverse problem is under-determined ( ≤64 channels, 68 regions), regional spectra are assumed independent, and the inferred sources do not localise motor imagery (Sec. 6.1). • Comparison with MANAS-2. We evaluate the released weights through our verified re-implementation with our own preprocessing (0.5–45 Hz, bipolar channels placed at the anode), which may differ from its training pipeline. Our numbers for MANAS-2 on mental arithmetic and sleep staging are close to those on its model card.
