## Abstract 

Cross-subject brain-to-image retrieval aims to identify viewed images from unseen subjects’ brain signals. How-ever, inter-subject variability hinders alignment of brain– image representations, while independent query ranking tends to concentrate predictions on a few candidates, mak-ing accurate retrieval difficult without target labels. We pro-pose STAGE (Structured Test-Time Adaptation with Global Entropic Soft-coupling), a structured test-time adaptation framework for recovering cross-subject alignment from un-certain brain–image correspondences. Moment calibration reduces statistical discrepancies between features, while coupling-weighted initialization establishes initial align-ment. Coupling-guided adaptation further refines residual feature mismatches, and soft-coupling ranking coordinates candidate competition across queries to prevent overly con-centrated matching assignments. Using only unlabeled tar-get batches, STAGE maintains consistent matching from feature calibration to image retrieval while keeping both encoders frozen. Experiments on three public datasets val-idate STAGE’s retrieval performance. On THINGS-EEG2, STAGE achieves 68.25% Top-1 and 90.47% Top-5, improv-ing Top-1 by 18.52 percentage points over SCORE recovery and CSLS on identical frozen NICE features. Calibrated on 200 queries and a 200-image gallery covering their identi-ties, STAGE retains 42.05% Top-1 when ranking 1,854 im-ages with the transform frozen.

Related work reconstructs images from EEG [1, 16] and fMRI [20, 27], with diffusion mod-els providing strong image priors [2, 21, 29]. Existing contrastive learning methods align brain signals with pre-trained image representations, enabling retrieval of unseen concepts and image reconstruction [14, 19, 25, 28]. How-ever, subject-specific models rely on extensive paired data, limiting their scalability. To reduce calibration costs for 1arXiv:2611.29538v1 [cs.CV] 22 Nov 2026 new users, recent studies have explored cross-subject trans-fer [26, 31] and test-time adaptation, which adapts pre-trained models using unlabeled target data [30]. Further efforts have investigated adaptation strategies based on em-beddings and retrieval scores [5, 13]. Nevertheless, inter-subject distribution shifts hinder brain–image representa-tion alignment, while independent query ranking tends to concentrate retrieval results on a small subset of candidates. Fig. 1(a) illustrates the leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) evaluation setting, where a brain encoder trained on the re-maining subjects retrieves images for a held-out subject. During test-time adaptation only unlabeled target batches and the image gallery are available; both encoders remain frozen, and source data are inaccessible. Robust cross-subject representation alignment and global retrieval with-out source data or target labels is therefore a key challenge. Inter-subject distribution shifts increase the uncertainty of the initial cross-modal correspondences [5], and exist-ing methods use mutual nearest neighbor (MNN) match-ing to select landmarks [4, 5]. As shown in the upper part of Fig. 1(b), MNN excludes non-mutual pairs that may be-come informative as alignment improves. When geometric estimation relies on a fixed initial landmark set, subsequent correspondence evidence also remains unused. Thus, pre-serving soft correspondence evidence across all pairs and iteratively updating both the correspondences and the ge-ometric transform are important for reliable cross-modal alignment. Independent query ranking concentrates multiple queries on a few candidates, leading to hubness [23]; as shown in the lower part of Fig. 1(b), this leaves other candidates un-derutilized. Density correction adjusts scores without con-straining total candidate mass [4, 5, 13], and query-aware Sinkhorn normalization balances assignments using uni-form marginals [22] but may over-allocate weights to un-supported candidates in expanded galleries. Thus, retrieval requires global query coordination and adaptive assignment based on batch–gallery composition. To address these challenges, we propose STAGE (Struc-tured Test-Time Adaptation with Global Entropic Soft-coupling), which uses global entropic soft-coupling (GES) to recover cross-subject alignment from uncertain corre-spondences and coordinate candidate competition. As out-lined in Fig. 1(c), moment calibration first aligns feature means and scales. Coupling-weighted initialization then alternates soft correspondence estimation and geometric alignment, incorporating initially excluded pairs as align-ment improves. Building on this geometry, coupling-guided adaptation uses refreshed soft teacher targets to refine resid-ual mismatch through a constrained transform while limit-ing feature drift. Finally, soft-coupling ranking coordinates candidate competition at fixed geometry, allowing weakly supported candidates to remain below capacity in expanded galleries. Both encoders and image embeddings remain frozen throughout. Our main contributions are: • STAGE, a structured test-time adaptation framework us-ing global entropic soft-coupling to recover cross-subject alignment and coordinate candidate competition, en-abling unseen-category image retrieval without target la-bels, encoder retraining, or source data access. • A coupling-guided alignment strategy that alternates soft correspondence estimation and geometric recovery, then refines the recovered features with refreshed soft teacher targets. Joint ranking coordinates candidate competition to limit matching-mass concentration. • Evaluation on three EEG and MEG benchmarks with multiple encoders and varying gallery sizes, retrieving images for unseen subjects from unlabeled target data and frozen encoders. 

## 2. Method 

2.1. Problem Setup 

At test time, cross-subject brain–image retrieval receives an unlabeled batch of neural responses from one target sub-ject, a known image gallery, and two encoders trained on source subjects and not modified afterwards; the task is to rank the gallery for every query of that subject. Let 

Zb ∈ RN ×d be the brain embeddings of the N queries of that subject and Zv ∈ RM ×d the image embeddings of the 

M gallery candidates, where d is the embedding dimension. The embeddings Zb are re-estimated; the final score matrix 

S ∈ RN ×M is computed from the re-estimated Zb and Zv ,its row i giving the ranks of the M candidates for query i.

2.2. Framework Overview 

STAGE recovers alignment from uncertain correspon-dences before coordinating candidate competition, through four consecutive test-time steps in Fig. 2. Moment calibra-tion (Step 1) corrects feature means and scales to make the two embedding sets comparable. Coupling-weighted ini-tialization (Step 2) alternates soft correspondence estima-tion and rigid alignment, allowing pairs excluded by an initial landmark selection to contribute as geometry im-proves. Coupling-guided adaptation (Step 3) refines the initialized features through a constrained similarity trans-form, using refreshed soft targets to preserve correspon-dence uncertainty during refinement. Soft-coupling rank-ing (Step 4) coordinates the final candidate scores across queries at fixed geometry. Steps 2–4 use global entropic soft-coupling (GES) for distinct purposes: pair weights for recovery, teacher and student targets for refinement, and joint ranking weights. 3.1. Setup 

Protocol. We evaluate 200-way brain-to-image retrieval on THINGS-EEG2 [8], THINGS-MEG from THINGS-data [11] and Alljoined-1.6M [33], using their released splits and the standard leave-one-subject-out protocol: each fold holds out one subject, the base is trained on the re-maining subjects, and at test time the pipeline adapts to the held-out subject from unlabelled responses and the known candidate set, with both encoders frozen. THINGS-EEG2’s ten subjects, THINGS-MEG’s four held-out subjects and Alljoined-1.6M’s twenty held-out subjects each run three seeds (seed 0/1/2). Top-1 is primary and Top-5 secondary. We average seeds within subjects, then across subjects. We jointly fit and rank closed-set batches with one query per candidate identity; preprocessing and trial averaging are de-tailed in Supplementary Section A. 

Training and deployment. After concept-disjoint epoch selection, we retrain on all source training data and re-port the final checkpoint. 3.4. Mechanism analysis 

A capacity bound against forced uniform mass. The gallery-expansion results test how joint ranking handles ad-ditional candidates. Both rules use alternating scaling [6], but standard Sinkhorn requires equal column mass, whereas capacities allow columns to remain below their bounds. For 

M > N , capacities avoid forcing equal mass onto unsup-ported candidates, explaining their advantage over equal-mass ranking in Fig. 3. 

Candidate competition at fixed geometry. To explain the within-row gains in Table 4, Fig. 5(a) varies only the coupling constraints on one frozen final cost matrix. ## A. Data, Evaluation Protocols, and Implemen-tation 

Evaluation population. The mechanism analyses use frozen NICE brain encoders [28] and frozen visual fea-tures under the source-training protocol of Jiang et al. [14]. THINGS-EEG2 has ten held-out subjects and three source-model seeds (0, 1 and 2); THINGS-MEG has four subjects at seed 0; Alljoined-1.6M has twenty subjects at seed 0. Section H extends MEG and Alljoined to all three source seeds at their main stage budgets. Each closed-set evalua-tion uses all 200 queries and all 200 official candidates. Ta-bles abbreviate the datasets as EEG2, MEG and AJ. Neither target identities nor their pair labels enter fitting. All rank-ing comparisons within a feature condition reuse its saved pairwise costs. 

Neural inputs and query construction. Repeated test re-sponses to the same image are averaged in signal space be-fore the frozen brain encoder. Thus, each held-out subject contributes 200 image-specific averaged responses, yield-ing 200 query embeddings. Table S1 specifies the signal inputs. EEG2 averages twenty repetitions from each of four sessions; MEG averages one response per test image from each of twelve sessions. For Alljoined, trials marked as dropped in the released metadata are excluded, and all re-maining responses to each image are averaged across ses-sions. Its repetition count therefore varies with the retained trials. The EEG inputs retain the baseline correction and multivariate noise normalization (MVNN) of their prepro-cessed data; MEG uses the released preprocessed epochs cropped to the post-stimulus interval. 

Source-model training. We follow the source-training framework of SAMGA [14], replacing its brain-signal en-coder with NICE [28] for the main results while retaining its visual branch and alignment procedure. Each LOSO fold learns from paired brain responses and images of the source subjects. The brain encoder produces a 1,024-dimensional feature, which a linear projector maps to 512 dimensions. A shared 512 → 512 linear map is applied to both modalities. After source training, the brain encoder, projectors, shared map and visual fusion weights are frozen; STAGE operates on their embeddings. 

Visual features and fusion. The frozen InternViT-6B-448px-V2.5 backbone [3] provides 3,200-dimensional CLS-token features from blocks 20, 24, 28, 32 and 36 (zero-based indices). Images are resized with bicubic interpo-lation to a shortest side of 448 pixels, center-cropped to 

448 × 448 , and normalized with ImageNet channel statis-tics. A shared 3, 200 → 1, 024 linear projection and five layer-specific 1, 024 → 512 projections precede SAMGA’s subject-aware layer fusion and the shared map. The pro-jections and fusion weights are learned on source subjects. At test time, fusion uses its learned global weights without a target-subject embedding. Image augmentation and text supervision are disabled. 

Source optimization. Table S2 summarizes the source-training settings. During epochs 1–20, the loss com-bines maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), which aligns the two feature distributions, with symmetric brain–image contrastive learning, which preserves image correspon-dence. The MMD weight decreases linearly from 0.9 to 0.5, with the complementary weight assigned to the contrastive loss. MMD averages Gaussian kernels with bandwidths 

{0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2}. During epochs 21–50, the shared map is frozen and the remaining trainable modules use the con-trastive loss alone. The contrastive logits normalize image features but leave brain-feature norms unchanged. Source-signal augmentation applies temporal smoothing with ker-nel size five and probability 0.3. The final checkpoint at epoch 50 supplies the frozen embeddings. 

Concept-disjoint validation and epoch selection. We partition only the official training set by image concept, us-ing random seed 42 to assign its 1,654 concepts to 1,489 training concepts and 165 validation concepts (approxi-mately 9:1). All images and associated brain responses be-longing to the same concept remain in one partition, and all subjects use the same concept split. Within each LOSO fold, only source-subject data enter this procedure. We train on the internal training partition and use validation Top-1 to select the number of epochs for both source-training phases: MMD plus contrastive learning, followed by contrastive learning alone. Once the two epoch budgets are fixed, we reinitialize the trainable source-model components and train from scratch on the complete official training set, including the internal validation concepts, using the fixed schedule. The final checkpoint supplies the frozen embeddings.
