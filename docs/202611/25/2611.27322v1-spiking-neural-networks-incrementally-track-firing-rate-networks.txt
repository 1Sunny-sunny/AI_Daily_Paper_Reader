# ABSTRACT 

Understanding how spiking neural networks (SNNs) efficiently process data streams is key to (i) studying how biological brains conserve energy for lifelong predictions and (ii) enabling efficient continuous AI inference. In this work, we propose a theory on how an SNN incrementally tracks its corresponding firing-rate network, a continuous-valued neural network (ANN). We prove that the output of a discrete SNN continuously tracks the instantaneous ANN output implicitly determined by the SNN input. Based on this principle, we develop LIF-Stream, a conversion framework for efficient SNN inference over unbounded data streams. It enables the converted SNN to reuse its prior activation state and compute only the activation updates between frames, thereby reducing the average number of time steps needed per frame. Specifically, LIF-Stream controls the SNN to continuously track the reference ANN output, which gives a high-accuracy prediction for ev-ery frame. Evaluation on three large-scale streaming inference benchmarks with distinct modalities shows that LIF-Stream achieves the best efficiency-accuracy trade-off compared to high-accuracy SNN techniques. Code will be released at 

https://github.com/snuhcs/LIF-Stream .

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Understanding how biological brains efficiently process continuous sensory streams throughout their lifetimes can advance the design of efficient, always-on AI systems. Brains save metabolic energy on static scenes while selectively scaling cognitive compute on surprising events (Attwell & Laughlin, 2001; Friston, 2010). Spiking neural networks (SNNs) computationally model these biological neural systems to (i) study their underlying information processing mechanisms and (ii) implement extremely energy-efficient AI systems with neuromorphic hardware (Roy et al., 2019). SNNs are networks of spiking neurons that mimic neuronal dynamics and communicate through binary spikes (Maass, 1996b; Gerstner & Kistler, 2002). Since they fundamentally process the time series of binary events (spike trains) in a biophysical manner, they are naturally expected to process streaming data efficiently. However, existing high-accuracy SNN techniques, including direct SNN training and ANN-to-SNN conversion methods, overlook the need to leverage historical states to efficiently process new inputs, especially for unbounded streams. We first derive a theoretical principle governing the transient dynamics of discrete SNNs, which underlies how an SNN can efficiently process information through time. It is challenging to analyze the discrete SNNs since they are discontinuous dynamical systems. We overcome this by extending the ANN-to-SNN conversion theory (Rueckauer et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2021; Oh & Lee, 2024) to the transient regimes through the lens of reference tracking. Specifically, we prove that SNN consisting of discrete LIF neurons (LIF-SNN) continuously attracts toward the instantaneous output of its corresponding continuous-valued clipped-ReLU network (ANN). This ANN is a firing-rate network (Gerstner et al., 2014): its activations are the targets of the exponentially filtered firing rates of the SNN neurons. Thus, the ANN output is a time-varying reference target that SNN dynamics tracks. LIF-SNN dynamics is a damped fixed-point iteration with a constant step size that permanently tracks this target. For static inputs, it oscillates within the small neighborhood of the static equilibrium. Although SNNs with discrete Integrate-and-Fire (IF) neurons match the ANN output exactly at steady 1arXiv:2611.27322v1 [cs.NE] 20 Nov 2026 Preprint state, they cannot guarantee tracking of a changing input, because their step sizes decay through time and their membranes accumulate charge debt (Sec. 3). Based on our theory, we introduce LIF-Stream , a conversion framework for efficient SNN inference over unbounded data streams, in which the SNN incrementally tracks its continuous-valued ANN counterpart across an unbounded input stream, producing a prediction for every frame. Concretely, LIF-Stream is a two-time-scale SNN control process: The outer loop continuously encodes the input stream to implicitly drive the reference ANN for accurate frame-wise prediction. The inner loop allocates a few time steps per frame for the SNN to continuously track this reference ANN output, which is a time-varying target, while retaining its states across frame boundaries. The framework is a recipe into which any frame-wise ReLU1 ANN can be plugged, configured only by the leak τ , the number of ticks (SNN time steps) per frame T , the input encoder, and an optional warm-up. LIF-Stream efficiently processes streams, since SNN retains prior activation values and incrementally computes to close the gap between ANN and SNN activations. For streams where consecutive frames are similar, SNNs need only a few ticks per frame on average to maintain high frame-wise accuracy. This finding challenges the common belief by showing that ANN-to-SNN conversion can be as efficient as direct training methods on streaming data without specialized training or fine-tuning. For such incremental computation to persist indefinitely, we argue that ANN-to-SNN conversion techniques should pivot from IF to LIF neuron. While the IF neuron’s time-diminishing step size and charge debt prevent it from tracking continuously, the LIF neuron’s constant step size ensures permanent and autonomous alertness. We comprehensively evaluate our framework across diverse streaming inference tasks spanning vision, audio, and neuromorphic (event-stream) benchmarks. Comparisons with state-of-the-art SNN tech-niques show that LIF-Stream generally achieves the best efficiency-accuracy trade-off. LIF-Stream is significantly more tick-efficient than conversion techniques, while surpassing directly trained SNNs in peak accuracy with comparable low-tick accuracy. Furthermore, LIF-Stream is natively compatible with standard digital neuromorphic hardware out-of-the-box, as it operates without post-training model calibration, customized neuronal dynamics, or non-binary spike communication. In-depth analyses demonstrate that this tick efficiency stems from our incremental computing approach, which naturally exploits small inter-frame prediction deltas by computing incremental state updates rather than full activations.

# 3 CONTROLLING SNN S TO EFFICIENTLY PROCESS DATA STREAMS 

In the last section, we discovered a core principle governing the transient dynamics of SNN. We now leverage this principle to establish a framework that processes data streams accurately and efficiently with SNNs. Specifically, Theorem 2.2 shows that SNN constantly updates its output a step towards its corresponding ANN output. We control the dynamics of SNN using this governing principle so that SNN continuously tracks its corresponding ANN as a reference model. Our LIF-Stream framework is a two-time-scale control that continuously adapts SNN to its time-varying reference model. It consists of (i) an inner loop, in which the SNN output closely tracks the output of the reference ANN model, and (ii) an outer loop, in which the reference ANN continuously evolves its output given input stream. Simply put, we continuously tune the reference ANN, which is easier to train and stably infers, to robustly control the unstable and discontinuous SNN computation. Figure 2 (left) illustrates the concept of our framework. SNN continuously updates its output to adapt to a new reference ANN output. For continuous, temporally correlated streams, the activations change gradually rather than abruptly. When the prior SNN state is already close to the target ANN output, and the reference ANN output changes minimally, the SNN requires only a small number of time steps to process a new frame. Hence, SNN naturally exploits the temporal redundancy without sacrificing accuracy, whereas conventional DNN inference should fully recompute regardless of temporal correlation. 

Perpetual Inference Incremental computation is most effective when it can operate continuously without external reset or intervention. However, SNN inference systems frequently employ global reset that re-initialize all the neuronal states each frame to start with a clean slate. For our framework, this introduces three key drawbacks: (i) it completely discards the prior states that SNNs rely on for streaming efficiency, (ii) SNNs struggle to recover the accuracy after the reset, and (iii) it triggers global synchronization, which makes real-time systems less responsive to sudden events. We observe that LIF neurons should be used instead of IF neurons to make neural computation perpetually incremental. Prior approaches using IF neuron (or its variants) are fundamentally limited because IF-SNNs have a time-diminishing step size . The number of time steps (i.e., ticks) to track the reference model scales linearly over time, and thus it freezes after a few frames. Thus, in practice the IF conversions we compare against reset every frame. While recent IF-based conversion techniques show high accuracy in ultra-low ticks, they are only effective in the first few ticks after global reset. In contrast, LIF neuron’s time-constant step size makes the SNN permanently alert. LIF-SNN can continuously track the reference model’s trajectory closely without any intervention. It is also naturally robust to long time horizon and scene cuts in input streams. Furthermore, the IF neuron cannot guarantee continuous tracking under any decoder (including rate coding) because of charge debt : negative input cannot leave an IF membrane as spikes and piles up as a debt that silences the neuron for a time proportional to the history. We prove in Appendix B.5.4 that no decoder gives an IF neuron a lag bound that holds over a stream, whereas leak lets the debt of an LIF neuron fade and keeps its tracking error bounded (Corollary 2.4). 

Initial Warm-up. SNNs can exhibit a cold start phenomenon: a temporary initial accuracy drop where the SNN output y(t) requires several ticks to warm up from y(0) to match the reference target 

y∗(T1). This can be problematic in real-world scenarios when an agent should wake up fast and react. To resolve this, we dynamically schedule more time-steps solely for the first frame, e.g., 32 ticks in Figure 2 (right). This design preserves both initial accuracy and a low average-case tick budget.

# 4 EVALUATION 

To demonstrate generalizability, we extensively evaluate our framework across three distinct stream processing modalities, including vision, audio, and event streams. We benchmark against ANN-to-SNN conversion and direct SNN training baselines (see Appendix D for implementation details). 

Streaming Object Detection. We assess streaming detection by zero-shot transferring models pretrained frame-by-frame on MS COCO (Lin et al., 2014b) directly to the Argoverse-HD bench-mark (Li et al., 2020b). COCO provides large-scale object detection pretraining across 80 classes, while Argoverse-HD provides high-frame-rate (30 FPS) egocentric video sequences annotated at 2D bounding-box level across eight road-object overlapping categories (24 validation streams, 15,062 frames). We report frame-wise mAP@50 evaluated sequentially over streaming frames. For conver-sion baselines, we adapt YOLOv8 (Jocher et al., 2023) for SNN conversion (60.7% AP50 on COCO, ReLU1). Full experimental setups are in Appendix E. 

Streaming Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR). We train and evaluate streaming ASR on the LibriSpeech dataset ( ∼960 hours of speech across 3–15 s clips) (Panayotov et al., 2015). To simulate a real-time streaming environment, audio signals are processed sequentially in small, fixed-size chunks rather than feeding full utterances simultaneously. Candidate transcriptions are served at a low latency every 33 ms to mirror real-time serving pipelines, with final output decisions committed every 1.28 s to ensure prediction stability. Accuracy is measured via Word Error Rate (WER). We base our conversion backbone on an adapted SummaryMixing architecture (Parcollet et al., 2024) (7.62% WER on LibriSpeech); Detailed model and training specifications are provided in Appendix F. 

Gesture Recognition on Event Streams. We benchmark streaming event processing using the standard DVS-Gesture dataset (1,342 event recordings across 11 gesture classes captured by a DAVIS240 sensor) (Amir et al., 2017). In contrast to offline clip-level classification, we evaluate online streaming inference: the model must continuously feed event streams and classify gestures using only the event history received so far. The task involves 12 target classes, including 11 gesture categories and an additional background (NULL) class. Performance is measured by frame-wise classification accuracy. As a conversion backbone, we adapt GestureNet (Massa et al., 2020) (a TCN-based model reaching 91.1% streaming accuracy). Complete setup details are in Appendix G. 

Results. Table 1 shows that LIF-Stream generally achieves the best efficiency-accuracy trade-off among SNN techniques: (i) it is substantially more tick-efficient than conversion methods while maintaining peak accuracy, and (ii) it achieves higher peak accuracy with competitive low-tick performance compared to direct training methods. Prior conversion approaches degrade sharply in streaming tasks at low tick budgets ( T < 8). Conversely, while direct training performs well at ultra-low ticks, its peak accuracy remains constrained, and it fails to train on complex backbones matching conversion baselines beyond DVS-Gesture (e.g., streaming object detection and ASR). Importantly, LIF-Stream is natively compatible with standard digital neuromorphic hardware sup-porting soft reset, as it operates out-of-the-box without post-training model calibration, customized neuronal dynamics, or non-binary spike communication. These structural properties also preserve the full ANN-level peak accuracy ceiling of our method. Prior conversion methods frequently sac-rifice peak accuracy to compensate for low-tick latency (via parameter altering (Li et al., 2021) or discontinuous activation functions (Bu et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023)). Similarly, direct SNN training suffers from an inherent accuracy gap with ANNs due to discontinuous spiking mechanisms and surrogate gradient approximations, which trade exactness for smoothness. This tick-efficiency stems from our incremental computing approach. Streaming inference tasks frequently exhibit high inter-frame similarity, which LIF-Stream exploits to reduce the average computational cost required for the SNN to track the reference ANN prediction. Consequently, our framework achieves high accuracy with low frame-wise ticks. Qualitative Evaluation. Figure 5 illustrates the streaming inference trajectory to highlight how LIF-Stream efficiently handles consecutive frames unlike reset-based frame-wise approaches. Inference is evaluated on a stream from Argoverse-HD, featuring complex egocentric driving scenes in contrast to the static person-detection scenarios in Figure 2. Reset-based methods reinitialize their hidden states to the origin at every frame, requiring them to cover a significantly longer distance in state space per frame. In contrast, LIF-Stream computes small state updates between the prior state and the new target prediction, effectively exploiting inter-frame prediction deltas. As a result, it converges faster with fewer ticks. Additionally, our initial warm-up mechanism positions the SNN state near the ANN output at the first frame, effectively preventing early mispredictions. 

Compatibility with IF-SNN conversions. Our approach is conceptually orthogonal to advanced conversion techniques that calibrate or optimize IF-SNNs, as it is built upon a distinct theoretical foundation. We evaluate our streaming inference framework alongside existing ANN-to-SNN conversion methods to examine how these techniques interact. Specifically, we apply our framework across diverse conversion methods (while maintaining IF neurons) and measure their AP50 accuracy over ticks on Argoverse-HD to determine if incremental computing benefits advanced conversion techniques. As shown in Figure 6, our streaming SNN inference does not transfer to existing IF-neuron-based conversion methods. Although these techniques are engineered to accelerate early-step inference in static frames, their gains do not propagate across frames, as our streaming framework relies theoretically on LIF neuron dynamics. Consequently, this demonstrates that streaming SNN inference calls for tailored optimizations beyond standard static IF conversion approaches. # 5 RELATED WORKS 

Direct SNN Training. A common approach for building high-accuracy SNNs is to directly train them with backpropagation-based methods (Lee et al., 2016). The learning signal backpropagates across time steps, and surrogate gradients (Neftci et al., 2019) relax the discontinuous firing rule with smooth approximations. Techniques such as OTTT (Xiao et al., 2022), e-prop (Bellec et al., 2020), mixed-mode differentiation (Zenke & Neftci, 2021), or event-driven BP (Zhu et al., 2022) further improve computational efficiency. BPTT extends to advanced sequence processing architectures such as spiking transformers (Zhou et al., 2022; 2023) or state-space SNN models (Stan & Rhodes, 2024; Shen et al., 2025). These methods do not compute states incrementally. In contrast, LIF-Stream reuses prior states to compute only incremental activation updates without specialized training, and guarantees this property perpetually over unbounded streams. Furthermore, directly trained SNNs generally exhibit an accuracy gap relative to their ANN counterparts (Fang et al., 2021; Sengupta et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2022) due to the gradient mismatch introduced by surrogate gradient approximations. 

ANN-to-SNN Conversion is an alternative of direct training to build a high-performance SNN that can match the ANN accuracy. Cao et al. (2015) and Diehl et al. (2015) first empirically demonstrated its feasibility. Rueckauer et al. (2016; 2017) established its theoretical basis by finding a steady-state equivalence between discrete IF neuron firing rates and clipped ReLU activations. Because early conversion methods took too many time steps even for a single static input, subsequent works focused on improving the static SNN accuracy in early steps (Diehl et al., 2015; Li et al., 2021; Hao et al., 2023b). Deng & Gu (2021) formulated the layer-wise conversion errors, and Bu et al. (2023) categorizes this error to propose a clip-floor function to replace ReLU1. Huang et al. (2025) propose a new differential multi-threshold neuron that communicates rate delta, and Hwang et al. (2024) converted Transformers using a custom winner-takes-all neurons and one-spike phase coding. Few works keep neuron states across multiple frames. Kugele et al. (2020) convert an ANN to IF neurons and keep membrane potentials across frames as a warm start, but their ANN is a streaming-rollout network trained over multiple frames. Unlike us, they note that the carried state can slow convergence, and they neither analyze state reuse nor claim it as a contribution. Moreover, they bound the membrane from below to avoid long silence after negative input. As we prove in Remark B.12, such a bound breaks the conversion relation, since the firing rate no longer equals ReLU1 of the mean input. Diehl et al. (2016) keep IF states within a sentence and reset between sentences, and P´ erez-Carrasco et al. (2013) run leaky event-driven neurons continuously with a hand-tuned leak. None of these works analyzes why state reuse works or when it fails; our theory explains both, and shows that a plain LIF neuron on an ordinary frame-wise ANN suffices (Appendix B.5.4). We contribute to the conversion theory in two novel directions: (i) we reveal the principle underlying the transient SNN dynamics which fundamentally enabled the ANN-to-SNN conversion, and (ii) we make the converted SNNs efficiently process the sequence or streaming inputs without any additional training or fine-tuning. We also challenge the conventional dependence on IF neurons, showing why LIF neurons should be primarily used for stream processing. 

SNN Theories. Continuous-time SNNs have been widely studied theoretically in terms of computa-tional power (Maass, 1996a;b) or dynamics (Gerstner et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2021; Mancoo et al., 2020). However, directly applying these results to discrete-time SNNs causes errors that accumu-late over time (Mancoo et al., 2020; Niesen & Hall, 2004). Discontinuous discrete dynamics are more difficult to analyze, and hence relatively few works have studied discrete-time SNNs; through symbolic dynamics (Cessac, 2008), ergodic theory (Cessac, 2011), geometric expressivity (Nguyen et al., 2025), or optimization theory (Oh & Lee, 2024). We provide a new perspective by viewing the transient dynamics of discrete SNNs as continuous attraction toward an instantaneous ANN. This bridges transient SNN dynamics, reference tracking, and the conversion theory. Our view is closest to spike-coding networks in computational neuroscience (Boerlin et al., 2013; Den` eve & Machens, 2016), in which exponentially filtered spike trains track a target signal and each neuron spikes only when this reduces the readout error, so that computation is incremental by construction. The targets have grown from linear dynamical systems to nonlinear dynamics (Thalmeier et al., 2016; 10 Preprint Alemi et al., 2018) and convex piecewise-linear input-output functions akin to ReLU layers (Mancoo et al., 2020). In all of these, the connectivity and thresholds are derived from, or learned toward, the target, which requires specific structure such as fast recurrent inhibition or dendritic nonlinearities. In contrast, we start from an arbitrary trained deep ReLU1 network, convert it with standard conversion into standard LIF neurons, and prove that the resulting dynamics already track the instantaneous fixed point of the corresponding firing-rate network, a static map of the current filtered input, under a contraction condition that every feedforward network satisfies. Incremental computation thus comes for free from conversion, which lets us run deep networks incrementally on data streams. Sigma-delta modulation (de Jagar, 1952; Steele, 1975; O’Connor & Welling, 2016) conceptually links to our work. In detail, Yoon (2016) interpreted LIF neuron as a variant of sigma-delta modulator, an analog-to-digital converter that communicates only quantized temporal differences between nodes. Following works applied this idea for sequence processing DNNs by computing only quantized activation deltas (O’Connor & Welling, 2016; O’Connor et al., 2017; Zambrano et al., 2019). In contrast, we view SNN computation through the lens of reference tracking. The concept of closing the temporal activation gap is a byproduct of controlling SNN dynamics for efficient stream processing. 

Incremental Computation of Neural Activations. Delta networks (Neil et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2018; Parger et al., 2022; Yousefzadeh & Sifalakis, 2022) reuse prior activations and incrementally compute only activation differences. However, the efficiency gain only comes when the activation deltas are sparsified through quantization, and thus the ANN should be trained to be aware of this quantization effect to reduce its impact on stream processing accuracy. Techniques for vision tasks exploit the temporal redundancy in their features by computing feature changes from events (Cavigelli et al., 2017; Dutson et al., 2022; Buckler et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022). More coarse-grained approaches adapt neural computation through early exit (Teerapittayanon et al., 2016), test-time block scaling (Fang et al., 2018; Han et al., 2021), or caching (Huynh et al., 2017; Narayanan et al., 2018). Unlike them, our work performs fine-grained, neuron-level incremental computation with SNNs, and computes to narrow the ANN-SNN activation gap instead of the inter-frame ANN activation gap. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

We reveal a core principle underlying discrete SNN dynamics: an instantaneous ReLU1-ANN serves as a moving reference target that drives the output trajectory of the discrete SNN. Upon this, we build LIF-Stream, an efficient stream processing framework that controls a discrete SNN to continuously adapt to the reference ANN’s output trajectory, thereby incrementally computing the streaming perception. Comprehensive evaluations across three large-scale benchmarks with distinct modalities show that our method achieves the best efficiency-accuracy trade-off. LIF-Stream is deployable out-of-the-box only with a trained ReLU1-ANN, requiring no post-training model calibration, customized neuron dynamics, or non-binary spike formats. # TECHNICAL APPENDICES 

# A DISCUSSIONS AND LIMITATIONS 

On-device Always-on Inference. A prominent application of this technique is on-device continuous inference. These workloads are typically embodied and event-driven, meaning that sensory inputs are temporally correlated rather than batched. Crucially, surprising events are usually rare, and a proper, composed reaction is often more adequate than immediate precision. Computational resources are scarce on mobile devices; the static amortized cost must remain low while dynamically scaling up when sudden events occur abruptly. Our technique precisely satisfies these conditions: our average-case cost reduction, combined with the energy efficiency of neuromorphic computing, leads to ultra-low cost in static scenes with high temporal redundancy, while adaptively allocating higher computation only during surprising events. In contrast, standard ANNs consume significant energy on mobile devices, making true always-on execution infeasible unless they are (i) constantly plugged into power, or (ii) triggered on-demand by humans or lightweight wake-up models. However, the trigger-based approach (ii) either loses immediate always-on responsiveness or inherently sacrifices the accuracy of the main model during non-triggered intervals. By contrast, our technique enables SNNs to maintain permanent responsiveness with ultra-low latency, without sacrificing model accuracy. It is thus a candidate enabling technique for ubiquitous, low-cost, always-on sensing applications; demonstrating it on a neuromorphic chip is future work. 

Biological Implications. Since SNNs serve as simplified models of biological neural networks, our theory offers valuable insights into how biological brains efficiently sense, perceive, and react continuously throughout their lifespan. Biological brains do not reprocess full-resolution sensory inputs for every inference, nor do they exhibit global state resets. Like SNNs, the brain may persistently retain neural activations and compute only to minimize the error between current spike-coded activations and underlying continuous-valued signals. This mechanism conserves energy in static environments while maintaining constant readiness to respond to unexpected events. In this perspective, the intrinsic leakage in LIF neurons enables unbounded stream processing without state resets, analogous to how biological forgetting via neuronal leakage clears capacity for new information, thereby facilitating lifelong inference in biological agents. Nevertheless, biological neuronal dynamics are continuous and substantially more complex, and hence extending our theoretical framework to continuous-time and biophysically detailed models, such as the Hodgkin-Huxley (Hodgkin & Huxley, 1952), adaptive LIF (Gerstner et al., 2014), or Izhikevich (Izhikevich, 2003) models, remains an essential direction for future work to validate this hypothesis. 

Predictive Coding. Our theoretical framework aligns closely with the principles of predictive coding (Friston & Kiebel, 2009). Predictive coding views the brain as an active prediction engine :rather than constructing perception purely bottom-up by processing raw sensory inputs, the brain uses internal generative models to continuously predict incoming signals. Within our formulation, the top-down predictions correspond to the prior SNN output y(t), while the bottom-up prediction error represents the activation gap y∗(t) − y(t) relative to the target ANN output y∗(t). The internal state is subsequently updated through adaptation dynamics that drive y(t) toward y∗(t).In this 18 Preprint sense, the neural network computes only the prediction error rather than re-evaluating the full input scene, directly analogous to how our SNN operates exclusively to close the ANN-SNN activation gap. This error-driven scheme ensures extreme bandwidth and energy efficiency by transmitting prediction errors instead of full-resolution sensory data, while enabling rapid response through temporal anticipation. Consequently, our framework provides a formal mechanism demonstrating how predictive coding operates within and benefits SNNs from an ANN-SNN equivalence perspective. An important extension of predictive coding pertains to learning, which we discuss next. 

Online Learning for SNNs. Extending our theoretical framework to train the corresponding ANN online over continuous data streams, and thereby train the SNN dynamically, represents a promising research direction. The traditional paradigm of ANN-to-SNN conversion relies on pre-training an ANN offline prior to conversion. However, extending this framework to online learning disentangles ANN-SNN correspondence from a purely static conversion pipeline, establishing this equivalence as a general paradigm for studying SNNs and potentially biological neural networks. Our theory naturally connects to local, bio-plausible learning methods that assign credit based on per-neuron activation error signals, including predictive coding (Rao & Ballard, 1999; Friston & Kiebel, 2009), equilibrium propagation (Scellier & Bengio, 2017), and contrastive Hebbian learning (Movellan, 1991). While STDP (Kheradpisheh et al., 2018) also measures activation differences over time rather than direct value encodings, its precise theoretical link warrants further investigation. Prior conversion techniques offer limited implications for online SNN training since they lack an online adaptation perspective; at equilibrium, the corresponding ANN retains few signal components to exploit beyond static state values. Consequently, training traditionally resorts to standard backprop-agation or, for feedback architectures, Deep Equilibrium methods such as implicit differentiation (Bai et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2021) and phantom gradients (Geng et al., 2021). While DEQ training concepts offer valuable insights, these approaches remain computationally expensive and require global coordination, highlighting the advantages of our adaptive formulation. 

Hardware Implementation. Our stream processing SNN framework uses only operations sup-ported by neuromorphic hardware platforms with LIF neurons and soft reset. As a cornerstone model in computational neuroscience, the LIF neuron is widely supported across modern neuromorphic platforms, including Intel Loihi (Davies et al., 2018; Orchard et al., 2021), IBM TrueNorth (Akopyan et al., 2015), SpiNNaker (Furber et al., 2014; Gonzalez et al., 2024), and custom FPGA-based implementations (Han et al., 2020). Crucially, our method avoids the need for global state resets, a requirement inherently imposed by prior conversion techniques, allowing each neuron to operate independently without global synchronization beyond a shared clock. This offers a significant hardware advantage over recent trends aiming for high-accuracy SNNs. Such approaches typically introduce non-standard components alongside basic neuronal dynamics, including synchronized bitwise operations across spike trains (Zhou et al., 2022), customized neuron dynamics (Oh & Lee, 2024), group dynamics (Hwang et al., 2024), or multi-bit spiking mecha-nisms (Guo et al., 2024; Xing et al., 2024). These additions incur substantial silicon complexity overheads without yielding proportional energy efficiency gains, making custom chip fabrication impractical. We evaluate energy efficiency theoretically, following standard, hardware-agnostic evaluation practices in recent theoretical SNN literature (Zhou et al., 2022; 2023; Kundu et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2021; Oh & Lee, 2024). These estimates are not hardware measurements, and how much of the advantage carries over to physical neuromorphic silicon remains to be measured. We leave full-scale hardware deployment as direct future work for efficient mobile applications. 

Beyond ReLU1. With a standard LIF neuron the reference ANN must use ReLU1 and affine operators. The theory itself only needs a neuron whose dynamics form a fixed-point iteration with a constant step size, so neurons that can approximate general multivariate nonlinearities, such as the sign-gradient neurons of Oh & Lee (2024), would lift this restriction when run with a constant step size. This trades away the standard-neuron property that matters for deployment on existing neuromorphic hardware. 

Sequence Processing. Our current framework focuses on converting feedforward architectures to achieve high per-frame accuracy, and thus it does not yet directly apply to sequence models. Never-19 Preprint theless, extending our theoretical formulation to sequence modeling represents a highly promising research direction for two key reasons: (i) our theory is the first to leverage the transient dynamics of SNNs to enable practically efficient stream processing, and (ii) our framework demonstrates that SNN inference over sequential inputs is inherently stateful. A motivating insight is that a DEQ can be viewed as a convergent RNN , specifically, an RNN processing a static input over an extended time horizon. Since an SNN at equilibrium corresponds to a DEQ, extending an SNN to time-varying inputs may yield a corresponding RNN with analogous transient dynamics. Alternatively, the transient dynamics and hysteresis behavior of SNNs could be linked to associative memory frameworks, such as Modern Hopfield Networks (Ramsauer et al., 2020; Krotov & Hopfield, 2016), enabling SNNs to execute attention mechanisms or normalized state transitions purely through intrinsic neuronal interactions. Consequently, exploring how SNNs can actively utilize prior state information, rather than merely computing activation deltas, to infer new sequential predictions remains a compelling direction for future work.

# D BASELINE METHODS AND IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

This appendix describes how LIF-Stream is implemented (Appendix D.1), how the baselines are implemented (Appendix D.2–Appendix D.3), and the protocol that is shared by every row of Table 1 (Appendix D.4). All methods are implemented in a single PyTorch codebase on top of spiking-jelly (Fang et al., 2023), so that every arm of a comparison shares the same data pipeline, the same decoder, and the same metric code. Each baseline is implemented directly from the equations of its own paper; where a published method offers several settings we use the one its authors report as best. D.1 LIF-S TREAM 

Convertible ANN. Our framework requires only that the reference ANN be a composition of affine operators and the bounded activation ReLU1 (x). Concretely, every architecture we use is built from convolutions, linear layers, batch normalization, average pooling, and ReLU1. Operators that are not affine (e.g. max-pooling, layer normalization, softmax attention) are replaced by affine equivalents before training, so no approximation is introduced at conversion time. Task-specific post-processing that contains no learnable parameters – non-maximum suppression for detection, CTC collapse for speech, arg-max for classification – is executed outside the converted graph, exactly as in the ANN. 

Conversion. Conversion follows Algorithm 8. The model is traced into a graph, each Conv– BatchNorm pair is folded into a single affine operator, and each ReLU1 site is replaced by the composition n ◦ aτ of an input rescaling aτ (x) = ( τ − 1) x + 1 and a soft-reset LIF neuron with 

θ = 1 , urest = 0 , R = 1 . Because ReLU1 is bounded by construction, the firing threshold is fixed at 1

and no activation statistics, calibration set, or per-layer search is required ; this is the only structural difference from the conversion baselines in Appendix D.2, all of which fit at least one parameter per layer from data. The single data-independent step is the membrane pre-charge of Algorithm 8: firing 33 Preprint is disabled, the zero input is propagated once, and each neuron’s resting influx is stored as its initial membrane potential un(0) together with the output codec state y(0) . This is a one-off correction of the constant term b(t) in Equation (14); it is computed once at conversion time and never re-applied. 

Streaming inference. Streaming inference follows Algorithm 1. The membrane potentials of all neurons and the input/output codec states are initialized once at the beginning of a stream and then persist across frames; there is no global reset, no per-frame re-initialization, and no cross-frame communication other than the neuronal state itself. Each frame k is allotted T ticks, and the prediction for frame k is read from the output codec at the last tick of that frame. Inputs are encoded with the sample-and-hold scheme of Algorithm 2: at each tick the encoder emits the current frame unchanged, and the change between frames reaches the SNN through the exponentially coded input ˜x(t), which follows the new frame at the leak rate τ −1 

> τ

.

Hyperparameters. The framework has two hyperparameters: the leak τ and the per-frame tick budget T . We use τ = 8 throughout (except the Best column), and sweep T ∈ { 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 , 64 },which is the axis reported in Table 1. For the Best column, we use τ = 16 and measure the accuracy in T > 128 , just like all the other baseline methods. An optional warm-up grants the first frame of a stream 4T extra ticks so that the very first prediction is not read from a cold membrane; its cost is amortized over the whole stream and it is disabled unless stated. The only exception is the Argoverse-HD entry of LIF-Stream at T = 16 in Table 1, which uses a warm-up of 64 (= 4 T ) ticks. No other tuning is performed, and the same τ is used for all three tasks. F.1 DATASETS 

We train and evaluate on LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015), the standard English read-speech corpus derived from LibriVox audiobooks and sampled at 16 kHz. F.1.1 LICENSES 

LibriSpeech is distributed under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 License, and is used here for non-commercial academic research. F.2 MODEL 

The reference ANN is a windowed SummaryMixing Conformer encoder (Parcollet et al., 2024) with a CTC head, adapted so that the whole encoder is a composition of affine operators and ReLU1. SummaryMixing is the natural starting point for a convertible streaming encoder: it replaces self-attention’s data-dependent T × T mixing with a constant one – a uniform mean over time – which is an affine operator, whereas softmax attention is not. 

Front-end. 80-bin log-mel filterbank features at a 10 ms hop, normalized with a fixed global CMVN fitted once on the training set and frozen (per-utterance CMVN is neither causal nor foldable into the network), followed by a convolutional subsampling of ×4, giving one encoder frame every 40 ms. 

Window assembly. The encoder consumes one window per call, formed by concatenating the previous chunk and the current chunk, [ prior chunk ∥ current chunk ]. This is the design decision that keeps the encoder feed-forward: the published streaming SummaryMixing variant accumulates its summary statistic across calls, which is a cross-call recurrence, whereas taking the mean over a two-chunk window makes the same summary a reduction inside a single forward pass – an ordinary 

AvgPool1d . With a chunk of 32 encoder frames and a left context of 32 frames, the window spans 

2.56 s and the model emits a 1.28 s chunk of frame posteriors per call. The first window of a stream is zero-padded on the left and its mean is renormalized accordingly. 

Block. Each of the 18 encoder blocks applies two per-frame branches f (X) and s(X) (Lin-ear + ReLU1), averages s(X) over the window with a single AvgPool1d whose kernel equals the window length, broadcasts the resulting summary back to every frame, concatenates it with f (X),and projects with a further Linear + ReLU1. A depthwise convolution module (kernel 31 or 63) and a feed-forward sandwich complete the block, as in a Conformer. The model dimension is 256 ; the encoder has ≈ 25 –28 M parameters and 128 –182 spiking sites depending on the arm. 

Substitutions for convertibility. GELU/Swish are replaced by ReLU1; LayerNorm by masked BatchNorm, since LayerNorm computes per-sample statistics at run time and therefore cannot be folded into the preceding affine layer; and the streaming variant’s cumulative masked mean by the windowed AvgPool1d described above. The head is CTC, as in the source recipe; CTC collapse and beam search run on the decoded posteriors, outside the converted graph. F.3 TRAINING SETUP

G.1 DATASETS 

We use DVS128 Gesture (Amir et al., 2017), the standard neuromorphic gesture benchmark, recorded with a DAVIS128 event camera at 128 × 128 spatial resolution. Streaming, not clip-wise. Most recent work evaluates this dataset clip-wise: each pre-segmented gesture is cut out in advance, binned by constant event count, and classified as a whole. That protocol is not causal – constant-count binning needs the end of the clip to place its bins, and cutting the clip out presupposes that the gesture has already been segmented. We therefore evaluate on the

7.4% . Because the gestures are repetitive, a 2 s window still contains complete motion cycles. Since a streaming evaluation also encounters rest periods, the stream task carries a 12 th null class. G.1.1 LICENSES

H.1 INTEGRATION WITH IF CONVERSION METHODS 

Question. Our framework and the advanced IF conversion methods optimize different things – ours makes the trajectory track a moving reference across frames, theirs makes a single bounded simulation more accurate – so it is worth asking whether the two compose. 

Porting. For each published conversion method we take the converted network exactly as that method produces it, using the same implementation that produces its row in Table 1, and run it under two protocols that differ by exactly one call: the method’s own per-frame reset, and our streaming protocol in which the state is initialized once per sequence and then persists. Nothing else changes – same weights, same calibration, same decoder, same frames, same score threshold – so any difference is attributable to the reset alone. Methods that pre-charge each neuron to a fixed initial potential (e.g. 

θ/ 2 for QCFS and SlipReLU) store that value as the neuron’s reset value, so it is re-applied by the reset call and only by the reset call; AdaFire likewise clears its burst state per frame. 

Measurement. All arms are scored over one identical frame set – whole sequences, never strided – with AP@50 at a score threshold of 0.001 , and sweep T ∈ { 8, 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 } (plus T = 256 

for the IF arm, whose published protocol needs a far larger budget). The dense ANN on the same frames provides the ceiling. Because the per-frame-reset baselines and the streaming arm are scored on the same frames, the ratios are meaningful even where the absolute values differ from the full-validation numbers of Table 1. Some of these baselines require a source ANN trained with their own activation, or an unbounded-ReLU network plus calibration data, so the comparison necessarily crosses checkpoints; this is structural rather than a choice, and is itself part of the point, since a bounded activation needs nothing fitted. 

Is the IF failure a decoder problem? The streaming arm above reads each frame as the mean of its own T ticks, a tumbling window. Question. Tick cost is normally quoted per frame, which silently treats the frame rate as given. It is a design choice, and the two protocols respond to it in opposite ways: with a per-frame reset every 42 Preprint frame pays the full transient, so cost per second is T × fps, exactly linear in the frame rate with no property of the video entering; with a persisting state a higher frame rate means a smaller change between consecutive frames, so each frame needs fewer ticks. 

Setup. We re-use the settle-tick definition of Appendix H.2 unchanged – same detection-space criterion, same matching threshold, same ϵ grid – and sweep the sampling rate by striding the source video: strides 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10 of the native 30 fps stream give 30 , 15 , 10 , 7.5, 6, 5, 3 fps. Each point is the full validation split at that rate. Cost per second of video is fps × settle( fps ). The reset arm needs no run: its frames are independent, so its cost is T × fps analytically, and we use the tick budget at which it attains the same accuracy as the streaming arm on the same data. Striding is what the streaming protocol otherwise forbids, precisely because it widens the inter-frame gap and removes the redundancy persistence exploits; here that is the independent variable. Consequently the numbers in this study are comparable only across its own sweep, the 30 fps column being the one it shares with the main streaming results. 

Reporting. We fit a power law to cost per second against frame rate for each arm and report the exponent, together with the censored fraction at every point; points whose censoring is large enough to understate the mean are excluded from the figure rather than reported with a caveat. The claim the measurement supports is the ratio of the two exponents – reset grows linearly with frame rate by construction, while the streaming arm grows sublinearly – so the advantage widens as the stream is sampled more finely. H.5 SYNAPTIC OPERATIONS AND ENERGY AT MATCHED ACCURACY 

Ticks are the unit of Table 1, but a tick does not cost the same for every arm. We therefore count synaptic operations per frame with the counter of the DVS leg, unchanged: a layer driven by spikes pays one accumulate (AC) per spike and synapse, a layer driven by analog values (the stem) pays a multiply-accumulate (MAC). MACs arise only in the stem, because the float encoder feeds each frame to it as analog values at every tick (App. D); they scale with T and account for 3–5% of the energy in every row. A spike-based stream encoder removes them, since the stem then also receives binary spikes and pays ACs instead; this costs little accuracy, as the population-lookahead spike encoder stays within 0.4 AP50 of its float counterpart at every tick budget (Appendix C.1).
