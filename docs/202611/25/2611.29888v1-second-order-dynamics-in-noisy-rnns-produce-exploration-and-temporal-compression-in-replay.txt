## Abstract 

Biological neural networks (like the hippocampus) can internally generate “replay” resembling stimulus-driven activity. Recent computational models of replay use noisy recurrent neural networks (RNNs) trained to path-integrate. Replay in these networks is described as Langevin sampling, but this theory alone fails to capture the environmental exploration and temporal compression observed in real biological replay. To close this gap, we re-examine spontaneous noisy RNN activity (“replay”) as Langevin sampling, one timestep at a time, from a time-series distribution resembling lived experiences (unlike previous works which defined sampling as Monte Carlo inference over a static distribution). We provide a normative second-order Langevin sampling framework wherein spike-frequency adaptation drives spatial exploration at the cost of speed, and momentum via dendritic input differentiation drives temporal compression. We numerically verify our framework via simulations of noisy RNNs trained on 2D triangular and T-maze navigation activity, and others trained on place cell activity of synthetic rats. Crucially, we observe that simultaneous adaptation and momentum increases both replay exploration and speed, and provide one of the first accounts for biologically plausible temporal compression in rate-based RNN replay. Our findings encourage experiments into whether dendritic input differentiation, or other mechanisms, may support temporal compression in memory circuits like the hippocampus. 

## 1 Introduction 

During quiescent periods such as sleep or wakeful resting, some neural circuits internally generate activity resembling that of active periods (Tingley and Peyrache, 2020). Such “replay” phenomena have been observed in the prefrontal (Euston et al., 2007, Peyrache et al., 2009), sensory (Kenet et al., 2003, Xu et al., 2012), motor (Hoffman and McNaughton, 2002), and entorhinal cortices (Gardner et al., 2022); the anterior thalamus (Peyrache et al., 2015); and the hippocampus (Buzsáki, 1986, Skaggs and McNaughton, 1996, Nádasdy et al., 1999, Lee and Wilson, 2002, Foster, 2017). Of these circuits, the hippocampus is particularly interesting because its robustness in tasks like navigation (O’Keefe and Nadel, 1978, Burgess et al., 1994, McNaughton et al., 1996) and planning (Pfeiffer and Foster, 2013, Miller et al., 2017) during active states seems crucially tied to its spontaneous activity during quiescent states (Buzsáki, 1989, 2015, Tononi and Cirelli, 2014, Ólafsdóttir et al., 2015, 2018). While some works have produced replay using supervised generative models (Deperrois et al., 2022), most existing models of hippocampal activity treat replay as an emergent byproduct of careful network design. While these models reproduce aspects of replay, they are typically motivated by empirical findings and lack theoretical justification. A more principled model of hippocampal function with emergent replay is path-integration (Krishna et al., 2024, Levenstein et al., 2024), wherein neural circuits predict dynamic environment or task variables from imperfect observations thereof, e.g., tracking position from velocity measurements. This normative description of the hippocampus as a sequence predictor (Levy, 1989, Stachenfeld et al., 2017) matches hippocampal encodings of upcoming stimuli (Davachi and DuBrow, 2015) and prediction errors (Aitken and Kok, 2022, Miller et al., 2023), and neural activity sweeps that represent possible future trajectories (Kay et al., 2020, Johnson and Redish, 2007). Unlike traditional, hand-crafted models of hippocampal circuits (i.e., continuous attractor networks (Samsonovich and McNaughton, 1997, Battaglia and Treves, 1998)), sequential predictive learning models are trained from data. Nonetheless, they account for the emergence of place cells (Levenstein et al., 2024, Recanatesi et al., 2021, Chen et al., 2024), grid cells (Cueva and Wei, 2018, Sorscher et al., 2019), and head direction cells (Cueva et al., 2020, Uria et al., 2022); can incorporate phenomena like theta oscillations (Levenstein et al., 2024); and exhibit quiescent replay activity (Krishna et al., 2024, Levenstein et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024). Krishna et al. (2024) provided the first theoretical foundation for replay in noisy path-integrating networks, proving their quiescent (i.e., noise-driven, without inputs) activity is diffusive replay (Stella et al., 2019) generated via Langevin sampling (Besag, 1994), one timestep at a time, from the time-series distribution of awake activity. Crucially, this definition of time-series sampling for replay is different from how modern AI models generate time-series—RNN replay is made one timestep at a time, without modifying any other timesteps, whereas AI models modify all timesteps simultaneously—and different from other definitions of sampling in computational neuroscience— replay samples from a temporally structured time-series distribution resembling lived experiences, whereas other types of sampling (Dong et al., 2022, Aitchison and Lengyel, 2016, Hennequin et al., 2014, Chen et al., 2025) use external inputs to generate temporally unstructured time-series whose individual timesteps are gathered for Monte Carlo calculations (Figure 2). Afterwards, Levenstein et al. (2024) introduced a mechanism to induce exploration in replay via spike-frequency adaptation, i.e., negative feedback. Exploration is the notion that replay expresses a variety of behavioral sequences (Davidson et al., 2009, Pfeiffer, 2020), and is associated with long trajectories in neural space, visitation of multiple attractor basins, and transitions that were not present in awake activity. Adaptation can destabilize attractors and induce sudden transitions in replay activity (Itskov et al., 2011, Dong et al., 2021, Li et al., 2024, Levenstein et al., 2024), thereby facilitating exploration, and is thought to play a key role in replay dynamics in vivo (Levenstein et al., 2019). However, existing theory on noisy path-integrating networks does not account for these mechanisms, or capture the temporal compression of replay sequences relative to awake sequences of 2activity in vivo (Nádasdy et al., 1999, Buzsáki, 2015, Michelmann et al., 2019, Farooq and Dragoi, 2019). To our knowledge, temporal compression is not captured in replay from any trained RNN model, and so far our understanding of its mechanisms is limited to short-term facilitation and phase precession in hand-crafted spiking networks (Leibold et al., 2008, Jaramillo and Kempter, 2017). Overall, while noisy path-integration is a promising model of hippocampal function and replay, its theory leaves crucial details of biological replay unaccounted for: exploration and temporal compression. We close this gap with a normative second-order Langevin sampling framework for spontaneous neural activity (replay). We show that spike-frequency adaptation drives spatial exploration but introduces a dynamical drag that slows replay. To recover replay speeds more akin to biology, we introduce momentum via dendritic input differentiation, and relate it to underdamped Langevin sampling. Using noisy RNNs in several continuous navigation tasks, we demonstrate that introducing both adaptation and momentum in spontaneous RNN dynamics enables replay trajectories to visit multiple attractor basins (exploration) while moving quickly (temporal compression). These results provide the first formal account of temporal compression in rate-based predictive networks and offer testable predictions regarding temporal compression and dendritic processing in memory circuits like the hippocampus. ## 2 Background: Replay as Langevin Sampling 

Neural circuits in several regions of the brain, particularly memory areas like the hippocampus, generate spontaneous replay of activity from when the organism was active or awake. Traditional models have replay emerge from hand-crafted networks, but we are interested in recent models that prove replay emerges as a byproduct of learning in path-integrating networks like the hippocampus (Krishna et al., 2024). Without external inputs (e.g., sensory cues), these networks undergo Langevin dynamics to sample from the distribution of awake activity they were trained on. Here we: (1) define Langevin dynamics, (2) summarize how path-integrating noisy RNNs implicitly learn generative Langevin dynamics, (3) specify the crucial distinction between sampling in replay and sampling in other fields of computational neuroscience, and (4) establish the limitations of first-order Langevin sampling that motivate second-order dynamics for replay in neural circuits. In Section A.5 we discuss the exact nuances in characterizing non-stationary noisy RNN activity (replay) as Langevin sampling.

2.3 Replay Sampling vs. Other Types of Sampling 

We study path-integrating noisy RNNs that, during quiescent states without external input, replay episodes of awake activity. Our RNNs generate trajectories in a causal, real-time fashion, one timestep at a time, unlike modern AI models which can generate all timesteps in a time-series simultaneously. Crucially, our definition of sampling as time-series generation one timestep at a time should not be confused with the kind of Langevin sampling used by other works in computational neuroscience to explain how the brain may perform probabilistic calculations like Bayesian inference (Dong et al., 2022, Aitchison and Lengyel, 2016, Hennequin et al., 2014, Chen et al., 2025). We study time-series distributions: replay p({r(t)}Tt=0 ), awake activity p({s(t)}Tt=0 ) (T is the final timestep), and how they compare—each time-series is a single sample from a time-series distribution with rigid temporal structure and non-stationarities ( p(r(t1)) ̸ = p(r(t2)) ∀ t1̸ = t2). Meanwhile, inference networks use Langevin sampling as a means of generating sequences of activity {r(t)}Tt=0 wherein each timestep is a separate sample from a static, stationary distribution: r(t) ∼ p(s) ∀ t. In inference networks, time simply becomes a means of gathering samples: with enough time, there are enough samples to compute static expectations, covariances, or posteriors via Monte Carlo. Replay networks generate episodes of awake activity, while inference networks compute static statistics. In Figure 2 we further illustrate the crucial distinction between these two notions of sampling. Nonetheless, one might think existing techniques to accelerate Monte Carlo sampling in inference networks (Dong et al., 2022, Aitchison and Lengyel, 2016, Hennequin et al., 2014) should easily transfer to replay sampling networks, but adaptation clearly illustrates this is not the case. Dong et al. (2022) show that adaptation accelerates Monte Carlo sampling over a static, constant input—they have an external input anchoring the network’s target state, allowing negative feedback (adaptation) to rapidly destabilize the initial state without repelling the trajectory once it reaches the target. In contrast, our networks internally generate replay without inputs. Because no external drive exists to encourage reaching targets, negative feedback counteracts score-driven convergence towards learned attractors, causing the slowing we observe later. Adaptation accelerates input-clamped inference, but not necessarily internally-driven replay generation.

## 3 Second-Order Langevin Sampling for Neural Replay 

Based on the previous section, here we assume that trained RNNs have well-estimated the score function, and follow it during quiescent (internally-driven) activity to generate replay via Langevin dynamics. Here we ask how modulating the dynamics of such RNNs affects or biases the replay distributions. We see that adaptation (negative feedback) incurs a non-ideal form of second-order Langevin sampling, so we propose a complementary alternative that uses momentum and could be implemented in neurons via input differentiation.

Biological plausibility of input differentiation. We qualify the plausibility of Theorem 3.5 by answering three critical questions: 1. How can a single neuron differentiate its inputs? One straightforward way for a single neuron to calculate the recent derivative of its inputs is through dendritic delays: two different dendrites carry identical copies of input signals at different time delays, which are subtracted where the two branches converge. Another way to differentiate inputs is through high-pass filtering. 2. How can the brain modulate the gain (strength) of the input differentiation? Neuromodulators in the brain like acetylcholine (ACh) can modulate the properties of neurons. We propose they might also be able to modulate the strength of specific dendritic branches (i.e., ones computing inputs’ recent derivatives), particularly if those branches have special receptors triggered by the neuromodulators. For example, SK (small conductance calcium-activated potassium channels) channels and muscarinic M1 receptors, which ACh modulates (Giessel and Sabatini, 2010, Buchanan et al., 2010), could be concentrated in the derivative-computing dendritic branches. 3. How might the gain modulation correspond with active/quiescent (i.e., wake/sleep) cycles? As discussed in Section 2.4, the brain already changes levels of neuromodulators like ACh during wake/sleep cycles; this is how we justify increasing the adaptation strength ba during quiescent activity compared to awake activity. Overall, our aim is not to say concretely that the brain must implement momentum and must do so via input differentiation, but rather to suggest it as a topic of future experimental study. Indeed, little is known about how the brain performs temporal compression of spontaneous activity. 

## 4 Numerical Results 

Now we examine how adaptation and biologically plausible momentum (Equations 12 and 16) bias replay distributions in simulated RNNs trained to path-integrate. We first see that adaptation usually slows replay, while momentum quickens it. Then we confirm that adaptation induces exploration, and crucially show that momentum does not prevent exploration, but rather complements it by increasing the distance that replay paths travel.

## 5 Conclusions and Future Work 

By re-examining replay in path-integrating noisy RNNs as Langevin sampling, we established that first-order dynamics are insufficient to account for the speed and flexibility of biological replay. We demonstrated analytically and empirically that spike-frequency adaptation acts as a form of non-ideal second-order sampling: while it successfully destabilizes local attractors to encourage exploration, it simultaneously introduces a dynamical drag that slows replay generation. To resolve this exploration-speed trade-off, we introduced a biologically plausible momentum mechanism via dendritic input differentiation. When combined, momentum counteracts slowing from adaptation, temporally compressing replay sequences while preserving, and even enhancing, multi-attractor spatial exploration. Our findings suggest that temporal compression in networks like the hippocampus requires second-order circuit mechanisms. While components of sequential predictive networks have been mapped to specific hippocampal subregions (Chen et al., 2024), future work could refine our proposed mecha-nisms by incorporating biological constraints like Dale’s principle via explicit interneuron populations, by extending our Langevin framework to spike-based predictive learning models (Asabuki and Fukai, 2025, Saponati and Vinck, 2023, Bono et al., 2023), or by confirming our findings in more complex environments (Levenstein et al., 2024, Wood et al., 2018). 10 5.1 Limitations & Testable Predictions 

Our theoretical framework carries several necessary abstractions. First, we assume the intrinsic noise in biological recurrent networks is Gaussian isotropic white noise to permit exact score estimation. Biological noise likely exhibits complex spatiotemporal correlations that could alter sampling dy-namics. Second, directly comparing simulated replay to in vivo recordings remains challenging, as biological replay is sparse, sporadically embedded in background noise, and displays varying degrees of temporal compression. Comparing our temporal compression results to observations in vivo is also made challenging by a lack of consensus in the order of magnitude of temporal compression in the latter, which depending on modeling assumptions can range from 1–2x (Denovellis et al., 2021) to 8–20x (Lee and Wilson, 2002, Jeunehomme and D’Argembeau, 2019, Buch et al., 2021). Nonetheless, our framework yields explicit testable predictions for experimental neuroscience. Be-cause neuromodulators like acetylcholine (ACh) modulate both spike-frequency adaptation and specific dendritic receptors (e.g., muscarinic M1 and SK channels), we propose that the drop in these neuromodulators during sleep not only disinhibits adaptation but may also facilitate dendritic input differentiation. We predict that targeted pharmacological increase of these specific neuromodulators during quiescence would severely attenuate the temporal compression of replay events. We also pre-dict that second-order modifiers (momentum, adaptation) will improve models of replay as diffusive dynamics (Stella et al., 2019, Wu and Wei, 2025).

A.1.2 Activation Function 

In our work, we have mostly used RNNs with ReLU or leaky ReLU activation functions (except in Section F); our reasons for doing so are threefold. The first is that ReLU or ReLU-like activations, unlike tanh activations, do not always saturate. This can mitigate vanishing gradients in RNNs (although care must be taken to avoid exploding gradients), but more importantly we have observed that this reduces the strength of fixed attractors learned by path-integrating RNNs, which is useful for exploration (in Section F we observe that replay in saturating RNNs explores less in the triangle task). The second is that, compared to RNNs with multiplicative gating interactions (e.g., GRUs and LSTMs), ReLU RNNs are more biologically plausible insofar as they can be easily interpreted as the nonnegative, sparse firing rates of spiking networks. The third is that most other previous works in sequential predictive coding tend to use ReLU activations, and have shown no significant differences in results when using more complex nonlinearities: • Ali et al. (2022), some of the first authors to report the emergence of predictive representations emerging in RNNs, use ReLU activations. • Krishna et al. (2024) primarily use ReLU networks, and report similar results with GRU networks. • Levenstein et al. (2024) use ReLU activations in conjunction with layer normalization, but report similar results when layer normalization is removed. • Chen et al. (2024) use tanh for most results, but when they care about the emergence of sparse, localized, nonnegative activations (i.e., place cells), they use ReLU activations. • Sorscher et al. (2019), Xu et al. (2025), and Zhang et al. (2022) were all interested in the emergence of grid cells in predictive representations and primarily used ReLU or ReLU-like activations; those that tried using other nonlinearities did not observe notable differences in results. • Tang et al. (2024), who were also interested in grid cells, use both ReLU and tanh activations, and also report no notable differences between the two. • Tang et al. (2023) use linear networks, and notice no significant difference when using tanh activations.

19 A.3 Metrics A.3.1 Speed: Reach Times 

In the T-maze, triangle, and biased rat tasks, awake trajectories have clearly defined endpoints that they reach. Replay trajectories also aim for these endpoints, although second-order dynamics modifiers like adaptation and momentum (underdampening) affect how quickly or how closely replay paths reach them. We quantify these changes by measuring the timesteps required for replay paths to get within a certain radius of their endpoints (for example, the timesteps required for −−→

AB paths to get within a 0.1 |−−→

AB | radius of B). 

A.3.2 Exploration: Path Lengths 

One way we quantify exploration in replay paths is through path length. We calculate this simply as the sum of velocity magnitudes. 

A.3.3 Exploration: Regions Visited 

Another way we quantify exploration in the T-maze and triangle tasks is through regions visited . As illustrated in Figure 6, regions are portions of input space where all points are closest to the same endpoint. In the triangle task, there are 3 endpoints (which all also act as initial points), while in the T-maze there are 2 endpoints and 1 shared starting point, which for the purposes of region assignment we also consider an “endpoint”. Thus, in each task there are 3 endpoints, and thus 3 regions. Awake trajectories go from one region to another and stay there, but replay trajectories might proceed to visit more regions. We define a “visit” as a contiguous presence within a single region for at least 10 timesteps; naturally, exploratory replay sequences can re-visit previously visited regions, which is how their “regions visited” metric can exceed 3. 

A.3.4 Exploration: Additional Metrics 

For completeness, we add two other measures of exploration: mean displacement , proposed by McNamee et al. (2021), and variance. They are simply defined as Ep(r(t)) [|r(t) − r(0) |] and 

Ep(r(t)) [Var( r(t))] . Higher mean displacements and variances generally correlate with increased path length and exploration. We plot them in Figure 8 and Figure 9. 

A.3.5 Wasserstein Distance 

We seek to compare distributions of trajectories. In most of our tasks, these are objects of dimension 

T × 2, where T is the number of timesteps (recall that T-maze and triangle tasks are already in 2D, while rat trajectories are analyzed after being projected into 2D space). To compare such high-dimensional distributions, we use Wasserstein distances (Panaretos and Zemel, 2019): • In the T-maze and triangle tasks, we first calculate the Wasserstein distance between awake and replay paths belonging to the same direction (e.g., −−→

AB in the triangle task). Since paths along the same direction should resemble, if not obey, Gaussian processes, these paths should approximately be normally distributed, and so we can use the closed-form equation for the Wasserstein distance between two normal distributions. After computing Wasserstein distances for each direction, we take the average as our final distance. KL divergence might have also worked in this task, but in practice the covariance matrices of the (T × 2) -dimensional distributions were singular. • In the rat experiments, decoded 2D trajectories are not easily decomposed into groups of Gaussian processes, so instead we apply sliced Wasserstein distances (Bonneel et al., 2015) to compare the (T × 2) -dimensional distributions. Sliced Wasserstein distances are essentially calculated by taking many random projections of two distributions onto 1D, where Wasserstein distances have a closed form. Computing KL divergences instead would have been challenging, since rat path distributions are high-dimensional and do not admit straightforward estimations of probability density. 20 A.4 Discretization and Implementation of Noisy RNNs

A.5 Deviations from Traditional Langevin Sampling 

We must note a few minor distinctions between our replay RNNs and traditional Langevin sampling. 

Stationarity. We treat neural replay as a sequence of events, and are thus interested in the joint distribution of replay activity at all timesteps p  {r(t)}Tt=1 

. This distribution, however, is not necessarily stationary across t. Stationarity would imply that p(r(t)) has no need or intention of traversing along any meaningful path. While a path-integrating RNN does perform gradient ascent along log p(r(t)) , it does so in a piecewise manner along t rather than jointly along all t

simultaneously. This variation on gradient ascent, in combination with the non-stationarity of p(r(t)) ,means that Langevin sampling guarantees do not hold. For this reason, we make modifications to our RNNs that under stationary Langevin sampling theory might seem unprincipled. 

Momentum (explicit underdampening). The friction term λv applied to the velocity v(t) is subtly different from γ in Equations 3 and 2. λv ∈ [0 , 1] can be interpreted as trying to map γ ∈ [0 , ∞)

onto [0 , 1] . Like γ → ∞ , λv → 1 removes any dependency of v(t + ∆ t) on v(t).21 Noise scaling. RNN replay, unlike Langevin sampling, is sensitive to the variance of noise used. We found that omitting the √2 factor in front of σr η(t) worked best. For this reason, we also omitted 

λv as a scaling term on σr η(t). If λv is allowed to modulate √2σr η(t), we found that it is easy to show improvements in replay fidelity when λv < 1, but we found these to come from noise scaling rather than from momentum as a mechanism. 

Relation to diffusion models. Throughout this work we treat RNNs as generative models using a variant of Langevin sampling. Expressive generative models, in particular diffusion models whose activity resembles Langevin sampling, have received much attention from the machine learning community (Alemohammad et al., 2024, Luzi et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2023, Croitoru et al., 2023, Li et al., 2022, Gozalo-Brizuela and Garrido-Merchan, 2023, Song and Ermon, 2019). The fundamental differences between our RNNs and diffusion models are twofold. The first is that each timestep of a generated replay trajectory is generated sequentially and with only one effective step along the gradient of log-likelihood, whereas a diffusion model would generate all timesteps of a path simultaneously and with many steps along the gradient of log-likelihood. The second is that diffusion models use time-varying noise levels (annealed dynamics) and are explicitly conditioned on time as an input, whereas our RNNs use the same noise level across time and are never explicitly conditioned on time.
