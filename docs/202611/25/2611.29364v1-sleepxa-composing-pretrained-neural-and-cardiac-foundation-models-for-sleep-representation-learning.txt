ABSTRACT 

Physiological signals recorded during sleep provide synchronized views of neural, cardiac, respiratory, and other bodily systems, while preserving modality-specific information. Existing EEG and ECG foundation models already capture rich but distinct physiological structure, motivating an alternative to training a multimodal sleep model from scratch: composing existing pretrained models. We introduce SleepXA, a framework that uses synchronized polysomnography (PSG) to com-pose independently pretrained neural and cardiac foundation models. SleepXA combines pretrained EEG and ECG encoders with lightweight encoders for ad-ditional PSG modalities, and learns shared and modality-specific representations through sleep-guided alignment. We pretrain SleepXA on 9,230 overnight PSG recordings from three large cohorts and evaluate it on 27 downstream tasks span-ning sleep representation, clinical and disease prediction, and daytime physio-logical transfer. SleepXA ranks among the top two methods on 22 of 27 tasks. Controlled experiments further show consistent benefits across sensor configu-rations and EEG–ECG backbone choices, while pretrained initialization outper-forms architecture-matched training from scratch and SleepXA surpasses alterna-tive multimodal composition strategies. These results suggest that synchronized multimodal physiology can serve as a natural interface for composing existing physiological foundation models, yielding reusable representations that extend be-yond sleep to clinical and daytime physiological settings. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Sleep is a fundamental physiological process supporting cognition, metabolism, cardiovascular function, and mental well-being (Consortium et al., 2026). Clinical sleep assessment commonly relies on Polysomnography (PSG), which synchronously records multiple physiological signals, including electroencephalography (EEG), electrooculography (EOG), electromyography (EMG), electrocardiography (ECG), respiratory signals, and oxygen saturation (SpO 2) (Berry et al., 2017). Together, these modalities provide complementary yet coupled views of neural, cardiac, respira-tory, and other physiological processes during sleep, making PSG a natural substrate for learning representations relevant to both sleep analysis and broader health assessment. Recent sleep foundation models (FMs) have demonstrated the value of large-scale multimodal PSG pretraining for transferable sleep representations (Thapa et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Shuai et al., 2026; Hou et al., 2026; Park et al., 2026). Most existing approaches, however, treat heterogeneous PSG modalities largely as parallel inputs for joint encoding or fusion, and they are mostly trained from scratch. Pretraining such a sleep foundation model requires the availability of well-curated and paired multimodal signals, at a scale and diversity that are prohibitively difficult to obtain, raising concerns about the representational breadth of the resulting models. On the other hand, large-scale pretraining has advanced general physiological representation learn-ing (Gu et al., 2025), and has resulted in a series of state-of-the-art and open-sourced foundation models on the specific modalities belonging to PSG, such as cardiac (Li et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2026) and neural (Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025) signals. This motivates a complementary alternative to sleep representation learning: Can we reuse and com-pose existing pretrained modality-specific FMs to extract good representations of sleep physiology? 

However, such composition is non-trivial for the following three reasons. (i) First, existing neural and cardiac FMs are pretrained independently, and mostly on non-sleep datasets. They may produce representations that are not naturally aligned due to signal semantics, temporal scales, training objectives, etc., thus making direct concatenation or fusion suboptimal. (ii) Second, different modalities may contain shared yet complementary information. Na¨ ıvely sup-pressing them into a common modality-invariant representation space may remove useful modality-specific information that is not captured by other modalities. (iii) Third, the available modalities during sleep monitoring may vary substantially across settings, from full PSG to reduced clinical montages and wearable devices, making it challenging to learn representations that remain robust under partial modality availability. To address these challenges, we present SleepXA , a compositional framework that integrates independently pretrained modality-specific foundation models for sleep representation learning. SleepXA combines pretrained EEG and ECG FMs, with lightweight encoders for additional PSG modalities, and learns to separate shared common representation from modality-specific informa-tion, with three fundamental modules (common space alignment, modality-specific preservation, and common-specific disentanglement) as detailed in the Methods Section. We study this compositional paradigm at scale by pretraining SleepXA on 9,230 overnight PSG recordings from three large cohorts and evaluating it on 27 downstream tasks across 15 datasets, spanning general sleep representation, clinical and longitudinal prediction, and cross-context physi-ological transfer beyond sleep. SleepXA ranks first on 17 tasks and within the top two on 22. In particular, extensive experiments demonstrate that SleepXA is not merely a “better sleep model”, but also preserves broader modality-specific physiological knowledge inherited from the original FMs, while extending their capabilities to respiratory, cardiac, longitudinal, and out-of-sleep tasks. We further examine how pretrained initialization, EEG and ECG backbone choice, multimodal in-tegration, sensor availability, and data-efficient adaptation shape these capabilities. 2Preprint 

2 RELATED WORK 

Sleep Foundation Models. Large-scale PSG pretraining has become an effective paradigm for transferable sleep representation learning. Existing approaches explore complementary directions, including cross-modal contrastive learning in SleepFM (Thapa et al., 2026) and sleep2vec (Yuan et al., 2026), time-frequency and multi-objective pretraining in SleepGPT (Huang et al., 2026), robust pretraining and scaling across channel configurations in OSF (Shuai et al., 2026), hierar-chical macro- and micro-structure modeling in SleepMaMi (Park et al., 2026), and physiologically structured CNS-ANS modeling in Omni-Sleep (Hou et al., 2026). These studies primarily learn multimodal physiological representations with a dedicated sleep-pretraining pipeline. SleepXA in-stead investigates whether transferable physiological knowledge already embedded in independently pretrained EEG and ECG foundation models can be extracted and composed through conditional shared-specific alignment to facilitate sleep representation learning. 

Physiological Foundation Models. Large-scale foundation models have also been developed for individual physiological domains. For neural signals, LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024), CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025), BrainOmni (Xiao et al., 2025), and REVE (El Ouahidi et al., 2025) learn transferable representations from EEG or EEG/MEG recordings. For cardiac signals, ECGFounder (Li et al., 2025) and CSFM (Gu et al., 2026) learn reusable representations from large-scale ECG or ECG/PPG corpora. These models provide rich modality-specific physiology knowledge, but are pretrained independently and remain largely confined to their original physiological domains. Rather than simplistic late fusion to leverage these FMs in sleep PSG, SleepXA treats these as composable blocks and introduces a series of modules to align, preserve, and merge their representations for sleep representation learning. 

Multimodal Fusion and Alignment. Multimodal representations can be connected through direct feature fusion, cross-attention, or contrastive alignment (Tsai et al., 2019; Radford et al., 2021). Re-cent physiological studies further demonstrate cross-modal knowledge transfer: AnyPPG uses a pre-trained ECG encoder to guide PPG representation learning (Nie et al., 2026), while MindMix aligns EEG with pretrained acoustic representations (Liu et al., 2026). Common-specific factorization has also been explored to separate shared and modality-dependent information in multimodal learning (Qian et al., 2026; Meng et al., 2026). However, it remains unclear how multiple modality-specific FMs can be aligned into a shared and reusable representation. This is particularly challenging in sleep PSG, where modality configurations vary substantially across datasets/deployment settings.

4 EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Pre-training and downstream data. We perform multimodal alignment pre-training on 9,230 overnight PSG recordings from SHHS, MESA, and CFS (Quan et al., 1997; Chen et al., 2015; Red-line et al., 1995). Downstream evaluation spans 27 tasks across held-out Test SHHS1/2 and seven ex-ternal sleep cohorts (APPLES, WSC, SleepEDF20, HMC, ISRUC-1, MASS-SS2, and CAP) (Quan et al., 2011; Young et al., 2009; Kemp et al., 2000; Alvarez-Estevez & Rijsman, 2021; Khalighi et al., 2016; O’reilly et al., 2014; Terzano et al., 2001), together with seven non-sleep physiological datasets covering EEG, ECG, EOG, and EMG (Mumtaz et al., 2017; Miltiadous et al., 2023; Zyma et al., 2019; Tangermann et al., 2012; Clifford et al., 2017; Z´ u˜ niga-Castillo et al., 2025; Zheng & Lu, 2017). We evaluate SleepXA across these 15 datasets on 27 downstream tasks in three groups: 

general sleep representation , clinical and longitudinal prediction , and sleep-to-non-sleep physio-logical transfer . Complete dataset statistics, split construction, leakage control, and task definitions are provided in Appendix A.1. 

Evaluation and baselines. Foundation models are evaluated under linear probing (LP) and full fine-tuning (FT), using cross-subject splits whenever participant identifiers are available. We compare SleepXA against three groups of baselines: task-specific supervised models; physio-logical foundation models, including LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024) and CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025) for EEG and CSFM (Gu et al., 2026) for ECG; and sleep foundation models, with SleepFM (Thapa et al., 2026), SleepGPT (Huang et al., 2026), and OSF (Shuai et al., 2026) as the main baselines. Unless otherwise specified, SleepXA uses LaBraM and CSFM as its pretrained EEG and ECG en-coders, respectively. All methods use the same splits and metrics, with supported input modalities. Detailed optimization settings and task-specific evaluation protocols are provided in Appendices A.2 and A.3. 4.2 PERFORMANCE ACROSS DOWNSTREAM TASKS 

Figure 3 summarizes performance across the 27-task benchmark. To emphasize pretrained repre-sentation quality, we use an LP-first ranking protocol, with full details provided in Appendix A.3. 

SleepXA ranks first on 17 of 27 tasks and within the top two on 22 , demonstrating competi-tive performance across all three evaluation groups. The advantage is particularly consistent for general sleep representation and clinical prediction, while meaningful transfer is also retained on cross-context physiological tasks involving EEG, ECG, and multimodal inputs. Together, these results highlight the strength and broad transferability of SleepXA representations across diverse downstream settings.

We next evaluate epoch-level sleep staging as a direct test of cross-cohort transfer. On Test SHHS1/2 and the two external cohorts APPLES and WSC, SleepXA achieves the best performance among the evaluated foundation models under LP, reaching accuracy scores of 84.07%, 77.63%, and 85.16%, respectively (Table 1). Robustness to physiological foundation-model backbones. To test the FM-agnostic of our com-position, we test all 2 × 2 combinations of two EEG foundation models (LaBraM and CBraMod) and two ECG foundation models (CSFM and ECGFounder (Li et al., 2025)). Across all four pairs, pretrained initialization outperforms matched scratch training by 1.98-6.12 points in accuracy on WSC sleep staging (Fig. 7; Appendix A.4.6), showing that the gains are not specific to the default LaBraM+CSFM configuration. 

Comparison with multimodal integration strategies. We compare SleepXA with representative fusion and alignment alternatives in four configurations: WSC sleep staging with full-PSG, core-PSG (EEG+EOG+EMG), or EEG-only inputs, and ECG AF recognition on CinC2017. SleepXA performs best across all WSC settings and shows a clear LP advantage on CinC2017, while FT largely narrows the gap (Fig. 8). These results suggest that SleepXA’s alignment improves the quality of frozen representations by preserving and organizing complementary pretrained modality-specific knowledge. More details are provided in Appendix A.4.7. 

Ablation of major alignment objectives. We independently remove condition-aware alignment (CA), stage-conditioned specific preservation (SP), and condition-relative disentanglement (CD). As shown in Fig. 9, removing any module degrades WSC sleep staging and OSA severity prediction under both LP and FT. On CinC2017, alignment improves LP but adds little after FT, as FT may unlock AF-relevant knowledge already encoded in the pretrained ECG FM. Together, these results demonstrate each module’s complementary contribution, with their larger effect under LP indicating that they primarily improve pretrained representation quality (Appendix A.4.8). 

Alignment with limited sleep stage labels. To evaluate SleepXA under conditions of limited sleep stage labels, we compare four pretraining alignment settings: full labels, no stage guidance, hybrid pseudo label guidance, and partial label guidance. The latter two settings retained the expert SHHS labels and used pseudo labels or no stage labels, respectively, for MESA and CFS training. Both alternatives preserve much of the performance obtained with full labels, indicating that expert annotations are not required for every pretraining cohort. Even without stage guidance, WSC sleep staging performance decreases only modestly, although larger drops occur in OSA classification accuracy and AF recognition under LP. The AF gaps narrow after FT. These results suggest that 9Preprint SleepXA can adapt to incomplete expert annotations through pseudo-label or partial-label guidance, while the impact of removing stage guidance varies across downstream tasks (Appendix A.4.9). 

Robustness to modality and supervision constraints. Finally, we evaluate robustness to reduced sensor availability and limited downstream supervision. SleepXA remains effective with reduced PSG inputs, including EEG-only input. We further compare single-domain SHHS, matched multi-domain, and full multi-domain pre-training under different downstream labeling ratios. Full multi-domain pre-training most consistently benefits sleep-domain transfer, whereas gains on more distant tasks are more dependent on downstream supervision and domain shift. Details are in Appendix A.5. 

5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced SleepXA, a compositional framework that uses synchronized PSG to integrate inde-pendently pretrained physiological foundation models. SleepXA combines an asymmetric architec-ture with conditional common-specific alignment to capture cross-modal common structure while preserving modality-specific physiology. Across 27 downstream tasks spanning sleep representa-tion, clinical prediction, and daytime physiological transfer, SleepXA demonstrates strong transfer across cohorts, sensor configurations, and adaptation settings. Controlled comparisons and ablations further show that the gains arise not only from pretrained physiological knowledge, but also from how heterogeneous pretrained representations are composed. These results support a compositional alternative to training multimodal physiological foundation models from scratch: synchronized PSG can provide a common observational basis for aligning existing physiological foundation models into transferable multisystem representations. A.1 DATASETS AND EVALUATION PROTOCOLS 

We evaluate SleepXA using a broad downstream benchmark spanning general sleep representa-tion, clinical and disease prediction, and daytime physiological transfer. Table A.1 summarizes the dataset-level configuration, including signal modalities, sample sizes, and evaluation protocols, while Table A.2 provides the definitions and prediction targets of the 27 downstream tasks. For multimodal alignment pre-training, we use three large synchronized polysomnography (PSG) cohorts: the Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS; Visits 1 and 2) (Quan et al., 1997), the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) (Chen et al., 2015), and the Cleveland Family Study (CFS) (Red-line et al., 1995). To prevent subject leakage across longitudinal SHHS visits, we first hold out Test SHHS1/2, comprising 1,000 participants with PSG recordings available at both SHHS Visits 1 and 2 (2,000 recordings in total), from all multimodal pre-training. After this exclusion, alignment pre-training uses 9,230 overnight PSG recordings. Test SHHS1/2 is reserved exclusively for downstream evaluation and provides the held-out SHHS population used for demographic prediction, sleep anal-ysis, OSA assessment, and current and longitudinal clinical tasks. We further evaluate generalization on multiple external sleep cohorts that are never used during alignment pre-training. These include APPLES (Quan et al., 2011), the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort (WSC) (Young et al., 2009), SleepEDF20 (Kemp et al., 2000), HMC (Alvarez-Estevez & Rijsman, 2021), ISRUC-1 (Khalighi et al., 2016), MASS-SS2 (O’reilly et al., 2014), and the CAP Sleep Database (Terzano et al., 2001). Together, these cohorts provide complementary evaluations of sleep staging, respiratory-event detection, OSA severity estimation, sleep-spindle detection, and sleep-disorder classification under different populations, recording configurations, and cohort sizes. Daytime physiological transfer is evaluated on seven additional physiological-signal datasets, none of which are used during alignment pre-training. These comprise major depressive disorder (MDD) diagnosis from EEG (Mumtaz et al., 2017), dementia classification including Alzheimer’s dis-ease (AD) from resting-state EEG using OpenNeuro ds004504 (Miltiadous et al., 2023), mental-arithmetic (MAT) recognition from EEG (Zyma et al., 2019), motor-imagery classification using BCI Competition IV-2a (Tangermann et al., 2012), atrial-fibrillation (AF) recognition from single-lead ECG using the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology 2017 Challenge dataset (Clifford et al., 2017), constant-force hand-gesture recognition from surface EMG using DS1 (Z´ u˜ niga-Castillo et al., 2025), and vigilance estimation using SEED-VIG (Zheng & Lu, 2017). SEED-VIG is evaluated un-der both EEG-only and EEG+EOG settings, yielding eight transfer settings across the seven datasets. We use daytime physiological transfer as a compact umbrella term for physiological downstream tasks outside nocturnal PSG settings, including awake-state, ambulatory, and task-specific record-ings; the term does not imply that every recording, such as those in CinC 2017, was explicitly acquired during daytime hours. The downstream benchmark is organized into three groups. General Sleep Representation Eval-uation probes fundamental sleep representations through demographic prediction, sleep staging, apnea/hypopnea detection, and sleep-spindle detection, using both Test SHHS1/2 and external sleep cohorts. Clinical and Disease Prediction evaluates OSA severity, sleep-disorder classification, cur-rent disease status, and longitudinal prediction of subsequent sleep-related and cardiovascular out-comes. Finally, Daytime Physiological Transfer evaluates cross-context transfer from overnight PSG to independent non-sleep physiological tasks across EEG, ECG, EOG, and surface-EMG set-tings. When participant identifiers are available, we split the data by participant, keeping all record-ings and sessions from the same participant in the same split or fold. A.3 BASELINES AND DOWNSTREAM EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Baseline methods We organize the compared methods into three groups. (1) Non-foundation models include task-specific supervised architectures, general-purpose time-series models, and conventional self-supervised representation-learning methods. We select base-lines according to the characteristics of each task, prioritizing strong domain-specific models with public implementations when available and using a unified set of general-purpose baselines other-wise. The general-purpose baselines are selected following representative benchmark suites used in LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024), BrainOmni (Xiao et al., 2025), BIOT (Yang et al., 2023a) and CSFM (Gu et al., 2026). Depending on the input modality and task, these include ST-Transformer architectures for EEG (Song et al., 2021), CNN–Transformer models (Peh et al., 2022), ResNet1D-based architectures commonly used for ECG analysis (Ribeiro et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2026), general time-series models including TimesNet (Wu et al., 2023) and iTransformer (Liu et al., 2024), and ContraWR (Yang et al., 2023b) as a conventional self-supervised EEG representation-learning base-line. For sleep-related tasks, we additionally include representative domain-specific supervised meth-ods. For sleep staging, we compare against DeepSleepNet (Supratak et al., 2017), SleePyCo (Lee et al., 2024), U-Time (Perslev et al., 2019), AttnSleep (Eldele et al., 2021), XSleepNet (Phan et al., 2022), ASTGSleep (Chen et al., 2025), XSleepFusion (Hu et al., 2025c), and SleepXViT (Lee et al., 2025). For respiratory event detection, we include Hybrid Transformer (Hu et al., 2022), XSleep-Fusion (Hu et al., 2025c), and IPCT-Net (Hu et al., 2025b). For OSA severity estimation, because publicly available OSA-specific baselines are limited, we use the unified general baseline set above and additionally include TSDNet (Hu et al., 2025a) as an OSA-specific supervised method. For sleep spindle detection on MASS-SS2, we compare against RED-Time (Tapia & Est´ evez, 2020) and SUMO (Kaulen et al., 2022). (2) Physiological foundation models include LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024) and CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025) for EEG-based evaluation, and CSFM (Gu et al., 2026) for ECG-based evaluation. These baselines assess whether the proposed multimodal alignment provides additional benefits beyond large-scale unimodal physiological pretraining. (3) Sleep foundation models include SleepFM (Thapa et al., 2026), SleepGPT (Huang et al., 2026), and OSF (Shuai et al., 2026). We select these three major sleep foundation models as representative 18 Preprint baselines because they cover recent and complementary approaches to sleep representation learning while providing publicly available pretrained weights and sufficiently documented downstream eval-uation pipelines for reproducible comparison. Our goal is not to exhaustively enumerate all existing sleep foundation models, but to establish a representative and reproducible comparison set spanning different pretraining paradigms and input configurations. Whenever applicable, we use their offi-cially released checkpoints, supported input modalities, preprocessing pipelines, and downstream adaptation protocols. 

Common downstream evaluation protocol. We evaluate pretrained foundation models using lin-ear probing (LP) and full fine-tuning (FT). In LP, the pretrained backbone is frozen, and only a task-specific linear prediction head is trained. In FT, the prediction head and the backbone layers selected by the fine-tuning schedule are optimized jointly. Epoch-level tasks use individual epoch represen-tations, whereas whole-night and subject-level tasks aggregate epoch representations according to their task protocols. 

Cross-task ranking protocol. To emphasize pretrained representation quality, foundation models are ranked primarily by LP performance, which evaluates downstream utility without modifying the pretrained backbone; FT is used only to break LP ties. A supervised baseline is ranked ahead of a foundation model only when it surpasses both its LP and FT performance, so that task-specific supervised training is considered superior only when it also exceeds the fully adapted foundation model. For each task, ranking is based on its primary evaluation metric, such as Macro-F1 for classification tasks, AUROC for disease prediction tasks, and the corresponding regression metric (e.g., MAE or Pearson’s r) for continuous prediction tasks. For models with public implementations or pretrained checkpoints, we follow their documented configurations and adapt inputs as required. Downstream signals undergo dataset-specific prepro-cessing, including channel selection, filtering, resampling, normalization, and sample construction where appropriate, following established evaluation protocols from prior foundation-model stud-ies (Jiang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025), and the preprocessing recommendations provided with each dataset. Within each task, compared methods use the same splits, prediction targets, and eval-uation metrics. Input modalities follow each baseline’s supported configuration; for the sEMG task, EEG foundation models are used as the closest available baselines due to the lack of comparable pretrained sEMG foundation models. 

Evaluation metrics. We use task-specific evaluation metrics following commonly adopted pro-tocols for the corresponding benchmarks and prior literature. For sleep staging, each 30-s epoch is classified into five AASM stages: Wake, N1, N2, N3, and REM, following the AASM scoring manual (Berry et al., 2017). We report accuracy (Acc), macro-F1 (MF1), and Cohen’s κ, which measure overall accuracy, class-balanced performance, and agreement beyond chance, respectively (Supratak et al., 2017; Phan et al., 2022; Eldele et al., 2021). For respiratory-event detection, we report area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) and area under the receiver operating char-acteristic curve (AUROC), which are informative under class imbalance, together with accuracy for comparison with prior sleep-event detection studies. For obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) assessment, the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) is evaluated both as a continuous clinical measure and through conventional severity categories defined by AHI thresholds (Berry et al., 2017). Accordingly, we report mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and R2 for AHI regression, together with accuracy and macro-F1 for OSA severity classification (Hu et al., 2025a). For other disease and clinical classification tasks, we primarily use AUROC for imbalanced binary outcomes and MF1 for multiclass classification benchmarks. Regression and continuous physiolog-ical prediction tasks use the corresponding standard regression or correlation metrics. Metrics for all 27 downstream tasks are also summarized in Table A.2. Heterogeneous Sleep-Disorder Generalization. We further evaluate whether the learned repre-sentations transfer beyond sleep-disordered breathing to heterogeneous sleep pathologies using the CAP Sleep Database (Terzano et al., 2001). The cohort includes multiple disorder categories, in-cluding nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy (NFLE), REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), periodic leg movements (PLM), insomnia, narcolepsy, and other sleep disorders. We formulate a subject-level binary classification task that distinguishes participants with sleep disorders from healthy controls and use subject-level five-fold cross-validation because of the limited cohort size. We evaluate two sensing configurations: full multimodal PSG and a unified central-EEG setting. The full-PSG setting examines whether the multisystem representation transfers to heterogeneous pathological conditions, whereas the EEG-only setting tests whether disease-related information remains accessible under substantially restricted sensing. As shown in Fig. A.4, SleepXA maintains effective transfer across both settings. With full PSG, the frozen representation reaches 0.798 Macro-F1, indicating that disorder-related information is already accessible without adapting the pretrained backbone. A.4.4 DISCUSSION OF DAYTIME PHYSIOLOGICAL TRANSFER

Instance and sleep-stage alignment. The two terms in Eq. 2 implement contrastive alignment at the instance and sleep-stage levels. For Linst , synchronized representations from two available modalities of the same epoch form a positive pair, while eligible epochs from other participants in the same cohort serve as negatives. The objective is computed bidirectionally between each valid modality pair. For Lstage , we maintain learnable modality-invariant prototypes py for the five sleep stages. Each common representation is contrasted against these prototypes, with the prototype of its sleep stage as the positive. In the reverse direction, each prototype is contrasted against the corresponding batch stage centroids computed within each cohort and modality. These two directions are combined to obtain Lstage .

Modality-specific preservation. For each cohort and sleep stage, we consider the modalities ob-served in that group and normalize their specific representations and learnable prototypes. In the representation-to-prototype direction, each smi is contrasted against the prototypes of the observed modalities for stage yi, with qmyi as its positive target. In the prototype-to-centroid direction, we first compute a centroid for each observed modality within the cohort-stage group. Each prototype is then contrasted against these centroids, with the centroid of its own modality as the positive target. Both directions use temperature-scaled cross-entropy and are applied only when at least two modal-ities are observed in the group. The resulting terms are aggregated across valid stages and cohorts.
