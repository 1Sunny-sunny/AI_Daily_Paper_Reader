## ABSTRACT 

Speech brain-computer interfaces aim to restore communication by translating neural activity into text, yet end-to-end decoders have historically trailed cas-caded systems in accuracy. We introduce a Multi-Agent System for BraIn-to-Text (MAS-BIT), the first multi-agent framework for end-to-end brain-to-text decod-ing from intracortical recordings. MAS-BIT coordinates three specialized agents: a neural agent that interprets continuous neural representations, a phonetic agent that decodes soft phoneme posteriors, and an arbitration agent that integrates their complementary evidence with direct access to neural representations to generate the final transcription. The expert agents communicate with the arbiter through token-embedding-based latent communication, constructing continuous messages as probability-weighted combinations of token embeddings. These messages re-tain alternative token hypotheses and provide a differentiable interface through which sentence-level supervision jointly trains the neural encoders and special-ized agents across participants. MAS-BIT achieves state-of-the-art end-to-end decoding performance on the Brain-to-Text ’24 and ’25 benchmarks, with word error rates of 6.929% on ’24 and 3.606% on the ’25 public test set (3.892% on the private test set). These results surpass the published BIT cascade on the ’25 public set and narrow the gap on ’24, without ensembling independently trained models or using external language-model rescoring. 1

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Speech brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) aim to restore communication by translating neural activity associated with attempted speech into text (Akbari et al., 2019; Moses et al., 2021; Metzger et al., 2023; Willett et al., 2023; Card et al., 2024; Wairagkar et al., 2025). Accurate decoding requires recovering linguistic content from noisy recordings and limited paired training data. Cascaded sys-tems combine a neural-to-phoneme decoder with external n-gram/WFST decoding (and optional rescoring) to reconstruct sentences. In contrast, end-to-end systems connect neural representations directly to a pretrained language model and optimize for the target transcription (Feng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026). In the Brain-to-Text benchmark comparisons reported by Zhang et al. (2026), end-to-end decoding remains less accurate than cascaded decoding. End-to-end decoding is nevertheless attractive on both modeling and deployment grounds. By map-ping neural activity directly to text, it avoids the information bottleneck and error propagation of a separate phoneme-to-word stage, directly aligns neural representations with sentence-level ob-jectives, and leverages pretrained language models without fixed pronunciation lexicons or heavily tuned search heuristics. Moreover, conventional WFST-based cascades rely on a separate decod-ing stack of large n-gram/WFST graphs and external language-model rescoring that can impose hundreds of gigabytes of host-memory overhead (Table 2), whereas an end-to-end decoder offers a single, substantially lighter inference pipeline. We therefore ask whether end-to-end decoding can approach or surpass cascaded accuracy while preserving these advantages.

1arXiv:2611.29457v1 [q-bio.NC] 22 Nov 2026 Preprint A neural encoder provides two views of the same recording: continuous hidden representations and phoneme posteriors obtained from a supervised phoneme readout. The hidden representations expose learned neural features to a language decoder, while the posteriors organize this evidence around phoneme identities and their relative probabilities. Posterior-based interfaces in speech recognition illustrate how such structured distributions can condition a language model (Ma et al., 2025). For brain-to-text decoding, these two views suggest a division of labor: one expert interprets continuous neural representations, and another interprets explicit phonetic evidence. Their differ-ent inputs can lead to different transcription hypotheses, even though both originate from the same neural observation. We investigate whether these specialists can learn to provide complementary evidence for a common final transcription. The communication interface determines which parts of the experts’ judgments reach the final de-coder. Passing a single generated text sequence conveys the selected tokens but discards their rela-tive support against alternative tokens at each position. The discrete selection also blocks ordinary backpropagation through the message into the expert probabilities that produced it. Prior work on differentiable communication and continuous exchange between language-model agents provides alternatives to this text interface (Foerster et al., 2016; Pham et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2026). For neural decoding, we seek an interface that conveys information about competing token hypotheses and allows the final transcription objective to shape the experts through their messages. Here, we introduce an end-to-end Multi-Agent System for Bra In-to-Text ( MAS-BIT ) that separates specialist decoding from arbitration. A neural expert reads continuous encoder representations, a 

phonetic expert reads soft phoneme posteriors, and an arbiter integrates their messages with direct neural evidence. The roles share one language-model backbone with separate adapters. Rather than passing only selected text, the experts communicate through probability-weighted token embed-dings that retain alternative hypotheses and allow the final transcription loss to supervise the experts through their messages. Participant-specific input projections allow two BIT-initialized encoders to process recordings from either participant, and staged specialization followed by joint optimization trains the encoders and agents together in a single end-to-end system. We evaluate one jointly trained model on the Brain-to-Text ’24 and ’25 benchmarks, obtaining 6.929% WER on the ’24 holdout and 3.606%/3.892% on the ’25 public/private subsets. On the ’24 holdout and ’25 public subset, MAS-BIT achieves state-of-the-art end-to-end performance, surpass-ing BIT’s single-model decoder and end-to-end ensemble without ensembling independently trained models or external language-model rescoring (Zhang et al., 2026). It also outperforms the published BIT cascade on ’25 public and narrows the gap on ’24, while substantially reducing memory use and decoding time relative to the reproduced cascade and BIT + LightBeam (Feghhi et al., 2026). Controlled comparisons show that specialized experts outperform a single decoder given both views (including capacity-expanded variants) and sequential refinement controls matched in parameters and decoding passes; on the validation sets, the arbiter recovers correct transcriptions when both drafts are wrong. These results show that jointly trained specialists make better use of neural and phonetic evidence than feeding both views to one decoder or selecting between expert drafts. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Speech neuroprostheses and end-to-end neural decoding. Intracortical speech neuroprostheses commonly decode intermediate phonetic units before language-model-based sentence reconstruc-tion (Willett et al., 2023; Card et al., 2024). More recent work directly connects neural representa-tions to pretrained language models (Feng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026). In particular, BIT com-bines cross-task, cross-species neural pretraining with a phoneme-adapted encoder and an audio-LLM decoder (Zhang et al., 2026). MoDAl combines parallel neural encoders through alignment and decorrelation losses (Chen & Chin, 2026), while BrainWhisperer adapts a pretrained Whisper encoder–decoder to neural inputs (Boccato et al., 2026). Other direct decoders use a BART word decoder (Olak et al., 2026), transfer Wav2Vec2 representations to character CTC decoding (Fiedler et al., 2025), or train a character-CTC Conformer (Khanday et al., 2026). Fogg et al. (2026) train a shared neural phoneme decoder across six participants using participant-specific input projections.

Multi-agent and differentiable language-model communication. Multi-agent language-model systems improve generation by separating proposal generation from downstream aggregation (Du et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). MAS-BIT adopts a fixed two-expert–one-arbiter topology with a shared frozen language-model backbone and role-specific LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022). Each expert specializes in its input evidence, and its generated draft is replayed as latent messages optimized via the downstream arbiter loss. 

## 3 METHODS 

MAS-BIT maps a neural recording to a sentence through three language agents: two specialized experts, the neural expert N and phonetic expert P , and an arbiter A (Figure 1). The experts read complementary representations of the same recording and send latent messages to the arbiter. The arbiter combines these messages with direct neural evidence to produce the final transcription. ## 4 EVALUATION 

4.1 TASKS 

We evaluate MAS-BIT on attempted speech decoding using the Brain-to-Text ’24 and ’25 datasets from participants T12 and T15, respectively (Willett et al., 2023; 2024; Card et al., 2024). In this task, we record neural activity while participants attempt to articulate a sentence, and the decoder predicts the sentence’s transcription. We measure performance by corpus word error rate (WER): the total number of word substitutions, deletions, and insertions divided by the number of reference words; lower WER indicates more accurate decoding. We train one model jointly on 8,800 T12 and 8,072 T15 utterances, using participant-specific input projections and shared encoders, interfaces, and language agents. We use all 880 and 1,426 provided labeled validation utterances for checkpoint selection. Our benchmark comparisons, including all five controls, report performance on the hidden holdouts of 1,200 and 1,450 utterances. T15 pub-lic and private holdout scores are reported separately. Checkpoint selection minimizes the pooled word-edit count across both provided labeled validation sets, with decoding settings fixed before holdout evaluation. All predictions are generated without access to reference transcriptions, and every utterance in the declared split contributes to evaluation. Appendix A.5 gives details. 4.2 BASELINES 

We benchmark MAS-BIT against published end-to-end and cascaded decoders, examine candidate-sentence counts in our cascaded reproduction, and evaluate five core ablations of the multi-agent framework and adapter-capacity and larger-backbone variants of the single-agent control along with sequential dual-view refinement, detached-latent-message, and message-only arbiter controls. 

Published baselines. Our primary comparison uses reported corpus WER on matched partici-pant and hidden-holdout subsets (Table 1). End-to-end decoding without ensembling generates text from neural input without an external WFST, n-gram model, pronunciation lexicon, or indepen-dent language-model reranker; phoneme pretraining, auxiliary CTC losses, and staged training are compatible with this definition. (2026), we pass CTC phoneme logits to an OpenWebText2 5-gram language model and rescore the candidate sentences with OPT-6.7B (Zhang et al., 2022). We vary the number of retained candidates,

Each of five core controls degrades accuracy across all three holdout splits, isolating the roles of expert specialization, phonetic evidence, latent messages, cross-participant joint training, and staged training (Figure 3B–C; Table 16). 

Specialization makes better use of the same input evidence. Specialized experts and arbitration reduce WER relative to a single agent given both neural representations and phoneme posteriors, whose holdout WER is higher than MAS-BIT by 3.06 percentage points on T12 and 2.52/2.78 points on T15 public/private. ## 6 DISCUSSION 

Summary. We introduce MAS-BIT, an end-to-end speech decoder that combines neural and pho-netic experts through latent communication and signal-grounded arbitration. Rather than leaving interpretation and generation to one decoder, dedicated experts interpret continuous neural states and soft phoneme posteriors while an arbiter resolves their disagreements with direct access to the recording, recovering sentences missed by both experts. Jointly trained across participants after staged specialization, one model sets a new end-to-end state of the art on Brain-to-Text ’24 and ’25 (6.929% and 3.606%/3.892% WER), surpasses BIT’s published cascade on ’25 public and comes within 0.58 points of it on T12, outperforms reproduced cascades at every candidate limit, and aligns cross-participant interfaces with speech and language geometry while preserving distinct views (Ap-pendix E). 

Limitations and future directions. Our evaluation is offline and covers two participants. For online streaming BCIs, our encoders use bidirectional attention over complete utterances and se-quential drafting precedes arbitration; adapting multi-agent coordination to causal encoders and lower-latency message passing is an important next step, alongside scaling shared interfaces to larger cohorts, inner speech, and continual drift adaptation. Extending decoding to inner speech will also require explicit user consent and privacy safeguards (Kunz et al., 2025). 10 Preprint

## A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

A.1 DATA AND INITIALIZATION 

Section 4.1 describes dataset splits and checkpoint selection. Input features are concatenated as threshold crossings followed by spike-band power. Each input bin spans 20 ms, giving C = 256 channels for T12 and C = 512 for T15. The encoder uses Gaus-sian smoothing, non-overlapping five-frame patches, and seven bidirectional Transformer blocks. T12 uses 128 electrodes, block-wise z-scoring, and subsequent day-level normalization estimated from that day’s training trials. T15 uses all 256 electrodes and the normalized features supplied in the released HDF5 files. Participant-specific preprocessing retains the released 1,250/2,500-frame limits for T12/T15; both encoder trunks support the 2,500-frame horizon used for T15 input. Tran-scriptions are lowercased, and punctuation other than apostrophes is removed. Phoneme targets are generated from the transcriptions with g2p en (Park & Kim, 2019), with stress markers removed, spaces mapped to silence, and a final silence appended. The 41-class inventory contains 39 ARPAbet phones, silence, and blank. The two released BIT models supply their neural encoders, native input projections, and matching phoneme readouts. MAS-BIT introduces two cross-participant input projections, a neural fusion projector, two phoneme embedding tables, two message-role vectors, and three LoRA adapters on top of the pretrained Qwen text decoder. The fusion projector independently layer-normalizes both 384-dimensional encoder states, concatenates them, and applies a 768 → 1024 → 2048 MLP with GELU between the linear layers. The two 41-row phoneme embedding tables are initialized with standard deviation scaled by 1/√2 before their expected embeddings are summed. These are trained in a single run, beginning with the alignment stage. The neural encoder uses a width of 384, seven layers, six attention heads, a feedforward width of 512, GELU activations, and rotary positional embeddings (Su et al., 2024). Its participant-specific patch input layer applies LayerNorm, a linear projection from 5C to 384, and another LayerNorm. Gaussian smoothing has a standard deviation of two input bins (40 ms); embedding and Transformer dropout are 0.2 and 0.4. The text decoder has width 2048 and 28 layers. Each role’s LoRA is applied to the query, key, value, output, gate, up, and down projections, with rank 8, scaling parameter 32 (a factor of 32 /8), and dropout 0.2.

Role-specific prompts. Text delimiters identify each role’s continuous input, followed by an in-struction to produce an English sentence. The neural expert’s prompt names neural activity, the phonetic expert’s prompt names phoneme probability features, and the arbiter’s prompt names neu-ral activity and both experts’ transcript estimates. Every training example enters the augmentation pipeline. Time warping and masking follow prin-ciples used in SpecAugment (Park et al., 2019). First, we draw two interior cut points and remove duplicates to define temporal segments; we then resample each segment linearly by a factor drawn from N (1 , 0.05 2) and clip it to [0 .9, 1.1] . Trials shorter than nine frames retain their original time grid. We reject a proposed warp if it exceeds the input limit or leaves fewer encoder patches than required by the target-phone count plus adjacent repeated phones. The remaining transformations still apply when warping is rejected. Second, we mask each electrode independently with probabil-ity 0.05 , zeroing its threshold-crossing and spike-power features together across the trial. Third, raw temporal spans of 10–20 frames are sampled with a cumulative span-length budget of ⌊0.1Taug ⌋,where Taug is the post-warp sequence length; the last span is shortened to the remaining budget, and overlaps are allowed. Fourth, we add independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.2

in normalized feature units. These operations precede the encoder’s Gaussian smoothing. Let Saug = ⌈Taug /5⌉ be the number of valid augmented patches. After the patch input projection, sample a contiguous span length uniformly from 1 to ⌊0.2Saug ⌋ and place it uniformly among valid starting positions. Replace its patches with the frozen pretrained mask embedding, retaining se-quence length and attention validity. No span is applied when ⌊0.2Saug ⌋ = 0 . One augmentation realization is shared across the three agents for each example, including during the alignment stage. Evaluation retains deterministic smoothing and disables these stochastic corruptions. 16 Preprint A.4 OPTIMIZATION AND REPRODUCIBILITY

A.5 CHECKPOINT SELECTION AND EVALUATION 

Each epoch is evaluated by greedily generating sentences on all provided labeled validation utter-ances. The reference-word denominator is fixed, so minimizing edit count is equivalent to minimizing corpus validation-set WER. Ties retain the earlier checkpoint; stage transitions do not reset patience, and training may stop within a block. The T15 training set includes the three released “do nothing” control trials. Representations and references. We extract every provided validation utterance (880 T12 and 1,426 T15) using the frozen checkpoint’s FP32 inference path at batch size one. For each valid 29 Preprint time position, we retain the encoder states H1, H 2, the fused neural interface ZN , and the phonetic interface ZP defined in Equation 1. Each transformation, including the nonlinear fusion projector, is applied before temporal averaging. In the two-neural-expert control, the second input view is the output of its separate neural projector. The audio reference is the mean-pooled Qwen3-ASR-0.6B audio-tower output on fixed-voice TTS of the transcript; the text reference is the mean-pooled final-layer Qwen3-ASR-1.7B text-decoder state on the transcript. Comparisons across training states use the same reference features and utterance identities.
