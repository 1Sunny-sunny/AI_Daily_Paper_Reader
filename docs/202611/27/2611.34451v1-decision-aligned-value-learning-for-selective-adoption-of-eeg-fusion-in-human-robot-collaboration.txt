Decision-Aligned Value Learning for Selective
Adoption of EEG Fusion in Human-Robot
Collaboration
Xinjia Yu1, Jing Yang1, Yang Zhou1, Tielin Shi1, and Tao Cheng2,*
Abstract—Electroencephalography (EEG) captures neural ac-
tivity associated with human perception and decision-making.
It provides human-robot collaboration (HRC) systems with
measurable evidence for event recognition, situation assessment,
and action preparation. External sensing cannot easily provide
such evidence. However, the quality and complementarity of
this evidence vary across samples, and existing EEG fusion
methods do not explicitly determine when EEG improves a
prediction. We therefore framed selective EEG use as a sample-
wise incremental-value estimation problem and proposed Value
Estimation and Constraint-Aware Triage for Adoption (VECTA).
VECTA separates the generation of an EEG-informed Fusion
prediction from the decision about whether it should replace
the existing Base prediction. The framework uses system gain to
quantify the loss reduction from Base to Fusion and matching
gain to assess whether benefits arise from sample-matched EEG.
A signed-value learner estimates system gain from decision-time
evidence and prioritizes high-value Fusion outputs under budget
constraints. Cross-subject evaluations on three HRC datasets
showed that matched Fusion outperformed shuffled controls.
Estimated gains tracked realized gains, with Spearman corre-
lations of 0.347–0.612 and positive-gain areas under the receiver
operating characteristic curve (AUROCs) of 0.708–0.842. Under
budget constraints, VECTA reduced Brier loss and harmful
Fusion adoption and achieved the highest mean normalized
AUBC across three datasets among the selective-decision methods
evaluated. Validation on three external multi-modal datasets
showed that VECTA could be applied directly as a post-hoc
decision layer to task-specific predictors without retraining. These
results show that selective Fusion adoption can translate the
uneven utility of EEG into system-level gains.
Index Terms—Human–robot collaboration, EEG, multi–modal
information fusion, selective decision-making, incremental value
estimation.
I. INTRODUCTION
I
N industrial human-robot collaboration (HRC), safe and
efficient coordination requires robots to recognize workers’
ongoing actions and understand a broader range of infor-
mation about their human partners [1], [2]. Relevant human
information includes whether the worker is cognitively ready
and able to continue safely as task demands change [3],
understands the current task context, stage, and interaction
turn [4], [5], has perceived task-relevant events that may not
be fully captured by robot sensing or predefined rules [6]–
[8], and is preparing a particular action [9]. Access to such

information complements established sensing pathways and
provides a more complete and reliable basis for understanding
the human partner and supporting collaborative decisions [10],
[11]. These factors concern internal human states, task aware-
ness, cognitive appraisal, and emerging intentions. Existing
HRC perception systems primarily rely on vision, motion,
force, and system-state signals to characterize the external
environment and capture externally observable information
about the human partner, such as actions, postures, movement
trajectories, and physical interactions with the robot [12]–[14].
However, these interactions are largely external manifestations
of ongoing perceptual, cognitive, and physiological processes.
Conventional sensing focuses on these manifestations, but
much of the information about the underlying internal states
and evolving processes remains insufficiently captured [1],
[15].
Electroencephalography (EEG) has been widely studied in
HRC because it captures neural activity with high temporal
resolution [16]. Compared to peripheral physiological signals,
EEG provides more direct access to perceptual and cognitive
processes and to some related internal information that may
not be fully expressed through speech, actions, or explicit
commands [17]. These properties make EEG a promising
sensing modality. Despite this potential, EEG is not uniformly
reliable. It is affected by a low signal-to-noise ratio, artifacts,
intersubject variability, temporal nonstationarity, and task-
dependent changes in neural responses [18]. Its contribution
also varies across samples. For some samples, EEG improves
the existing prediction. For others, it adds little value or leads
to a worse decision than the established system would have
made without EEG [19]. Such sample-level heterogeneity calls
for the selective adoption of EEG-informed Fusion.
Existing EEG-assisted HRC studies have demonstrated the
task-level utility of neural information through task-relevant
decoding and multi-modal prediction [6], [9], [20]–[22]. These
findings show that EEG can provide useful task-level infor-
mation. The next step is to translate this utility into sample-
wise decisions. This raises two related questions. The first is
whether the incremental value of EEG over an established
non-EEG predictor can be estimated for each sample using
information available at decision time. The second is whether
these estimates can be used to determine which Fusion outputs
to adopt under a prescribed budget. Addressing these questions
can reduce the effects of sample-level unreliability and focus
EEG use on the decisions it is most likely to improve. This
downstream allocation problem is closely related to established

paradigms for selective decision-making, such as selective
prediction, learning to defer, and algorithmic triage [23]–
[25]. Selective prediction adds an abstention mechanism to
a predictor to manage the trade-off between predictive risk
and coverage [26]. Learning to defer determines whether
each instance should be handled by the model or delegated
to an expert, depending on which is expected to perform
better [27]. Algorithmic triage distributes a limited decision
budget across automated and expert decisions to optimize
overall system performance [25], [28]. However, the positive
incremental value of EEG cannot be fully characterized by
the confidence of either prediction alone [29]. This value
is the reduction in predictive loss when the EEG-informed
Fusion prediction replaces the Base prediction. Despite their
principled foundation for sample-wise allocation, the standard
formulations of these paradigms rarely account explicitly for
this nested relationship. The incremental value also depends on
the unknown ground-truth outcome and is therefore unavail-
able when the adoption decision must be made. This challenge
calls for a dedicated decision model to guide the selective
adoption of EEG-informed Fusion under such uncertainty.
To address this problem, we proposed Value Estimation and
Constraint-Aware Triage for Adoption (VECTA), a decision-
aligned framework for the selective adoption of EEG-informed
Fusion predictions. VECTA learns to estimate the sample-wise
system gain of adopting Fusion at decision time. Under a
prescribed adoption budget, it ranks samples by their estimated
system gain and adopts the Fusion predictions for the highest-
ranked samples. The main contributions of this work are
summarized as follows:
• We formulated the question of when EEG-informed Fu-
sion should influence the final decision as a sample-wise
incremental-value estimation problem. We introduced
system gain to quantify the benefit of Fusion over Base
and guide adoption, and matching gain to independently
audit the contribution of sample-matched EEG evidence.
• We developed VECTA to separate the generation of EEG-
informed predictions from the decision to adopt them.
Using the information available at decision time, the
signed-value learner estimated how much the prediction
loss for each sample could decrease or increase if Base
were replaced with Fusion. The resulting gain estimates
guided adoption under a given budget.
• We demonstrated the broader applicability of VECTA
by evaluating it on three primary HRC datasets and
validating it downstream as a post-hoc decision layer
over frozen multi-modal predictors on three external
datasets. The downstream results show that VECTA
yields measurable system-level gains when EEG provides
identifiable complementary evidence and conservatively
retains Base when positive EEG value is sparse. These
findings support the use of VECTA as a value-guided
decision layer in existing HRC prediction systems.

B. VECTA: Sample-Wise System Gain Estimation
The system gain Gs,i, defined in Sec. II-A, depends on the
ground-truth label yi and is therefore unavailable at decision
time. VECTA addresses this limitation by estimating Gs,i from
observable information for the current sample. Fig. 2(b) shows
the estimation workflow. The existing-system, EEG-only, and
EEG-informed predictors first produce their outputs. These
outputs are combined with descriptors of EEG quality and
temporal contribution to form a value feature vector. A signed-
value learner then maps this vector to an estimated system
gain bGs,i. This estimate represents the expected reduction in
prediction loss if the EEG-informed prediction is adopted.

adoption budget B

To examine whether matching-specific value can be inferred
from information available at decision time, an independent
copy of the signed-value learner is trained using Gm,i as
its target. This produces the auxiliary estimate bGm,i. The
auxiliary branch uses the same value-feature construction and
signed-value architecture as the main system-gain estimator
but is optimized independently. Its estimates are used to assess
the predictability of matching gain. The realized Gm,i values
for held-out samples are used to audit whether the samples
prioritized by VECTA benefit from correctly paired EEG.
Neither bGm,i nor the realized Gm,i enters the adoption policy.
All routing decisions are based only on bGs,i.
D. Decision-Aligned Value Learning
In each outer leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) split, one sub-
ject is held out for final testing, and the remaining subjects
are divided into F inner subject folds [32]. For each inner
fold, the upstream predictor is trained on the other F −1
folds and applied to the held-out subjects to generate Base,
EEG-only, matched-Fusion, and shuffled-Fusion predictions.
The F sets of out-of-fold (OOF) predictions are concatenated.
They are then divided by subject into fitting and validation
subsets for VECTA optimization and early stopping, respec-
tively. This procedure ensures that each prediction record
is generated by an upstream predictor that was not trained
on the corresponding subject. After the OOF predictions are
generated, the upstream predictor is retrained on all outer-
training subjects and applied to the outer test subject. Thus,
the upstream predictor is fitted F +1 times in each outer split.

III. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Experimental Setup
We evaluated VECTA on three public EEG datasets in-
volving human–robot interaction. ErrP-CoAdaptation [34] ex-
amined participants’ perception of errors in targets selected
by a robot. It contained 4,790 samples from 16 participants,
of which 2,399 were evaluation samples with an error rate
of 46.23%. ErrP-HRI [35] focused on distinguishing correct
from erroneous system outcomes following observed robot
actions. Of its 11,435 samples from 12 participants, 9,162
were evaluation samples with a system-error rate of 35.32%.
HRC-NextActor [4] was designed to predict the next actor (the
human or the robot) during collaborative task planning. It con-
tained 21,815 samples from 11 participants, of which 17,452
were evaluation samples with a 51.75% Human-class rate. The
Supplementary Material provides details of the dataset-specific
preprocessing, feature construction, and upstream predictions.
Together, these datasets covered human perceptions of ma-
chine decisions, task performance, and upcoming collaborative
actions. This coverage enabled VECTA to select reliable EEG
evidence to help the system better understand human cognitive
states.
We used outer LOSO evaluation, with 16, 12, and 11 folds
for ErrP-CoAdaptation, ErrP-HRI, and HRC-NextActor, re-
spectively. For inner subject-level cross-fitting, we set F = 3.
We assigned 20% of the subjects in each outer-training set to
the VECTA validation set for early stopping. Each outer fold
was repeated using seeds 42, 52, and 62, resulting in 117 runs.
We estimated all normalization statistics exclusively from the
outer-training data and did not use any held-out samples for
personalization.
VECTA used a two-layer MLP with a hidden dimen-
sion of 48; both were trained with AdamW at a learn-
ing rate of 10−3 and subject-disjoint early stopping. The
matching gain was estimated from eight within-subject and
within-stage EEG shifts. Exact-quota budgets were B
∈
{20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100%}, with 40% as the primary set-
ting. VECTA was compared to five established selective-
decision methods: SelectiveNet [23], L2D [24], LUT [25],
post-hoc L2D [27], and CL2DC [28]. Supplementary Section

IV. CONCLUSION
This study investigated when EEG should be used as
complementary evidence for an established HRC predictor.
We formulated this need as a sample-wise incremental-
value estimation problem and proposed VECTA, a decision-
aligned framework that selectively adopted EEG-informed
Fusion predictions. VECTA used system gain to quantify
the decision-level benefit of Fusion over Base and matching
gain as an independent audit of the contribution of sample-
matched EEG. A structured signed-value learner estimated
the otherwise unavailable system gain from prediction states,
model disagreement, EEG quality, and temporal contribution.
Ranking and policy objectives aligned the estimated value
with budget-constrained adoption. Across the three primary
datasets, VECTA produced system-gain estimates that signif-
icantly tracked realized gains. At a 40% adoption budget,
the selected Fusion outputs captured a disproportionately
large share of the full-adoption benefit in all three datasets.
VECTA also remained competitive with established non-oracle
selectors across different Base strengths and EEG utility pro-
files. Additional analyses showed that correctly matched EEG
carried task-relevant information, although its contribution to
the existing system varied across samples. VECTA reduced
harmful Fusion adoption, and the ablation results showed
that the complete objective improved global gain ordering.
In downstream validation using frozen upstream predictors,
VECTA yielded measurable gains on RITHM and EgoBrain.
It largely retained Base on Human-to-Robot Handover when
positive incremental value was scarce.
VECTA was evaluated retrospectively using precomputed
upstream predictions, and the adoption decision was made
after the Fusion output had been generated. Thus, the current
budget regulates the use of Fusion outputs in final decisions but
does not account for EEG acquisition or Fusion computation.
Future work will evaluate VECTA prospectively in real-time
HRC, develop uncertainty-aware value transfer across subjects
and tasks, and jointly optimize EEG acquisition, computation,
and adoption in multi-class and continuous decision settings.
