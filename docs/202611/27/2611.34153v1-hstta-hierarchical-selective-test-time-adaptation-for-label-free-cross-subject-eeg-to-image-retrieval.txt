Abstract 

Cross-subject EEG-to-image retrieval suffers from substan-tial performance degradation on new subjects due to subject shift. Test-time adaptation (TTA) can mitigate this shift by adapting pretrained models with unlabeled target-subject EEG. However, most existing TTA methods struggle to match supervision granularity to each query’s prediction confi-dence, leaving many queries without reliable pseudo-labels. To address this, we propose a novel framework named Hi-erarchical Selective Test-Time Adaptation (HSTTA). HSTTA builds a hierarchy over gallery image embeddings, uses hi-erarchical selective prediction to assign each EEG query a supervision granularity matching its current prediction con-fidence, and constructs adaptation objectives accordingly. We further propose Cross-Session Consistency Calibration (CSCC), an unsupervised method that calibrates the global confidence threshold to target a predefined accuracy level for hierarchical predictions. Moreover, we introduce Reciprocal Hierarchical Backoff (RHB), which moves queries lacking reverse-matching support to coarser nodes to mitigate error reinforcement. Experiments on two public benchmarks and ablation studies validate the effectiveness of HSTTA and its key components. On THINGS-EEG2, HSTTA outperforms the strongest EEG-specific TTA baseline by 17.9 (13.4) and 8.9 (3.7) percentage points in Top-1/Top-5 accuracy, respec-tively, under cosine (CSLS) ranking. Further analyses show that HSTTA yields more accurate pseudo-labels during adap-tation, supporting more reliable model updates.

On the widely used THINGS-EEG2 [9] benchmark, advanced methods have achieved high intra-subject Top-1 accuracy (over 90% in 200-way retrieval [17]). However, a substantial performance gap persists in cross-subject settings (Top-1 be-low 20% for most representative baselines [1, 33, 40]). This gap limits deployment, making improved cross-subject de-coding an important step toward practical EEG-based visual brain–computer interfaces. The cross-subject gap stems mainly from subject shift, where individual differences in visual cortex [27], head 1arXiv:2611.34153v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 anatomy, and electrode placement make the same image evoke different EEG responses across subjects. A com-mon approach is to fine-tune a pretrained model using a few labeled EEG–image pairs from the new subject [12], but this requires extra calibration recordings, raising deploy-ment costs. Test-time adaptation (TTA) instead adapts a pretrained model using unlabeled EEG from the new sub-ject, offering a way to handle subject shift without paired labels. Existing TTA methods adapt models through training-free feature transformations or gradient-based parameter up-dates [26]. Training-free methods fix the parameters and align test feature statistics [15] or estimate transformations of features or similarity scores from pseudo-labels [3, 16, 18]. Gradient-based methods typically update a subset of param-eters by minimizing entropy [20, 24, 36] or cross-entropy with pseudo-labels [14]. Therefore, pseudo-label reliability largely determines adaptation performance, making reliable selection central to TTA research. In cross-subject retrieval, single-image matches for new subjects are often unreliable, so directly applying entropy minimization or pseudo-labeling may induce confirmation bias [45], reinforcing errors and degrading retrieval perfor-mance. To mitigate this, existing methods typically select reliable matches using adaptive entropy thresholds [24], bidi-rectional consistency [3, 45], or confidence margins [14]. As Fig. 1(a) shows, selected queries receive single-image or partial-label supervision over candidate images [14], while less reliable ones are downweighted or excluded. However, many EEG queries lack sufficient confidence to support reli-able single-image matches, yet the model may still associate them with a group of similar images. For these queries, permissive filtering may introduce incorrect single-image su-pervision, whereas strict filtering may exclude them, leaving coarse-grained matching information unused. Thus, a key limitation of existing methods is their limited supervision granularity, which prevents them from fully adapting the supervision level to each query’s confidence. To address this limitation, inspired by hierarchical selec-tive classification (HSC) [11], we propose Hierarchical Se-lective Test-Time Adaptation (HSTTA), a novel framework that builds a hierarchical tree over gallery image embed-dings and assigns each EEG query a supervision granularity appropriate to its confidence. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the model may be uncertain whether a query matches a cat im-age but still confidently associate it with a group of animal images, enabling group-level adaptation. Specifically, we adopt HSC’s climbing rule, tracing parent links from the best-matching leaf to the first node meeting a global confi-dence threshold. Since calibrating HSC’s threshold requires labels unavailable in our TTA setting, we propose Cross-Session Consistency Calibration (CSCC), which calibrates the threshold without labels using cross-session prediction consistency of repeated EEG responses to the same stimulus. To mitigate the reinforcement of incorrect pseudo-labels dur-ing offline TTA, we further propose Reciprocal Hierarchical Backoff (RHB), which backs off queries lacking reverse-matching support to coarser nodes. The final selected nodes and their paths define the adaptation objectives. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: • To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to apply hier-archical selective prediction to TTA. We propose HSTTA, which assigns each unlabeled EEG query a supervision granularity appropriate to its prediction confidence and constructs adaptation objectives accordingly. • We propose CSCC and RHB to support reliable hierarchi-cal selective prediction without labels. CSCC calibrates the new subject’s global confidence threshold, while RHB mitigates error reinforcement during adaptation. • HSTTA substantially improves pretrained SAMGA’s [17] cross-subject retrieval, achieving the highest Top-1 accu-racy among evaluated methods on THINGS-EEG2 [9] and Alljoined-1.6M [43] in standard retrieval. Experiments with disjoint galleries and other encoders demonstrate its applicability. Adaptation analyses show higher pseudo-label selection accuracy than baselines. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. Cross-Subject Brain-to-Image Retrieval 

Cross-subject brain-to-image retrieval transfers models trained on source subjects to unseen subjects [15, 38], but inter-subject neural variability degrades accuracy [17, 47]. Some studies introduce subject-specific calibration mod-ules that require labeled target-subject data before deploy-ment [10, 25, 28, 42], while others learn subject-invariant representations [17, 37]. Recent studies explore label-free test-time calibration to improve cross-subject EEG-to-image retrieval. SATTC [15] recalibrates similarity scores through whitening, adaptive CSLS [21], and mutual-rank struc-tural priors. SCORE [3] recovers target-subject coordi-nates through orthogonal Procrustes alignment using mu-tual nearest-neighbor pairs. Unlike SCORE’s shared global transformation, HSTTA selects pseudo-label granularity ac-cording to each query’s prediction confidence and adapts the pretrained EEG encoder through gradient updates. 

2.2. Hierarchical Selective Prediction 

Selective prediction allows models to abstain when confi-dence is insufficient [7], whereas hierarchical selective pre-diction backs off to a coarser node of a class hierarchy when fine-grained predictions are uncertain [5, 41]. In image clas-sification, DARTS [5] predicts the node with the highest transformed expected reward on a WordNet-derived [30] ImageNet [4] hierarchy, tuning its trade-off parameter λ on validation set to meet a target accuracy. 2.3. Test-Time Adaptation 

Test-time adaptation adapts pretrained models to distri-bution shifts using unlabeled data. Among training-free methods, T3A [16] adjusts the classifier using class-wise pseudo-prototypes, while TDA [18] refines predictions through positive and negative pseudo-label caches. Among gradient-based methods, TENT [36] updates normalization-layer affine parameters through entropy minimization. For cross-modal tasks, TCR [24] addresses query shift through prediction refinement and representation regular-ization, CLIPTTA [20] uses a soft contrastive objective, and UATTA [45] guides adaptation using bidirectional retrieval disagreement. PASLE [14], our closest baseline, assigns sin-gle pseudo-labels to confident samples and candidate label sets to uncertain ones, progressively refining them during adaptation. However, its candidate sets are flat, margin-based sets that ignore the structure among gallery images, and in offline adaptation, where the same queries are revisited over many epochs, its progressive refinement can reinforce early pseudo-label errors. HSTTA instead selects each query’s supervision granularity on a hierarchy built from gallery em-beddings via HSC [11], while RHB backs off queries that fail reverse matching checks to coarser ancestor nodes.

4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Datasets and evaluation protocols. We evaluate cross-subject retrieval on THINGS-EEG2 [9] and Alljoined-1.6M [43] under leave-one-subject-out. Both test sets con-tain 200 unseen concepts, each with one image and ap-proximately 20 EEG trials per session across four sessions. Following SCORE [3], we evaluate standard 200-way re-trieval and transfer to an unseen gallery. For the latter, the test gallery is randomly split three times into disjoint 100-concept subsets A and B; we adapt using only A’s images and unlabeled EEG, then freeze the model for 100-way re-trieval on B. In this protocol, the Ward tree is built on A

only, with K ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 , 100 } and mid-level nodes at K ∈ { 16 , 32 }. We report final-epoch Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy under cosine and CSLS ( k = 10 ) ranking, av-eraged over held-out subjects and three adaptation seeds; unseen-gallery results are further averaged over the three splits, giving nine runs per subject.

4.2. Main Results 

Tab. 1 shows that HSTTA achieves the highest Top-1 ac-curacy on THINGS-EEG2 under both protocols. In stan-dard 200-way retrieval, it improves frozen SAMGA’s Top-1 accuracy from 25.65 (34.55) to 59.22 (61.22), exceeding SCORE and the strongest learnable baseline CLIPTTA by 17.9 (13.4) and 2.5 (4.1) percentage points, respectively (p = 0.07 (p = 0.004 ) against CLIPTTA). Although CLIPTTA achieves higher cosine Top-5 accuracy in this setting, HSTTA leads on all four disjoint-gallery metrics (all p < 0.05 ), demonstrating generalization to concepts excluded from adaptation rather than merely fitting the adap-tation gallery. TENT and UATTA underperform GA under both protocols, suggesting that repeated offline updates may reinforce unreliable predictions and limit adaptation gains. On Alljoined-1.6M, SAMGA reaches only 8.30 (10.57) Top-1 accuracy even on source subjects’ test images. Ahigh target accuracy α leaves most queries at coarse levels, so we lower α to 50% , yielding a level distribution com-parable to THINGS-EEG2 (Sec. 4.5). HSTTA leads on all four standard-retrieval metrics, but its gains over PASLE are not significant ( p > 0.05 ). On the disjoint gallery, learn-able methods generally underperform SATTC (SAW) and SCORE. With this weak backbone, unreliable fine-grained pseudo-labels may encourage overfitting and limit transfer, favoring training-free transformations.

4.5. Reliability and Sensitivity of CSCC 

Per-session averaged EEG views of the same stimulus pro-vide noisy observations of a shared evoked response, form-ing label-free positive pairs [32]. Inspired by using noisy observations as references [22], CSCC uses cross-session consistency as a label-free proxy for hierarchical accuracy: it checks whether one view’s pseudo-label set contains an-other’s top-1 retrieval and selects the smallest θ at which this consistency reaches the target α. In Fig. 3(a), empirical hier-archical accuracy generally tracks α and meets the target in 135 of 144 THINGS-EEG2 settings, supporting the proxy’s effectiveness in the evaluated settings. However, shared prediction errors across sessions can cause consistency to overestimate hierarchical accuracy, so the target accuracy is not guaranteed. Additional calibration analyses are pro-vided in the supplementary material. Fig. 3(b) examines HSTTA’s sensitivity to α. An overly high α pushes queries to coarse nodes or the root (bottom), reducing the number of queries receiving Lsup supervision and degrading perfor-mance (top, blue). An overly low α yields finer but less accurate pseudo-labels and can hurt held-out generalization (orange, THINGS-EEG2). Despite this accuracy–specificity trade-off [5], HSTTA remains stable around the selected α.

4.7. Effect of Supervision Granularity 

Fig. 5(a) shows that HSTTA excludes only 36 queries at epoch 0 and assigns correct multi-image supervision to 99 others, whereas PASLE and SCORE each exclude 96. Fig. 5(b) shows that, in standard retrieval, replacing the supervision of uncertain queries with single-image supervi-sion or excluding them reduces Top-1 accuracy from 59.2% to 50.7% and 54.9%, respectively. These results indicate 8that adapting the supervision granularity to each query’s confidence within the hierarchy exploits additional coarser-grained matching information and improves performance. 

5. Conclusion 

We presented HSTTA for label-free cross-subject EEG-to-image retrieval, using a gallery hierarchy to match supervi-sion granularity to each query’s prediction confidence and construct adaptation objectives accordingly. CSCC cali-brates the global confidence threshold without labels, while RHB mitigates pseudo-label error reinforcement. Analyses show that HSTTA improves over learnable TTA such as PASLE by producing fewer incorrect pseudo-labels and pre-serving useful matching information from uncertain queries through coarser supervision. Extensive experiments demon-strate the effectiveness of HSTTA, which offers a new ap-proach to label-free cross-subject adaptation.

S1.1. Datasets and Preprocessing 

THINGS-EEG2. THINGS-EEG2 [9] contains EEG recordings from 10 subjects viewing natural object images in a rapid serial visual presentation paradigm. Signals were acquired at 1,000 Hz with a 64 -channel EASYCAP arranged according to the 10–10 system, online band-pass filtered at 0.1–100 Hz, and referenced online to Fz, yielding 63 

recorded channels. The training set contains 1,654 concepts with 10 images per concept, each presented 4 times, totaling 

16 ,540 training images per subject. The test set contains 

200 concepts disjoint from the training set, each represented by a single image presented 80 times, 20 in each of 4 ses-sions. Following the standard preprocessing of [33], epochs are extracted from −200 to 1,000 ms relative to stimulus onset, baseline-corrected using the 200 ms pre-stimulus in-terval, cropped to 0–1,000 ms, downsampled to 250 Hz, and whitened with multivariate noise normalization. We keep the model-specific EEG preprocessing and image feature extrac-tion of each pretrained model unchanged from its original implementation. For TTA and evaluation, the 80 repetitions of each test stimulus are averaged into one query, yielding 

200 queries per subject. CSCC is applied once before adap-tation to calibrate bθ from the four 20 -trial session averages. 

Alljoined-1.6M. Alljoined-1.6M [43] contains EEG recordings from 20 subjects viewing the same THINGS images as THINGS-EEG2 in a rapid serial visual presenta-tion paradigm across 4 sessions. Signals were acquired at 

256 Hz with a consumer-grade 32 -channel Emotiv Flex 2 system. Each subject views the 16 ,540 training images about 

4 times each and the 200 test images about 80 times each, roughly 20 per session. Following the official preprocessing pipeline [43], epochs from −200 to 1,000 ms are baseline-corrected, downsampled to 250 Hz, whitened with session-wise multivariate noise normalization, and cropped to 0–

1,000 ms. Trials with missing triggers are discarded, and for any subject whose released data deviate from this pipeline, we regenerate the data from the raw recordings using the of-ficial code. Queries and CSCC session averages are formed as for THINGS-EEG2. 

S1.2. Baseline Implementations 

All methods start from the same frozen pretrained model and use the same queries, gallery, and evaluation under cosine and CSLS [21] ranking with k = 10 . Under the disjoint-gallery protocol, alignment statistics, fitted transformations, and model adaptation use only the EEG and images of sub-set A, while subset B is used only for 100 -way evaluation. Training-free baselines keep the encoder parameters fixed and transform only the frozen embeddings: • SATTC [15] recalibrates retrieval scores through whiten-ing, density-adaptive CSLS, and mutual-rank structural priors. Following SCORE [3], we use only its subject-adaptive whitening (SAW) step, which whitens target EEG embeddings with their own mean and shrunk covariance, as the full method with default hyperparameters underper-forms frozen SAMGA in our setting. • SCORE [3] comprises Recovery-Aware Source Training and Coordinate Recovery at Deployment. As all methods share the same frozen pretrained model, we use only the latter, which applies moment matching as in Eq. (3) and a regularized orthogonal Procrustes rotation fitted on mutual nearest-neighbor pairs under CSLS. Learnable baselines construct training objectives from label-free query–gallery predictions and update the encoder pa-rameters offline: • Global Alignment (GA) adapts the encoder with Lglob dist 

alone, aligning the per-dimension mean and standard devi-ation of rescaled EEG embeddings with those of the gallery without pseudo-labels. All other learnable baselines add 

Lglob dist to their own losses, with γ set as in Eq. (15). • TENT [36] minimizes the entropy of each query’s predic-tions over the full gallery. • TCR [24] treats each query and its top-1 image as a pseudo-pair, builds a reference queue from the least-shifted pairs early in adaptation, and uses this queue to jointly opti-mize a weighted entropy, a uniformity regularizer on query embeddings, and a term matching the modality mean gap to the queue’s estimate. • CLIPTTA [20] computes a soft contrastive entropy within each batch using each query’s top-1 image as its pseudo-label, while maximizing the marginal entropy over the gallery to prevent prediction collapse. We remove its memory bank. • UATTA [45] uses the frozen model once to select queries whose forward and backward retrievals agree, estimate their uncertainty from the discrepancy between bidirec-tional retrieval probabilities, and take each query’s top-1 image and several hard negatives as candidates. S1.3. Implementation Details and Hyperparameter Selection 

Environment. All test-time adaptation methods and eval-uations are implemented in Python 3.13 and PyTorch 2.7.1 with CUDA 11.8, and the complete list of dependencies and versions will be provided in our released repository. Each experiment is run on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. 

SAMGA Pretraining and Visual Features. SAMGA [17] takes multi-layer visual features as inputs to its image branch. Following Shallow Alignment [6], on which SAMGA builds, we extract the mean patch-token features from layers 20 , 24 ,

28 , 32 , and 36 of InternViT-6B-448px-V2.5 [2]. At test time, these five layers are fused using only the global layer weights learned during training, following the offi-cial cross-subject setting. On both benchmarks, we pretrain SAMGA under the leave-one-subject-out protocol with its official cross-subject configuration and use the final check-point after the full 50 epochs. EEG and image embeddings are retrieved in a 512 -dimensional shared space. 

Temperature Selection. The temperature τ is shared by the matching probabilities in Eq. (5), the training logits in Eqs. (12)–(13), and the predictive probabilities of the learn-able baselines. On THINGS-EEG2, we set τ without labels according to the sharpness of the frozen model’s matching probabilities. For each held-out subject, we compute the matching probabilities pi between the 200 queries and 200 

gallery images via Eqs. (3)–(5), and take exp( H(pi)) as the effective number of candidate images for query qi, which reflects how many images the model is effectively choosing among. This quantity equals 1 when the probability mass concentrates on a single image and k when it is uniform over k images, and it increases monotonically with τ . We therefore find, for each held-out subject, the τ at which the median effective number over all queries equals 4, and take the median across held-out subjects. This rule yields 0.0216 

for SAMGA, which we round to τ = 0 .02 , and after the same rounding yields 0.04 , 0.02 , and 0.05 for VE-SDN, NeuroBridge, and BrainHIVE in Sec. 4.4 of the main paper. 

Loss Weight γ. The weight γ in Eq. (15) is computed once at epoch 0. Using the initialized model and query local-ization, we compute the ℓ2 norms of the gradients of Ldist 

and Lsup with respect to all trainable parameters, and set 

γ = r ∥∇L dist ∥2/∥∇L sup ∥2, where r is the preset gradient-norm ratio. This value remains fixed throughout adaptation. At the start of adaptation, the gradient norm of γLsup is thus 

r times that of Ldist , making γ independent of the absolute 13 scales of the two losses. The learnable baselines follow the same rule, computing γ from Lglob dist and their own losses. 

S2. Semantic Structure of the Hierarchical Tree 

We provide additional implementation details for construct-ing the gallery hierarchical tree described in Sec. 3.2. Taking SAMGA as an example, we apply Euclidean Ward cluster-ing to the raw 512-dimensional embeddings from the frozen image branch. SAMGA trains a separate image projection head on each fold’s source subjects, so we build one Ward tree per leave-one-subject-out fold from its projected embed-dings. Using embeddings directly from a fixed pretrained image backbone would allow all folds to share one tree per gallery. Each tree is built solely from image embeddings and remains fixed during adaptation. Fig. S1 contrasts two types of hierarchies. Fig. S1(a) shows a WordNet-based [30] class hierarchy for selected concepts, the type of hierarchy used by DARTS [5] and HSC [11] on ImageNet. Fig. S1(b) shows the actual Ward tree of one held-out subject on THINGS-EEG2, from which we select four test images each of animals, food, and vehicles to illustrate how they merge level by level from single images at K=200 into three nodes at K=16 . Each circle marks the node containing the enclosed images. To quantify whether the Ward tree places semantically similar images in the same node, we use the 53 high-level categories of THINGSplus [34] as a reference and com-pute the pairwise precision [29] at each level K, i.e ., the fraction of within-node image pairs that share at least one high-level category. Randomly grouping the 200 test images gives an expected value of 14.9%. As shown in Fig. S2, the Ward trees built from the image-branch embeddings of SAMGA [17] and BrainHIVE [47] exceed chance at all lev-els, with pairwise precision increasing toward finer levels and reaching 63.0% and 56.0% at K=64 , respectively. Thus, the Ward tree groups semantically similar images without category labels, supporting the use of tree nodes as semantic neighborhoods for EEG queries.

S4. Generalization across Pretrained Models 

We extend the experiments in Sec. 4.4 of the main paper to more representative recent EEG visual decoding meth-ods on THINGS-EEG2 to examine the performance of TTA methods across them. Specifically, we evaluate eight pretrained models, namely NICE [33], ATMS [23], VE-SDN [1], UBP [40], NeuroBridge [46], BrainHIVE [47], Shallow Alignment [6], and SAMGA [17]. We observe that these models respond inconsistently to TTA. We first con-duct a basic analysis with the non-learned moment matching (MM) of SCORE [3], i.e ., Eq. (3) of the main paper, which aligns EEG embeddings to image embeddings dimension-wise. As shown in Tab. S1, MM does not improve, and even reduces, cross-subject accuracy on NICE, ATMS, UBP, and NeuroBridge, whereas it improves accuracy on VE-SDN, BrainHIVE, Shallow Alignment, and SAMGA. We con-jecture that the modality gap between the EEG and image embeddings of NICE and similar models lies not only in global location and scale but also in the relative relations among samples. Since MM only shifts and rescales the queries, it leaves these relations unchanged and may thus fail to improve, or even degrade, retrieval. Moreover, we conduct full TTA experiments on all eight models. All implementation details of HSTTA follow Sec. 4.4 of the main paper, and we report the accuracy at the last epoch. Results are shown in Tab. S2. Since HSTTA also builds on MM, it yields clear gains on all pretrained models where MM is effective, consistently outperforming PASLE and outperforming SCORE on all of them except Shallow Alignment. For NeuroBridge, on which MM is ineffective, HSTTA remains effective, suggesting that hier-archical selective supervision may mitigate the degradation caused by global alignment. On NICE, ATMS, and UBP, SCORE, PASLE, and HSTTA all degrade performance. We leave improvements for such models to future work. 

S5. Qualitative Results 

S5.1. Image Retrieval Examples 

Fig. S5 shows retrieval examples before and after TTA on THINGS-EEG2 with SAMGA as the pretrained model. The examples are from held-out subject 10, and all three methods 16 rank the 200 gallery images by cosine similarity. Before adaptation, SAMGA ranks none of the viewed images first. SCORE moves two of them to the top, whereas HSTTA ranks four first and raises the remaining coverall from 34th to 2nd, behind suit , another clothing item. In these examples, most of the other images that HSTTA ranks near the top are also semantically related to the viewed image. For cruise ship , HSTTA ranks aircraft carrier and boat right after it, whereas SAMGA and SCORE rank unrelated images such as treadmill near the top. Together with the disjoint-gallery results in Tab. 1 of the main paper, this indicates that HSTTA improves how the encoder maps the new subject’s EEG into the semantic image space, rather than merely fitting pseudo-labels on the adaptation set. Moreover, the gains of HSTTA are not limited to queries with correct single-image pseudo-labels. Turkey and coverall never receive a correct single-image pseudo-label during adaptation and are supervised mainly at coarse nodes, yet they still reach ranks 1 and 2, respectively. This is consistent with the findings on supervision granularity in Sec. 4.7 of the main paper.

Taking held-out subject 10 as an example, we visualize the
