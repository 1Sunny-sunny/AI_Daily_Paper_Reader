ABSTRACT
Visual brain decoders are typically trained and evaluated on stimuli drawn from
similar semantic distributions, leaving their ability to reconstruct unseen concepts
poorly understood. We study this concept generalization problem by withhold-
ing semantic concepts from a target subject’s training data. We find that three
recent fMRI-to-image decoders degrade substantially for the held-out concepts,
even when the concept remains available for multi-subject models. In contrast,
brain encoders that map a pretrained visual representation directly to voxels are
markedly more robust to the same semantic holdout. Motivated by this asymme-
try, we use such an encoder to generate synthetic fMRI responses for new im-
ages and add the resulting image-fMRI pairs to decoder training. Across three
decoders, four subjects, and twelve semantic concepts, synthetic data recovers a
substantial fraction of the performance lost on unseen concepts without requir-
ing additional, time-consuming fMRI acquisition. We further show that the same
approach improves general decoding performance across a wide range of data
budgets. Analyses of encoder training conditions, encoder designs, and synthetic-
data controls show that the gains depend on both encoder generalization and the
semantic content of the synthetic supervision, rather than merely increasing the
amount of training data. Together, our results show that encoder-generated fMRI
can transfer semantic generalization to decoders.

INTRODUCTION
Recent fMRI-to-image decoders produce reconstructions that are often semantically faithful to the
viewed stimulus (Scotti et al., 2024; Beliy et al., 2026). These results are obtained under a protocol in
which training and test images are drawn from the same pool of semantic concepts: a decoder asked
to reconstruct a zebra at test time has already been trained on other zebras and the responses they
evoked. The protocol therefore leaves a basic question unanswered: Can a decoder reconstruct a
concept it was never trained on? A deployed decoder will inevitably face such concepts. Shirakawa
et al. (2025) recently showed that the semantic overlap between training and test stimuli inflates
apparent generalization, but the only remedy available so far is the one that limits brain decoding in
the first place: collecting paired image-fMRI data for every new concept and every new subject, a
time-consuming and expensive process.
We make this question measurable with a concept generalization setting (Figure 1a). We take a
concept C as a COCO supercategory, remove every image containing C from the target subject’s
training set, and evaluate only on images containing C. Because standard reconstruction metrics
compare global embeddings and can score a reconstruction highly even when the object of interest
is absent, we complement them with object-detection metrics that ask whether C itself is recovered.
Under this setting, three recent decoders (Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023; Scotti et al., 2024; Beliy et al.,
2026) degrade sharply across four subjects and all twelve supercategories; e.g., MindEye2 (Scotti
et al., 2024) retains only 22% of its concept recall. Notably, the degradation persists even though C

remains available in the data of the other subjects used for cross-subject pre-training or joint training.
Decoders thus largely fail to transfer a concept seen in other brains to a subject who never saw it.
Brain encoders, which map images to fMRI, behave differently under the same holdout: when their
predicted fMRIs are decoded, an encoder trained without C from the target subject retains 87%
of its Object Recall, much higher than the case of the MindEye2 decoder (Figure 1b; Sec. 4.1).
Although these two ratios compare two specific pipelines rather than isolate an intrinsic advantage
of the encoding direction, the asymmetry suggests using the encoder to transfer its robustness to the
decoder through data. We generate synthetic fMRI responses for images containing C and add the
resulting image-fMRI pairs to the decoder’s training set. Because the intervention acts on the data
alone, it applies to any decoder architecture or training strategy without modification, and it requires
no additional scanning. Across the three decoders, the synthetic data recovers a large fraction of the
generalization gap without any real fMRI for C.
Since the encoder is trained on the same data as the decoder, why should its synthetic data help?
By varying the encoder’s training conditions and the composition of the synthetic data, we identify
two sources of the gain. First, the encoder is inherently robust to unseen concepts: even an encoder
that never saw C in any subject yields substantial gains, which we trace to its pretrained visual
backbone: a ridge regression on frozen backbone features already helps and a different pretrained
backbone gives similar gains, while a randomly initialized one gives almost none. This robustness
is not automatic across encoder designs, as synthetic data from SynBrain (Mai et al., 2025) gives
little recovery. Second, the encoder transfers exposure to C from other subjects, which decoders fail
to exploit on their own. Controls further show that the gain is not a data-volume effect; it requires
correctly paired synthetic responses to images of C itself, although the synthesis pool need not be
selected for C as long as it covers it.
Finally, the use of encoder-generated synthetic data is not specific to concept generalization. When
the synthesis pool consists of images unseen by the target subject, it improves all three decoders at
every target-subject data budget from 225 to 9,000 images for most metrics, by an amount equivalent
to 1.9–2.4× as many real training images, depending on the decoder (Sec. 5.3).
While using encoder-generated fMRI to train decoders is not new in itself (Sec. 2.3), our contribu-
tion is a systematic study of concept generalization in recent decoders and of the conditions under
which synthetic data addresses it. Our contributions are as follows. (i) We introduce the concept
generalization setting, with concept-level recall metrics, and show that three recent decoders fail
it even under cross-subject training. (ii) We show that brain encoders built on a pretrained visual
representation are far more robust to the same holdout, and that transferring this robustness through
encoder-generated fMRI for images of the held-out concept recovers a large fraction of the gap
across three decoders, four subjects, and twelve concepts. (iii) We show that the gain stems from the
encoder’s inherent robustness and from cross-subject transfer rather than from added data volume,
and identify what the synthetic data must contain (the held-out concept, correctly paired) and what
the encoder must provide (a pretrained visual representation with a direct readout to voxels). (iv) We
show that the same recipe improves decoding at various data budgets on most metrics, equivalent to
1.9–2.4× as many real training images.

BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
2.1
VISUAL BRAIN DECODING AND ENCODING
From neuroimaging experiments that produce paired visual stimulus-fMRI response data, two com-
plementary tasks can be defined: brain decoding and brain encoding. Brain decoding learns a map
from fMRI to image, reconstructing the stimulus given the brain response. Brain encoding inversely
learns a map from image to fMRI, predicting the brain response given the stimulus. One of the most
widely used datasets is the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) (Allen et al., 2022), where eight subjects
viewed images drawn from COCO (Lin et al., 2014). NSD is the main dataset we use in this work.
By incorporating diffusion models, brain decoding models rapidly evolved, albeit requiring sepa-
rately trained models for each subject (Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023; Scotti et al., 2023). Since this
paired data is costly, some recent decoders utilize cross-subject (pre-)training to help increase the

which models the response probabilistically, and TRIBE v2 (d’Ascoli et al., 2026), a multimodal
encoder which is also capable of predicting subject-agnostic cortical surface responses.
2.2
SEMANTIC GENERALIZATION FOR VISUAL BRAIN DECODING
Whether a decoder can handle concepts absent from its training set is a question that has been raised
before (Horikawa & Kamitani, 2017), but one that most recent decoding work does not engage with.
Since NSD draws training and test images from the same pool, Shirakawa et al. (2025) argue that
the resulting overlap inflates performance. They cluster NSD stimuli in CLIP text-feature space,
withhold one cluster from training, and find that decoder performance drops and that the standard
metrics overstate their generalization capabilities. Their proposed solutions concern dataset design
and representation choice rather than a method that closes the gap for an existing dataset.
We later propose a concept generalization setting that builds on this diagnosis. We define the held-
out concept from ground-truth annotations and evaluate decoders that train on multiple subjects
(Sec. 3.1), use additional metrics to measure whether the concept itself is recovered (Sec. 5.1), and,
unlike prior work, show a working solution that does not need to collect additional fMRI (Sec. 4).
2.3
USING ENCODER-GENERATED SYNTHETIC FMRI FOR DECODING
To reduce the burden of data collection, a line of work produces synthetic neural responses for
images that were never shown to the subject. Earlier work trains the decoder on such images
through a fixed encoder, which supplies surrogate responses (St-Yves & Naselaris, 2018) or a cycle-
consistency signal (Gaziv et al., 2022), the latter reporting gains on categories never seen in training.
Their unpaired image pool is, however, not chosen with the test categories in mind: in Gaziv et al.
(2022) the test categories are absent from the paired data and from the unpaired pool alike. More
recently, brain encoders have been used to generate synthetic fMRI for decoder training: Brain-IT
(Beliy et al., 2026) and SynBrain (Mai et al., 2025) add encoder-predicted responses to images drawn

DATA-LIMITED SETTINGS FOR BRAIN DECODING
As paired image-fMRI training data is slow and expensive to acquire, the data efficiency of brain
decoders is essential to gauging their practical utility. We use the term data efficiency to represent
the ability to attain satisfactory performance, even when the training data is limited for the target
subject (the subject it will be evaluated on). For decoders that train with multiple subjects (Scotti
et al., 2024; Beliy et al., 2026), a common practice is to only limit the data from the target subject,
not the other subjects. The reasoning is that in practice, data from other subjects is a one-time cost
shared by every deployed decoder, whereas target-subject data must be collected for each subject.
Target-subject data can be restricted along two distinct axes. The commonly addressed one is quan-
tity of the available data. The other is semantic coverage: which concepts those data depict. We
introduce the semantic axis as the concept generalization setting (Sec. 3.1), and the quantity axis as
the limited-data decoding setting (Sec. 3.2).
3.1
CONCEPT GENERALIZATION: LIMITING THE SEMANTIC COVERAGE
The concept generalization setting (Figure 1a) measures how a decoder behaves on a concept for
which the target subject contributed no training data. Given a target concept C, a concept which we
want to measure the generalization capability for, we exclude every image containing C from the
training set of the target subject. The model is then evaluated only on images containing C.
Defining the concept.
To introduce a large semantic gap between train and test time, we take C
to be a COCO supercategory, for example animal. We filter images using their ground-truth COCO
annotations, where an image is considered to contain C if its annotation includes at least one instance
of any subcategory belonging to C. We use ground-truth annotations rather than clusters in a learned
feature space (Shirakawa et al., 2025) for two reasons. A cluster-based holdout retains training
images in which the concept is present but not dominant, which leaks the concept into training; the
annotation rule removes it entirely. It also keeps the holdout independent of any model’s feature
space, so that the holdout and the evaluation are not defined by the same representation. We use all
12 supercategories, each defining its own training set and separately trained encoders and decoders.
Can concept exposure from other subjects solve concept generalization?
Given that recent de-
coding and encoding methods optionally allow multi-subject training, whether C is included for the
other subjects must also be accounted for. For the main experiments we assume C remains available
for the subjects used in pre-training, which we call the transfer-concept setting. For multi-subject
models, this gives an opportunity to learn about C from other brains, a question that per-subject
holdout protocols (Shirakawa et al., 2025) cannot pose. It also makes the setting deliberately favor-
able to existing models: if they still lose performance, concept generalization cannot be achieved by
their inherent cross-subject transfer capabilities and a different solution is needed. We additionally
explore settings where C is also absent for other subjects, as part of Sec. 6.
3.2
LIMITED-DATA DECODING: LIMITING THE QUANTITY
Most existing work on data efficiency instead limits the amount of data: the target subject’s training
set is subsampled to a small budget while the pre-training subjects retain their full data. We follow
the same protocol and specify the exact budgets in Sec. 5.

represent or even exclude some of the semantic content. For example, for our random 225-image
training splits, equivalent to the 1 hour splits from previous work (Scotti et al., 2024; Beliy et al.,
2026), on average 31.5 out of the 80 COCO categories appear on 5 or fewer images from the training

GENERATING SYNTHETIC TRAINING DATA VIA BRAIN ENCODERS
4.1
MOTIVATION FOR USING BRAIN ENCODER
Using encoder-generated synthetic data for concept generalization is motivated by the asymmetry
illustrated in Figure 1b. To compare the encoder and decoder on similar footing, we score the en-
coder by decoded recall (defined in Sec. 6): its predicted fMRI for images with C is reconstructed by
a fixed MindEye2 readout trained with C present, so that the readout’s own concept-generalization
limits do not confound the measurement, and scored with the concept metrics given in Sec. 5.1.
Under this holdout, the encoder retains 87% of Object Recall and 93% of Relaxed Recall, whereas
MindEye2 retains 22% and 37%, respectively. Since the encoder and the decoder convert infor-
mation in opposite directions, these two ratios describe two specific pipelines and do not isolate an
intrinsic advantage of the encoding direction; we use them as motivation, and test the actual utility of
the encoder’s predictions by training decoders on them (Sec. 5). The pattern persists with a readout
trained on real data only (Appendix D.1). In this comparison, the encoder is more robust than the
decoder, which motivates using its predictions to train decoders; Sec. 6 further examines why.
4.2
RECIPE FOR GENERATING SYNTHETIC TRAINING DATA
The cost of paired image-fMRI data lies in acquiring the fMRI, not the image. A brain encoder can
therefore supply the missing half by predicting responses to novel images, and the resulting pairs
can be used to train brain decoders. The procedure is summarized in Figure 2.
The recipe.
Let D = {(xi, yi)}N
i=1 denote the target subject’s training set, where xi is an image
and yi the corresponding measured fMRI response. We train a brain encoder Eϕ : x 7→ˆy on D,
initializing it from an encoder pre-trained on other subjects, so the encoder sees no target-subject
fMRI beyond what the decoder already has. Given a new pool of images U = {uj}, we form
a synthetic training set S = {(uj, Eϕ(uj)) : uj ∈U} and train the decoder on D ∪S. The
choice of U is the one component that varies across our experiments, and we specify it for each
setting in Sec. 5. Because this is a data-level intervention, the synthetic pairs can train any decoding
architecture without modification, regardless of its input shape or training strategy.

Evaluation.
We report the standard decoding metrics: low-level (Pixelwise correlation, SSIM,
Alex(2), Alex(5)) and high-level (Inception, CLIP, EffNet and SwAV). For concept generaliza-
tion experiments, we additionally report Object Recall and Relaxed Object Recall, adapted from
SEED (Park et al., 2026): using object detectors, we measure the ratio of reconstructions that ex-
actly contain the object (Recall) or at least contain the same supercategory (Relaxed Recall), limited
to objects that belong to C. For example, if C is animal, and the ground truth includes a zebra, Recall
counts the reconstruction correct if it also includes a zebra, while Relaxed Recall counts it correct
if it contains any animal. Identification metrics compare global embeddings against a distractor and
can remain above chance even when the held-out concept is absent from the reconstruction; Shi-
rakawa et al. (2025) report exactly this discrepancy under a semantic holdout. We therefore aim to
measure concept recovery directly through these metrics. Refer to Appendix B for more details.

from 1.7 to 2.9 (the remaining metrics fall in the same range; confidence intervals in Appendix G.4),
with a median over the seven identifiable metrics of 2.4 for Brain-Diffuser, 2.2 for MindEye2, and
1.9 for Brain-IT.
Extension to video decoding.
As a preliminary check of whether the recipe transfers beyond static
images, we apply it to fMRI-to-video decoding with NEURONS (Wang et al., 2025) on the cc2017
dataset (Wen et al., 2018), training on 10% or the full amount of data. The same Universal Encoder,
jointly pre-trained on NSD and the other cc2017 subjects, predicts a response from a single frame
of each clip, a framewise approximation that does not model temporal dynamics (Appendix F).
Synthetic data improves the video decoder at both budgets, with the largest absolute gains on the
semantic metrics: at the 10% budget, video-level 50-way accuracy rises from 0.185 to 0.235, recov-
ering 68% of the gap to the full-data model, and at the full budget the gains remain significant on
most metrics (Appendix F). That a still-image encoder improves a video decoder suggests the recipe
can apply beyond image decoding, even under this approximation.
6
WHY DOES ENCODER-GENERATED DATA HELP?
The encoder is trained on the same image-fMRI pairs available to the decoder, thus it uses no addi-
tional information from the data, yet its synthetic responses help the decoder. While related work
attributes this to data augmentation or data enlargement (Mai et al., 2025; Beliy et al., 2026), the
large improvement for concept generalization suggests the synthetic data contain additional infor-
mation for C. We first test whether this concept-specific information indeed drives the improvement,
then vary the encoder’s training conditions and design to locate where its knowledge of C comes
from, and finally ask whether standard encoder metrics can predict the value of its synthetic data.
8


Preprint. Under review.
Concept-specific content of synthetic data matters.
A simpler explanation of the gains in
Sec. 5.2 is that any additional training pairs help. We test this on subject 1 with two controls that
add the same number of pairs as the main condition, using encoder predictions for images without
C: non-C images pairs these predictions with the images they were predicted for, adding data but no
information about C; wrong fMRI pairs them with the C images instead, so the decoder sees C as
a target but never with the response it elicits. The controls recover 2% and 12% of the Object Re-
call gap between the unseen decoder and one trained on real fMRI for C, against 64% for correctly
paired synthetic data (Appendix D.3). Neither more data nor exposure to C images alone suffices;
the gain requires the encoder’s response to C itself. Two further controls refine this (Appendix D.3).
Shuffling the synthetic responses among the C images, which keeps supercategory-level information
but breaks the pairing, removes most of the gain, so correct pairing within the concept is needed.
Conversely, the pool does not have to be selected for C: a general pool fixed in advance recovers
nearly as much as targeted synthesis. What matters is whether C is represented in the synthesis
pool, not whether the pool was selected for it. Precision also recovers together with recall, and the
false-positive rate stays below that of a decoder trained on real fMRI for C (Appendix D.6), so the
gain is not explained by a tendency to generate C more often.
Comparing encoder variants.
Table 2 defines five variants along two factors: whether C was
available during cross-subject pre-training and during target-subject adaptation. The oracle variants
observe real target-subject responses to C and cannot serve as synthesis sources; they are reported
as upper bounds. Each of the three remaining variants is evaluated by a probe decoder, a MindEye2
trained from scratch (to prevent cross-subject information leak from decoder pre-training) on real
data plus that variant’s synthetic pairs. Transfer-concept is the variant used throughout Sec. 5.
Evaluating encoders.
We score each encoder in two ways. Fidelity is the Pearson r between
predicted and measured fMRI. Decoded recall measures how semantically decodable a prediction
is: we reconstruct images from the encoder’s predictions with a decoder, a MindEye2 trained with C
present from the full budget plus synthetic data, and apply the concept metrics of Sec. 5.1. Since the
decoder has seen C, it can detect and recover the semantic content from the encoder’s prediction. As
the decoder is not a perfect judge, we intend to use this only to compare encoders (Appendix D.1).
Where does the encoder’s knowledge of C come from?
We read Table 2 first by the probe de-
coder, since that is the quantity we ultimately care about. Scratch-blind, which never sees fMRI
for C from any subject, already recovers 41% of the Object Recall gap; cross-subject transfer is
therefore not required, and this baseline robustness must come from the encoder itself, which we
trace to its pretrained backbone below. At the full budget, pre-training on other subjects without C
(transfer-blind) adds almost nothing: Object Recall moves from 0.218 to 0.229, and the two probe
decoders are statistically equivalent (Appendix G.2). Exposure to C in the pre-training subjects
(transfer-concept) is what adds: the probe decoder improves on every metric in Table 2, by +0.049
in Object Recall, and reaches 59% of the gap. The gain thus has two sources: the encoder’s inherent
robustness to unseen concepts, and its transfer of concept exposure from other brains (Sec. 5.2). At
the finer granularity of single COCO categories (category generalization), every effect shrinks with
the semantic gap and the concept-specific transfer is no longer significant (Appendix D.2).
Overall encoder metrics do not suffice to predict the value of its synthetic data.
One might
expect a more accurate encoder to yield better synthetic data. The two transfer steps above show
otherwise: the step that barely helps the probe decoder, pre-training without C, raises Pearson r
by 0.060, while the step that helps on every metric, exposure to C, raises it by only 0.007. Pre-
training makes the predicted response more accurate overall; seeing C, from the target subject or
from others, is what puts C into it. Decoded recall is designed to measure the latter and does recover
this ordering (scratch-oracle exceeds transfer-blind by 0.118 and matches transfer-concept), but it
does not track the probe decoder either: it credits the two transfer steps about equally (+0.089 and
+0.119), whereas the probe decoder credits only the second. Overall fidelity is thus informative but
not sufficient. Across encoder designs, what tracks the decoder benefit better is the encoder’s fidelity
on the held-out concept itself: the CLIP score of images decoded from its predictions and within-
concept identification, which needs no decoder, both order the tested encoders by their decoder
benefit, whereas voxel-wise correlation does not (Appendix D.5). We present these as exploratory
diagnostics rather than validated predictors.

The encoder generalizes where the decoder does not.
The scratch-blind result shows that the
encoder predicts useful responses to a concept it never saw in any subject’s fMRI, and that a decoder
trained on those predictions inherits part of that ability. Since no fMRI for C enters that encoder, this
robustness should come from its pretrained visual backbone: the DINOv2 representation already
covers C, and a per-voxel readout fit on other concepts extends to it.
Consistent with this, freezing the backbone instead of LoRA-tuning it leaves the decoder gains
unchanged (Appendix D.4). We test this explanation directly by varying the encoder design (Ap-
pendix D.5). A ridge regression from frozen DINOv2 features, with no cross-subject data and no
backbone tuning, already yields useful synthetic data; replacing DINOv2 with OpenCLIP ViT-bigG,
the backbone MindEye2 uses, gives similar gains; and a frozen, randomly initialized backbone gives
almost none. The pretrained backbone therefore plays a major role, and the benefit is specific neither
to DINOv2 nor to this encoder. It is, however, not automatic across encoder designs: synthetic data
from SynBrain (Mai et al., 2025), retrained under the same holdout, gives little benefit when used
as synthetic training data. We therefore find this technique useful for encoders that map a pretrained
visual representation directly to voxels, but the benefit may vary depending on the encoder design.
This alone does not explain the asymmetry, since the decoders also build on pretrained components
(CLIP and diffusion priors) that have seen C and still fail; for example, the encoder built on Mind-
Eye2’s own backbone remains robust to the holdout while MindEye2 does not. The difference is
where that pretrained knowledge sits. For the encoder it sits at the input: C images already land in
a representation that handles them, and only a smooth map from that representation to noisy voxels
has to be learned. For the decoder it sits at the output: the decoder must learn which fMRI patterns
to map there, and for C it is never shown any. Synthetic pairs supply exactly the missing supervision,
with quality bounded by the encoder. Semantic coverage is therefore an axis along which such an
encoder degrades gracefully and the decoder does not, and encoder-generated data is a way to move
the decoder along it.
7
CONCLUSION & LIMITATIONS
We showed that in the concept generalization setting, in which a semantic concept is withheld from
the target subject’s training data, three recent fMRI-to-image decoders substantially lose their abil-
ity to reconstruct the held-out concept, even when it remains available from other subjects. Brain
encoders that map a pretrained visual representation directly to voxels, trained on the same data, are
far more robust to this holdout, and generating synthetic fMRI for images of the concept transfers
part of that robustness to decoders: across three decoders, four subjects, and twelve concepts, it re-
covers 37-87% of the gap on high-level and concept-recall metrics without any additional scanning.
Our analyses indicate that this gain stems from the encoder’s ability to predict responses to concepts
absent from its fMRI training and from its transfer of concept exposure across subjects, rather than
from added data volume. Beyond the holdout, the same procedure improves decoding on nearly
every metric at every data budget we tested, by an amount equivalent to 1.9–2.4× as many real
training images. More broadly, our results suggest that decoders should be evaluated on concepts
outside their training distribution, and that encoders offer a practical route to covering them.
Limitations.
Our suggested solution for concept generalization has two main limitations. First,
synthetic neural responses are not a perfect substitute for real ones: under an equal training budget,
real fMRI for C still outperforms encoder-generated fMRI. Synthetic data should therefore be used
when collecting real responses to C is impractical, not as a replacement for them. Second, synthetic
pairs for images of C must be included in the training set. With a general synthesis pool, C need not
be known in advance in the condition we tested, as long as the pool covers it (Sec. 6); a pool without
C gives no such gain, and a concept that becomes relevant only after the decoder is trained cannot
be accounted for without retraining or fine-tuning.

A
ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
Decoder training protocols.
As in the original paper, MindEye2 is first pre-trained on seven other
subjects and then fine-tuned to the target subject. We utilized the pre-trained MindEye2 checkpoints
provided by the authors, and only fine-tuned the models ourselves. Brain-IT is jointly trained with
all eight subjects. Brain-Diffuser is trained from scratch on the target subject as it offers no multi-
subject training method.
Decoder input format and the corresponding synthetic fMRI.
Depending on the decoding
model, the input fMRI voxel space may differ. While all models use single-trial GLM beta re-
sponses as the input, MindEye2 and Brain-Diffuser use the nsdgeneral region supplied by the NSD
authors which includes around 15k voxels, Brain-IT uses a larger region (Gifford et al., 2023), re-
sulting in around 40k voxels. Therefore, separate versions of encoders were trained for each fMRI
voxel format, and they were used to generate appropriate fMRI inputs for each decoder. For all eval-
uations of the encoder outputs such as Pearson correlation, the results for the nsdgeneral encoder
(corresponding to MindEye2 and Brain-Diffuser inputs) were reported.
Additional details for evaluation metrics.
The low-level and high-level evaluation metrics (In-
ception, CLIP, EfficientNet and SwAV) follow the protocol of previous brain decoding literature:
AlexNet(2), AlexNet(5), Inception and CLIP are two-way identification metrics, where the respec-
tive embeddings for the ground truth image, the reconstruction, and another reconstruction are ex-
tracted, and the percentage chance of the reconstruction being closer to the ground-truth than to
another reconstruction is reported. EfficientNet and SwAV report the average correlation distance,
calculated using embeddings extracted from the respective models.
Data counts for each supercategory.
Table 3 lists the number of training images containing C for
each target subject (out of 9,000) as well as the number of test images containing C (out of 1,000).
Images may contain more than one supercategory, so columns do not sum to the set size.
Subsampling method for limited data experiments.
Unlike previous work, which subsamples
by scan session and therefore retains an uneven number of repetitions per image, we subsample
by unique image and keep all of its repetitions, giving cleaner control over the budget. NSD shows
each image three times in random order across 40 sessions, so a session-based split exposes different
images a different number of times and yields training sets of varying size.
B
EVALUATION METRICS
We report the standard set of low-level and high-level reconstruction metrics used in prior
work (Scotti et al., 2023; 2024; Beliy et al., 2026). PixCorr is the pixel-wise correlation between
the ground-truth and the reconstructed image, and SSIM is the grayscale structural similarity in-
dex (Wang et al., 2004); both are low-level. Alex(2) and Alex(5) are two-way identification accu-
racies computed from the second and fifth layers of AlexNet, and Incep and CLIP are the corre-
sponding accuracies from InceptionV3 pool features and the final layer of CLIP ViT-L/14, form-
ing a sequence of increasing abstraction. Eff and SwAV report the average correlation distance in
EfficientNet-B1 (Tan & Le, 2019) and SwAV-ResNet50 (Caron et al., 2020) feature space, for which
lower is better.
All two-way metrics ask whether a reconstruction is closer, in the relevant feature space, to its own
stimulus than to an another reconstruction. They are therefore comparative, and a reconstruction
that is structurally plausible but semantically empty can pass them; this is the reason we adapt the
object-detection metrics of Sec. 5.1 for the semantic-holdout experiments.

OBJECT RECALL AND RELAXED RECALL
In order to supplement standard high-level metrics that compare global embeddings and can score
a reconstruction highly even when the object of interest is absent, we adapt metrics that focus on
whether the target concept itself was recovered. We follow SEED (Park et al., 2026) and run an
object detector, namely MM-Grounding-DINO (Zhao et al., 2024), on both the ground-truth image
and the reconstruction, then ask whether the detected content matches. Formally, we index every
quantity by the subject s ∈S and the decoder variant v ∈V. Let O (|O| = 80) be the set of COCO
subcategories and G the set of 12 COCO supercategories, which are the same supercategories as the
held-out concept C in the main text. Each subcategory has a parent supercategory given by the map
π : O →G, and for a set X ⊆O we write π(X) = {π(o) : o ∈X}.
We prompt the detector with all 80 COCO class names and, on both ground-truth and reconstructed
images, discard detections with confidence below 0.3 or bounding-box area below 50 pixels, to filter
noisy results.

Which model produces the reconstructions.
Each holdout excludes an entire supercategory, so
there are 12 separately trained models per subject and variant. Both metrics for a supercategory γ,

trained with C = γ: for example, zebra is scored with the animal-excluded model, bus with the
vehicle-excluded model, and so on. This keeps every number a concept measurement, and it is

bDi ⊆O for the detected object categories on its reconstruction. We only score an image if the
COCO annotation and the detector run on the ground-truth image agree that the object being mea-
sured is present. The annotation confirms the object is genuinely in the scene, and the ground-truth
detection confirms the detector is able to find it without any detector-side error. For a subcategory
o, the qualifying set Qo therefore contains the test images for which both Ai and Di include o. For
a supercategory γ, the qualifying set Qγ contains the test images for which both Ai and Di include
at least one object of γ.
This condition ensures that the trivial perfect reconstruction, which is a reconstruction identical to
the ground truth, always gets a perfect score for both metrics. Otherwise, even the perfect recon-
struction would not get a perfect score if any detector-side error occurs.
The two metrics.
Recall is defined per subcategory: Ro is the fraction of images in Qo whose
reconstruction contains the exact subcategory o. If the ground-truth image shows a zebra, the re-
construction must contain a zebra. Relaxed recall is defined directly on the concept: eRγ is the
fraction of images in Qγ whose reconstruction contains any object of γ, so a reconstructed giraffe
still counts even if the ground truth is a zebra, as a giraffe is still an animal just like a zebra. We
define relaxed recall at the image level rather than per subcategory because its hit condition does not
depend on which subcategory is present; computing it per subcategory would only reweight images,
for instance counting an image that contains both a zebra and a giraffe twice with the same outcome.
The relaxed variant is informative because a decoder that has never seen a concept often produces
a semantically adjacent object rather than an unrelated one, and this near-miss behavior is invisible
to exact recall. For any single image, an exact hit is also a relaxed hit, and Qo ⊆Qπ(o); the two
metrics are, however, averaged over different sets, so relaxed recall exceeding recall is an empirical
observation rather than an identity. The gap between them indicates how much of the supercategory
a decoder recovers without pinning down the specific object. Algorithm 1 states the full procedure.

D.1
DECODED RECALL FOR ENCODERS
Following prior work that evaluates encoders by decoding their predictions (Zhang et al., 2025; Mai
et al., 2025), we score encoders with the concept metrics of Appendix B.1. For every test image
containing C, we predict the target subject’s response, reconstruct images from the predictions with
a fixed readout, and apply Algorithm 1 unchanged. The qualifying sets depend only on annotations
and ground-truth detections and are therefore identical to those used for decoders. The readout is
the full-data MindEye2 for the same subject, trained with C present and with encoder-generated
synthetic data (Sec. 5.3), so it can read encoder outputs as well as any decoder we have. The
encoder retention in Fig. 1b is the decoded recall of the transfer-concept encoder divided by that of
the transfer-oracle encoder.
Because the readout’s synthetic training data come from an encoder that saw C, the readout is ex-
pected to favor the transfer-oracle variant over the held-out ones. The retention fractions in Fig. 1b
are therefore conservative, and the decoded columns of Table 2 should be read for ordering rather
than for the size of differences between the scratch and transfer families.
Scope of the comparison and a real-data-only readout.
The readout is intentionally trained with
C: when an encoder does produce a response that carries the concept, the readout has to convert it
into an image containing C, and a readout that never saw C would add its own concept-generalization
limits to the measurement. The retention ratios of Fig. 1b nevertheless compare two fixed pipelines,
which differ in their input, noise and readout, and do not isolate an intrinsic advantage of the encod-
ing direction; we use them as motivation for the method, whose utility is tested by training decoders
(Sec. 5).
To check that the pattern does not depend on the readout’s synthetic training, we repeat the analysis
on subject 1 with animal held out, using a MindEye2 readout trained on real data only (Table 8).
Each encoder’s retention is relative to its own oracle, decoded by the same readout. The pattern is
unchanged: the encoder retains most of its recall under the holdout, while the decoder retains almost
none. Retention is graded across encoder variants: it is highest with cross-subject exposure to C, and
still about half for scratch-blind, which never saw fMRI for C from any subject. The oracles exceed
real test fMRI, possibly because encoder predictions are free of trial noise.

Results.
Table 9 reports both controls for subject 1. Neither closes the gap: both fall well short of
correctly paired synthetic data on Object Recall (−0.21 and −0.18) and Relaxed Recall (−0.28 and

from correct pairing. The benefit of synthetic data therefore requires both that the images depict C
and that they are paired with the encoder’s prediction of the response to them. Neither additional
data volume nor exposure to C images alone is sufficient.
Within-concept shuffling and a fixed general pool.
The wrong-fMRI control pairs C images with
responses to images without C, so it cannot tell whether the remaining advantage of correct pairing
comes from coarse supercategory information or from image-specific information. Both controls
above also leave open whether the synthesis pool must be selected for C. We therefore add two
controls on subject 1 with animal held out (Table 11).
Shuffled within C pairs each C image with the synthetic response of a different C image, which
keeps supercategory-level information but breaks the pairing. It raises only Relaxed Recall (0.054
to 0.209), while the other metrics stay similar to or fall below the unseen baseline. Supercategory-
level supervision alone is therefore not enough: correct pairing within the concept is needed.
Fixed general pool replaces the C images with a pool that is fixed in advance and not selected for
the test concept: the entire training set of subject 2 (9,000 images, of which 1,929 contain animals,
against 1,934 held out from subject 1). It recovers nearly as much of the gap as targeted synthesis.

alone does not help on C, as the non-C control shows. Together, the two results indicate that what
matters is whether C is represented in the synthesis pool, not whether the pool was selected for it:

D.4
FROZEN ENCODER BACKBONE
The Universal Encoder adapts its DINOv2 backbone with LoRA during pre-training. If the en-
coder’s robustness to C came from this fMRI-driven adaptation rather than from the pre-trained
representation, freezing the backbone should reduce it. We retrain the transfer-blind and transfer-
concept encoders with DINOv2 frozen, so that fMRI training updates only the remaining encoder
parameters, and train the probe decoder on their synthetic pairs (Table 9).
Freezing the backbone has no detectable effect on the decoder gains from either encoder. For
transfer-blind, Object Recall is 0.222 with LoRA and 0.239 frozen (Relaxed Recall 0.432 and 0.447);
for transfer-concept, it is 0.301 and 0.287 (0.519 and 0.515). Both frozen encoders are statistically
equivalent to their LoRA counterparts on all four high-level metrics and both object-detection met-
rics, with 90% confidence intervals for the Object Recall difference within ±0.033 (Appendix G.3).
The low-level metrics are mostly inconclusive rather than different; the only significant difference
is a small SSIM decrease for transfer-blind (−0.003). Since transfer-blind never observes C from
any subject, its ability to produce useful responses to C must come from the pre-trained representa-
tion itself. The concept-specific advantage of transfer-concept over transfer-blind also persists with
a frozen backbone (Object Recall +0.049 [0.028, 0.070], Relaxed Recall +0.068 [0.039, 0.097],
both p < 0.001), so concept-specific transfer through other subjects does not require adapting DI-
NOv2. Whether freezing modestly reduces this advantage is unresolved (the difference in advantage
is 0.030 [0.000, 0.059] in Object Recall). Equivalence holds at the margin used throughout the paper
but not at a stricter margin of half the concept-specific advantage, so we cannot exclude differences
of that size. These results are for a single subject and do not account for variation across training
runs. Appendix D.5 tests the role of the backbone more directly.

Encoder-side diagnostics.
Table 14 compares encoder-side metrics with the decoder gap recov-
ered. Besides voxel-wise correlation and decoded recall (Sec. 6), we test two metrics that do not
require training a decoder with synthetic data. Decoded CLIP is computed like decoded recall, but
reports the CLIP metric of the reconstructed images. Within-concept identification needs no decoder
at all: it measures how often an encoder’s prediction is matched to the correct image among the
held-out concept images from training, after subtracting the mean response over all images.
Overall voxel-wise correlation does not consistently capture differences in synthetic-data utility, as
encoders with similar correlation can differ widely in their impact on the decoder. Decoded CLIP
and within-concept identification order the tested encoders by their decoder benefit, and decoded
recall agrees except for the two weakest encoders. Consistent with Appendix G.2, overall fidelity
is informative but not sufficient, especially across encoder families; what separates the encoders is
their fidelity on a concept held out from their training. In practice, both diagnostics can be computed
by holding out a proxy concept for which the subject has measured responses. We present them as
exploratory diagnostics rather than validated predictors, and a general-purpose metric for decoding
and other downstream tasks remains future work.

Encoder adaptation and synthesis.
The Universal Encoder (Beliy et al., 2024) is jointly pre-

the target subject. This joint pre-training spans two datasets, two field strengths and two stimulus
modalities at once, which the encoder accommodates without architectural change: all weights other
than the per-voxel embeddings are shared across voxels, subjects and datasets, so a new subject is
absorbed simply by adding its voxels to the embedding table. Because the encoder predicts responses
to still images, we sample a single frame from each 6-frame clip and treat it as the stimulus for the
corresponding fMRI sample. Given the synthesis pool U, we predict a response for each 2s window
independently, from its single frame. Following standard practice in video decoding, we apply a
fixed temporal offset between the video and the fMRI scans, using the same offset as NEURONS
when generating synthetic responses.

G
STATISTICAL TESTS
This section describes the statistical procedures behind the claims in the main text and the appendix,
and summarizes their outcomes. Point estimates are given in the corresponding result tables and
are not repeated here; per-contrast estimates, confidence intervals and p-values not listed below
are provided with the released results. Unless stated otherwise, tests are two-sided, p-values are
Holm-corrected within the stated family, and confidence intervals (CIs) are 95% percentile bootstrap
intervals from 10,000 resamples. Since each condition is trained once, all tests account for the
sampling of subjects, concepts and test images, but not for variation across training runs.
G.1
CONCEPT GENERALIZATION ACROSS DECODERS
Procedure.
For each decoder and metric, we average within each of the 48 subject-concept cells
(4 subjects × 12 supercategories) and compare conditions with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over
cells, Holm-corrected across the 20 planned comparisons per decoder (10 metrics × 2 contrasts).
CIs are bootstrapped over cells, stratified by subject, and recovery fractions are computed from
aggregate means. As sensitivity checks, we repeat each comparison with paired t-tests and with
linear mixed models with crossed random intercepts for subject and concept, and, for the two recall
metrics, with logistic mixed models on individual qualifying images. The two-way identification
metrics are computed against the full pool of remaining test images, so no distractor sampling enters
the comparison. The complementary test set (Appendix C.1) is analyzed in the same way, with 16
comparisons per decoder (8 metrics × 2 contrasts).
Results.
Both the concept generalization gap (Seen vs. Unseen) and the gain from synthetic data
(Unseen + syn. vs. Unseen) are significant for every decoder on every metric except SSIM, which
the holdout leaves essentially unchanged for Brain-Diffuser and Brain-IT and shifts by only 0.004
for MindEye2. All sensitivity analyses agree, with one exception: the PixCorr gain for Brain-
IT is significant under the Wilcoxon test (pHolm = 0.022) but not under the mixed model (p =
0.063). Object Recall improves for each of the twelve concepts under every decoder. Table 18
reports the fraction of the gap recovered by synthetic data, and Table 19 compares the effect of each
manipulation on images with and without C.

Probe decoder.
Transfer-blind and scratch-blind are statistically equivalent on nine of ten probe-

G.3
SYNTHETIC-DATA CONTROLS AND FROZEN BACKBONE
Procedure.
All models in Table 9 are trained on subject 1 and evaluated on the same test images,
so we use a paired stratified bootstrap over images. Images are resampled within each subcategory’s
qualifying set Qo for Object Recall, within each supercategory’s qualifying set Qγ for Relaxed
Recall, and within each supercategory’s test set for the remaining metrics. The same resampled
indices are used for every condition, and stratum sizes and macro-averaging weights are held fixed.
Each control is tested against the unseen baseline on all ten metrics (20 comparisons) and against
transfer-concept on the two recall metrics (4 comparisons). Equivalence, of the non-C control to the
unseen baseline and of each frozen encoder to its LoRA counterpart, is assessed with TOST at the
margin of Appendix G.2, reporting 90% CIs. SSIM is excluded from equivalence claims because
the holdout barely changes it, which makes its margin degenerate.
Results.
Both controls fall significantly below transfer-concept on both recall metrics (pHolm <
0.001). The non-C control is equivalent to the unseen baseline on all four high-level metrics and both
recall metrics, and significantly improves PixCorr, SSIM and Alex(5). Wrong fMRI significantly
raises both recall metrics and significantly lowers Alex(2), Alex(5) and SSIM.
Both frozen encoders are equivalent to their LoRA counterparts on all four high-level metrics and
both recall metrics (pHolm ≤0.002), with 90% CIs for the Object Recall difference within ±0.033.
The low-level metrics are mostly inconclusive; the only significant difference is a small SSIM
decrease for transfer-blind. With a frozen backbone, transfer-concept still significantly exceeds
transfer-blind on both recall metrics (pHolm < 0.001). Whether freezing reduces this advantage is
unresolved: the difference in advantage is 0.030 [0.000, 0.059] for Object Recall and 0.019 [−0.020,
0.058] for Relaxed Recall, and at a stricter margin of half the LoRA advantage, equivalence holds
only for Relaxed Recall of transfer-concept. Excluding the four subcategories with fewer than five
qualifying images changes none of these decisions.

G.5
ENCODER PRE-TRAINING ACROSS BUDGETS
Procedure.
Figure 5 compares three conditions per budget: the baseline and synthetic data from
either the pre-trained or the scratch encoder.
We use the image-level paired bootstrap of Ap-
pendix G.4, Holm-corrected within metric. The budget dependence of the scratch encoder’s relative
benefit is tested in two ways: a one-sided paired bootstrap of the change, from 225 to 9,000 images,
in the ratio of scratch to pre-trained improvement, and a mixed model on subject-level scores with
an encoder × log x term and a random intercept per subject.
Results.
Both encoders improve on the baseline in nearly every budget-metric contrast (47 of 48
for the pre-trained encoder and 42 of 48 for the scratch encoder; the exceptions are SSIM and, for
the scratch encoder, CLIP at three budgets). The pre-trained encoder’s advantage is significant in 41
of 48 contrasts; it is not significant for SSIM at 225-900 images, nor at 9,000 images for PixCorr,
Inception, CLIP and EffNet. Averaged over the four metrics in the upper panel of Figure 5, the
ratio of scratch to pre-trained improvement is 0.21 at 225 images and 0.77 at 9,000. Its increase
with budget is significant on all eight metrics, and the mixed model confirms the encoder × budget
interaction on all eight (pHolm ≤0.04).
