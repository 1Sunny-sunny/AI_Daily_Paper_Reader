Abstract
Domain shift is a major problem for training machine
learning models. While Continual Learning (CL) assumes
distribution shift during training and Continual Test-Time
Adaptation (CTTA) considers new domains at test time, se-
tups that require explicit online adaptation to domain shift
during both phases remain under-explored.
We propose
continual learning with domain preview (CL-P), a new com-
bination of CL and TTA where both train and test data suf-
fer from distribution shift. The training phase of CL-P is
analogous to domain-incremental learning while the testing
phase consists of a TTA phase on a future domain (which we
call preview), and an evaluation phase on all seen domains.
The model must then adapt to new domains at train and test
time. In this paper, we evaluate mainstream CL and TTA
methods that adapt only at train or test time, as well as their
naive combination. Experimentally, TTA heavily damages
the domain on which the CL method has just trained and
increases forgetting. Notably, this damage mostly comes
from the BatchNorm statistics shared by all domains. In
that sense, we propose DOSE (Dosing by Online Statistics
of Each domain), which allows a seamless combination of
any CL and TTA methods by adaptive domain inference at
test time, where the adaptation is applied to each image in
proportion to the probability that it comes from the preview
domain. Through extensive experiments with ResNet and
ViT backbones, various combinations of CL and TTA base-
line methods and datasets considering diverse domains, we
show that DOSE matches or improves on CL methods alone
in all 24 settings and on the naive combination in all but
one. The code is provided in the supplementary material.
1. Introduction
Continual learning (CL) and test-time adaptation (TTA) are
well-established paradigms in machine learning. Usually
studied separately, they both tackle the more generic prob-
lem of domain shift. In domain-incremental CL, a model
trains on a sequence of labeled domains and is evaluated on
how well it retains what it has learned. In continual TTA
(CTTA), a pretrained model adapts online to a changing
stream of unlabeled data [30, 31, 51]. However, in real-
world applications, a model can face both kinds of shift.
An image classifier, for example, may start receiving im-
ages from a new camera before anyone has labeled them,
while images from earlier cameras keep coming in. Until
these labels arrive, the same deployed model must classify
the images of every camera, so that its accuracy on the new
camera and on the earlier ones matters during this delay,
and not only after the next training phase.
We introduce continual learning with domain preview
(CL-P) to study how CL and TTA methods interact in this
situation (Fig. 1). Specifically, each task consists of three
phases. During the training phase, the model is trained on
a labeled domain with any CL method. During the preview
phase, it receives an unlabeled stream from the domain it
will train on next, and can adapt to it online with any TTA
method [33, 34, 49]. Finally, during the evaluation phase,
the adapted model is evaluated on all domains seen so far,
including the preview one. Similarly to label delay [10],
data thus arrive before their labels, except that the delay
here spans a whole domain.
On the one hand, CL methods alone have to rely on gen-
eralization when they encounter the preview domain, since
they have yet to train on that domain. Naturally, their per-
formance on the preview domain stays far below what they
reach once they have trained on this domain with labels
(Tab. 1). On the other hand, TTA methods can use the un-
labeled preview immediately. While they improve accuracy
on the preview domain, applying the adapted model to ev-
ery image can drastically reduce performance on the do-
main the model has just trained on. The direct combination
of CL and TTA methods (CL + TTA) therefore helps on the
preview domain, at the cost of accelerated forgetting across
tasks.
Experimentally, we find that most of this damage comes
from the BatchNorm statistics, which are shared by all do-
mains and re-estimated by TTA methods on the preview.

Notably, in our setup, the training phase and the preview
each contain images of a single domain, so the statistics of
every domain can be recorded separately. Therefore, we
propose DOSE (Dosing by Online Statistics of Each do-
main), which decides for each test image how much of the
preview adaptation to use. Specifically, DOSE compares a
few statistics of the early-layer features of the image with
the statistics recorded for every domain seen so far, and
weights the adaptation by the probability that the image
comes from the preview. Our contributions are as follows:
• We introduce continual learning with domain preview, a
setup that brings online supervised continual learning and
test-time adaptation into the same sequence of tasks.
• We analyze the strengths and limitations of CL and TTA
methods as well as their naive combination in our setup,
and show that most of the damage of TTA comes from
shared normalization statistics.
• We propose DOSE, a plug-in that estimates whether each
image comes from the preview domain and weights the
TTA update accordingly.
• We evaluate DOSE on six benchmarks with ResNet and
ViT backbones, four CL methods and 20 TTA methods,
and show that it matches or improves on the CL method
alone in all 24 settings and on CL + TTA in all but one.
2. Related work
Continual
learning.
In

learn-

regularization [23, 27],
replay [4, 6] and
prompt-based methods [43, 52, 53] limit forgetting, but
assume that the labels of a domain are available when it is
first met. Label delay [10] relaxes this assumption in online
CL, but its unlabeled data are the latest batches of the same
stream, so that the effect of adapting to a new domain on
the domains already learned is not measured.
Test-time data in continual learning.
DoSAPP [42] in-
terleaves supervised sessions with unsupervised ones on test
data from classes already seen, while ARC [7] corrects at
test time the bias of the classifier towards recent classes.
Both use test-time data to retain the past, not to adapt to a
domain that has not been trained on yet (Supp. Tab. 4).
(Continual) test-time adaptation.
TTA adapts a source
model to unlabeled target batches. It re-estimates normal-
ization statistics [26, 41], minimizes entropy [49], filters
samples [33, 34], adjusts the classifier [2, 22] or restricts the
updated layers [25]. Continual TTA [3, 11, 15, 24, 30, 31,
35, 38, 51, 55] adapts to a sequence of target domains and
fights error accumulation, while prompt- and adapter-based
variants [14, 29, 56] add extra parameters. These methods
are evaluated on the domain currently streamed, and some-
times on the source domain [33], but never on a sequence
of trained domains that a CL method keeps updating. Most
of them also normalize with the statistics of the test batch,
so their predictions depend on how a batch is composed, as
NOTE [15] shows. ReservoirTTA [48] routes each test sam-
ple to one of several adapted models by online clustering of
style features, but all its domains are adapted from a fixed
source model and never trained on with labels.

Per-domain normalization and experts.
Keeping nor-
malization statistics per domain is a known remedy for do-
main shift. DSBN [5] needs the domain of each test im-
age, AdaBN [26] re-estimates the statistics on one target
domain and UnMix-TNS [46] mixes several statistics per
image within one domain. In domain-incremental learning,
S-Prompts [52] infers the domain of a test image from fea-
ture centroids and other methods select a domain expert by
test-time training [45], but all of them learn each domain
from its labels before it is evaluated. Continual unsuper-
vised domain adaptation [8, 40, 44] adapts to a sequence of
unlabeled domains offline, with no later supervised task.
3. Continual learning with domain preview
In this section, we recall the CL and CTTA setups, then
show how our setup, continual learning with domain pre-
view (CL-P), combines them and why it requires its own
evaluation protocol.
3.1. Setup
Preliminaries.
Throughout this paper, a domain refers to
a distribution of images sharing the same label space but dif-
fering in appearance or content, such as images corrupted
by fog, paintings, or images taken by a specific camera.
In domain-incremental learning (DIL), a learner encoun-
ters domains 0, 1, . . . , T sequentially. At each task t, it is
trained on the labeled set Dtr
t and then evaluated on held-
out sets Dte
j of all domains j ≤t. In contrast, continual
TTA (CTTA) considers a trained model that adapts online to
a stream of unlabeled batches whose domain changes over
time, and evaluates it on this stream. We call a batch single-
domain when all its images come from the same domain,
and mixed when its images come from several domains.
Continual learning with domain preview lies in between
these two paradigms, where each task consists of three
phases (see Fig. 1).
Training phase.
During the training phase, the learner is
trained on Dtr
t with any CL method, as in DIL. Its batches
are single-domain, unless the CL method replays images of
older domains from a buffer, in which case they are mixed.
Preview phase.
During the preview phase, the learner re-
ceives an unlabeled stream Du
t+1 from domain t+1, which
the next task will train on, and it can adapt to this stream
with any TTA method. The preview thus lets the model
adapt to a new domain as soon as its first images arrive,
instead of waiting for their labels. Notably, all preview im-
ages come from this single new domain, so that the preview
batches are single-domain, which is exactly what TTA usu-
ally assumes. However, we consider that TTA methods are
only applied during the preview phase. The motivations be-
hind this restriction are twofold: 1) TTA methods require
additional computation at test time, which we consider lim-
ited in an online setup, and 2) TTA methods require test
data to come from one unique domain which does not dif-
fer significantly from the training domain (see [31, 34, 46]
and Sec. 4), which cannot be guaranteed outside the preview
phase.
Evaluation phase.
Finally, during the evaluation phase,
the learner is evaluated on all trained domains j ≤t as
well as on the preview images, before the labels of domain
t+1 arrive. Since the domain of a test image is unknown
in real-world scenarios, the evaluation batches are mixed
(Sec. 3.2).
Online data.
As in online CL with label delay [10], im-
ages arrive faster than they can be labeled. Each training
image is presented once in the CL stream, with replay al-
lowed for buffer-based methods. Each preview stream is
processed in one pass, and each preview batch is predicted
upon arrival1. Some TTA implementations additionally re-
visit unlabeled source training images to initialize auxiliary
statistics (Supp. Sec. C.1). Evaluation labels and the do-
main identities of evaluation images are unavailable. The
boundaries of the training and preview phases are known,
and each preview contains only the next domain.
3.2. Evaluation protocols
BatchNorm statistics.
A BatchNorm layer [21] normal-
izes each channel of its input with a mean and a variance,
then applies a learned affine map. During training, it nor-
malizes with the statistics of the current batch and keeps a
running average of them, which it stores and uses at test
time. Therefore, the stored statistics of a network describe
the data it was trained on, and every domain is normalized
with them. On networks with BatchNorm, most TTA meth-
ods re-estimate the normalization statistics on the test data,
which brings most of their gain [41, 49]. In our setup, TTA
thus replaces the statistics shared by all trained domains
with those of the preview. Therefore, we consider two pro-
tocols, which differ in the statistics used to normalize the
evaluation images.
Test-batch protocol.
In the test-batch protocol, which is
common in TTA, each image is normalized with the statis-
tics of its evaluation batch, so that its prediction depends on
the other images of the batch. While each batch holds a sin-
gle domain in CTTA, the domain of a test image is unknown

preview. In that sense, no prediction depends on the rest
of its batch, and mixed and single-domain batches give the
same score. We report both protocols, and every method is
always given the same statistics.

4. Limitations of existing approaches in CL-P
In this section, we analyze how existing CL and TTA meth-
ods, as well as their naive combination, behave in our setup.
We describe the main trends here, while Sec. 6 introduces
the benchmarks and gives the detailed results. Table 1 sum-
marizes these trends on ResNets.
Since the conclusions
hold for most TTA methods, each of its numbers is the me-
dian over the TTA methods we run that keep every core
component, then averaged over six benchmarks, rather than
the result of a single combination of CL and TTA methods.
CL methods ignore the preview domain.
Naturally, CL
methods are never exposed to the preview domain during
training, and they keep their weights unchanged during the
preview phase.
In that sense, they do not adapt to the
preview and rely solely on their generalization capabilities
when evaluated on the preview domain. As a result, CL
methods alone stay far below on the preview domain what
they reach once they have trained on it with labels (Tab. 1).
While ViTs generalize better across domains, this gap re-
mains large (Supp. Sec. G.1).
TTA damages the trained domains.
While various TTA
strategies exist, the main idea is generally to adapt the nor-
malization statistics (such as those of BatchNorm) or some
model weights, using the unlabeled test data. In our setup,
TTA is applied during the preview phase, where it updates
the model on the fly for the preview domain. Specifically,
when the adapted network is then used for every image with
4


the statistics estimated on the preview, the accuracy on the
preview domain rises sharply (Tab. 1). While this is espe-
cially beneficial for this domain, it comes at the cost of per-
formance on the previously learned domains, and above all
on the domain just learned, while forgetting also increases.
The cause is that all domains share the BatchNorm statistics
(Sec. 3.2). TTA methods re-estimate them on the preview,
and an old image normalized with the statistics of another
domain is misclassified. This is usually referred to as for-
getting of the source domain in CTTA [33, 51], except that
here it affects what the CL method has just learned. As a
result, naively combining CL and TTA methods performs
worse than the CL methods alone on average, even though
it improves on the preview domain (see Sec. 6.2 for a de-
tailed comparison).
Test-batch statistics are not a solution.
Since the dam-
age comes from the BatchNorm statistics, a naive fix is to
normalize each evaluation batch with its own statistics, as
TTA methods do on their test stream [41, 49]. Old images
then bring the statistics of their own domains, which re-
duces the damage a lot without removing it (Supp. Tab. 9).
However, this adapts to the evaluation data (Sec. 3.2), and
statistics estimated from a mixed batch fit no domain in par-
ticular, so that the same model scores clearly higher with
single-domain batches (Supp. Tab. 29), whose composition
cannot be chosen in practice.
Separate statistics would remove most of the damage.
Interestingly, most of the damage of TTA comes from nor-
malizing the images of every domain with the statistics of
a single domain, namely those that TTA estimated on the
preview. Specifically, when each evaluation batch holds a
single domain and is normalized with its own statistics (Or-
acle), each image is normalized with the statistics of its own
domain. In that case, the naive combination barely damages
the domain just learned, and memory-free CL forgets about
as little as a ViT, which keeps no domain statistics (Oracle
in Tab. 1). While test images cannot be sorted by domain
in practice, the training phase and the preview of our setup
each contain a single domain.
Limitations.
Overall, two limitations remain. On the one
hand, the domain of a test image is unknown, so that the
naive combination adapts every image. On the other hand,
all domains share a single network, whose statistics and pa-
rameters only fit one domain at a time. Therefore, a method
for our setup should infer the domain of each image without
labels, and give each image the statistics and parameters of
its domain, without storing images.
5. DOSE: Dosing by Online Statistics of Each
domain
In this section, we present Dosing by Online Statistics of
Each domain (DOSE) which builds onto the analysis of
Section 4. The main motivation is to find an efficient in-
ference policy that allows leveraging TTA methods without
interfering with the CL training scheme that can easily be
plugged into the pipeline. To do so, DOSE combines a do-
main detector, described in Sec. 5.1, which infers the do-
main of each test image, and an adaptive model, described
in Sec. 5.2, which sets the statistics and parameters used for
each image from those learned by CL and TTA and from
the detected domain. An overview of the methodology is
given in Figure 2.
5.1. Domain detection
By definition of our setup (Sec. 3.1), the training phase and
the preview each hold a single domain, so that the statistics
measured during each of them describe one domain. We
consider two kinds of domain shift. On the one hand, low-
level shifts, such as a new camera or a change of weather,
change the statistics of the pixels. On the other hand, se-
mantic shifts, such as paintings instead of photos, change
the content of the images, which appears in the statistics of
their features. Therefore, for each image x, DOSE computes
a vector s(x) that captures both, namely the channel means
and log-variances of the network input, for the pixels, and
of the output of the first block (ResNet first stage, ViT patch
embedding) of a frozen copy of the initial network, for the
features. The recorded statistics of a domain are the mean
and variance of s(x) over its training images, or over the
preview stream, which form a diagonal Gaussian of a few
kilobytes. The copy is frozen because the trained network
changes at every task, so that statistics recorded at one task
would no longer describe its features at a later task.
For a test image x, DOSE then infers the domain from the
recorded statistics. Under a uniform prior over the recorded
domains (the preview, the latest domain t and every older
one), the posterior is

Statistics of the trained domains.
The second branch of
Eq. (3) needs the statistics of each trained domain. The
latest domain uses the statistics stored in θtr during its
training phase. With a replay buffer, the training batches
are mixed, so that these statistics remain a mixture of all
trained domains and also serve the older ones. Without a
buffer, they follow the latest domain only, while normal-
izing the older domains with the statistics of the preview
instead, as CL + TTA does, makes their accuracy collapse
(Supp. Tab. 21). Therefore, without a buffer, DOSE keeps
a bank, namely a copy of the mean and variance of every
BatchNorm channel of θtr at the end of each training phase.
It stores no image, only these two numbers per channel and
per trained domain. However, the bank can become out-
dated. Unlike the recorded statistics, which come from a
frozen network, it describes the activations of the trained
network, which the CL method keeps updating after task k.
The images of domain k then no longer match the statistics
stored for them, and where the recorded statistics cannot
separate the domains, always keeping the bank can score
below the CL method alone. Therefore, a bank check de-
cides at each task whether to keep the bank. Since storing
labeled images would break memory-free CL, it follows the
test-then-train scheme of online CL, where each of the last
200 training images of the task is first predicted with and
without the bank, then trained on. The bank is dropped only
if it is significantly less accurate, under an exact one-sided
sign test at 5% on the images that exactly one of the two
classifies correctly. No image is kept, and 200 is the size of
the replay buffer of our experiments (Sec. 6.1).
Parameters outside BatchNorm.
TTA methods also
change parameters outside BatchNorm, such as the Layer-
Norm layers [1] of a ViT or the convolution weights [3, 51],
which can damage the old domains. With a replay buffer,
a shared-parameter check predicts the buffer images after
each preview with these changes either shared by every im-
age or dosed like the statistics, and doses them only if this
is significantly more accurate under the same test. Without
a buffer, the changes are shared as in CL + TTA.

The preview domain
has no classifier of its own yet, so DOSE builds class pro-

6. Experiments
6.1. Experimental setup
Benchmarks.
Since no existing benchmark provides the
three disjoint sets of each domain (Sec. 3.1), we build
six benchmarks (Supp. Tab. 8 and Sec. F) that cover the
two kinds of shift of Sec. 5.1.
Low-level shifts change
the statistics of the pixels, which is what TTA was de-
signed for. C100-C and IN-C follow the 15 corruptions
of CTTA [17, 51] on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet (14 tasks),
while C100-TF and Inet-TF (on Imagenette) start from
clean images, followed by five strong corruptions (5 tasks).
Semantic shifts change the content of the images, as in
domain-incremental CL. C20 changes the sub-class of each
of the 20 super-classes of CIFAR-100 at every task, while
DomainNet [36] changes the style across six domains. On
ViTs, we also use ImageNet-R [19] and ImageNet-A [20].
Backbones and CL methods.
As backbones, we use
ResNets (a ResNeXt-29 and a ResNet-50), whose Batch-
Norm layers are the source of the damage of Sec. 4, and a
ViT-B/16, which uses LayerNorm. The main CL methods
are experience replay with a buffer of 200 images (ER) [6],
a strong baseline of online CL, and fine-tuning without any
buffer (FT, memory-free), for applications that cannot store
images (Supp. Sec. E). We select one CL learning rate per
backbone on validation streams of other benchmarks, which
share neither the images nor the kind of shift of the evalu-
ated one, and keep it fixed during evaluation (Supp. Sec. D),
unlike online CL that adapts it [32]. TTA settings and im-
plementation changes are detailed in Supp. Sec. C.
TTA methods.
We run 20 TTA methods using implemen-
tations adapted to CL-P, with method-specific changes doc-
umented in Supp. Sec. C.1. Tables 1 and 2 report the median
over the 18 implementations (13 on ViTs) that keep every
core component of the original method, and the other anal-
yses average Tent [49] and ROID [31], which are typical
(Supp. Sec. G).
Baselines.
We compare DOSE with two baselines that use
the same CL and TTA methods. The CL method alone (no
TTA in the tables) ignores the preview and predicts every
image with the network θtr that it has trained. The naive
combination CL + TTA (+TTA in the tables) runs the TTA
method during the preview, predicts every image with the
adapted network, as TTA methods are usually deployed, and
starts the next task from this network, while DOSE starts it
from θtr (Sec. 5.2).
Protocol.
Unless stated otherwise, we use the batch-
independent protocol, and every evaluation batch mixes
all evaluated domains (Sec. 3.2).
All numbers are aver-
aged over 5 seeds, and gains are tested with a paired t-test
(Supp. Tab. 10).
6.2. Main results
The naive combination is worse than ignoring the pre-
view.
Table 2 gives the scores, and Supp. Sec. G.1 gives
the numbers behind the trends of Sec. 4.
On ResNets,
CL + TTA is below the CL method alone by 1.8 points with-
out memory and by 14.3 points with a buffer (Tab. 2), and
it is below in 11 of the 24 settings, although TTA raises the
preview domain by up to 26 points (Tab. 1). In contrast,
DOSE is at least as good as the CL method alone in 24 of 24
settings, by 7.7 points on ResNets and 0.9 points on ViTs
on average, and above CL + TTA in 23 settings, by 15.7
and 0.6 points. Method by method, it is at least as good as
CL + TTA in 280 of 304 pairs of method and setting, and the
medians over TTA methods in Tab. 2 are never more than
0.1 points below the CL method alone. Individual combina-
tions can perform substantially worse: on C100-C with ViT
and CoTTA, DOSE is 62.6 points below CL alone with FT
and 9.7 points below it with ER (Supp. Tab. 15). Taking the
median over methods for each seed, the gain is significant
(p < 0.05, paired t-test) in 21 of 24 settings against the CL
7


method alone and in 23 against CL + TTA (Supp. Tab. 10).
The appendix also gives the test-batch protocol, held-out
and balanced scores, other CL methods and segmentation
(Supp. Tabs. 22, 23 and 29 and Secs. H.4 and H.5).
Where the gain comes from.
DOSE without a TTA
method (Supp. Tab. 19) applies the recorded statistics, the
bank and the prototypes to the network that TTA never
adapted, which amounts on ResNets to per-domain Batch-
Norm with the inferred domain. It gives 3.7 of the 7.7 points
that DOSE gains over the CL method alone on ResNets (up
to 20.4 on Inet-TF without memory), and TTA adds the
other 4.0 (up to 14.2), while on ViTs the two parts give
0.4 and 0.5 points. TTA provides an additional gain on av-
erage, although the benefit depends on the method and set-
ting. Hard dosing with the inferred domain performs within
0.1 points of soft dosing in our ablation (Tab. 3). These
results support domain-specific statistics and routing as im-
portant components, but do not establish a substantial ad-
vantage of soft over hard dosing. TTA lowers DOSE in two
settings, by at most 0.4 points. Notably, Continual Nor-
malization [37], a BatchNorm-aware CL method, does not
replace the recorded statistics (Supp. Tab. 36).
Memory-free CL gains the most.
Without storing any
image, FT with DOSE reaches 76.7 on Inet-TF and 66.4 on
C100-TF, above ER alone with its replay buffer (70.8 and
63.1), since the recorded statistics and the bank give each
domain its own statistics. More generally, the gain follows
what TTA can learn, with 10.9 points on corruptions and 2.2
on the style shift of DomainNet on ResNets. On C20, whose
sub-class domains the recorded statistics cannot separate,
the bank check drops the bank, and DOSE achieves accuracy
close to the CL method alone (63.9 against 63.7 without
memory).
ViTs show smaller aggregate gains in our setting.
On
ViTs, TTA barely changes the score of the CL method
alone (+0.6 and +0.1 points), while DOSE gains 0.7 to 1.0
points on every type of shift and ties it on ImageNet-R and
ImageNet-A (Supp. Tab. 24). Although TTA gains up to 15
points on corrupted previews, the preview only weighs 0.17
to 0.29 of the score (Supp. Sec. H.9). DOSE runs at 840 to
2,900 images/s on ResNets (CL + TTA: 1,250 to 4,300) and
260 to 269 on ViTs (265 to 275, Supp. Tab. 25).

7. Limitations and conclusion
Limitations.
This work presents an attempt at combining
CL and TTA. However, the proposed setup could be ex-
panded further. Namely, we only studied the online case,
with one epoch of training and one pass over a preview
of a few thousand images per task. This limitation is even
more apparent with ViTs, as TTA gains increase with pre-
view length (Supp. Fig. 3), or the number of adaptation up-
dates. Moreover, while the bank stores no image, the bank
check that decides whether to use it relies on the last 200
training images of each task, which are not selected and
only come from the latest domain. It can therefore drop a
bank that would help the older domains, and it cannot see
damage that appears on older domains only, as in memory-
free segmentation. In addition, DOSE assumes that the pre-
view only holds the new domain. This allows us to isolate
domain-specific statistics, but future setups should consider
multi-domain previews. Fortunately, DOSE remains com-
petitive even in such scenarios as we briefly explored in ap-
pendix Supp. Sec. H.8. Where domains differ by content
rather than appearance, the recorded statistics cannot sep-
arate them, and DOSE may offer little benefit over the CL
method alone. Finally, DOSE only fixes how TTA is com-
bined with CL, and it does not change how the CL method
itself forgets. Future work should investigate how to pro-
vide an end-to-end method that does both TTA and CL.
8


Conclusion.
In this work, we introduced CL-P, a new
setup that aims to combine CL and TTA. In real-world ap-
plications, a model can see the next domain before its labels
arrive. TTA methods can use this preview, but on backbones
where every domain shares the BatchNorm statistics it costs
the domains the CL method has just learned. DOSE con-
trols adaptation using estimated domain membership and
improves aggregate performance in most evaluated settings.
Longer previews, sample selection for mixed previews and
recorded statistics that see content shifts are the next steps.

A. Comparison with related setups
Table 4 compares CL-P with the setups that are the closest to ours. On the one hand, CTTA, continual unsupervised domain
adaptation and initially labeled streams adapt to new domains without labels, but they never receive the labels of these
domains, so that no CL method trains on them afterwards. On the other hand, label delay, DoSAPP and ARC combine
supervised training with unlabeled data. However, in label delay, the unlabeled data are the latest batches of the same stream,
and the model is evaluated on these batches only. In DoSAPP and ARC, the unlabeled data come from classes already seen,
and they serve to retain the past. Therefore, CL-P is the only setup where the unlabeled data come from a whole domain that
the model will train on next, and where the adapted model is evaluated on every trained domain as well as on this domain.

B. Components, settings and costs of DOSE
CL training. ER with a reservoir buffer of 200 images (FT: none), one epoch per task, training batch 10, replay batch 64,
Adam without weight decay; learning rate 3·10−5 (ResNet), 10−5 (ViT), selected on validation (Sec. D), CODA-Prompt
10−3 (official); ViT: gradient checkpointing, inputs resized to 224. DER++: α = 0.1, β = 0.5. TTA stream batch size
100 (ResNet; DomainNet 64) or 64 (ViT); evaluation batch 100 / 64 / 32.
Hardware. The experiments ran on NVIDIA A4500, RTX 2080 SUPER, RTX 3080, RTX 5070, RTX A1000 and RTX PRO
6000 GPUs with PyTorch 2.6 to 2.8. Systems that are compared share the configuration and the seeds of their runs.
Preview pass. The TTA method is re-initialized from θtr(t) and run online, one pass over the shuffled preview, with its
official optimizer (Sec. C).
Recorded statistics (domain posterior). Frozen copy of the initial network, pruned to its first block (ResNet first stage, ViT
patch embedding). Per image, the moment vector s(x): channel means and log-variances of the network input and of
the output of this block. The recorded statistics of a domain are the running mean and variance of s(x) (parallel update
of Chan et al.) over its images: a trained domain’s over its last 1000 training images, accumulated during its training
stream with a no-gradient forward; the preview’s over the preview stream, on the forward the prototype step already
runs, prequentially. Posterior: diagonal Gaussians, uniform prior over the preview, the latest domain and every older
domain (--dose-rule posterior --dose-sensors stem0 --dose-domains all).
12


Dosing (BatchNorm). Equation (3) in every BatchNorm layer, per image: the preview’s statistics (exponential average
during the preview, momentum 0.1) with the adapted affine parameters, against the trained domains’ statistics mixed
by moment matching (mixture mean and variance) with θtr’s affine parameters. Trained domains’ statistics: latest
domain, θtr’s stored statistics; older domains with a buffer, the current stored statistics; without a buffer, the bank:
θtr’s stored statistics at the end of each domain’s task, two numbers per channel (0.2 MB per domain on a ResNet-50)
(--dose-split bn --dose-bank verify).
Dosing (head and other changes). Class prototypes built online during the preview (C × (d+1) numbers, prior-corrected
soft labels), added as log p + λ cos(z, µc)/τ, τ = 0.05, on every network. Changes of the TTA method outside Batch-
Norm: shared, or dosed per image after the shared-parameter check (module by module: convolutions and linear layers
by their output interpolation, LayerNorm and GroupNorm by the interpolation of the two affine maps; otherwise the
log-probabilities of θtr and of the dosed network mixed with λ) (--dose-shared verify).
Bank check and shared-parameter check (online). No image outlives its stream; both checks use 200 images, the size of
ER’s buffer (--dose-check online --dose-check-images 200). Bank (memory-free CL, tasks t ≥1):
during the last 200 images of domain t’s training stream, each batch is scored before the optimizer step that trains on
it, in eval mode, by the current network with every BatchNorm normalizing with the posterior mixture of the trained
domains’ statistics (the recorded statistics of the latest domain, built on this stream, against those of the older domains;
the latest domain’s statistics are the stored ones, an older domain’s come from the bank) and with the stored statistics
alone; two hit counts are kept, and the decision applies to the task’s deployment. Shared changes (when the TTA
method changed tensors outside BatchNorm): after the preview, the deployed network with those changes shared or
dosed scores the replay buffer (its images in storage order, at most 200); without a buffer the changes are shared. An
option is dropped (the bank) or switched (dosed) only if the alternative is significantly more accurate: exact one-sided
sign test on the discordant images, level 5%. The decisions hold for the task.
Test-batch protocol. Single-domain batches: every BatchNorm layer on the batch’s statistics with the adapted affine pa-
rameters (--route-adapted-bn batch --route-trained-bn full). Mixed batches, memory-free: θtr’s
branch normalizes each old image with a flat-top kernel average of its batch neighbours’ moments (bandwidth: median
distance to the B/8-th nearest neighbour), shrunk towards the pooled old images with a pseudo-count of one batch, and
with the latest domain’s stored statistics in proportion of its posterior (--dose-protocol official).
θtr. Stored as a diff of the tensors the TTA method changed (exact; tested), including the BatchNorm statistics that Tent,
EATA, SAR and ROID strip; a full copy when the structure changes (RoTTA, NOTE, ViDA, DePT, DPCore, DAS) or a
container owns a changed tensor (CoTTA’s ViT position embedding).
Next task. Training resumes from θtr, the network before TTA, which DOSE keeps for its trained branch (--train-from
snapshot). From the adapted network, as in CL + TTA, is an ablation, which changes the score by at most 0.4 points
on the benchmarks of Tab. 3, but costs up to 14.1 points on C100-C without memory (Sec. H.2). Conversely, giving
CL + TTA the same start does not close the gap. We also scored CL + TTA reset to θtr after each preview, so that the CL
method trains exactly as alone and CL + TTA only changes the network it deploys. Over the 950 runs behind Tab. 2 in
which this variant was logged (525 on ResNets and 425 on ViTs, not logged for Norm, ReservoirTTA and some methods
with a buffer), it scores 45.2 on ResNets and 67.2 on ViTs, against 45.4 and 69.0 for CL + TTA from its adapted network
and 58.3 and 70.1 for DOSE, which is above it in 90% of these runs. In the test-batch protocol, DOSE uses the same
test-batch statistics as the baseline (item above).
Overhead at test time. One pass through the frozen first block per image, the per-image mixing of every BatchNorm layer,
and, when the shared changes are dosed and cannot be split by module, a second forward pass of θtr. During the preview:
one extra forward pass per batch (recorded statistics, BatchNorm statistics and prototypes on the same forward).

ViDA, DePT, DPCore, DAS and POEM (Sec. C.1), and Tab. 11 gives it over every method. The adapted network is deployed

C.1. Implementation details of the TTA methods
Each implementation is integrated into one module of our framework and checked against available official code or the
published equations. The algorithmic code is unchanged, except for the changes below. SAR, Tent, Norm, LAW, and PALM
need none of the method-specific ones.
The official code assumes a single adaptation phase on a fixed source model. Four changes are therefore common to all
methods.
Reset. At each preview, the method restarts from the network that the CL method has just trained, θtr(t). Its source, anchor,
and teacher copies, its optimizer state, and its memories are re-initialized from this network.
Source data. Some methods read source data: EATA for its Fisher information, POEM for its protector, and ReservoirTTA
for its style statistics. They read, without labels, the training images of the domain the network was just trained on, at
the start of each preview. These additional source-data accesses are exceptions to a strict single-pass protocol.
Next task. When the next task keeps the adaptation (CL + TTA, and DOSE in the ablation of Tab. 31), it starts from the
network that the method deploys. For CoTTA, PETAL, RoTTA and ViDA, this is the teacher and not the internal
student. On a ViT, CoTTA’s student collapses during a preview while its teacher does not, and starting from the student
would train the collapse into the CL model.
Class count. Some entropy margins are hard-coded for the 1000 classes of ImageNet (0.4 ln 1000). This is above the maxi-
mum entropy of 10 to 100 classes and would disable the filter, so we set them to 0.4 ln C (EATA, RDumb, LCoTTA and
POEM). The redundancy margins of EATA and RDumb use the official value for the class count (0.4 for 10 classes, 0.1
for 100 and 0.05 for 1000), log-interpolated to 0.264 for the 20 classes of C20.
Method-specific changes:
RDumb. The official code is ResNet-only. On a ViT, our port adapts the LayerNorm affine parameters, as Tent and EATA
do. Its reset target is the network at the start of each preview.
ROID, CMF. Our ROID normalizes by the actual batch size instead of the configured one, and builds its augmentations at
the true input resolution. CMF shares the same port and loss code, and its DomainNet WeightNorm branch is not used.
PETAL. No SWAG checkpoint exists for our backbones. Its mean model is therefore the network before the preview and
its covariance is one, so its penalty becomes 1
2λ∥θ −θanchor∥2, which is negligible at the official λ = 10−8. We also
clamp a quantile index where the official code reads past the end of an array when the percentile is 1.
RoTTA. Its robust BatchNorm replaces BatchNorm at the first adaptation step, because doing it at construction would erase
the pretrained statistics before CL training. It is converted back to standard BatchNorm before the next task, with the
adapted statistics and affine parameters.
NOTE. The source model is trained with instance-aware BatchNorm from the start, as NOTE requires. The official code
predicts one image at a time. We predict a batch at once in evaluation mode, which gives the same predictions, and
insert its images in the memory in order.
Surgical. It adapts the first block, the choice of its paper for input-level shifts.
LCoTTA. The subspace is updated at every batch. The official 50 or 100 batches assume domains of about 780 batches,
while ours have 8 to 20, so the subspace would never be built.
ViDA. No ViDA checkpoint pre-trained on the source exists for our backbones, so the adapters start from zero. Its numbers
are therefore not directly comparable to those of its paper.
DPCore. The prompt is added to the first patch tokens instead of prepended, so that it is the identity at zero. Its source
statistics come from the training images of the latest domain.
DePT. No official code is released. Our port follows the paper: prompt tuning on a frozen ViT, pseudo-labels from memory-
bank prototypes, and a filter on agreement and confidence. An L2 pull of the prompt stands in for its hierarchical
regularizer.
14


DAS. It is run on ViTs only, as in the official configurations. Its labeled source batch is drawn from the replay buffer of the
CL method, the only labeled data the setup holds, so it runs with ER only. The official method draws it instead from
a knowledge base of class images generated beforehand with Stable Diffusion, which our setup does not have. We use
fp32 instead of mixed precision.
ReservoirTTA. It wraps Tent, EATA, SAR and ROID as in the official variants. Its style threshold comes from at least 1,000
batch styles of the training images of the latest domain, and the reservoir is rebuilt at each preview.
POEM. Its protector uses the entropies of the unadapted network on 2,000 unlabeled training images of the latest domain,
while the official code holds out 25% of a source validation set.
For DOSE, the hooks that record the statistics are re-attached to the network a method deploys before each recorded forward
pass, because PETAL and RoTTA create their teacher at their first adaptation step.

E. Backbones and CL methods
On C100-TF and C100-C we use the AugMix ResNeXt-29 [18] from RobustBench [9]. On Inet-TF, DomainNet, IN-C
and C20 we use the torchvision ImageNet ResNet-50 [16], with CIFAR images upsampled to 112 pixels. C20 needs a
backbone that has never seen the fine classes of CIFAR-100. The ViT is a ViT-B/16 [12] from timm. The CL methods
are experience replay with a buffer of 200 images (ER), fine-tuning without any buffer (FT, memory-free), DER++ [4] and
CODA-Prompt [43]. Each task is trained for one epoch with Adam. The learning rates, 3·10−5 for ResNets and 10−5 for
ViTs, are selected on validation, and CODA-Prompt keeps its own 10−3.
F. Datasets
We use six domain-incremental benchmarks with a fixed label space. C100-TF and Inet-TF (Imagenette) have 5 tasks each:
a clean domain followed by corrupted ones, generated once per image with the official code for Cifar100-C and ImageNet-
C [17] at severity 5. C100-C (14 tasks) uses the 15 corruptions of CIFAR-100-C. Each domain has its own fifteenth of the
CIFAR-100 training images, corrupted with the official generator. Its preview and evaluation images are disjoint halves of
the released test images, which every corruption reuses as in all CIFAR-100-C benchmarks. C20 (4 tasks) uses the 20 super-
classes of CIFAR-100. Each task uses a different fine class of every super-class, so the shift is semantic rather than statistical.
DomainNet [36] (5 tasks) uses the 100 most frequent classes over clipart, infograph, painting, quickdraw, real and sketch, a
shift of style. IN-C (14 tasks) uses the 15 corruptions of ImageNet-C at severity 5. On ViTs we also use ImageNet-R [19]
and ImageNet-A [20] (200 classes), whose test sets are about ten times smaller. Table 8 gives the number of images per
task. The training images of a task are labeled and seen once. The preview is the unlabeled stream that TTA adapts to, and
the evaluation set holds the held-out images of a domain. Training, preview and evaluation images never overlap, under any
corruption.
Data splits.
For each domain, training, preview and evaluation images form three separate sets. In particular, the preview
stream of domain t+1 and the labeled training set of task t+1 are two disjoint sets of images of the same domain. The next
task thus trains on the preview domain, but never on the preview images themselves, so that their evaluation does not depend
on what the next task learns.

G.1. Why the baselines fall short
This subsection gives the numbers behind the trends of Sec. 4, from Tabs. 1 and 9. On ResNets, the CL methods alone only
reach 30.4% on the preview domain without memory and 43.2% with a buffer, while the same domain reaches 71 to 72% once
it has been trained on with labels (Tab. 1). While ViTs generalize better across corruptions, the gap still remains 18 points
(63 to 65% against 82%, Tab. 9). With TTA, the accuracy on the preview domain rises to 56% (+26.0 and +12.5 points), but
the old domains drop by 11 and 24 points, and the domain just learned by 25 to 28 points (up to 65 on Inet-TF). Forgetting
also rises, from 34.1 to 36.6 points without memory and from 11.5 to 32.9 with a buffer. This forgetting is measured on the
deployed network at the last task, so that it includes the damage of the adapted network on the old domains. Before TTA, the
network forgets 32.9 points without memory and 9.0 with a buffer, against 33.5 and 12.2 for the CL method alone (seed 0 of
Tent, SAR, EATA and ROID on the six benchmarks). With test-batch statistics, the damage falls to about 8 points without
memory and 4 with a buffer, and with a buffer the old domains remain below the CL method alone (62.6 against 65.9). In
contrast, on single-domain test batches (Oracle), CL + TTA only loses 0.6 points on the domain just learned without memory
and 2.6 with a buffer, and the forgetting of memory-free CL drops from 34 to 10.9 points, close to that of a ViT (11.6).

G.4. Ablations and stored statistics
Table 20 gives every variant of DOSE on every benchmark, of which Tab. 3 keeps the most informative ones. Table 21 shows
why CL + TTA cannot simply keep the statistics it estimated during TTA: applying the statistics of the preview to every image
collapses the old domains, whichever way they are recorded.
Notably, a hard dose, namely per-domain BatchNorm with the inferred domain, scores the same as the soft dose within 0.1
points. The posterior finds the domain of 97% of the Inet-TF images, but only 27% for the sub-class domains of C20, where
chance is 25% (Fig. 5). At the last task, the bank check keeps the bank on Inet-TF and drops it on C20. The shared-parameter
check doses the changes of RoTTA [55], Surgical [25] and PETAL [3] in 86, 62 and 10% of the runs, while it shares those of
every other method.
Table 19 gives DOSE without a TTA method on every benchmark of Tab. 2. The preview is then only used for the recorded
statistics of the new domain and for the prototypes, which the unadapted network builds from its own predictions, while the
images of the new domain keep the BatchNorm statistics of θtr.

H. Additional analyses
H.1. Same statistics for every method
Since TTA methods are usually evaluated with the statistics of the test batch, which our main protocol forbids, we check that
the gain of DOSE does not come from the protocol. In Tab. 29, every method gets the same statistics. With the statistics of
each evaluation batch, which mixes all evaluated domains (Sec. 3.2), DOSE is above CL + TTA by 4.4 points without memory
and 3.6 with a buffer. On single-domain batches (Oracle), CL + TTA scores 7.0 points more without memory and 2.7 with
a buffer than on mixed ones, with the same model. In that case, DOSE reduces to CL + TTA with the prototype head and
is at most 0.6 points below it in all 12 settings, and up to 1.4 above. Without memory, DOSE with its own statistics stays
below the Oracle, by up to 13.7 points on IN-C, because single-domain batches hand CL + TTA fresh statistics of every old
domain, which memory-free CL cannot keep. With a buffer, DOSE is at most 0.5 points below it. UnMix-TNS [46], built for
correlated labels within one domain, is below CL + TTA with batch statistics in all 12 settings, and below DOSE in all 12.

H.2. Why the next task starts from θtr
With the next task from the adapted network, as in CL + TTA, DOSE on C100-C without memory reaches 60 to 61 points
with Norm, CoTTA and POEM, but 41 to 50 with Tent, RoTTA, SAR, CMF, ROID and EATA (Tab. 31). RoTTA changes
the structure of the network, so that without a buffer its changes are shared and DOSE equals CL + TTA. With the five other
methods, the older domains collapse right after the preview, from the second task on. With ROID, domains 0 and 1 fall to
14.9% after the preview of task 2, against 56.1% with Tent at the rate of the PALM benchmark (2·10−5), while their posterior
weight on the preview is about 3% with every method and the latest domain is unaffected. The damage therefore comes from
the statistics that the bank gives the older domains. These five methods adapt with a learning rate of 10−3 on CIFAR, and
C100-C has the longest preview of the CIFAR benchmarks (5,000 images, against 1,000 for C100-TF). The next task then
carries their larger change, and the statistics stored in the bank for the older domains no longer match their activations. The
bank check cannot see it, since it scores images of the latest domain only, which the bank barely affects. Therefore, DOSE
starts the next task from θtr (Sec. 5.2). Table 30 changes one choice at a time, at the official rates. Starting the next task
from θtr removes the collapse, and the three methods then score 60.3 to 60.9, above DOSE without a TTA method (58.2). A
preview of 1,000 images removes more than half of it (54.8 to 56.5). Without the bank, the older domains no longer collapse
at task 2, but memory-free CL then forgets them over the following tasks, as without DOSE.

H.3. TTA learning rate and cost
Every TTA method keeps its official learning rate, which its authors tuned on their own benchmarks and backbones (for Tent
on CIFAR, the value of the CoTTA code, Tab. 5). In our setup, the preview domain has no labels before its task, so that this
rate cannot be tuned, and it may be too large for a new backbone, benchmark or CL method. Notably, a too large learning
rate can make the TTA method collapse on the preview, and CL + TTA then predicts every image with the collapsed network.
Therefore, we test how each method copes with a wrong learning rate by multiplying the official one by ten (Tab. 17). In that
case, several methods collapse under CL + TTA, such as CoTTA on ViT C100-TF (1.6), while DOSE stays above CL + TTA
in 23 of 25 pairs and above the CL method alone in 16. A smaller rate does not protect CL + TTA either, since its damage on
ResNets comes from the statistics of the preview and not from the size of the update: on C100-C, Tent at 2·10−5 instead of
10−3 gives 43.8 against 42.0 without memory and 43.0 against 45.2 with a buffer. It only helps the adapted start of DOSE,
which scores 60.3 against 45.0 without memory, whereas the start from θtr scores 60.7 at 10−3 (Sec. H.2). Regarding cost,
DOSE adds one pass through the frozen first block per image. At the last task, an image costs 1.46 to 1.67 times the forward
time of CL + TTA on ResNets and 1.01 to 1.02 times on ViTs (Tab. 25).
H.4. Stability against plasticity
The score A averages the accuracy over all domains seen so far. Wang et al. [50] note that the average accuracy of continual
learning mostly rewards stability, and that plasticity needs its own measure. The same holds for A, where the preview
domain weighs only 1/(t+2) at task t. Table 32 therefore reports a balanced score B = 1
2(old + new), which gives the
preview domain the same weight as all trained domains together. Under B, the gains of DOSE grow. On average over the
six benchmarks, DOSE is above the CL method alone by 15.8 and 5.7 points on ResNets (FT and ER) and by 1.7 points
on ViTs with both, against 10.0, 2.8, 0.8 and 0.5 points under A. DOSE is at least as good as the CL method alone in all
24 settings and as CL + TTA in 22 of them. CL + TTA with test-batch statistics is above DOSE only on three memory-free
ResNet benchmarks (C100-C, DomainNet and C20).

averaged over domains and tasks. Images are used at half resolution. BN Stats Adapt, Tent and CoTTA, the three baselines
of the Cityscapes-to-ACDC experiment of CoTTA, follow its official code, with one image per step. Its Tent keeps the
network in evaluation mode, adapts the affine parameters of every normalization layer and minimizes the cross-entropy of
its own predictions. CoTTA averages its teacher over 7 scales and their flips. This Tent was written for SegFormer, whose
BatchNorm layers are only in its head. On DeepLabV3+ we therefore also run the original Tent, which normalizes each
test image with its own statistics and minimizes the entropy, as in our classification experiments. CoTTA keeps the stored
statistics, so its two protocols coincide. We also run SegFormer-B5, the network of the Cityscapes-to-ACDC benchmark of
CoTTA, with the official mmsegmentation Cityscapes weights. Its encoder uses LayerNorm, and its only BatchNorm layers
32


are the five of its head. It is trained with AdamW at a tenth of its official rate, as DeepLabV3+ with SGD. On SegFormer
we run BN Stats Adapt, Tent and SVDP [54], a recent method with official code for this benchmark. SVDP adds sparse
learnable prompts to the most uncertain pixels of the input. We use the settings of its official run (prompts on 0.1% of the
pixels, learning rate 10−4), and each image is predicted with prompts placed on its own uncertainty, as in the official code.
Its prompt map is defined for the size of the ACDC images, so we resize it for Cityscapes images. DOSE is unchanged,
except that it has no prototype head and that its two checks compare images by pixel accuracy. The prototype head would
keep one prototype per class, the mean feature of the preview pixels weighted by their predicted probability of that class, and
score every pixel against these prototypes. We tested it, and it lowers the score in 13 of the 14 settings of Tab. 33, by 1.0
point on average and mostly on the preview condition. We therefore leave it out. We use 5 seeds. With the Tent of CoTTA’s
code on DeepLabV3+, CL + TTA scores 51.2 with FT and 58.5 with ER, and DOSE 50.8 and 59.9. This Tent leaves the
statistics untouched, so there is little for DOSE to dose. On SegFormer, with BN Stats Adapt and Tent, CL + TTA and DOSE
stay within 0.4 points of the CL method alone in our protocol (Tab. 33). With the statistics of each test image instead, BN
Stats Adapt costs 0.7 points without a buffer and 1.4 with one. The five BatchNorm layers of the head are all that DOSE can
dose. SVDP changes every weight and adds prompts. It raises the preview condition by 2.8 points over the CL method alone
without hurting the trained ones, with and without a buffer, and DOSE stays within 0.3 points of CL + TTA (66.4 and 66.7
against 66.7 and 67.0).

H.8. Stress test: a preview mixed with trained domains
Our protocol spends the preview on the next domain, as in a targeted rollout where the data of the next task is collected.
Adapting to an uncurated stream in which recurring and new domains mix is the problem of general continual TTA, such
as NOTE [15], RoTTA [55] or ReservoirTTA [48], which DOSE can wrap (Tab. 13). We nevertheless evaluate this harder
case. We add to each preview as many images of the trained domains as it holds, in equal shares per domain, so that half of
the stream is old. They come from the halves of the held-out sets that the task does not score. The preview domain is still
scored on its own images. Such a stream changes what TTA learns. CL + TTA gains up to 24 points, but not from the new
domain: its statistics now also describe the trained domains, which it sees again. The recorded statistics and the BatchNorm
statistics of the preview, in turn, describe a mixture. DOSE can however use its recorded statistics during the preview. With
the filter, an image that the recorded statistics of a trained domain explain better than those of the preview so far is left out of
the TTA update, the preview’s record and its statistics. This is the posterior of Sec. 5 with the images seen so far, so it needs
no threshold. The filter starts after 100 images. Table 34 gives the scores (Tent and ROID, ER and FT, 5 seeds). With half old
images, DOSE stays the best system on TF/Inet and on C100-C (62.6 against 54.4 for CL + TTA). On DomainNet and C20, it
falls 1.4 and 0.6 points below CL + TTA, which profits from seeing old images again, and the filter does not help there. The
filter drops 83% of the old images but also 33% of the new ones (5 seeds). On the pure preview, it drops 26% of the images
(6% on Inet-TF, 59% on C20) and costs at most 0.5 points. A ViT keeps no domain statistics that old images could pull
toward a mixture. With half old images, CL + TTA and DOSE move by at most 0.6 points on ViTs, and DOSE stays above
CL + TTA. Mixed previews on ResNets thus call for a sample-selection or stream-clustering step in front of DOSE, which we
leave to future work.

H.9. ViT gains and preview length
On ViTs, the prompt and adapter methods built for them (DPCore, DePT and ViDA) move the preview by less than 1 point in
6 to 78 updates. The methods that adapt LayerNorm with stronger losses (ROID, CMF, DAS and EATA) gain 6 to 14 points
on corrupted previews (Tab. 16) and lose at most 1.8 points on the trained domains. Yet the score rises by at most 3.7 points,
because the preview weighs only 0.17 (IN-C) to 0.29 (C100-TF) of it. The gain of TTA follows the amount of preview data.
Over every TTA method and without memory, its median on the preview is 7.9 points on IN-C (20,000 preview images), 2.8
on C100-C (5,000), 0.9 on C100-TF (1,000) and 0.3 on Inet-TF (about 400). On C100-C, the gain grows from 2.2 to 5.8
points between 500 and 5,000 images (Fig. 3), and the official SAR code on ImageNet-C reaches 51% after the 50,000 images
of the usual protocol, against 38% after 5,000 (Sec. G.6). Longer previews provide both more images and more adaptation
updates; these experiments do not isolate their respective effects. Labeled data is scarce too. IN-C and C100-C give 25 and
33 labeled images per class and task (Tab. 8), and the CL method alone scores lowest on them.
H.10. Dosing what TTA changes on a ViT
A ViT has no domain statistics, and TTA methods change its LayerNorm parameters, prompts or adapters. DOSE shares
these changes unless the shared-parameter check doses them (Sec. 5). On ViTs, dosing every changed tensor by λ changes
the score by −0.1 and −0.2 points (Tab. 3). The reason is that these changes barely hurt the trained domains of a ViT, by at
most 2.4 points on the domain just learned (Tab. 9), so there is little to protect. On C100-C and IN-C, where TTA gains most
on ViTs, dosing every changed tensor costs 0.9 and 0.7 points (1.3 without a buffer), so sharing remains the better default.
The prototypes, in contrast, need their weight λ on ViTs. We gave them a weight of one on every image (Tent/ROID,
5 seeds). They then gain up to 1.4 points on C20 and C100-C, where the trained and preview domains look alike. On
ImageNet-R and ImageNet-A they lose 0.3 to 1.6 points in each setting (37 of 40 runs), mostly on the trained domains, and
on ImageNet-A they fall below the CL method alone. Averaged over the seven benchmarks, the gain is only 0.2 points. The
weight λ keeps the prototypes off the images of the trained domains, and this is what makes them safe.

H.12. BatchNorm-aware CL
On ResNets, the damage of TTA comes from the BatchNorm statistics that all domains share (Sec. 4). Therefore, we also
run two CL variants that change how BatchNorm is used, with everything else unchanged (ER and FT, Tent/ROID, 5 seeds).
Continual Normalization (CN) [37] applies a GroupNorm without affine parameters (32 groups) before every BatchNorm
layer of the pretrained network. Cumulative statistics (Cum.) replace the running average of BatchNorm by the average over
every training batch so far. Since Cum. only changes how the CL training estimates the statistics, we run it as the CL method
alone.
Without memory, CN raises the CL method alone from 43.4 to 50.2 points, while with a buffer it is 1.5 points below
BatchNorm (56.8 against 58.3, see Tab. 36). Cum. is below CN in both cases. Notably, CN makes CL + TTA less harmful,
but it does not remove the damage. With a buffer, TTA still lowers the accuracy on the trained domains from 57.9 to 47.9
points on DomainNet and from 31.8 to 21.2 on IN-C, and DOSE brings it back to 56.9 and 31.7. On CN, DOSE is at least as
good as CN alone in 21 of 24 pairs of TTA method and setting, and as CN with TTA in 20. Overall, DOSE on BatchNorm
remains the best system. It is at least as good as the better of CN and Cum. alone in 24 of 24 pairs, and scores 58.7 against
53.5 for CN alone on average.

H.14. Why CODA-Prompt is not above DER++
CODA-Prompt learns each new domain as well as FT. However, it has no buffer and a single shared classifier head, so it
forgets across domains like FT. Its published gains are for class-incremental learning, with logit masking and many more
epochs per task, which our single-epoch domain-incremental protocol does not use.
