Learning Neurovascular Codes for EEG-to-fMRI Synthesis
Yiming Sun1,2, Yifan Wang1, Aosong Feng3, Yijia Ma4, Ben Cheng2, Xurui Wang1, Yufan He5, Yucheng Tang5,
Qing Lyu3, Daguang Xu5, Pei-Fen Kuan1, Mingzhou Ding6, Wen Li4, Chenyu You1
1Stony Brook University, 2University of Toronto, 3Yale University, 4UTHealth Houston, 5NVIDIA, 6University of Florida
In this paper, we introduce Neurovascular Codes as shared EEG representations for fMRI generation. We first train
a separate EEG encoder to predict each fMRI signal, learning target-specific temporal and spectral features from the
same EEG window. After training, we freeze the encoders and project their concatenated hidden features into a shared
neurovascular code. Conditioned on this code, stochastic interpolant transforms Gaussian noise into a vector of fMRI
signals, allowing different responses to be sampled for the same EEG window. We evaluate our approach on eight public
simultaneous EEG-fMRI datasets, including four resting-state and four task-based datasets with 61,646 volume-level
pairs. Under within-subject and held-out-subject evaluation, our model improves average Pearson correlation over the
strongest encoder baselines by 19.1% and 19.8%, respectively. The corresponding improvements over the strongest
generator baselines are 18.8% and 47.9%. Beyond reconstruction accuracy, we also evaluate the generated signals
using subject-identification classifiers trained on real fMRI. Our method achieves the highest mean accuracy among the
evaluated generators. We hope that our work opens up new opportunities for cross-modal brain modeling.

Introduction
Learning representations that transfer across tasks is a central
aim of foundation models (Bommasani et al., 2021). This ap-
proach has been extended to brain signals, including electroen-
cephalography (EEG) and functional magnetic resonance imag-
ing (fMRI). EEG models (Kostas et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2024; Ouahidi et al., 2025) learn across participants
and recording settings. BrainLM (Caro et al., 2023) learns spa-
tiotemporal fMRI patterns for clinical prediction. Unlike stud-
ies that train on one modality alone, we use fMRI prediction to
supervise EEG representations for generation.
EEG captures rapid electrical fluctuations in the brain and
supports brain-computer interfaces (McFarland and Wolpaw,
2011; Lotte et al., 2018). fMRI measures blood-oxygen-level-
dependent (BOLD) signals (Ogawa et al., 1990; Logothetis,
2008) and maps brain regions and networks (Smith et al., 2013;
Glasser et al., 2016). Simultaneous EEG-fMRI recordings (Hus-
ter et al., 2012; Murta et al., 2015; Bullock et al., 2021) pair
these complementary measurements.

In this paper, we introduce Neurovascular Codes, termed NVCode, for EEG-to-fMRI synthesis. We
first train a separate EEG encoder to predict each fMRI signal from the same EEG window. Through these
prediction tasks, the encoders learn target-specific spatiotemporal (Vaswani et al., 2017; Dosovitskiy et al.,
2020) and spectral (Schirrmeister et al., 2017; Banville et al., 2021) features. After training, we discard the
prediction heads and freeze the encoders, retaining their hidden features for generation. A learned projection
maps the concatenated features into a shared neurovascular code that conditions a stochastic fMRI generator.
An EEG window may correspond to more than one plausible
fMRI response (Figure 2). Scalp EEG has spatial ambigu-
ity (Huster et al., 2012), and neurovascular coupling varies
across regions and individuals (Logothetis, 2008). We there-
fore condition a stochastic interpolant on the neurovascu-
lar code, using conditional velocity matching (Lipman et al.,
2022; Tong et al., 2023). The model learns a velocity field
that transforms Gaussian noise into a vector of fMRI sig-
nals. Each velocity prediction depends on the current values
of all target signals, allowing the field to model dependencies
among them. Different noise samples yield different response
vectors for the same EEG window. Averaging these samples
gives a predictive mean for each fMRI target. The individual
draws retain the joint variation across targets, which is not
visible in the mean alone.

We evaluate our approach on eight public simultaneous EEG-fMRI datasets, including four resting-state and
four task-based datasets with 61,646 volume-level pairs. The benchmark uses common target definitions,
data splits, and evaluation metrics.
We compare EEG encoders using a common generator architecture
and compare generation networks using identical cached EEG codes. We also ablate which target-specific
representations are supplied to the generator. Under within-subject and held-out-subject evaluation, our
method achieves relative gains in average Pearson correlation of 19.1% and 19.8% over the strongest encoder
baselines, respectively. The corresponding gains over the strongest generator baselines are 18.8% and 47.9%.
Beyond prediction accuracy, we evaluate marginal predictive distributions and functional connectivity com-
puted from predictive-mean time series. Our method achieves the lowest average functional connectivity
error in both comparisons and evaluation settings. With classifiers trained on real fMRI and held fixed, our
generated signals also achieve the highest mean subject-identification accuracy, exceeding accuracy on real
fMRI test signals by 3.1 percentage points on average. Our contributions are as follows:
1. We introduce Neurovascular Codes, shared EEG representations learned through target-specific fMRI
prediction and used to condition stochastic fMRI generation.
2. We establish a benchmark across eight public simultaneous EEG-fMRI datasets, covering four resting-
state and four task-based datasets. It uses common target definitions, data splits, and evaluation metrics
for comparisons in both within-subject and held-out-subject settings.
3. We compare EEG encoders, conditional generators, and target-specific representation sets under controlled
experimental settings. Beyond reconstruction accuracy, we evaluate the marginal predictive distributions
and functional connectivity computed from predictive-mean time series. We assess subject identification
performance using classifiers trained on real fMRI signals.

Related Work
EEG and fMRI representation learning.
Representation learning is widely used in brain–computer
interfaces (McFarland and Wolpaw, 2011; Lotte et al., 2018). Large-scale pretraining provides one way to
learn features that transfer across tasks (Bommasani et al., 2021). For EEG, BENDR (Kostas et al., 2021)
uses masked contrastive learning, while BIOT (Yang et al., 2023a) enables pretraining across heterogeneous
biosignal recordings.
LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024) predicts masked neural tokens, EEGPT (Wang et al.,
2024) pairs masked reconstruction with representation alignment, and REVE (Ouahidi et al., 2025) uses
2


masked autoencoding with electrode-coordinate embeddings. This line of work addresses variation in electrode
layouts (Yang et al., 2023a; Ouahidi et al., 2025) and supports a range of EEG decoding tasks (Lotte et al.,
2018; Wang et al., 2024).
For fMRI, BrainLM (Caro et al., 2023) learns spatial and temporal structure
through masked prediction and supports clinical prediction (Caro et al., 2023). During pretraining, these
methods construct learning targets from the input signals themselves. Our work instead uses fMRI prediction
to supervise EEG representation learning.
EEG-to-fMRI modeling.
EEG–fMRI studies relate electrophysiological activity to the BOLD re-
sponse (Ogawa et al., 1990; Logothetis, 2008) to investigate neurovascular coupling (Logothetis, 2008; Murta
et al., 2015) and constrain EEG source localization (Huster et al., 2012). fMRI also supports the study of
functional networks (Biswal et al., 1995; Van Den Heuvel and Pol, 2010) through statistical dependencies
across brain regions (Biswal et al., 1995; Friston, 2011). BEIRA (Kovalev et al., 2022) learns interpretable
mappings from EEG to BOLD signals, while SIREN-based prediction (Li et al., 2024a) uses sinusoidal net-
works to extract frequency information. NeuroBOLT (Li et al., 2024b) learns spatiotemporal and multiscale
spectral features and evaluates prediction on both resting-state and task recordings. UnEBOLT (Li et al.,
2026) predicts BOLD time series across brain parcels with a single adaptive decoder and estimates func-
tional connectivity from those predictions. NVCode uses target-specific prediction to learn EEG features
that condition stochastic generation of the fMRI signal vector.
Conditional generative models.
EEG-to-fMRI ambiguity and variable neurovascular coupling moti-
vate probabilistic modeling (Logothetis, 2008; Murta et al., 2015). Models use stochastic latents (Kingma
and Welling, 2013; Sohn et al., 2015), adversarial training (Mirza and Osindero, 2014; Isola et al., 2017),
diffusion denoising (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020; Karras et al., 2022), or cross-time consistency for
few-step sampling (Song et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024). Neural ODEs and FFJORD formulate continuous
normalizing flows (Chen et al., 2018; Grathwohl et al., 2018), whereas flow matching, rectified flow, and
optimal-transport conditional flow matching learn path velocities (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Tong
et al., 2023). Stochastic interpolants connect distributions through deterministic or stochastic dynamics (Al-
bergo et al., 2025).
NVCode conditions this framework on neurovascular codes and the full fMRI state,
allowing interactions among targets.
3
Neurovascular Codes
NVCode learns EEG representations through individual fMRI prediction tasks and uses these representations
to condition fMRI generation. We first train a separate EEG encoder for each fMRI target (Section 3.2).
With the encoders frozen, we then train a projector and a conditional velocity field together (Sections 3.3
and 3.4). The projector maps the encoder features to a neurovascular code, which guides generation from
Gaussian noise to a vector of fMRI signals. Appendix C provides architectural specifications, derivations,
and training and sampling details.
3.1
Preliminaries
Each observation pairs the 16 s EEG window x ∈RC×T immediately preceding an fMRI volume with its
BOLD target vector y ∈RN. Here, C and T count EEG channels and time samples. The vector contains
six DiFuMo region-of-interest (ROI) signals (Dadi et al., 2020) and one global signal (N = 7).
Projecting the target-specific EEG encoders’ hidden features yields the neurovascular code cψ(x) ∈Rdc
(Section 3.3). It conditions pθ(y | cψ(x)), with projector parameters ψ and generator parameters θ. Each
generated vector contains seven signals at one acquisition time point. Applying the model to successive EEG
windows yields time series for evaluation. Let µ, σ ∈RN denote the per-target mean and standard deviation
estimated only from the training split. The standardized generation endpoint is

Experiments
We evaluate NVCode on eight public simultaneous EEG-fMRI datasets (Table 1) under within-subject and
held-out-subject protocols. Section 4.1 compares EEG encoders and conditional generators under matched
pipelines; Section 4.2 examines temporal and spectral fidelity in representative scans; Section 4.3 tests whether
generated fMRI retains identity information accessible to a classifier trained only on real data; and Section 4.4
examines the effects of the main model components.
See Appendix A for dataset-level results and scan
examples, and Appendix B for details of the experimental protocols and metrics.
4.1
Main Experiment
Accuracy.
We first evaluate reconstruction for participants represented during training (Intra). NVCode
achieves the highest average Pearson correlation, 0.480, in both component comparisons. With the same
generator architecture fitted separately for each encoder, NVCode exceeds SIREN-based, the best encoder
baseline (0.403), by 19.1% (Table 2). With identical cached EEG codes, it exceeds CVAE, the best generation
baseline (0.404), by 18.8% (Table 3).
NVCode retains its correlation advantage when evaluated on unseen participants. Under Inter evaluation,
it reaches R = 0.423 across eight equally weighted datasets. This is 19.8% higher than SPaRCNet (0.353)
in the encoder comparison (Table 2) and 47.9% higher than EDM (0.286) with identical cached EEG codes
(Table 3). The larger generator gap in Inter makes it especially useful to separate the two controls: one
changes which EEG features reach the generator, and the other changes how the generator uses a fixed input.
Both controls evaluate new participants within each dataset’s acquisition setting.

Encoder comparison.
We vary the EEG representation while keeping the generator architecture fixed.
On held-out participants, NVCode improves on SPaRCNet in six of seven targets (Figure 4a). The largest
gain is in the global signal, from 0.324 to 0.487; both subcortical targets also improve. The per-target results
in Table 10 show how these gains contribute to the average across targets.
The encoder change also improves predictive-mean connectivity. Relative to SPaRCNet, FC-MAE falls from
0.163 to 0.135, a 17.2% reduction, while marginal CRPS stays at 0.344 to the reported precision (Figure 4b).
Thus, at fixed generator architecture, gains in how the signals vary together can occur with little change in
the marginal scores for the individual targets.
Generator comparison.
We next hold the conditioning input fixed and vary the generator. All baselines
in Table 3 receive the same cached codes from the NVCode projector, so differences arise after the EEG
representation has already been formed. The leading alternative in Avg. R changes from CVAE in Intra
to EDM in Inter, whereas NVCode retains the highest correlation under both protocols. Figure 5a shows
that this advantage extends across all seven signals relative to DDPM and CVAE on held-out participants.
Heschl’s gyrus, for example, reaches 0.475 versus 0.309 for CVAE, and the thalamus reaches 0.396 versus
0.294. The gains therefore span cortical and subcortical targets as well as the global time series.
The benchmark gives each dataset the same weight regardless of its number of scans. Tables 12a–12h separate
the dataset means and show seed variation where reported. This breakdown distinguishes the benchmark
average from performance on any one dataset.

4.3
Downstream Utility
Testing on synthetic fMRI.
We test whether EEG-conditioned fMRI preserves features that a classifier
trained on measured fMRI can use to identify participants. For each dataset, we fit the classifier on real
fMRI and keep it fixed for both evaluations. We then replace the held-out real test inputs with predictions
from matched EEG epochs while keeping the identity labels and test segments the same. The real-input row
in Table 6 establishes the reference for each dataset; the remaining rows report accuracy differences relative
to that reference. Because the classifier has never seen synthetic fMRI, it cannot adapt its decision rule to
the output of any particular generator. The comparison tests transfer across input types within a dataset,
using the same participant label set for every generator.
NVCode produces the largest mean gain, 3.1 percentage points versus 2.4 for DDPM, and ranks first on
seven of the eight datasets. On XP1, DDPM gains 2.4 points versus 1.9 for NVCode. The average gives
each dataset equal weight, so the lead reflects gains across acquisition settings rather than the contribution
of the largest test set. The table also separates the difficulty of identification on real fMRI from the benefit
of replacing those test inputs. For example, NVCode gains 4.3 points on NeuroBOLT and 4.5 on NatView,
although real-input accuracy differs substantially between the two datasets (86.9% and 74.7%).
All generators raise mean accuracy in this protocol. EEG-conditioned estimates may retain stable features
that the classifier uses while attenuating variation in the measured signals that matters less for identity.
The experiment measures how readily a fixed classifier can identify participants, whereas the reconstruction
analyses measure agreement with the recorded signals. Appendix B.3 reports the reverse test: classifiers
train on synthetic fMRI and are tested on the same held-out real fMRI epochs (Table 14).

5
Conclusion
We presented Neurovascular Codes for EEG-to-fMRI synthesis.
NVCode learns target-specific EEG
features through individual fMRI prediction tasks and projects them into a shared code for stochastic fMRI
generation. Across eight public datasets, it achieves the highest average Pearson correlation and the lowest
predictive-mean FC-MAE in both encoder and generator comparisons, under within-subject and held-out-
subject evaluation. Ablations favor regional conditioning combined with joint interpolation. Using fixed
classifiers trained on real fMRI, NVCode signals from matched EEG epochs achieve the highest mean subject-
identification accuracy among the generators evaluated under the same classification protocol.
Discussion.
More broadly, our results suggest that learning to predict fMRI can organize EEG repre-
sentations for conditional signal generation. Reciprocal generation would ask which information is shared
between the two modalities. An EEG window could be mapped to a seven-signal fMRI response and then
reconstructed from that response, testing which features survive the round trip. A cycle beginning in fMRI
could test whether an inferred EEG signal supports recovery of the original response pattern. Comparing each
cycle against mismatched windows would distinguish information about the current window from patterns
that recur across a scan. Scoring regional trajectories and cross-target structure separately would distinguish
features recovered in both directions from features supported by only one mapping. A cross-dataset cycle
could test whether these shared features remain recoverable when both mappings are evaluated on a new
acquisition setting without refitting. Scores for the two directions could then be reported separately against
the same mappings evaluated on matched windows in the original corpus. This would distinguish errors tied
to one direction from a loss of shared information when the dataset changes.

Reading the trajectories.
The guide compares amplitudes at one time; Pearson R compares aligned fluctuations
across the scan. For nonconstant observed and predicted amplitude vectors a and ba, an additive shift or

peaks and troughs but miss the guide, or match it without tracking the rest of the scan.

This appendix specifies the data construction, baseline adaptation, metric formulas, downstream utility probe

B.1
Experiment Setup: Data Preprocessing and Machine Requirements
Datasets and splits.
Table 1 summarizes eight public simultaneous EEG–fMRI datasets (Huster et al.,
2012; Murta et al., 2015), comprising four resting-state (Biswal et al., 1995; Fox and Raichle, 2007) and four
task-evoked (Laufs et al., 2003; Murta et al., 2015) settings. Their source cohorts report 184 participants,
while the 189 scans retained after scan-level exclusions yield 61,646 paired volumes. Each fMRI volume is
paired with the immediately preceding 16-s EEG window, so future EEG does not enter the model input.
Intra-subject evaluation uses 80% of volumes for training and 10% for validation.
Test volumes follow
a ⌈20/TR⌉-volume buffer that separates them from validation and prevents overlap between their EEG
contexts (Logothetis, 2008). Inter-subject evaluation (Lotte et al., 2018) uses disjoint training and held-out
subjects. Normalization statistics and checkpoint selection use only the training-subject partition.
Target signals, baselines and metrics.
Each volume defines a seven-dimensional target vector con-
taining six DiFuMo ROI signals and one global signal (Dadi et al., 2020; Murphy et al., 2009). Figure 3(b)
lists these targets: Cuneus, Heschl’s gyrus (H Gyrus), anterior middle frontal gyrus (MF Gyrus), anterior
precuneus, putamen, thalamus, and the global signal. Every model predicts this same ordered vector from
the same retained EEG–fMRI pairs and training-only normalization statistics. In Table 2, the generator
architecture, sampler, target ordering, and split policy are held fixed while the EEG encoder configuration
changes. In Table 3, the seven frozen NVCode encoders and fitted projector supply identical conditions while
the downstream generation network changes. Stochastic generators return K=50 samples for each held-out
EEG epoch. Avg. R and FC-MAE evaluate the predictive mean, whereas mean marginal CRPS uses the
complete empirical sample distribution. Dataset scores are first summarized over their internal splits and
then macro-averaged with equal dataset weight. Appendix B.2 gives the adaptation rules, metric definitions,
and aggregation procedure.
Cohort and scan-count conventions.
Table 1 distinguishes three levels of data accounting. Cohort Size
counts participants in the original source cohorts. Scans counts runs retained after scan-level exclusions, and
Pairs counts the volume-anchored EEG–fMRI observations from those runs before splitting. For NatView, the
two retained runs provide 571 paired volumes. NatView contributes one dataset-level score to the benchmark
mean, with the same weight as any other dataset.
Paired EEG to fMRI observations.
Each observation pairs the fMRI volume acquired at tv with
the preceding EEG interval [tv −16 s, tv). The recorded onset defines the alignment without an additional
hemodynamic lag shift. The repetition time is dataset dependent: TR = 2.0 s for XP1, Affective Music,
MULTI-CLARID, gradCPT, and Experience Sampling, and TR = 2.1 s for Sleep EEG–fMRI, NeuroBOLT,
and NatView. The fixed window supplies preceding EEG context for estimating the BOLD response at each
acquisition time (Logothetis, 2008).
EEG preprocessing and channel harmonization.
EEG-informed fMRI analysis combines electrophys-
iological preprocessing with BOLD modeling (Abreu et al., 2018) within broader multimodal brain imaging
workflows (Calhoun and Sui, 2016). All EEG segments are resampled to 200 Hz with the polyphase finite im-
pulse response resampler in mne.io.Raw.resample(..., method="polyphase"). The default anti aliasing
low pass filter is applied at the Nyquist frequency and no time shift is introduced. Each segment is cropped
to T = 3200 samples, and raw amplitudes are divided by 100 before being fed to the encoders. Native channel
counts vary across datasets, so we harmonize all recordings to the following 26 channels of the 10–20 montage:
C26 = {FP1, FP2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2,
F7, F8, T7, T8, P7, P8, FPZ, FZ, CZ, PZ,
POZ, OZ, FT9, FT10, TP9, TP10}.
Non EEG channels such as ECG, EOG and EMG are removed, along with carbon wire loop sensors. Missing
target channels are reconstructed by spherical spline interpolation against the standard 10–20 montage using
34


MNE Python with standard_1020. Channels present in the source recording but outside the target set are
discarded. Dataset provided referencing and artifact correction decisions are preserved. No dataset specific
EEG augmentation is applied.
fMRI signal construction.
For each 4D fMRI scan, we extract atlas level signals with nilearn’s
NiftiMapsMasker using the 64 component DiFuMo atlas (Dadi et al., 2020) at 2 mm resolution. The masker
is used only for soft mask spatial weighting; masker standardization and detrending are disabled so that all
reported statistics are controlled by the split dependent preprocessing below. From the 64 DiFuMo com-
ponents, we select six regional signals by label matching: Cuneus, Heschl’s gyrus, Middle Frontal Gyrus
anterior, Precuneus anterior, Putamen and Thalamus. Under nilearn 0.10.3, these correspond to compo-
nent IDs {16, 52, 46, 3, 28, 30} in the listed order. The seventh signal is the global signal (Murphy et al.,
2009), computed as the unweighted mean over all 64 DiFuMo components before filtering or scaling. The
seven signals are stacked into rs,v ∈R7 for scan s and volume v.
