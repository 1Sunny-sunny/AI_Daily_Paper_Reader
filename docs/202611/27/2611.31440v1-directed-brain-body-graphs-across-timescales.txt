Abstract
Simultaneous brain and body recordings combine signals sampled at very different rates with
processes that act on timescales from milliseconds to minutes. Placing all signals on one time
grid can distort the directed relationships estimated from them. Unmeasured common influences
can also make one signal appear to drive another. We introduce rate-matched Granger causality
(RMGC), which estimates directed predictive relationships without a common sampling grid.
Each relationship receives a robustness value, the strength of the weakest common influence
found within a specified class that can reproduce it. A neural extension tests for nonlinear
relationships. In a controlled benchmark, RMGC recovered more true relationships than four
established methods and an autoregressive model on a common grid (92% at a false-positive
rate of 5%). Given the same signal features, the common-grid model matched this recovery but
had more than twice the false-positive rate, and it reversed the relationship from respiration to
heart rate. The robustness value never overstated the strength of the driver behind a spurious
relationship, even for drivers outside the searched class. It separated true from spurious
relationships better than predictive strength did. In overnight sleep recordings and two awake
datasets, many brain–heart relationships could be reproduced by common drivers no stronger
than measured respiration. Robustness values only partly identified those that appeared when
respiration was omitted, particularly during wakefulness. Surrogate analyses showed elevated
background rates in one awake dataset. The approach applies to other signals recorded at
different rates or as events, provided that influences act with a delay.

Introduction
The brain continuously receives signals from the body while contributing to the regulation of
cardiac, respiratory and autonomic activity. The relevance of these exchanges extends to perception
and behaviour (reviewed in Azzalini et al., 2019). In humans, neural responses to heartbeats
predict the detection of faint visual stimuli (Park et al., 2014), while nasal respiration is associated
with the timing of limbic oscillations and performance in memory and emotion-related tasks
(Zelano et al., 2016). Such findings motivate the study of brain activity within its physiological
context. Understanding brain–body coordination requires examining how neural and peripheral
processes vary together, how their relationships are organized in time, and how those relationships
can be interpreted from simultaneous recordings.
Network physiology (Ivanov, 2021) provides a framework for investigating this coordination
across interacting physiological systems. It asks how the organization of relationships among
systems contributes to physiological function and changes with the state of the organism. Studies of
sleep have identified distinct patterns of connectivity among brain activity, cardiac and respiratory
signals, and other physiological processes, linking changes in network structure to transitions

between sleep stages (Bashan et al., 2012; Bartsch et al., 2015). These observations make the
relationships between systems an object of investigation in their own right. For brain–body
research, this perspective motivates joint analysis of multiple signals and careful consideration of
what an inferred connection between them represents.
One useful description concerns the direction of predictive relationships: whether the past of
one signal provides information about another beyond that already available from its own history
and the other observed signals. Granger causality formalizes this question (Granger, 1969), and
related analyses of predictive information have been used to characterize brain–heart networks
during sleep (Faes et al., 2014). A directed graph can therefore summarize how information in
recorded signals contributes to predicting their subsequent activity. Its interpretation depends,
however, on the temporal resolution of the observations and the variables included in the analysis.
Predictive direction alone does not establish a direct physiological pathway (Barrett and Barnett,
2013; Stokes and Purdon, 2017) or identify the effect of an intervention (Eichler and Didelez,
2010).
Physiological recordings pose particular challenges because differences in sampling rates
coexist with differences in the timescales of the underlying processes. Signals recorded using
electroencephalography (EEG) are commonly sampled hundreds of times per second, whereas
other sensors provide much slower measurements, and heartbeats form a sequence of events. A
rapidly sampled signal can also carry slowly varying information: relationships with peripheral or
haemodynamic signals may involve changes in neural power rather than the instantaneous electrical
waveform. Representing all measurements on a common grid can obscure these distinctions. Coarse
aggregation may lose fast temporal relationships, while a fine grid requires many past samples
to represent slower dependencies. When one step of an indirect path from A through B to C is
shorter than a bin, A can appear to act on C directly. On a fine grid, a slow signal must be filled
in between its samples, either by holding the last value or by interpolation. Interpolation uses
the next sample, so the filled-in values carry information from the future. Measurement noise in
such signals can create spurious directed relationships and suppress true ones (Nalatore et al.,
2007). Filtering leaves Granger causality unchanged in theory, but in practice it often changes
the inferred relationships, because filtered signals need many more past samples to be modelled
(Barnett and Seth, 2011). More generally, the relation between sampling intervals and interaction
delays can change which directed dependencies are detectable (Barnett and Seth, 2017). Joint
analysis therefore requires both an appropriate temporal representation and signal features that
preserve the information relevant to interactions across timescales.
A further difficulty arises because physiological signals can share influences that are absent
from the model. Respiration illustrates why this matters: it is related to both cardiac variability
and neural activity (Zelano et al., 2016), and omitting it can change the inferred directionality
of cardiovascular relationships (Porta et al., 2012). A common influence reaching two signals at
different delays can make one predict the other even without a direct interaction between them
(Granger, 1969). Volume conduction, the spread of electrical activity from one source through
the head to many scalp electrodes, leads Granger-causal measures to report spurious connectivity
between EEG channels (Haufe et al., 2013). The same study showed that time-reversed data,
used as surrogates, suppress this effect while preserving time-lagged interactions. Recording likely
common influences helps, but any set of measurements provides an incomplete view of physiological
regulation. An observed relationship consequently raises a second question alongside its predictive
strength: how susceptible is it to an unmeasured common influence capable of producing similar
evidence?
Existing methods provide complementary approaches to these problems. Mixed-frequency
Granger tests use observations collected at different rates (Ghysels et al., 2016), and multiscale
formulations quantify predictive interactions at different temporal resolutions (Faes et al., 2017).
Structural vector autoregressions have been identified from subsampled and mixed-frequency series
(Tank et al., 2019), and causal graphs have been learned from time series sampled at different rates
2


(Assaad et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025). Continuous-time models accommodate measurements taken
at irregular intervals (Driver and Voelkle, 2018). Neural approaches extend the range of nonlinear
relationships that can be represented (Tank et al., 2022). Recent neural methods combine several
ranges of lags or time windows (Fan et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2026) or treat irregular sampling
as missing entries on a regular grid (Cheng et al., 2023). All of these neural methods model the
signals on a single sampling grid.
Causal-discovery methods for time series address uncertainty arising from latent confounders
(Gerhardus and Runge, 2020). Sensitivity analysis offers a further perspective by quantifying
the influence an omitted variable would need to have to alter a regression-based conclusion,
with measured variables providing interpretable benchmarks (Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020). For
physiological network analysis, an important practical challenge is to combine an appropriate
temporal representation of each signal with an explicit assessment of how unobserved common
drivers could account for individual directed relationships. The broadest robustness benchmark
of time-series causal discovery tests 33 violations of model assumptions (Stein et al., 2026). It
deliberately leaves out the case in which coarse measurement resolution makes a lagged effect
appear as a bidirectional relationship. We know of no method that combines prediction at each
signal’s own rate, relationships across timescales and a per-relationship measure of robustness to
unmeasured common drivers, nor of a benchmark that tests these properties together.
Here we introduce rate-matched Granger causality (RMGC), which predicts signals at temporal
resolutions matched to their sampling rates while representing other signals through windows
of their own past observations. This avoids imposing a single common sampling grid. Fast
signals contribute information about both their mean and their power when predicting slower
targets. The neural-network extension of rate-matched Granger causality (RMGC-NN) provides
an additional test for nonlinear predictive relationships. Each detected relationship is accompanied
by a robustness value, obtained by searching for a simulated common driver that can reproduce
its predictive strength after its fitted contribution has been removed. The value describes the
weakest explanation found within the tested driver class and can be compared with a measured
physiological variable such as respiration. It provides a measure of sensitivity whose interpretation
remains conditional on the drivers considered.
We evaluate this framework in simulations and physiological recordings acquired during sleep
and wakefulness. A controlled benchmark tests interactions across timescales, different sampling
rates, heartbeat events, hidden common drivers, volume conduction and a nonlinear relationship.
Comparisons with established methods, including analyses with comparable signal features, and
component ablations examine the contributions of temporal representation and model design. In
overnight sleep recordings and two awake datasets, we assess how inferred relationships change
when respiration is omitted and use placebo and surrogate analyses to evaluate the specificity of
these changes and background detection rates. Together, these evaluations examine when RMGC
provides a useful description of directed brain–body relationships and how sensitivity to shared
influences should inform the interpretation of physiological networks.

Nodes.
The nodes of the graph are the signals given to the method; the method does not
discover them. Each node is one time series in one of three forms: a sampled signal (an EEG
derivation or a regional average of electrodes, breathing, skin conductance), an event stream
(heartbeats, entered as the rate of each completed beat, 60/RR, at the time of the beat), or the
stimulus (beat onsets), which can only be a source. A node may be a sensor channel as recorded,
or a signal derived before the analysis from one or more channels: a regional average of EEG
electrodes, the heart rate from the R-peaks of the electrocardiogram, the amplitude of a muscle
signal. Three rules apply in every data set. Nodes are defined before the analysis and kept the
same across conditions and recordings. No node may be a linear combination of others, which
would make the tests ill-posed. And deriving a node must not shift its timing relative to the
others: zero-phase filters whose effect is shorter than the time resolution at which the node is
analysed are allowed (as for R-peak detection and muscle amplitude), whereas filters with delays,
interpolation and resampling are not used. Unmeasured influences, such as the hidden drivers of
the benchmark, are not nodes. Table 1 lists the nodes of each data set.

Decision rule.
Every candidate edge is an ordered pair (u, v): u →v and v →u are separate
pairs, and the stimulus is never a target; m denotes the number of such pairs. Edges are decided
from the pairs’ p-values by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995):
the m p-values are sorted, p(1) ≤p(2) ≤· · · ≤p(m); k is the largest index with p(k) ≤0.05 k/m;
and the k pairs with the smallest p-values are declared edges (none if there is no such k). This
keeps the expected share of false edges among the declared ones, the false discovery rate (FDR), at
or below 5% when the tests are independent or positively dependent; tests computed on the same
recording are dependent, so the 5% is approximate. The stricter Bonferroni rule (p ≤0.05/m)
would bound the chance of any false edge but miss more true ones.
2.2
Rate-matched Granger causality
Levels, bins and home levels.
A node’s sampling rate, the resolution at which its samples
enter the analysis after any block averaging (Table 1), is distinct from its acquisition rate and from
the timescale of the process it measures: a fast signal can carry slow information, such as the power
of an EEG rhythm. Signals at different rates cannot be predicted at common instants without
resampling. RMGC instead defines three regular time grids, or levels, with steps ∆of 20 ms,
200 ms and 2 s (50, 5 and 0.5 Hz: fast, medium, slow). The interval between two consecutive grid
points, [tk, tk + ∆), is a bin, and a signal’s value in a bin is the mean of its samples in it. Each
signal has a home level: the fastest level not faster than its sensor, so that every bin contains
at least one sample. The heartbeat is assigned to the medium level and the stimulus to the fast
level. Each signal is predicted only at its home level, bin by bin, by its own least-squares model
(Section 2.1); we call the signal being predicted the target.
5


Own past.
The target’s own past enters its model as its values in the previous bins: 15, 20 and
8 bins at the three levels, that is 0.3 s, 4 s and 16 s. These spans are fixed a priori to cover each
level’s own dynamics: EEG rhythms within 0.3 s, breathing cycles and heart-rate responses within
4 s, slow autonomic drifts within 16 s. They are not tuned; halving or doubling them, together
with the window horizon, leaves the results essentially unchanged (Section S3.2).
Window pooling.
Every other signal u contributes to the target’s model a block of regressors
computed from u’s samples as recorded, at u’s own rate, without interpolating them onto the
target’s grid. The samples are grouped into time windows that end before the target bin starts,
so that only the past is used (the windows are causal). For a target bin starting at tk, window
j is Wj = [tk −ej+1, tk −ej), where the window edges are multiples of the bin width ∆of the
target’s level:
ej = j∆(j = 0, . . . , 8),
e9 = 16∆, e10 = 32∆, . . . (doubling while ej ≤16 s).

The first 8 windows have the target’s bin width; after that the widths double, so the windows are
fine near the present and coarse further back (Table S3). The furthest window ends 10.24 s, 12.8 s
and 16 s before the target bin at the three levels. Windows narrower than a source’s sampling
interval are merged with the next, so no window is empty and slow sensors contribute their freshest
samples. Window j contributes the mean of u’s samples in it, fj; heartbeats contribute the rate of
each completed beat and the stimulus the number of beats. A fast signal contributing to a slower
target (sampled at least four times faster than the target’s level) gives a second value per window,
the logarithm of the variance of its samples, gj, its power; this power channel is what lets an edge
from fast power to a slow signal be found. Mean and power belong to the same node: whether the
mean or the power of u improves the prediction of v, the edge is u →v.
Aliasing.
A window mean is an average followed by one value per window, so fast oscillations
can alias into it; the box-car average attenuates frequencies above the inverse window width
only moderately. This does not create false edges in the Granger sense: the window values are
functions of u’s own past samples, so if they improve the prediction of v, u’s past does. Aliasing
changes which aspect of u the test sees, not the validity of the test. The bins must, however, be
short compared with the coupling delays of interest (Section S3.2). Figure 1 shows the design.
With least-squares fits and the F test of Section 2.1, this is the core of the method, rate-matched
Granger causality (RMGC).
Source blocks.
The window values fj (and gj) of one source form that source’s block. Each
block is tested by the F test of Section 2.1, with all other blocks and the target’s own past kept;
if u’s block is significant in v’s model, the edge u →v is declared.
Bin pooling.
Bin pooling, which we report as an ablation, represents every source at the target’s
level instead: faster sources as their bin means and powers, slower sources as their last completed
bins, each with the level’s number of lags. Window pooling is preferred for two reasons. It needs
fewer regressors: in the benchmark, a medium-level target has 169 regressors with bin pooling
and 98–105 with window pooling, and fewer regressors give the tests more power. And with bin
pooling, slow sensors enter only through their completed 2-s bins, so a faster signal such as heart
rate can carry fresher information about a hidden driver than the slow sensor itself.

to produce an edge of that size if u had no influence on v. The strength of a driver H added to a
signal x as bH is its variance share,

In each signal it is measured on the values through which that signal enters v’s model: in the

slow driver therefore counts at full strength in the slow windows, where it can dominate a fast
signal such as EEG, even if it is small in the narrowest window. The attack has three steps.
1. Remove the edge. The fitted contribution of u’s block is subtracted from v’s current values,
keeping v’s own past as observed, so that u’s block explains exactly nothing. (v’s own past
is kept as observed because rebuilding it from the edited series would carry u’s influence
back into the model.)
2. Add a driver. A unit-variance driver is added to u with delay du = 0.25 s and to v’s bins and
own past with delay dv > du (receiving the driver first is the only way it can make u appear
to drive v), with variance share su in u and sv in v. In u the driver is added to the window
means, and for a source with a power channel the same series is added to its log-variance
values.

in the class sufficed to reproduce the edge; a high RV means that no weak driver was found in the
class, not that none exists. Drivers outside the class (other persistences or delays, other nonlinear
forms) might produce the edge with a smaller share, so the RV is an upper bound on the smallest
share over all possible drivers. Sources the driver does not touch are partialled out once per edge
(Lovell, 1963), so each bisection step is a small regression; the results are identical to refitting the
full model.
Benchmarking against a measured driver.
Following Cinelli and Hazlett (2020), the RV
is compared with a driver that is measured, breathing, whose share is defined in the same way:
in u, the largest R2 of u’s window values on breathing’s windows (Bu); in v, the R2 of v’s
bins on breathing’s windows (Bv), both in v’s model with breathing. The breathing multiple
k = RV/ max(Bu, Bv) is the strength a hidden driver needs, in units of breathing’s; k ≤1 means
that a hidden driver as strong as breathing’s largest effect on the two signals could produce the
edge. In short, a measures how much u’s past improves the prediction of v; the RV, how strong a
hidden driver would have to be to produce an edge of size a if u had no influence on v; and k,
that strength in units of breathing’s.
2.5
Evaluation design
Benchmark.
A simulator with known ground truth (Section S1) generates seven observed
signals at the rates of real sensors: a rhythmic stimulus, two EEG-like signals at 250 Hz, breathing
at 25 Hz, heartbeats as events, an fNIRS-like signal at 8 Hz and skin conductance at 4 Hz, with
six true edges within and across timescales, among them a fast-to-slow edge carried by EEG
power. Eight scenarios (clean; hidden arousal and hidden breathing, each mild and strong; a
stimulus tempo near heart rate; volume conduction between EEG sensors; a squared edge) have
eight replicates of 10 min each, 64 in all, with fixed seeds. The scenarios contain traps: edges
produced by hidden drivers, the reversed edge HR →RESP and the indirect edge SC →EEG2
that common grids create, and mixing between EEG sensors. We report recall, the false-positive
rate over non-edges and the share of replicates declaring each trap edge; five success criteria were
fixed in advance (Section S1).
Matched comparison.
The central comparison gives the established methods the same infor-
mation as RMGC: a vector autoregression on common grids at 50 and 2 Hz (grid VAR), multiscale
Granger causality (Faes et al., 2017) and PCMCI (Runge et al., 2019), each with the EEG power
columns that RMGC uses and with one test per ordered pair (Bonferroni over the pair’s grids
and columns, then one Benjamini–Hochberg procedure over the pairs). The comparison with the
8


methods as published, including neural Granger causality and LPCMCI, the fairness experiment
and the sensitivity analyses are in the supplement (Sections S2 and S3). We did not compare
with MIDAS regressions or mixed-frequency Granger tests (Ghysels et al., 2016; G¨otz et al., 2016)
directly: they require regular sampling in a fixed ratio of frequencies and test a few series at a
time, whereas the benchmark includes event streams and non-integer rate ratios and calls for a
graph over seven signals; their multivariate form, a VAR on stacked high-frequency samples, is
close to the grid VAR on its finest grid. Section S2 explains why VARX was not run separately
either.
Ablations and calibration.
Three ablations each remove one component of RMGC: the power
channel; rate matching, with every signal interpolated to 50 Hz; and window pooling, with every
source represented at the target’s bins (bin pooling). Calibration was checked on a null scenario,
the clean scenario with every edge and the stimulus effect removed, in 64 replicates.
Uncertainty.
Intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993)
(2000 resamples), resampling replicates within scenarios for the benchmark, and recordings or
participants for the real data.
2.6
Overnight sleep recordings
We used the healthy-subject recordings of the CAP Sleep Database (Terzano et al., 2001; Goldberger
et al., 2000); the six with a breathing channel (n1, n4, n6, n7, n8, n9) were analysed. The signals
were two EEG derivations (one central, one occipital or parietal where available; about 128 Hz,
with power values at slower levels), breathing (thoracic belt, or nasal airflow for n8, whose
thoracic channel was dominated by drift; about 25 Hz), heart rate from the R-peaks of the
electrocardiogram, and oxygen saturation when present (n1 only). Each night was cut into 10-min
segments within one sleep-stage group (wake, light non-rapid-eye-movement (NREM) sleep, deep
NREM sleep, rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep; at most 6 per group; a final segment of at least
8 min was kept). Segments with more than 5% implausible heartbeat intervals, or more than 10%
of 2-s EEG blocks with extreme amplitude, were skipped.
In every segment RMGC was run twice: with breathing, and with breathing hidden (removed
from the model); we call this the hide-breathing test. Every edge of the hidden run received its RV,
and breathing’s share in each signal was estimated from the breathing run. Edges present only in
the hidden run we call breathing-sensitive: they appear when breathing is omitted, which suggests,
but does not prove, that breathing contributes to them. An edge counts as breathing-sensitive
only if its p-value in the hidden run passes the Benjamini–Hochberg threshold of the run with
breathing (kα/m, with k the number of declared and m the number of tested pairs), so that it
cannot appear merely because the threshold moved with the number of pairs. As a control, a
placebo, a copy of breathing shifted by half its stream’s span, was added to the model and hidden
in the same way. Surrogates without coupling gave the base rate of brain–body edges: the EEG
of one recording with the other signals of another, in segments of the same sleep stage. Edges
present in both runs we call stable; stability says nothing about their physiological validity. The
test is whether the breathing multiple is small for the breathing-sensitive edges and large for the
stable ones.
2.7
Awake recordings: BBBD
The Brain, Body, and Behavior Dataset (BBBD; Madsen et al., 2025) contains simultaneous
EEG (64 channels), ECG, EOG, respiration and eye tracking from participants watching short
educational videos; all signals are released at 128 Hz. Experiments 4 and 5 recorded respiration:
43 and 48 participants, each with six recordings of one video (2.4–6.5 min; in experiment 5 the

same three videos twice, the second time with an incentive). Nodes: four regional EEG averages
(left and right frontal: F3, F5, F7, FC3, FC5 and their right counterparts; left and right parietal:
P3, P5, P7, CP3, CP5 and theirs; average reference), breathing (chest belt, averaged to 25.6 Hz),
heart rate from the hand-corrected R-peaks, pupil size (averaged to 25.6 Hz), and the EOG, the
first principal component of the six EOG channels, as a node for eye activity, which reaches both
the EEG and the measured pupil. A recording was skipped if its heartbeats, EEG, breathing,
pupil or EOG failed quality criteria of the same kind as for the other data sets. The hide-breathing
test, with its fixed threshold and placebo, was as for the sleep recordings, and the surrogates as
described below for both awake data sets; for the cross-participant surrogate, recordings of the
same video line up exactly. The established methods were run on each participant’s first recording
of session 1, a subset fixed in advance. Prevalence was computed per experiment.
2.8
Stress test: MultiPhysio-HRC
MultiPhysio-HRC serves as a stress test: its recordings, made with a dry-electrode headset during
tasks that include movement, strain the method’s assumptions more than the other data sets. We
used the raw physiological data of MultiPhysio-HRC (Bussolan et al., 2025): 52 participants in the
released data (the article reports 55 on the first day) recorded at 256 Hz with a 12-electrode dry
EEG headset and a sensor for the electrocardiogram, a chest respiration band, skin conductance
and trapezius EMG, synchronized by the acquisition software. On a first day each participant
performed rest, guided breathing, five cognitive tasks (Stroop easy and hard, N-back, mental
arithmetic, Tower of Hanoi) and two virtual-reality tasks; on a second day (42 participants), rest
and repeated manual and robot-assisted (cobot) disassembly tasks. Each task recording was split
by repetition into segments, stretches analysed one at a time like the sleep segments, and segments
of at least 2 min were analysed (median 4.1 min, up to 25 min). The nodes are listed in Table 1.
Segments with more than 5% implausible heartbeat intervals, a flat signal, or more than 10% of
1-s EEG blocks with extreme amplitude were skipped. The hide-breathing test, with its fixed
threshold and placebo, was the same as for the sleep recordings. In the guided-breathing task,
breathing →heart rate is a known edge and serves as a positive control. Results are reported by
task group, rest (both days), guided breathing, cognitive (the five cognitive tasks), virtual reality
(both tasks), manual and cobot (the repeated disassembly tasks), and by edge type: EEG–EEG,
brain–heart, brain–other body signals and body–body.
The established methods were run with and without breathing on a subset fixed in advance,
rest (rest 0) and guided breathing (meditation 0) of every participant, because running them
on every segment would have taken weeks; LPCMCI had the same 30-s limit.
Surrogates and timing.
Two surrogates without coupling gave base rates of brain–body edges
in both awake data sets: the EEG of one participant with the other signals of another during the
same task, and each participant’s own body signals shifted circularly by half the segment against
their EEG, which keeps every signal and destroys only their alignment. The trapezius EMG was
high-pass filtered causally. Block means are stamped at the end of their block, and stay stamped
at their block’s end in the time-reversed recording.

Validity and discrimination of the robustness value
Benchmark.
Across 64 replicates (eight of each scenario), RMGC declared 344 edges between
nodes (265 true, 36 faked by a hidden driver, 43 other false). Every faked edge had an RV at
most the actual share of the driver that produced it, measured by the same definition with the

4
Discussion
4.1
Rate matching and physiological timescales
Rate matching avoids the errors that resampling creates.
The common-grid failures in
the benchmark are all consequences of putting signals with different rates and timescales on one
grid: a coarse grid turns a fast relation into an instantaneous one and an indirect path into a direct
edge, a fine grid needs too many lags for slow relations, and held or interpolated samples reverse
edges. Predicting each signal at its own rate and letting every source enter through windows of its
own samples removes these errors without new statistical machinery: RMGC uses ordinary least
squares and F tests. The fairness experiment and the ablations confirm the diagnosis and separate
the contributions: given the same power features, a VAR on common grids matches RMGC’s
recall but not its false-positive rate, and it keeps the reversed and indirect edges; interpolating the
inputs onto one grid brings the reversed edge back into RMGC itself, while the indirect edge is
avoided by the long causal history of the windows, with or without rate matching. The fast-to-slow
edge, by contrast, needs power information, which any method can be given. Passing fast signals
to slower levels as power is what makes fast-to-slow coupling visible, a mechanism that matters for
neurovascular and brain–body questions where slow signals follow neural power, not raw voltage.
Beyond physiology.
Nothing in RMGC or in the attack is specific to physiological signals.
The approach applies whenever five conditions hold.
1. Several signals cover the same period, each recorded at its own rate, irregularly, or as event
times.
2. Influences act with a delay, so that a source’s past can be told apart from the target’s
present, and the question is which signals help predict which (Granger-type edges), not what
an intervention would do.
3. Within each analysed segment the relations are stable enough, and the segment long enough,
to span many times the longest window used (16 s here; months, years or decades elsewhere).
4. The influences are linear, or nonlinear in ways that power, squared or small-network terms
can represent.
5. For robustness values to be interpretable, plausible hidden drivers can be described by
their persistence or rhythm, and at least one candidate driver is measured, to serve as a
benchmark.
14


Econometrics is the most direct case. Macroeconomic and financial series arrive daily, monthly
and quarterly; aggregating them to the lowest common frequency is known to distort Granger
causality, and mixed-frequency tests were developed for exactly this reason (Ghysels et al., 2016;
G¨otz et al., 2016). Averaging high-frequency data over windows of increasing length is also an
established device there, in models of realized volatility (Corsi, 2009), and sensitivity analysis
benchmarked against an observed variable comes from the same tradition (Cinelli and Hazlett,
2020). What RMGC adds to these tools is a graph over many signals with multiple-testing control,
signals sampled at any rate or recorded as events rather than at regular intervals in a fixed ratio
of frequencies, separate levels for each timescale, explicit cross-scale edges and per-edge robustness
to hidden drivers. Other fields meet the same conditions. Climate and environmental monitoring
combines instruments sampled every second with daily measurements and slow proxies. Industrial
monitoring combines fast vibration or current sensors with slow process variables and alarm events.
Historical analysis works with annual prices or harvests, censuses every ten years, and dated events
such as wars, epidemics or reforms, records in which hidden common drivers such as climate or
institutions are the rule rather than the exception. The conditions also mark the limits, which our
real data illustrate: short or strongly changing series, influences that are mostly instantaneous at
the available resolution, and aggregate measures that mix several underlying sources (the analogue
of volume conduction) all weaken the method.
4.2
Interpreting robustness values
The robustness value is valid in simulation; it discriminates only with a benchmark
and enough data.
No method avoided strong hidden drivers, and edge size was misleading:
slow, persistent drivers fake large edges cheaply. The robustness value was valid in the benchmark,
also for drivers outside the searched class, and consistent with most breathing-sensitive edges in
all three data sets (k ≤1 for 84% in sleep, 59–100% in BBBD and 83–92% in MultiPhysio-HRC).
It discriminated well in the benchmark (AUC 0.80, and 0.94 relative to breathing), moderately in
sleep (0.88 overall, 0.75 within brain–heart edges) and weakly in the awake recordings (0.48–0.63
in BBBD, 0.58–0.66 in MultiPhysio-HRC). Comparing with a measured driver mirrors sensitivity
analysis in regression (Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020): the useful question is not whether an edge could
be confounded but whether a confounder as strong as a known one could explain it. Drivers that
act in ways the class cannot mimic are the limit: an oscillation that modulates amplitude faked
an edge that looked as robust as true ones, although its RV stayed below the driver’s actual share.
Like the smallest perturbation that an attack finds against a classifier, which bounds the true
minimum from above (Carlini and Wagner, 2017), the RV is an upper bound: drivers outside the
searched class might produce an edge with a smaller share.
4.3
Breathing as a common pathway in brain–heart networks
In sleep, about half of the brain–heart edges are fragile.
Most breathing-sensitive edges
(84%) were within reach of a driver weaker than breathing, as they should be, but so was about
half of the stable brain–heart edges, and 23% needed more than twice breathing’s strength. Within
brain–heart edges the breathing multiple did not beat edge size. We read this as a substantive
result: during sleep, many directed brain–heart edges between these signals could be produced by
a hidden driver of breathing’s strength, so they should not be interpreted as direct neural control
of the heart without further evidence. The bidirectional EEG edges in every segment are most
likely shared activity or mixing between scalp signals (Haufe et al., 2013); robustness to hidden
drivers cannot address them. Time reversal can: in half of the segments neither direction survived
it, and where one did it was almost always anterior to posterior, increasingly so with sleep depth.
On the same segments the established methods performed like RMGC; RMGC’s advantage on
real data appeared in MultiPhysio-HRC, where the coupling was weaker and the segments shorter;
in BBBD, every method except LPCMCI found breathing →heart rate in 93–99% of recordings.
15


In awake tasks, brain–body edges are very fragile.
A typical brain–heart edge needed a
hidden driver only about 0.4 times as strong as breathing, against about 1.0 in sleep, and the
breathing multiple separated breathing-sensitive from stable edges hardly better than edge size.
The MultiPhysio-HRC recordings differ from sleep in ways that all point to fragility: segments of
a few minutes with changing tasks, movement, dry electrodes, and more candidate hidden drivers
(arousal, posture, muscle activity). The last point also weakens the test itself, because stable edges
are probably confounded by drivers other than breathing and are a poorer reference than in sleep.
Yet BBBD, with none of these conditions and an error rate that passed the surrogate check, gave
the same picture: its stable brain–heart edges needed a median of 0.37 times breathing’s share,
against 0.38 in MultiPhysio-HRC. In both awake data sets, whatever the recording conditions, a
hidden driver weaker than breathing could produce most brain–heart edges. The positive control
and the breathing shares, which do not depend on the attack, behaved as physiology predicts.
In BBBD the background rate in surrogates was low (2–3% of the possible brain–body edges),
although its recordings are as short as MultiPhysio-HRC’s; there, recorded with dry electrodes
during tasks with movement, 12% were still declared with the body signals shifted circularly
against the EEG, against 2–4% in sleep. Background detection therefore depends on recording
conditions rather than segment length, and circular-shift surrogates are a cheap check for any new
data set. In MultiPhysio-HRC, short awake segments with drifts and task changes violate the
stationarity the tests assume, and hiding a placebo gave almost two thirds as many new edges
as hiding breathing. Awake edges must be read against these base rates. On the same awake
segments, RMGC found the known breathing →heart rate edge more often than the established
methods, especially during guided breathing (82% against 32–68%; at rest 70% against 30–62%),
but it also produced more breathing-sensitive edges: the sensitivity that finds real slow couplings
also picks up slow common drivers, which is why per-edge robustness values are needed.
4.4
Limitations and future work
Limitations.
In 64 null replicates RMGC’s false edges were consistent with a 5% false-discovery
rate (1 replicate with a false edge), while the matched grid VAR and PCMCI exceeded it. On real
data, surrogates gave low background rates in sleep and BBBD and about 12% of the possible
brain–body edges in MultiPhysio-HRC; these rates are an empirical diagnostic of background
detection, not the false-discovery rate of the real graphs. The robustness values are point estimates,
without uncertainty. Drivers that act in ways the searched class cannot mimic, such as amplitude
modulation, make faked edges look robust. The established methods ran on real data only on
subsets fixed in advance (one segment per sleep stage and recording, each participant’s first BBBD
recording, and rest and guided breathing in MultiPhysio-HRC), and LPCMCI was not feasible
there within its time limit. Time reversal is proven valid only for unidirectional coupling between
two variables (Winkler et al., 2016); for pairs coupled in both directions, such as breathing and
heart rate, its verdicts are unreliable. The benchmark is linear apart from the power and squared
edges, has two hidden drivers and was calibrated by hand: its parameters were chosen, not fitted
to data, and the strong variants were set to trap the baselines. Real physiology has artefacts and
nonstationarity it does not contain. The attack’s class has four kinds of driver acting in three
ways; the RV is an upper bound over it. LPCMCI ran on the coarse grid only, and the penalty
of neural Granger causality was at the edge of the range tried. RMGC treats each recording
separately; group conclusions rest on prevalence across participants. In the sleep data only two
EEG derivations per recording were used because the montages differ; in all real data sets, stable
edges are not ground truth. Edges are predictive, not interventional; only a stimulus provides
true interventions.
Future work.
Data sets with controlled stimuli (Uppal et al., 2025), to test directions against
interventions. Thresholds calibrated on surrogates, or detrending, for short nonstationary segments;
16


bootstrap intervals for the robustness values. A combination of the linear and nonlinear tests
that gives most of the threshold to the linear one (a weighted Bonferroni correction), so that
RMGC-NN loses fewer linear edges, and amplitude-modulating drivers in the attack’s nonlinear
class, so that robustness values can flag breathing-mediated nonlinear edges. Learned spatial
filters as EEG nodes: instead of fixed derivations or regional averages, each EEG node would be a
weighted sum of all electrodes, with the weights learned together with the graph as a first layer of
RMGC (constrained, for example, to be orthogonal, and shared across subjects), so that nodes
approximate underlying sources rather than mixtures and volume conduction is reduced. Also:
hierarchical models that pool edge sizes across subjects, and band-limited power channels.
5
Conclusions
Rate-matched Granger causality estimates directed graphs from signals recorded at different rates
and acting on different timescales, without a common sampling grid. On a benchmark it found
more true edges than vector autoregressions on common grids and four established methods, at an
equally low false-positive rate; given the same power features, a grid model and neural Granger
causality matched its recall but kept 2.4 to 3.5 times its false positives and the reversed and indirect
edges that resampling creates. In the MultiPhysio-HRC recordings, where the coupling is weaker
and the segments short, it found a known breathing-to-heart edge more often than the established
methods; in sleep and in BBBD, the established methods except LPCMCI found it about as often.
Its neural extension, RMGC-NN, finds nonlinear edges in simulation; on real recordings about
half of the edges it adds come from breathing, and their robustness values flag them as fragile
but cannot tell which, so we recommend RMGC as the default. The benchmark had several
sources of false edges: coarse or resampled grids (indirect paths declared as direct, reversed edges),
measurement noise, mixing between scalp signals, and hidden drivers. In the tested scenarios
RMGC avoided the errors of coarse or resampled grids; time reversal removed much of the mixing,
which ultimately needs better node definitions. Hidden drivers remain, and the robustness value
assesses how sensitive each edge is to them, within the searched class. The robustness value was
valid in simulation, also for drivers outside the searched class. Expressed relative to a measured
driver such as breathing, it identified the false edges caused by hidden drivers reliably in simulation
and moderately in sleep. In overnight recordings about half of the brain–heart edges could be
produced by a hidden driver as strong as breathing; in the MultiPhysio-HRC tasks 70–79% of
the stable brain–body edges could, as could 94% of the stable brain–heart edges in BBBD. The
method’s false-positive rate held in BBBD but exceeded the nominal level in the MultiPhysio-HRC
stress test. The approach is general: it applies whenever signals recorded at different rates or
as events influence each other with delays, over segments long and stable enough for the slowest
timescale, through effects its linear or nonlinear terms can represent, and, for robustness values,
with a measured candidate driver to serve as a benchmark. Mixed-frequency economic data,
climate and industrial monitoring, and historical records are examples.

Edges and edge classes.
The base system has six edges: STIM →EEG1 (50 ms); EEG1
→EEG2 (20 ms); SC →EEG1 (0.5 s); EEG2 →fNIRS through EEG2’s power (2 s, as in

if its source is the stimulus, within fast or within medium if both signals belong to that timescale,
and cross-scale if they belong to different timescales (SC →EEG1 and EEG2 →fNIRS). There is
no within-slow class: skin conductance is the only observed slow signal, because arousal is hidden.

S2
Extended comparisons with established methods
S2.1
Comparators
Common grids.
Every signal is put on one grid by causal binning (bin means, the last sample
when a bin is empty, the rate of the last completed heartbeat, beat counts), a vector autoregression
(VAR) is fitted with a fixed order (15 lags on the 50-Hz grid, 8 on the 2-Hz grid), and each ordered
pair is tested by the conditional Granger F test (L¨utkepohl, 2005; Barnett and Seth, 2014). We
used a 50-Hz grid with lags up to 0.3 s, a 2-Hz grid with lags up to 4 s, and the union of their
edges.
Established methods.
We compared four established methods, each run on the same two
common grids with the union of their edges, so that the comparison isolates the method from
the gridding, on all eight replicates of each scenario. On the real recordings, the grid VAR,
24


multiscale Granger causality and PCMCI tested each ordered pair once, as in the second version
of the fairness experiment (Bonferroni over the pair’s grids and columns, one Benjamini–Hochberg
procedure, power columns for the EEG nodes only); LPCMCI kept its own rule. We did not
run VARX (Parra et al., 2025) as a separate method: without exogenous inputs it is a vector
autoregression on a common grid, which the grid VAR covers. Its distinguishing feature, exogenous
inputs such as stimulus features, is already how every method treats the benchmark’s stimulus, a
source that is never a target, and has no counterpart in the real recordings, where no stimulus is
modelled. Multiscale Granger causality (Faes et al., 2017), implemented from the paper: a VAR
on the grid (order by BIC); for each scale τ, a causal finite-impulse-response (FIR) low-pass filter
(order 6, Hamming window; Oppenheim and Schafer, 2010; cutoff 1/(2τ) cycles per sample) and
downsampling by τ, written in state-space form; Granger causality at each scale from Riccati
equations (Barnett and Seth, 2015). We used scales 1, 5 and 25 on the 50-Hz grid and 1, 2 and 4
on the 2-Hz grid. Our implementation reproduces the paper’s first example (Granger causality
peaking at τ = 2 at 0.404, the reverse direction near zero). Significance follows the paper’s
application, by surrogates: 10 surrogate data sets in which every series is circularly shifted by
an independent random offset; the null values are pooled over pairs at each scale and fitted by
a gamma distribution, whose tail gives the p-values; a pair’s p-value is the smallest over scales
times the number of scales, and the Benjamini–Hochberg rule decides. When the Riccati solver
failed (eigenvalues on the unit circle, with a strictly periodic stimulus), it was retried with a
tiny regularization, and the scale was left out if it still failed; this happened only in a few scale
computations of the tempo scenario. Neural Granger causality (Tank et al., 2022), run with the
authors’ own code in its component-wise multilayer-perceptron form and with the settings of their
demonstration: one network per target node with a hidden layer of 100 units, a hierarchical group
penalty on the first-layer weights of each source node, and their proximal gradient training with
early stopping (at most 50 000 iterations). If, in the network of v, the first-layer weights of u are
not all zero after training, the edge u →v is declared. The penalty was chosen once, among eight
values from 0.0005 to 0.1, by the best F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) against
the ground truth of one clean replicate, and then fixed, which favours the method; the chosen
value, λ = 0.1, was the largest tried, so a larger one might have done better. PCMCI (Runge
et al., 2019) with partial-correlation tests (tigramite library; αPC = 0.05): a pair’s p-value is the
smallest over lags times the number of lags, and the Benjamini–Hochberg rule decides. LPCMCI
(Gerhardus and Runge, 2020), which allows hidden confounders, on the 2-Hz grid only because of
its cost: LPCMCI marks each lagged link from u to v as →(u drives v), ↔(both are driven by a
hidden confounder) or ◦→(one of the two, which LPCMCI cannot tell apart); a link marked →
or ◦→declares the edge u →v, and a link marked ↔is reported as hidden confounding, not as
an edge. Its run time varied strongly with the data, so it ran with a 30-s limit and then with half

the prediction.

S4.2
Time reversal: methods
Time reversal.
A directed influence u →v reverses when the data are reversed in time, whereas
dependence produced by mixing or other time-symmetric effects does not (Haufe et al., 2013;
Winkler et al., 2016). For a pair declared in both directions, the statistic is the net flow on the
original data minus the net flow on the time-reversed data, D = [a(u →v) −a(v →u)] −[˜a(u →
v) −˜a(v →u)], with a the edge size and ˜a the same on the recording mirrored in time (every
stream at its own rate). D is computed in five equal blocks, and a direction is kept if its mean is
significantly positive (one-sided t test, 5%). This is the difference-based variant of time-reversed
Granger causality (Winkler et al., 2016), whose validity is proven for unidirectional coupling
between two linear autoregressive variables; with several signals and possibly bidirectional coupling
it is a heuristic check. Edges declared in one direction only are not tested, and stimulus edges are
exempt.
Neural extension on the real data.
On the same segments of all three data sets, RMGC-NN
was also run with and without breathing. Edges it declared that RMGC did not (added edges)
received a robustness value from the nonlinear attack and their breathing multiple.
S4.3
Time reversal
On the benchmark, applied to the pairs that RMGC declared in both directions (Table S15), time
reversal removed the volume-conduction edge EEG2 →EEG1 in every replicate (100% to 0%),
lowered the share of replicates with the edge faked by strong hidden breathing from 100% to 75%
and the false-positive rate from 0.05 to 0.04, at essentially no cost in recall (0.92 to 0.92). Applied
to every edge, it removed nearly all false edges, including those faked by arousal, but lost 29% of
the true ones (recall from 0.92 to 0.66): five blocks give too little power for slow edges.
S4.4
The neural extension on the real recordings
In sleep, RMGC-NN added edges to RMGC’s in 9% of the 115 segments; with breathing hidden it
added 6 edges, 3 of them breathing-sensitive. In BBBD it added edges in 70% of the 518 recordings
(1.4 per recording), mostly from the EOG to heart rate and breathing and between pupil and
breathing; 64% of those it added with breathing hidden were breathing-sensitive, against 4% of
RMGC’s edges, and they were fragile (median RV 0.03, k ≤1 for 73%); it missed linear edges
in 75% of the recordings. In MultiPhysio-HRC it added edges in 94% of the 557 segments, 4.1
33


per segment, and 55% of those it added with breathing hidden were breathing-sensitive, against
6% of RMGC’s edges (both with the threshold recomputed). The added edges were fragile: their
median RV was 0.03 and their median breathing multiple 0.19, with k ≤1 for 77% of them (in
sleep, for 5 of the 6). The robustness values did not, however, separate the breathing-sensitive
added edges from the stable ones (AUC 0.50). The positive control was unchanged (breathing →

some of RMGC’s edges, in 33% of the sleep and 79% of the awake segments.
S4.5
Discussion of the nonlinear extension
The neural extension finds nonlinear edges, at a cost.
RMGC-NN keeps every linear test,
but its combined test gives each of its two tests half the threshold, so it finds slightly fewer linear
edges (medium recall 0.75 against 0.80); in exchange it alone among the RMGC variants found
the squared edge in every replicate, with the network size chosen by inner validation. Two design
choices made this possible: a small network on a few summaries per source, fitted to the residuals
of the linear model, and tests that use all data through cross-fitting. Networks fitted to every
window of every source would multiply the parameters; with the small datasets of physiological
studies, a neural model works best as an extension of a well-specified linear model rather than a
replacement.
On real data, what the network adds is largely breathing.
Breathing affects heart rate,
EEG amplitude and skin conductance, not always linearly. With breathing hidden, signals that
depend on it share residual dependence that is partly nonlinear: RMGC cannot see it, the network
can. In the awake recordings about half of the edges the network added were of this kind. Their
robustness values were low (median 0.03): the corrected search flags the added edges as fragile,
whether they come from breathing or not, but cannot tell which do. The power channel already
brings the most important nonlinearity, the dependence on a fast signal’s power, into RMGC. We
therefore recommend RMGC as the default and RMGC-NN when nonlinear effects are expected,
with the edges it adds checked against measured candidate drivers.
S5
Detailed real-data analyses
Prevalence across participants.
An edge counts as present in a participant if it is declared in
more than half of that participant’s segments in a task group. Following the logic of prevalence
inference (Allefeld et al., 2016), we ask whether an edge is present in most people, rather than
whether it is present on average. Each participant contributes a yes or a no. If at most half of
the population has the edge, a participant shows it with probability at most 0.5 + 0.5α: the half
who have it, plus false positives, at a rate of at most α = 0.05, among the others. A one-sided
binomial test against this bound, with the Benjamini–Hochberg rule across edges, asks whether
the edge is present in most participants.
