Technical Report — MEG2text:
Beyond Alignment in
Non-Invasive Brain-to-Text
Yiqian Yang

yiqian@uploading.tech

Abstract
Non-invasive brain-to-text seeks to recover language from brain activity without
surgery. A central challenge is turning learned brain–language representations into
accurate word predictions. We present MEG2text, a decoding system that combines
corrected training and evaluation, brain–text alignment, a class-balanced word
readout, and target-word language-model fusion. On LibriBrain100’s word-ranking
task, MEG2text reaches 64.12% mean Top-10 balanced accuracy, improving
on published MEG-XL by 16.92 percentage points across four corpora, with
gains on each corpus. Controlled comparisons show that both implementation
corrections and a learned readout contribute to this improvement. Candidate-level
analysis reveals that some words receive informative scores yet consistently lose to
competing candidates, while an identical-feature comparison shows that learning
the classification rule improves decoding without changing the representation.
These findings identify the decoding rule as a consequential part of non-invasive
brain-to-text and show how system improvements can make better use of aligned
neural features.
1
Introduction
Recovering language from non-invasive brain recordings could help restore communication for people
with severe paralysis. Progress toward this goal requires both learning useful neural representations
and converting them into accurate language predictions. MEG provides millisecond-resolution
measurements of brain activity without surgery, and LibriBrain100 [Mantegna et al., 2026] makes
more than one hundred hours of speech-listening recordings available for studying this problem. Its
word-decoding benchmark offers a concrete test: given a neural event at a known word onset, rank
the corresponding word among fifty candidates.
The MEG-XL baseline [Jayalath and Parker Jones, 2026a] approaches this task by aligning neural
features with frozen T5 word embeddings [Raffel et al., 2020] and ranking words by their similarity
to those embeddings. This connects representation learning and prediction through a single scoring
rule. Yet a score that distinguishes a word’s own events from other events can still remain below
competing words’ scores. We observe exactly this pattern in a retrained MEG-XL model: some
words have informative scores but never enter the top ten. The observation raises a broader question
for decoding systems: how can we turn the information available after alignment into more accurate
word predictions?
MEG2text addresses this question across the decoding pipeline (Figure 1). We first correct defects
in the released training and evaluation code so that the alignment model is trained and evaluated
as intended. We then retain the learned representation and fit a class-balanced classifier that learns
how candidate words compete. Finally, a frozen T5 decoder, conditioned on the target event through

We evaluate the complete system, isolate implementation and readout improvements through con-
trolled comparisons, and diagnose which words gain or lose correct predictions. Our contributions
are:
• A stronger complete MEG decoding system. MEG2text combines implementation repairs,
aligned neural features, learned classification and target-word T5 fusion. It improves
performance across all four benchmark corpora, with controlled experiments measuring the
gains from specific stages (Section 5.1).
• Evidence that similarity scoring leaves useful information untapped. Learning a class-
balanced readout on identical frozen features improves every corpus on both validation and
test. This comparison establishes that better word predictions are possible without changing
the representation, while retaining a linear scoring rule (Section 5.2).
• A diagnostic distinction between separation and retrieval. Word-level analysis identifies
informative scores that fail in the shared candidate ranking. Comparing readouts shows how
recovering missed words trades off against predictions elsewhere in the vocabulary, giving a
way to diagnose decoding failures beyond aggregate accuracy (Sections 5.3–5.4).
2
Related Work
Speech decoding by alignment.
Contrastive alignment enables identification of heard speech
by connecting non-invasive brain recordings to pretrained speech representations [Défossez et al.,
2023]. MEG-XL [Jayalath and Parker Jones, 2026a] learns long-context MEG representations, and its
LibriBrain100 baseline [Mantegna et al., 2026] adapts them to word classification through alignment
with T5 embeddings [Raffel et al., 2020] using a SigLIP-style loss [Zhai et al., 2023]. We retain this
representation-learning approach and examine the complete path from its implementation to the rule
used for word prediction.
2


Similarity scoring and fitted classifiers.
Linear probes test how well frozen representations sup-
port classification [Alain and Bengio, 2016]. CLIP compares fitted linear classifiers with predictions
based on text embeddings [Radford et al., 2021], and long-tailed recognition separates representation
learning from classifier fitting [Kang et al., 2020]. These precedents motivate our controlled com-
parison. The MEG encoder already receives word supervision during alignment, so our question is
whether fitting the downstream decision rule improves predictions from that supervised representation.
Word-level diagnostics then examine what changes in the resulting ranking.
Hubness and class priors.
In high-dimensional nearest-neighbour retrieval, some targets become
hubs that are returned much more often than others [Radovanovi´c et al., 2010]. This imbalance
has motivated neighbourhood-based score corrections for zero-shot retrieval [Dinu et al., 2015,
Conneau et al., 2018, Smith et al., 2017]; logit adjustment addresses class-prior effects in long-tailed
classification [Menon et al., 2021]. The uneven candidate frequencies we observe on LibriBrain100
motivate testing both families of correction. They provide comparisons for determining how much
can be recovered by adjusting similarity scores while retaining the text embeddings as classifier
weights.

Method
Representation.
Each labelled word occurrence defines an event i, with an MEG window spanning
−0.5 to +2.5 s relative to its onset. Following the original implementation, the encoder jointly
processes groups of up to 50 consecutive, overlapping windows. A pooling and projection head
produces a 1,024-dimensional vector hi for each event (Appendix A). Unless stated otherwise,
readouts use its frozen, normalized representation xi = hi/∥hi∥2. We denote the event’s word label
by yi and the normalized frozen T5 embedding of candidate k by ˆek = ek/∥ek∥2. The K = 50
candidate words are the classification labels.

The softmax trains each word’s score relative to all other candidates, and inverse-frequency weights

raw frequency of correct events. The bias is unpenalized. We fit on training labels and choose the

To test the choice of objective, we fit a second linear readout using squared loss against one-hot word
labels. It uses the same features, class weights and unpenalized bias, with a ridge penalty selected on
validation data. This comparison holds the representation and classifier form fixed while changing
how prediction errors are penalized.
T5 branch.
The T5 branch tests whether a pretrained language decoder contributes information
beyond the fitted classifier. Two trainable adapters map neural features to a memory sequence for
cross-attention and a query added to the start-token embedding of a frozen T5-large decoder. We
compare memory from all events in the input group (group memory) with memory from the target
event alone (target-only memory). Target-only memory retains the target’s contextualized feature from
joint encoding of the group. The decoder normalizes next-token scores over allowed continuations in
a candidate prefix tree and sums log-probabilities along each word’s path to obtain ℓik (Appendix A).

4
Experimental Setup
Data.
We use the public LibriBrain100 training release for Subjects 0–32 and the official Subject 0
validation and test partitions for Sherlock, TIMIT, MOCHA-TIMIT and TheMoth.1 Encoder align-
ment uses 15,613 groups comprising 780,650 events; Subject 0 contributes 95.87% of these events.
Readout fitting uses the 314,371 training events whose labels belong to the fifty-word candidate
vocabulary; events outside that vocabulary contribute to alignment but are excluded from readout
fitting. Validation and test contain 3,149 and 3,172 fixed-vocabulary events, respectively, covering all
fifty word classes. Appendix C gives the corpus-level counts.
Metric and baseline.
We report the equal-weight four-corpus mean of Top-10 balanced accuracy.
Within each corpus, we compute the fraction of correct top-ten predictions separately for each word
class and average over the classes present. A uniform random ranking has expected accuracy of 20%.
For diagnostics, a candidate is nominated whenever it enters an event’s top ten; its nomination rate is
the fraction of pooled test events where this occurs, irrespective of the true word. We compare with
published joint-training MEG-XL scores [Mantegna et al., 2026], whose four-corpus mean is 47.20%
(Table 1), using the same official partitions, candidate list and metric. All differences are computed
before rounding. A separate matched implementation control, described in Appendix B, measures
the effect of the repairs.
Backbone.
We initialize the encoder from the official MEG-XL V2 pretrained weights, freeze
the signal tokenizer, and train the encoder, word head and loss parameters. The original alignment
objective uses other words in each group as negatives and masks repeated words. We repair two defects
in the released implementation: evaluation left dropout active, and optimization and checkpoint
saving omitted the loss temperature and bias. All fitted readouts use the epoch-2 model selected
on validation data after two epochs of training (checkpoint A). Archived diagnostic scores use the
epoch-3 model selected after continuing the run to six epochs (checkpoint B).
Selection and test use.
Within each variant, validation data determine the hyperparameters and
training epoch, and training labels are used to fit the readout (Appendix A). Table 6 reports all 20
development variants; Section 7 describes how test feedback entered system selection.
Statistical analysis.
We estimate sampling uncertainty by resampling sentences within each corpus,
using 10,000 cluster-bootstrap replicates [Efron, 1979]. Every method is evaluated on the same
resampled sentences, giving paired score differences. We report 95% basic bootstrap intervals;
Appendix C defines these intervals and provides the percentile alternatives. The identical-feature
comparison uses checkpoint A, whose similarity score is 60.21%. The saved event-level comparisons
and diagnostics use fitted readouts on checkpoint A and similarity scoring on checkpoint B, whose
mean is 59.98%.
5
Results
5.1
Main comparison
The complete MEG2text system reaches 64.12% four-corpus mean Top-10 balanced accuracy, a
16.92-point improvement over published MEG-XL (Table 1). The improvement holds separately on
Sherlock, TIMIT, MOCHA-TIMIT and TheMoth.
The implementation control and readout ablations identify improvements at successive stages of
the system (Figure 2a). With our initialization, public data and two-epoch training configuration,
the unmodified official code reaches 53.04%. Correcting the two implementation defects raises
similarity-scoring accuracy to 60.21%, a matched gain of 7.17 points (Appendix B). Keeping these
backbone features fixed, the learned readout adds 3.77 points and target-word T5 fusion adds a further
0.14 (Table 2).

5.2
Readout comparison
On identical frozen features, the cross-entropy readout raises the four-corpus test mean from 60.21%
to 63.98% (Table 2). Every corpus improves on both validation and test. Because the encoder
outputs are unchanged, this comparison attributes the gain to learning the word-classification rule and
demonstrates useful information left untapped by similarity scoring.
The archived event scores support a paired comparison with checkpoint-B similarity scoring: the
cross-entropy readout improves the mean by 4.00 points, with a 95% interval of [+1.02, +5.72]
(Table 3).
Fitting a classifier is only part of the improvement: the choice of loss also matters in this comparison.
With features and class weights fixed, cross-entropy exceeds squared loss by 2.77 test points (paired
95% interval [+0.83, +4.43]) and scores higher on validation. Squared loss improves over similarity
scoring in two corpora and falls below it in the other two (Table 2). The objective that explicitly
compares candidate scores produces the more consistent improvement.
The ranking gains are concentrated beyond the first position. In the archived-score comparison,
cross-entropy changes Top-1 balanced accuracy by −0.21 points [−2.17, +1.91] and Top-5 by +3.44
[+0.52, +5.07] relative to checkpoint-B similarity scoring. Squared loss lowers Top-1 by 4.23 points.
Thus, the improved Top-10 result reflects better inclusion of the true word among leading candidates,
with little change in the cross-entropy readout’s Top-1 score.
5.3
Per-class analysis
Similarity scoring consistently misses six words that account for 943 of the 3,172 test events. Five—
the, of, we, for and she—never enter the top ten; i enters three times, but never when it is correct.
At the other extreme, were is nominated on 43.38% of all events (Figure 2b, Table 4). The missed
words include the first, third and fourth most frequent training candidates, while other common words
such as and, to and a have high hit rates. These zero-hit classes therefore reveal a systematic ranking
imbalance that affects frequently observed words as well as less common ones.
One-versus-rest AUROC distinguishes missed predictions from uninformative scores by testing
whether a candidate’s score is higher on its own events than on other events. Similarity scores for the
and of show weak separation, but we and she reach AUROCs of 0.75 and 0.72 despite never entering
the top ten (Figure 2c). Their scores contain information about the correct class, yet competing words
outrank them. A zero hit rate can thus hide two different problems: weak class separation and a
scoring rule that fails to turn available separation into retrieval.

The fitted cross-entropy readout nominates every candidate. In the archived-score comparison, the six
zero-hit classes reach hit rates of 68–83% (Figure 2d) and account for 734 of the 837 newly correct
events. Most recovered hits therefore come from words that similarity scoring consistently missed.
In this diagnostic comparison, the six recovered classes contribute +8.57 points to the four-corpus
mean, while changes in the remaining 44 contribute −4.57 (Appendix C). The net improvement
therefore comes from recovering persistently missed words at a smaller cost elsewhere in the
vocabulary. Table 9 reports the per-word separation and hit rates, showing which words benefit and
which lose correct predictions.
Recovering the zero-hit words alone does not explain why cross-entropy is the better readout. Squared
loss also nominates every word and gives the six classes a mean hit rate of 69.9%, with class separation
comparable to cross-entropy (Table 4). The difference lies in predictions elsewhere: on identical
features, cross-entropy correctly ranks 167 events that squared loss misses, while the reverse occurs
for 84 events. Its contribution to balanced accuracy improves for 29 classes and declines for 11. A
useful readout must therefore recover neglected words while preserving correct rankings across the
rest of the vocabulary.

Score corrections provide a complementary test of this ranking problem. Six evaluated corrections

5.4
Alternative readouts and the T5 branch
After establishing the linear readout, we test whether additional transformations improve its pre-
dictions. Seven alternatives add nonlinear, contextual, cross-layer or corpus-specific corrections to
its frozen scores (Table 5). None has a higher test mean than linear cross-entropy. For the three
with saved event-level predictions, paired intervals include zero and their upper bounds are below
1.2 points. The gated cross-layer correction improves validation accuracy but scores lower on test.
Within these tested designs, additional readout capacity does not provide a consistent improvement.
The remaining two alternatives change the input features of the squared-loss readout. Using interme-
diate word-head features after the GELU gives similar accuracy to using the final representation. A
kernel feature map improves the squared-loss result, but its test mean remains below linear cross-
entropy. In these comparisons, changing the fitting objective is more effective than either feature
extension.
The T5 branch tests whether a pretrained decoder adds useful predictions to the learned readout.
Target-only memory substantially outperforms group memory when each branch predicts words alone,
on both validation and test, but their fused results are close (Table 8, Appendix A). Target-only fusion
adds 0.14 points to the linear readout, bringing MEG2text to 64.12%. Thus, the large standalone
advantage translates into only a small advantage after fusion: the relevant system-level comparison is
the improvement beyond the existing classifier.
6
Discussion
The system gain reflects improvements in both learning and using aligned features. The imple-
mentation control establishes a gain from correcting training and evaluation; the identical-feature
comparison shows that the resulting representation supports better predictions than its original sim-
ilarity scores achieve. Evaluating an aligned encoder through a single scoring rule can therefore
understate its value for word decoding.
Word-level diagnostics help locate this gap. Informative scores with no nominations identify candi-
dates whose cross-candidate ranking deserves investigation before concluding that their representation
lacks useful information. The identical-feature readout comparison separately demonstrates that
predictions can improve while the encoder remains fixed.

7
Limitations
The study uses one training seed and test-informed development: the full system was selected by test
mean among 20 variants, and some score corrections were fitted or tuned on test labels. Bootstrap
intervals quantify test-sampling uncertainty, excluding retraining and between-session variation. The
benchmark evaluates Subject 0 with known word onsets and fifty candidates; this subject contributes
about 96% of training events. Overlapping windows expose timing cues [Jayalath and Parker Jones,
2026b], some TIMIT windows share samples across partitions, and possible training exposure to
MOCHA-TIMIT test sentences remains unverified. Generalization to unseen participants and open-
vocabulary decoding therefore requires separate evaluation. The published-baseline comparison
also includes differences in pretraining, public-data coverage and training configuration, while the
original paper leaves the exact checkpoint and launch configuration unspecified. Our matched control
measures the two repairs jointly with one seeded export; the residual gap to the published baseline
remains unattributed. The 3.77-point readout gain holds features fixed, but has no computed paired
interval. The +4.00 interval and six-class diagnostic use different checkpoints and do not isolate the
readout’s effect or establish the cause of the zero-hit pattern. Finally, the fitted classifier requires
labelled examples, and the comparisons omit training-fitted offsets, standalone nonlinear cross-
entropy classifiers and encoder fine-tuning. In-sample offsets match the fitted readout’s aggregate
score, so the design recommendations apply to the evaluated readouts and T5 configurations.
8
Conclusion
MEG2text improves non-invasive word decoding by addressing the full path from brain–text align-
ment to candidate ranking. The complete system reaches 64.12% mean Top-10 balanced accuracy on
LibriBrain100, exceeding published MEG-XL by 16.92 percentage points with gains across all four
corpora. Controlled comparisons establish improvements from correcting training and evaluation and
from fitting a class-balanced readout to the same aligned features. Word-level analysis shows why the
decoding rule matters: a candidate can distinguish its own events yet consistently lose to competing
words. In this setting, similarity-based retrieval underestimates the word-decoding performance
attainable from the aligned representation. Comparing it with a learned decision rule gives a more
informative assessment of what these neural features support.

Checkpoints.
Checkpoint A was selected by pooled validation among the first two epochs (31,226

(mean 64.03%).
Backbone variants.
Three backbone variants test alternatives to the original feature aggregation and
are evaluated by similarity scoring (Table 6). Conditional temporal pooling learns input-dependent
weights over time; static temporal pooling learns fixed temporal weights for each channel; a spatial
bottleneck projects the 306 input channels onto 32 learned components. The table records the selected
epoch within the first two training epochs and, for continued runs, the later selected epoch.
Fitted readouts.
We fit the cross-entropy readout from zero initialization on all 314,371
fixed-vocabulary training events using full-batch L-BFGS [Liu and Nocedal, 1989]. For λ ∈
{10−6, 10−5, 10−4}, pooled validation accuracy is 64.38%, 64.17% and 62.17%, respectively, select-
ing λ = 10−6. The corresponding four-corpus validation means are 64.03%, 64.69% and 63.59%;
selection uses the pooled criterion recorded in Table 7. Optimization uses double precision, a strong-
Wolfe line search, and two phases with gradient tolerances 10−7 and 10−8. We accept a fit when its
largest absolute gradient component is below 10−6. In increasing order of λ, the fits terminate after
1,856, 577 and 108 iterations with maximum gradient magnitudes of 3.4 × 10−8, 1.3 × 10−7 and
4.1 × 10−7. All three fits take about nine minutes on one GPU. The squared-loss readout is solved in
closed form.
Alternative readouts.
Seven alternatives add corrections to the frozen cross-entropy scores. E6
uses a zero-initialized nonlinear network on normalized features, and E11 adds LayerNorm after its
first linear layer. E9 supplies context through a two-layer bidirectional GRU over the word group.
E12 and E13 project final and intermediate word-head features to 512 dimensions and combine
them with equal weights or learned channelwise gates, respectively. E16 adds corpus-specific class
biases, and E17 adds corpus-specific linear terms. Two further alternatives fit squared-loss readouts
on different features: E7 uses the 2,048-dimensional activations after the word head’s GELU, and
E8 uses a 1,024-centre Nyström approximation to an RBF kernel. The gated cross-layer correction
reaches a validation mean of 66.39% versus 64.03% for linear cross-entropy, but their test means are
63.87% and 63.98%, respectively.
T5 branch.
Each T5 adapter applies LayerNorm and a linear map to normalized neural features
scaled by 32. Learned position embeddings locate memory vectors relative to the target event.
Candidate token sequences form a prefix tree: the decoder normalizes logits over the allowed next
11


tokens at each branch and sums log-probabilities along each candidate’s path to obtain ℓik. We train
the adapters with class-weighted negative log-likelihood using weights N/(Knk), where N is the
number of training events. Training uses seed 42, batch size 256, AdamW with learning rate 10−3
and matrix weight decay 10−3, gradient clipping at 5.0, and a 30-epoch cosine schedule ending
at 10−5, for 36,870 updates. At saved epochs 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 and 30, we fit α on training data by
class-weighted cross-entropy and evaluate the fused validation mean. This selects epoch 10 for group
memory and epoch 20 for target-only memory. The target-only branch scores 62.55% alone and
64.22% after fusion on validation; group memory scores 19.18% and 64.23%, respectively.

Implementation Control and Configuration Audit
To measure the two repairs jointly, we run the unmodified official source at commit 97da041 with
the same V2 initialization, public training data, fixed-50 Subject 0 validation criterion, seed 42
and two-epoch budget (31,226 updates) as checkpoint A. This preserves evaluation-time attention
dropout and the omission of loss temperature and bias from optimization and checkpoint saving. The
control scores 57.53%, 51.51%, 59.48% and 43.65% on the four corpora, for a mean of 53.04%.
The corrected implementation reaches 60.21%, establishing the combined 7.17-point gain under this
configuration. The control predictions were exported with evaluation seed 42 and the original attention
dropout enabled. The control archive includes the configuration, source verification, predictions and
results.
The configuration audit distinguishes the published description, released defaults and settings used in
our runs. Released defaults allow 50 epochs, select on a frequency-based vocabulary of 250 words,
and include all configured subjects in validation. Our matched comparison selects within two epochs
on fixed-50 Subject 0 validation. The public V2 initialization has 113,000 pretraining steps on five
datasets, compared with 35,000 steps on three datasets for the older public checkpoint. The loader
also halves already-reduced recordings from the broader subject pool, retaining approximately the
first quarter of the original sessions for Subjects 13–22 and the first eighth for Subjects 23–32. Both
sides of the repair comparison use the same public files, initialization and selection rule.
The archived fusion replay matches all 3,172 fixed-vocabulary test events and candidate orders. For
target-only T5, seven recovered hits contribute 0.291286 points and three lost hits subtract 0.147129,
giving 0.144157 points. Group-memory fusion recovers one hit and loses one, for 0.019248 points.
