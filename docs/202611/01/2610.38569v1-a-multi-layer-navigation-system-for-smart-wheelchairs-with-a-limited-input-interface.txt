A Multi-Layer Navigation System for Smart Wheelchairs with a
Limited Input Interface
Piero Simonetto 1, Anna Polato1, Francesco Pasti1,Muhammad Rameez ur Rahman 2, Sebastiano Vascon 2,3,
Nicola Bellotto1, Emanuele Menegatti1,4, Stefano Tortora1, Luca Tonin1,4
Abstract— Smart wheelchairs are robotic devices that gather
information from the surroundings and allow people to safely
drive through various environments. They usually rely on the
interpretation of high-level user’s intentions, typically decoded
through human-robot interface (HRI) or brain-machine inter-
face (BMI). However, navigating in an unknown environment
and accurately understanding user commands remain signif-
icant challenges. Misinterpretation of commands or the envi-
ronment can lead to user frustration, distrust of the system, or
even unsafe situations. In this paper, we present a novel Multi-
Layer Navigation System (MLNS) for a smart wheelchair that
enables navigation in unknown environments while interpreting
and safely contextualizing user’s intentions even in the case of
limited and faulty commands. The stack relies on a Virtual
Sensors-Driven Potential Fields (ViSPF) method for obstacle
detection that accounts for the wheelchair’s actual size, a Model
Predictive Control (MPC) module for trajectory generation,
and an open-vocabulary computer vision algorithm for target
identification. Finally, a system based on Priority Queue (PQ)
was implemented for target selection. We validated our system
on a real smart wheelchair with experiments involving 10
participants without disabilities, including scenarios with faulty
input interfaces. The results demonstrate the robustness and
effectiveness of our system in real-world environments, proving
its potential for practical applications in assistive technologies.
I. INTRODUCTION
Assistive Technology (AT) refers to any device and
software designed to maintain and improve the functional
capabilities of an individual, enabling their inclusion and
participation in all areas of society. Recently, several studies
have focused on the development of intelligent powered
wheelchairs and on implementing appropriate human-robot
interface (HRI) to enable people with severe motor or
cognitive disabilities to control these systems [1], [2], [3].

Researchers have focused on hardware and software so-
lutions that provide alternative interfaces to the traditional
joystick and that allow end-users to communicate their
intentions to the navigation system without using their upper-
limbs. The most common approaches rely on deep learning
techniques for detecting and interpreting user commands—
e.g., hand gestures [4], [5], eye movement [6], and voice
commands [7]. Other methods are based on the use of a
joystick controlled by the lips [8], tongue [9], or head-
neck [10]. Recently, similar commands have been generated
by decoding brain activity using brain-machine interface
(BMI) [2].
Despite these advances, the integration of robotics in
the assistive technology field is still at its infancy. Indeed,
many solutions remain at the research or early validation
stage, often demonstrating incremental improvements un-
der controlled laboratory conditions. Their robustness and
generalizability in real-world assistive scenarios are still
limited. In practice, HRI for AT are highly sensitive to
noise, environmental variability, sensor occlusions, and user-
specific motor and cognitive fluctuations. As a consequence,
they typically support discrete, high-level commands (e.g.,
arXiv:2610.38569v1  [cs.RO]  28 Oct 2026


left, right, stop, move) rather than continuous, intention-
rich control signals. Although such abstractions simplify
command decoding, they strongly constrain the interpretation
of user intent. This limitation becomes particularly critical in
navigation tasks involving close interaction with objects or
people, where fine-grained adjustments, continuous modula-
tion, and context awareness are required. In these situations,
even small misclassifications or latency-induced errors may
propagate into large trajectory deviations, reducing usability
and increasing cognitive load. Therefore, understanding how
to integrate such a limited input interface in the control of
AT devices is not merely a performance optimization, but a
prerequisite for effective navigation.
Furthermore, safety is a priority in these applications.
Indeed, over the last few decades, many obstacle avoid-
ance algorithms for smart wheelchairs have been evaluated.
Among these, Artificial Potential Fields (APFs) have gained
particular attention due to the ability to generate smooth
trajectories and the straightforward implementation where
attractive and repulsive forces are combined to guide the
robot away from obstacles while steering it toward free
spaces [11].
In this work, we introduce a novel semi-autonomous nav-
igation system for smart wheelchairs to operate in unknown
environments with limited user input while maintaining
adaptability and safety. The proposed architecture combines
reactive obstacle avoidance, predictive trajectory planning,
and computer vision algorithms for target identification
within a framework designed for assistive mobility.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows.
Section II reviews related works, identifies the limitation
of current approaches, and defines the aim of this research.
Section III presents the assistive platform and the developed
navigation system, as well as the experimental protocol.
Section IV reports the results of the experiments followed
by discussion, future work, and conclusions in Sections V-
VI.
II. RELATED WORKS
As mentioned above, research in HRI for AT must address
limitations that extend beyond technical performance. Re-
peated misinterpretations, unpredictable behaviors, or lack of
transparency can compromise user trust in both the interface
and the assistive system as a whole [12]. To mitigate the
impact of noisy or unreliable user inputs, several works have
shifted toward fully autonomous navigation paradigms [13],
[14]. In these systems, the user typically specifies only a
high-level destination, while the robot autonomously man-
ages local motion planning and obstacle avoidance. Although
this strategy reduces cognitive load and increases robustness
against command misinterpretation, it also reduces direct
user involvement in the navigation process, potentially af-
fecting perceived agency and acceptance. Empirical evidence
suggests that many users prefer semi-autonomous or shared-
control strategies, where assistance enhances safety without
removing control authority [15]. Within this framework,
several approaches rely on adaptive assistance mechanisms
in which the level of autonomy is modulated according to
the user’s needs or explicit requests [16], [17].
In these systems, machine intervention is triggered in
the presence of risk, instability, or degraded performance.
Some shared-control architectures move beyond event-based
arbitration and instead maintain a continuous contribution
from both the user and the autonomous system. Rather than
alternating authority, these approaches blend human intent
and autonomous intelligence in real time, establishing a co-
operative interaction [18], [19]. A critical requirement in such
frameworks is the reliable estimation of user intention. In
this direction, Casado et al. propose an intention-prediction
method based on a generative diffusion model for short-
term trajectory planning in a shared-control smart wheelchair
paradigm [20]. The technique estimates user intention by
jointly modeling user inputs and environmental information,
producing motion plans that reflect both contributions.
Similarly, several works propose an alternative shared-
control strategy based on Bayesian inference [21], [22], [23].
More advanced solutions work directly with neural signals
to decode user’s intentions by exploiting BMI-driven shared
control to drive smart wheelchairs or telepresence robots [2],
[24], [25]. Their probabilistic formulation explicitly accounts
for uncertainty in intention decoding, reinforcing the central
role of intention prediction in cooperative navigation.

remain widely adopted. The most investigated approaches
are the Vector Field Histogram (VFH) [26], the Dynamic
Window Approach (DWA) [27], [28], and APFs. While VFH
and DWA provide structured velocity selection mechanisms,
APFs remain particularly attractive in assistive robotics due
to their intuitive formulation, smooth motion generation, and
low computational cost. However, APFs methods are also
affected by well-known limitations, extensively analyzed in
foundational works such as [29]. These include local minima,
where attractive and repulsive forces balance out, oscillatory
behavior in narrow corridors or doorways, sensitivity to
parameter tuning, and susceptibility to sensor noise. Several
assistive robotics works have attempted to adapt and enhance
APFs formulations to overcome these limitations. For ex-
ample, in [2] authors implemented a APFs-based navigation
strategy in a BMI-driven smart wheelchair and validated it
with end-users. Although the system demonstrated feasibility
in controlled environments, oscillations in narrow passages
emerged due to sensor noise and the intrinsic limitations
of the APFs formulation. Other works have adapted APFs-
based navigation to shared-control frameworks, demonstrat-
ing their feasibility in hands-free interfaces and reporting
improvements in safety and user experience metrics, in-
cluding reduced perceived cognitive workload [30], [31]. A
more modern direction involves integrating predictive control
mechanisms into the navigation stack. MPC has been adopted
as a trajectory planner in smart wheelchairs [32].
MPC is particularly suitable for tracking trajectories that
resemble natural user motion patterns [33], while explicitly
handling system constraints. Still, its deployment in assis-


tive platforms presents several structural limitations. The
wheelchair is usually modeled as a unicycle robot, which
reduces computational complexity and facilitates real-time
implementation, but it neglects relevant dynamic effects. As
a result, prediction accuracy may be degraded, potentially
affecting trajectory optimality and tracking performance. As
a consequence, collision avoidance is encoded in the distance
between the obstacle and the center of the robot, ignoring its
real geometry. This may create incoherence between the full
range of feasible paths and the set computed by the collision
avoidance system. Finally, the computational time strictly
depends on the spatial configuration, density, and geometry
of the obstacles in the environment. In cluttered scenarios or
constrained passages, a higher number of active constraints
can significantly increase the complexity of the optimization
problem and slow down convergence, compromising real-
time execution.
The problem of accurately representing the wheelchair’s
physical footprint has been addressed through simplified
geometric hardware-based approximations. Most approaches
model the platform as either circular or elliptical [34],
[35] to facilitate analytical treatment and reduce compu-
tational complexity. Alternatively, some systems rely on
multiple proximity sensors distributed along the perimeter
of the wheelchair (e.g., ultrasonic arrays) to approximate
its occupied space during navigation [31], [36]. One of the
earliest works explicitly addressing wheelchair geometry in
navigation is [37], which proposed modeling the platform
through a discrete set of representative points and introduced
the use of five control points for computing repulsive forces
within a VFH-based framework.
Motivated by the limitations discussed above, this work
addresses three main gaps in current assistive navigation
systems. First, we introduce an enhanced ViSPF formulation
that explicitly accounts for the actual geometry and footprint
of the wheelchair, overcoming the simplifications associated
with center-based collision constraints and circular approxi-
mations, without compromising real-time performance. Sec-
ond, we propose a multi-layer navigation architecture that
integrates ViSPF and MPC to leverage their complementary
strengths while addressing the computational burden typi-
cally associated with MPC-based navigation, improving real-
time feasibility even in cluttered environments. Third, we
develop an HRI framework based on a PQ mechanism and
a computer vision module to improve intention inference
and environmental awareness. Finally, we experimentally
evaluated the robustness of the proposed approach under
noisy input conditions characteristic of multiple common
HRIs for AT. The system was implemented and tested on a
real smart wheelchair platform in semi-autonomous assistive
scenarios.
III. MATERIALS AND METHODS
A. Customized powered wheelchair
In this work, we customized a commercially available
powered wheelchair (18.68E HI-LO Electric, Vassilli s.r.l.,
Padua, Italy) as shown in Figure 1. The velocity and position
of the wheelchair were tracked using two K¨ubler KIS40
encoders attached to the front wheels by belt and the
resulting odometry was computed according to [38]. Two
Hokuyo URG-04LX-UG01 LIDARs were mounted in front
of the wheelchair to detect obstacles, while a RealSense
D435i camera, placed above the user’s head, captured the
environment with a field of view similar to the user’s per-
spective. The user controlled the wheelchair using a Logitech
F710 joystick. All components were connected to a Lenovo
Thinkpad T15g Gen 2, 11th Gen Intel Core i9-11950H @
2.60 GHz x 16, 32 GB RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080.
We used ROS Noetic 1.16 to provide a common interface
for custom components. An R-Net chipset (Curtiss-Wright
Corporation, Davidson, United States) was used to connect
the R-Net CAN-bus network of the wheelchair to the external
laptop.
B. Multi-Layer Navigation System (MLNS)
The architecture of MLNS is reported in Figure 1. It is
composed of three main elements: a custom ViSPF layer,
to ensure proactive and safe navigation in real-time, a MPC
layer used to build smooth trajectories, and a PQ layer to fuse
user’s commands and decisions autonomously taken by the
intelligent navigation system. The PQ relies on visual infor-
mation provided by OpenNav [39], a computer vision module
composed of YOLO-World [40] and MobileSAM [41] and
designed for real-time open vocabulary 3D object detection.
The navigation stack computes the velocity command in a
real-time reconstructed local map that tracks the possible
obstacles. The map was used by both the MPC and ViSPF
control layers. Since the wheelchair occludes its backside
to the lasers, a spatio-temporal voxel plugin [42] was used
to track obstacles in this area. By construction, the field of
view of the laser in this setup did not ensure that the space
right behind the wheelchair was empty. For this reason, we
decided to inhibit direct reverse commands.
C. Virtual Sensors-Driven Potential Fields (ViSPF) Layer

the robot’s entire footprint, and to prevent collisions with
any part of it, we modified the APFs layer to reproduce

point, each vertex of the rectangle area of the wheelchair pi,

that reported in [2]. During navigation, the obstacle positions
are provided in polar coordinates in pi centered reference
frame. In order to set an upper limit on the computation time,
the angular values were discretized into N = 36 sectors.

D. MPC and trajectory generation
A MPC is exploited to generate a trajectory from the cur-
rent wheelchair position to a given target (see next section).
Our implementation of the MPC is based on the problem
defined in Eq. 3 of [33]. In this formulation, obstacles
are identified by clustering the wheelchair’s surroundings
and representing them as discrete points at a distance that
accounts for the wheelchair’s physical dimensions. To en-
sure that the solver generates reliable solutions, specific
constraints have been implemented: the maximum number
of obstacles is limited to 50, and the planning horizon is
restricted to the next 30 seconds of motion.
Conversely to traditional approaches—where the MPC
is used to directly provide the command velocity—the
generated path is sub-sampled at fixed intervals of 0.3 m
and the resulting samples are considered as attractors for
the ViSPF layer. This solution ensures a safe control even
if environmental dynamics change faster and prevents the
classical oscillations of the artificial potential fields.
E. Target selection: Priority Queue (PQ)
MPC requires a target to model a path. The choice of target
is expected from the PQ that periodically selects the current
goal among the available ones. We provide the following two
implementations. PQ A exposes two targets: the one sent by
the user and the one sent by default by the system. The
user’s input is the first choice and overcomes the default
behavior. PQ B exposes three targets: The user’s one, the
default’s one, and a target provided by the computer vision
module OpenNav. The OpenNav target stands in the middle,
it overcomes the default behavior but follows the user’s input.
Regarding the type of target, the user sends it via a
limited input interface based on the joystick described below.
OpenNav sets a target on a surrounding object, the closest to
the moving direction of the wheelchair, as described above.
Eventually, the default target point is set in front of the
wheelchair, 2 meters ahead, every 4 seconds.
In this work, we experimentally evaluated both PQs,
investigating how OpenNav can influence their usability and
navigation itself in the presence of misinterpreted commands.
F. Limited input interface
The MLNS is tested on experiments in which the user
communicates to the robot through a limited input inter-
face. More precisely, the user can choose only between
two turning directions (i.e., left and right) as in the case
of head-neck joystick. The direction is selected through
the directional buttons of a joystick and transformed into
a point in the space around the wheelchair. Furthermore,
we introduced a minimum refractory period of 3 seconds
between consecutive user commands to emulate anti-tremors
filters usually implemented in this kind of interfaces. Finally
we designed a perturbation process that reverts the user’s
command with a fixed probability of 0.2 to simulate wrong
button presses.
G. Experiments
The semi-autonomous navigation system of the smart
wheelchair was evaluated by 10 users without disabilities
(3 females, 7 males, 27 ± 3 years old). Users sat on the
wheelchair and were asked to navigate from a fixed starting
point to a target destination (identified by a common object)
by sending commands to the wheelchair using the joystick
directional buttons.


Each user was asked to navigate from a starting point A
to one of the targets reported in Figure 3 (i.e., two desks and
one person identified respectively by letters D1, D2, P1 on
the map). The users autonomously decided when the target
was reached by stopping the navigation.
Each user performed 12 navigation runs for each target,
4 for each of the three following modalities based on PQ
policies (Section III-E):
• Baseline (B): user’s input priority (PQ A), no perturba-
tions;
• Without OpenNav (WO): user’s input priority (PQ A),
with perturbations;
• With OpenNav (ON): user’s input or OpenNav priority
(PQ B), with perturbations.
In the first modality B, users had full control of the
wheelchair and no errors were introduced in the input
interface. Conversely, in WO and ON users evaluated the
system with a faulty input interface and without and with
the OpenNav module, respectively. During navigation, par-
ticipants were blinded to the active condition, which was
randomized across trials. In ON, OpenNav was configured
to detect the ”desk” for Target D1 and D2 and the ”person”
for Target P1.
IV. RESULTS
First, it is worth to mention that the MLNS allowed
the wheelchair to move in a cluttered real environment
without any crashes and among 360 trials conducted by 10
participants, the system achieved a 99.72% task completion
rate, with only one interrupted run (Target D2, approach ON)
after user request.
Figure 4A presents the normalized heatmap aggregating all
user trajectories (central panel). The white dashed lines rep-
resent the average B trajectories for each target. The spatial
distribution highlights the dominant motion paths emerging
across trials, providing a global view of path consistency
and dispersion. Figures 4B-D illustrate the results separately
for each target (D1, D2, and P1) and for each modality (B,
WO, and ON). Heatmaps reveal clear differences in spatial
dispersion. WO trajectories exhibit greater variability, with
occasional deviations far from the nominal path. These paths
correspond to runs in which perturbations in user commands
led to unintended deviations. In contrast, the implementations
of B and ON result in a visibly reduced dispersion and
a more concentrated flow of trajectories toward the target.
This suggests that perception-driven assistance effectively
constrains the set of feasible motion strategies, promoting
more repeatable and stable navigation behavior, similar to
the baseline (i.e., without input perturbations).

V. DISCUSSION
The proposed MLNS showed robust performance in an
unknown environment, effectively mitigating the well-known
limitations of both MPC and APFs approaches. With the
ViSPF method that provides reactive obstacle avoidance and
MPC that allows predictive trajectory optimization, their
integration enables the system to take advantage of the com-
plementary strengths of both algorithms. The execution of
user commands relies on the MPC process, which introduces
computation-dependent latency. During this short planning
interval, the wheelchair maintains the last MPC calculated
trajectory while the ViSPF layer continues to enforce local
collision avoidance constraints, with a processing rate of
50 Hz. This layered interaction ensures continuous safety
and ensures a stable and smooth navigation experience.
In addition, the cooperation between the predictive MPC
layer and the proposed reactive ViSPF layer overcomes the
classical oscillatory behaviors typically observed with pure
APFs-based navigation, especially in confined environments

The integration of OpenNav into PQ proved to be an
effective design choice. Across all tasks, the trajectories
generated with ON remain closer to the B than those obtained
with WO, indicating stronger alignment with the user-driven
paths of B (not affected by perturbations). In general, the
pipeline WO exhibits greater spatial dispersion, reflecting
increased variability in navigation behavior. For targets D1
and P1, the efficiency gains introduced by OpenNav are
evident. Compared to the corresponding configuration WO,
users require fewer corrective commands and achieve shorter
completion times, while maintaining trajectory profiles that
closely resemble the baseline. This suggests that OpenNav
reduces the interaction effort without compromising user in-
tent. Nevertheless, target D2 requires additional discussions.
The task consists of approaching a table located at the end of
a corridor, with additional tables positioned along both sides.
Two critical phases characterize this scenario: the initial turn
required to enter the corridor and the selection of the target
table. The corridor entry maneuver is strongly dependent on

the initial user command in combination with the collision

Regarding table selection, the presence of multiple nearby
tables may lead OpenNav to prioritize the closest feasible
target. In such cases, users may issue additional commands
to override or refine the system’s choice and continue toward
the intended table. This interaction dynamic highlights a
balanced human-robot synergy. Within this framework, the
human component becomes essential in resolving environ-
mental ambiguities. This dynamic explains the slightly higher
number of commands and longer execution times observed
for target D2 under the OpenNav condition (Section IV), and
is a clear reflection of the shared-control paradigm adopted
in this study and of the collaborative nature of the system.
Overall, OpenNav is particularly beneficial during the
early and mid phases of navigation, when the user is still
far from the target and can assess and, if necessary, refine
the system’s choice. Its benefit decreases during the final
approach to the target locations, when users typically prefer
to manually adjust the wheelchair’s orientation for precise
alignment.
VI. CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we present and experimentally vali-
date a novel multi-layer navigation framework for smart
wheelchairs that integrates a new Virtual Sensors-Driven
Potential Fields layer, Model Predictive Control, and Open-
Nav computer vision algorithm within a unified architecture.
The proposed system enables the smart wheelchair to plan
motions in space accounting for the entire footprint of the
wheelchair. Moreover, we introduce and evaluate two priority
queue-based pipelines to regulate a hierarchy of control
commands while preserving user authority at the highest
level. The PQs are designed to evaluate the system in the case
of a limited input interface. We tested the setup with a real
device and with 10 users in order to verify the performance.
Experimental results indicate that users were able to navigate
the environment successfully, while relying on the assistive
system, suggesting effective human–robot cooperation under
constrained control conditions. The user maintains control of
the wheelchair, with the only exception of a not reachable
direction. In this case, the system tends to carry the user
to the nearest safe position. The configuration incorporating
OpenNav demonstrated clear advantages in tasks involving
distant targets, enabling simpler and more direct trajectories,
shorter completion times, and requiring fewer corrective
inputs. Future studies will include validation on a larger
population of users and in more complex and heterogeneous
environments, in order to evaluate the system performance
under realistic assistive conditions and to further assess its
robustness and adaptability.
