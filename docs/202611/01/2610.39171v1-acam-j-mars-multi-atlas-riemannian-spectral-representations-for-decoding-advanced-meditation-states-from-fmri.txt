ACAM-J-MARS: MULTI-ATLAS RIEMANNIAN SPECTRAL REPRESENTATIONS
FOR DECODING ADVANCED MEDITATION STATES FROM FMRI
Jian Wang1,2, Winson F. Z. Yang1,2, Wugeng Zheng3, Matthew D. Sacchet1,2
1Meditation Research Program, Psychiatry, MGH and Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA
2Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Radiology, MGH and Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA
3College of Engineering, Northeastern University, Boston, MA, USA
ABSTRACT
Inferring jhana-type advanced concentration absorption med-
itation (ACAM-J) states from fMRI data is challenging due
to the spatially distributed nature of meditation-related ef-
fects, substantial inter-individual variability that can exceed
within-subject state differences, and limited sample sizes.
Conventional machine-learning approaches often formulate
this problem as unimodal, Euclidean, atlas-based classifi-
cation, thereby neglecting the non-Euclidean geometry of
functional connectivity matrices and underrepresenting tem-
poral information in functional magnetic resonance imaging
(fMRI); these limitations can lead to suboptimal classifi-
cation performance.
We propose ACAM-J-MARS (Multi-
Atlas Riemannian Spectral representation), which computes
functional connectivity using multiple atlases, represents
symmetric positive definite (SPD) connectivity matrices on a
Riemannian manifold, projects them into a log-Euclidean tan-
gent space, and augments connectivity-derived features with
low-frequency BOLD spectral energy to better resolve subtle
state differences under heterogeneous variability. Evaluated
on 204 segments from 22 practitioners, ACAM-J-MARS
improves performance across multiple backbone models,
achieving a balanced accuracy of 0.757 for early versus late
ACAM-J classification and 0.545 for three-class classifica-
tion, corresponding to a 10.8-point absolute improvement
over the strongest baseline. These findings suggest that the
proposed representation improves the decoding of advanced
meditative brain states and may enable downstream appli-
cations such as closed-loop neurofeedback and real-time
psychophysiological brain-computer interfaces.1
Index Terms— Advanced meditation; fMRI classifica-
tion; Riemannian geometry; Manifold learning

With increasing absorption, practi-
tioners report steadier attention, reduced sensory awareness,
and quieter discursive thought [4, 5]. Advanced meditators
can enter ACAM-J volitionally during scanning [6, 7], mak-
ing ACAM-J a rare model of internally generated, repeatable
brain-state changes. 7T fMRI case studies of a single ad-
vanced meditator have linked ACAM-J to dynamic brain
states [8], connectivity gradients [9], cortical eigenmodes
[10], and network modularity [11], and related work points
to changes in nonlinear processing and near-critical whole-
brain dynamics [12, 13]. However, because most evidence
comes from one person, it is unclear whether ACAM-J states
have a shared neural signature across practitioners. A prior
cross-subject classifier using local voxel synchrony achieved
∼66% accuracy in distinguishing state pairs in one held-out
meditator [14].
A decoder of ACAM-J states that gener-
alizes across people would turn single-case findings into a
population-level account, but three challenges hinder this.
First, changes across ACAM-J states likely reflect distributed
cortical–subcortical interactions for attention and arousal

[15], not focal activations. Second, functional connectivity
is strongly individual [16] and can dominate transient state
changes [17], so models trained to minimize average error
may learn subject identity rather than ACAM-J state. Third,
whole-brain connectivity is high-dimensional relative to the
data (204 segments, 22 practitioners), making overfitting
likely, and end-to-end deep networks offer little advantage in
this setting [18].
Most connectivity decoders compute Pearson correlations
within a cortical atlas and vectorize the matrix in Euclidean
space [19], losing key structure: the non-Euclidean geome-
try of SPD correlation matrices [20], contributions from sub-
cortical and brainstem regions [21], and temporal dynamics.
Bigger models cannot recover this lost information: multi-
layer perceptrons, CNNs, and Transformers stay near chance
on raw BOLD or derived features, while simple classifiers on
the same features perform well (Fig. 1).
To address these challenges, we propose ACAM-J-MARS
with the main contributions as follows:
• Unified geometrical–spectral representation:
We in-
troduce ACAM-J-MARS, which fuses multi-region con-
nectivity (cortical, subcortical, brainstem, cerebellar) in
the log-Euclidean tangent space of SPD matrices with re-
gional low-frequency spectral energy. This shared vector
space reduces inter-subject variability through translation
adjustment, avoiding labeled calibration data.
• Classifier-agnostic decoding gains:
The feature space
supports robusst decoding. A parameter-free nearest class-
mean classifier achieves 0.757 balanced accuracy for early
vs. late ACAM-J (+10.8% over the best baseline). Five
other linear/centroid classifiers score 0.70–0.77 on the
same features.
• Objective absorption biomarker:
Across 22 practi-
tioners, we identify a cross-subject neural correlate of
absorption (ACAM-J) states. This objective measure com-
plements introspection and supports real-time monitoring
and closed-loop neurofeedback.

4. EXPERIMENTS
Data and Tasks.
We use 204 fMRI ACAM-J segments
from 22 advanced meditators, each with a self-reported
ACAM-J1–J8 label. The atlas set A includes Schaefer200
(cortex), Tian-S2 (subcortex), and brainstem and cerebellar
atlases with pa ∈{200, 34, 54, 10} [27, 28, 21, 29], yield-
ing d = 22,533 features. Main tasks are early (J1–J2) vs.
late (J6–J8) and 3-class early/middle (J3–J5)/late. We also
evaluate a median split (J1–J4 vs. J5–J8) and J1 vs. J8.
Protocol & Comparisons.
We use subject-wise cross-
validation [30] with disjoint subjects (Itr ∩Ite = ∅). Fr´echet
means, standardisation, and class means are computed on
Itr only. We report balanced accuracy (BA), plus accuracy,
AUC, and macro-F1 for additional contrasts. We compare:
(1) single-atlas Euclidean FC with NCM and ridge; (2)
multi-atlas FC in Euclidean and tangent space; (3) spectral
vs. tangent features alone; and (4) alternative decoders on
ACAM-J-MARS features (1D CNN, reduced AlexNet), plus
deep networks on raw BOLD. To relate features to anatomy,
we map each feature block’s stage-wise change relative to
ACAM-J1 to brain regions (Table 1, right).

5. RESULTS
Fig. 1 and Tab. 2 show that deep neural network classifiers
perform near chance when trained on raw BOLD time series
or ACAM-J-MARS features, whereas linear and centroid-
based decoders reach much higher accuracy (0.65–0.77).
This likely reflects (i) the lack of local spatial structure in
tangent-space coordinates exploited by CNNs, and (ii) over-
fitting of flexible non-linear models in high-dimensional,
low-sample settings (d = 22,533; N = 204) [18].
Tab. 1 and Fig. 3 show that single-atlas Euclidean FC
yields 0.579 (NCM) and 0.649 (ridge) for binary classifica-
tion. Adding subcortical atlases increases accuracy to 0.641.
Four-atlas tangent-space FC improves to 0.696, and adding
spectral features plus subject-level alignment reaches 0.757
(+10.8 points vs. Euclidean baselines).
In Fig. 3b, for ternary classification, spectral features
score 0.405 and tangent-space features 0.485. Fusing both
with Schaefer200 increases accuracy to 0.515, and fus-
ing across all four atlases to 0.545.
Baseline maps from
Schaefer200 show broadly uniform decreases, while ACAM-
J-MARS maps show anterior–posterior gradients.
Tab. 3 shows Euclidean FC is near chance on the median
split and ACAM-J1 vs. ACAM-J8 (AUC = 0.53, 0.47), while
both versions of our representation are well above chance
(AUC = 0.69–0.78). In both contrasts, the tangent-space com-
ponent alone matches full ACAM-J-MARS (AUC = 0.70 vs.
0.69; 0.75 vs. 0.77). Spectral and alignment features drive
early vs. late ACAM-J performance (BA 0.696 to 0.757, Ta-
ble 1), with the full representation reaching AUC = 0.79–0.81.

connectivity estimates in tangent space, low-frequency spec-
tral power features, and within-subject alignment procedures.
Using a nearest class mean decoder, our method achieves a
balanced accuracy of 0.757 for binary discrimination of early
versus late ACAM-J and 0.545 for three-level classification.
Our results suggest that ACAM-J states are associated with
distributed patterns of functional connectivity and power in
slow BOLD fluctuations.
However, interpretation is con-
strained by reliance on self-reported labels, a single-cohort
dataset comprising 22 practitioners, and an alignment strat-
egy that requires multiple runs per participant. Future work
should validate the approach in independent samples and as-
sess prospective real-time applications, including meditation-
time neurofeedback derived from the proposed features.
