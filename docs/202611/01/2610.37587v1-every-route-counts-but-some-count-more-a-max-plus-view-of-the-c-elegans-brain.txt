Abstract
Brains do not simply add up every signal they receive: they select which routes
dominate. Yet connectomes are usually analyzed with tools that sum over all
paths and place neural states in Euclidean spaces, blurring the feedback circuits
that may sustain internal states. Here we treat neural signaling as routing instead
of diffusion and describe it with max-plus algebra, in which signals accumulate
along a path and the strongest route prevails. We propose a model in which the
functional connectome of Caenorhabditis elegans emerges from the time-varying
combination of electrical, synaptic, monoaminergic, neuropeptidergic and hor-
monal layers, and analyze five networks within this framework. Each collapses
onto a handful of dominant feedback loops, which recover well-characterized cir-
cuits and point to new candidates for experimental study. Unlike linear spectra,
these structures keep the biological meaning of the data, and their order-based
description suggests that brain states live in lattices rather than Euclidean spaces.
Keywords: Connectomics, Caenorhabditis elegans, Max-plus Algebra, Brain States,

1 Introduction
Understanding how nervous systems actually function is a central objective of neu-
roscience. A systems-level explanation requires relating the properties of individual
neurons to the organization that emerges through their interactions. These interactions
allow signals to propagate through the network and return through recurrent circuits,
contributing to the ongoing evolution of neuronal activity. Determining how this orga-
nization arises from different mechanisms of communication, and how it changes over
time, is an essential part of the effort to reverse engineer a nervous system [1]. This
perspective places the relationship between connectivity and internal dynamics at the
center of the analysis.
The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans provides a particularly tractable system in
which to investigate this relationship. Its adult hermaphrodite contains 302 neurons,
most of which can be individually identified across animals and experimental datasets.
Reconstructions of its anatomical connectome describe the chemical synapses and
electrical gap junctions linking these cells [2, 3]. Complementary maps characterize
potential communication through monoamines and neuropeptides, while studies of
hormonal signaling reveal additional mechanisms through which physiological condi-
tions influence nervous-system function [4–6]. Together, these resources support the
study of several forms of neuronal communication within a common cellular reference
system, allowing network-level properties to be traced back to identified neurons and
their interactions.
These communication mechanisms, however, differ in both signal-transmission
times and the duration of their effects. Gap junctions permit direct ionic current
between coupled cells, supporting electrical transmission with minimal delay. Fast
chemical synaptic transmission involves neurotransmitter release and postsynaptic
receptor activation, typically operating on a millisecond timescale. Monoaminergic sig-
naling can modulate neuronal activity over seconds to minutes, while neuropeptidergic
signaling spans rapid responses and sustained effects lasting minutes or longer [4, 7, 8].
Hormonal pathways further link neuronal operation to physiological processes whose
temporal properties depend on the particular signaling system [6]. These temporal
properties determine when each mechanism contributes to neuronal interaction and
how long its effects persist.
For a model of neuronal interaction, both the onset and persistence of these effects
matter. Rapid transmission can modify the immediate propagation of activity, while
sustained modulation can change the contribution of particular connections over longer
intervals. The resulting network therefore depends on which communication mecha-
nisms are engaged and how their effects develop and decay. This temporal organization
motivates a description in which the effective contribution of each connectivity layer
varies over time.
These combined influences can be described through a functional connectome,
understood here as the effective network of directed neuronal interactions under
particular conditions. An experimental view of this network is provided by the signal-
propagation atlas of Randi et al., which combines stimulation of individual neurons
with whole-brain calcium imaging to measure responses in other identified cells [8]. The
atlas captures interactions mediated by the communication routes engaged under the
2


experimental conditions, including polysynaptic and extrasynaptic effects. Its network
architecture differs from that of the anatomical connectome, illustrating how struc-
tural wiring and measured signal propagation provide complementary descriptions of
neuronal organization [9]. Within a dynamical model, the functional connectome can
consequently be viewed as a time-dependent operator whose organization reflects the
contributions of the underlying signaling mechanisms.
The relevance of this temporal organization becomes apparent when considering
internal states. In C. elegans, for example, persistent roaming and dwelling states
are regulated by serotonergic and peptidergic signaling, linking changes in neuronal
communication to sustained modes of operation [7]. Within and across behavioral
states, population activity evolves through coordinated trajectories, usually described
in low-dimensional Euclidean embeddings, and individual neurons can change how
they encode behavior [10, 11]. Models of whole-brain activity further describe effective
interactions through combinations of dynamical operators whose contributions vary
with context [12]. These observations motivate the hypothesis that the persistence and
reconfiguration of internal states may depend in part on the recurrent organization of
the functional connectome. Examining that organization provides a way to identify the
neuronal circuits and communication mechanisms that could support such regimes.
A useful mathematical description of this organization should make it possible to
follow interactions from individual connections to complete neuronal circuits. Exist-
ing analyses of the C. elegans connectome have identified communication pathways,
related neuronal position to network controllability, and used spectral representations
to describe large-scale connectivity [13–15]. For the model considered here, a partic-
ularly relevant feature is feedback: directed routes through which an influence can
return to a neuron or group of neurons. The weights of the connections along these
routes determine which loops are most strongly represented according to the mea-
surements encoded in the network. Their location and connections to the surrounding
neurons provide a way to examine how local recurrent structures are embedded within
a whole-system architecture. Changes in the available connections or their weights
can then alter which recurrent routes dominate under a specified rule for combining
interactions.
Most of these analyses, however, rest on assumptions that limit what they can say
about feedback. Community detection and rich-club analyses usually work on undi-
rected, often unweighted, representations and frequently merge chemical and electrical
contacts into a single graph [16–18]; they describe segregation and integration, but
not which modules are recurrent or how they are ordered along the direction of prop-
agation. Linear spectral methods and centrality measures keep direction and weight,
but add the contributions of all walks, so their leading mode is dominated by densely
connected hubs and their remaining modes mix many neurons with signed or complex
coordinates [19, 20]. What is missing is a description that takes direction and weight
into account, keeps each communication mechanism separate, and returns the specific
closed routes that dominate each network.
A further assumption concerns the space in which neural states are described.
Whole-brain activity is usually represented as points of a Euclidean space, in which
activity patterns combine by weighted superposition, their similarity is measured
3


by distances, and structure is sought in linear subspaces or low-dimensional mani-
folds [10, 21]. This representation carries an implicit model of how signals propagate. In
the taxonomy of network communication models, connectome signalling ranges from
diffusion, in which influence spreads along all available walks and accumulates addi-
tively, to routing, in which signals follow selected optimal paths [19]. A Euclidean state
space is natural only at the diffusive end of this spectrum. Selective forms of polysy-
naptic signalling, such as thresholding, competition between inputs, and the gating
of one pathway by another, choose among alternative routes rather than add them,
so the states they produce are not closed under superposition. Moreover, many ques-
tions about internal states are questions of order rather than distance: which neurons
are engaged together, which activity patterns contain others, and which patterns are
indistinguishable to downstream neurons. A Euclidean space provides a metric but
no intrinsic hierarchy between qualitatively distinct states. These properties call for a
state space organized by order and selection, a lattice in which patterns are compared
by inclusion and any two patterns have a well-defined least upper bound and greatest
lower bound.
Max-plus algebra offers a direct way to formulate this perspective. It is a tropi-
cal algebra in which maximization replaces ordinary addition and ordinary addition
replaces multiplication [22, 23]. Applied to a weighted network, these operations accu-
mulate weights along successive connections and select the largest accumulated weight
among alternative routes. Max-plus algebra is therefore the algebra of the routing end
of the communication spectrum. Its order dual, min-plus algebra, replaces maximiza-
tion by minimization and is the algebra of shortest-path routing; the two are exchanged
by a change of sign, so that selecting the strongest route on weights is equivalent to
selecting the shortest route on costs. Together they make the spaces of states ordered
by construction: the maximum of two activity patterns is their least upper bound
and their minimum is their greatest lower bound, so these spaces form lattices rather
than Euclidean vector spaces. This makes dominant paths and recurrent loops explicit
objects of the analysis. Its value for the present problem lies in linking a network-level
description to identifiable feedback circuits and in providing an algebraic rule through
which changes in connectivity can change the dominant recurrent organization.
This path-based interpretation also gives spectral analysis a concrete biologi-
cal meaning. Max-plus spectral quantities identify recurrent loops with the greatest
average edge weight and describe their relation to the neurons connected to them.
The interpretation of that weight follows the biological quantity represented in each
dataset, such as anatomical contact extent, supported ligand–receptor interactions,
or measured response amplitude. The resulting description connects compact recur-
rent circuits with the larger neuronal domains that can influence them or be reached
from them. The generalized framework also accommodates networks containing several
recurrent regions, preserving their distinct organization and directed relationships [24].
These properties make max-plus suitable for examining a model in which signaling
mechanisms acting over different timescales alter the relative prominence of recurrent
routes.
Tropical algebra has also been explored as a tool for modelling neuronal networks.
In a preprint, Patel [25] proposed a threshold model of timing dynamics, in which each
4


neuron updates its state once a fraction of its inputs has arrived, and used its max-
plus formulation as a first attempt to extract backbone structures from the C. elegans
neuronal network. That approach simulates activity on a single wiring diagram; here,
we instead analyze the spectral structure of the connectivity itself, separately for each
communication layer.
We therefore propose a model in which the time-dependent functional connectome
emerges from the max-plus composition of electrical, chemical-synaptic, monoaminer-
gic, neuropeptidergic, and hormonal connectivity layers, each engaged to a different
extent over time. Within this model, the activation of a signaling layer can merge
recurrent neuronal groups or change which feedback circuit dominates within them,
providing a candidate mechanism for the reconfiguration of internal states.
The available datasets measure different biological quantities, from anatomical con-
tact extent to ligand–receptor compatibility and evoked calcium responses, and cannot
yet be combined into this composite operator. We therefore analyze the five available
networks (gap junctions, chemical synapses, monoaminergic and neuropeptidergic sig-
naling, and the experimentally measured signal-propagation atlas) separately within
the same max-plus framework. For each one we identify its recurrent domains, its dom-
inant feedback circuits, the upstream and downstream neuronal populations attached
to them, and we relate these structures to known neuronal function.
This paper focuses on the biological application and presents the method at the
level needed to interpret the results. The underlying theory, in which the nervous
system is treated as a dynamical system over a dual pair of max-plus and min-plus
semifields whose state space is organized as a hierarchy of lattices rather than a
Euclidean space, is developed in a companion paper [26].
2 Results
In this section, we first present a simplified version of the theoretical model, in which
the functional connectome is a time-dependent composition of neuronal communica-
tion layers, and describe its implications for recurrent organization. This version is
sufficient to interpret and apply the method and to show what it adds to the analy-
sis of neuronal connectivity; the full mathematical development of the model is given
in a companion paper [26]. We then move to empirical measurements and examine
the electrical, chemical-synaptic, monoaminergic, neuropeptidergic, and experimen-
tally measured functional networks individually, identifying their dominant recurrent
circuits and their relationships with the surrounding neuronal populations. Finally, we
compare these structures with conventional connectivity analyses.

For each ordered pair of neurons, the composite retains the largest gated contribution
among the layers. The formulation assumes that all layers are expressed on a common
scale and remains open to further communication mechanisms. Over the operational
timescale considered we treat electrical coupling as a stable scaffold (αG ≡e), whereas
the remaining gates switch on and decay with the kinetics of each signaling mechanism.
The same anatomical and molecular resources can therefore contribute differently to
the functional connectome at different times.
This composition has three consequences for the organization of the modeled
nervous system (formal statements in [26]).
1. Engaging a layer can merge recurrent groups, but never split them. The graph of
A(t) is the union of the graphs of the engaged layers. Neurons that can reach
each other through electrical coupling therefore remain mutually reachable, and
connections contributed by other layers can close new routes between groups. The
partition of neurons into recurrent components is always equal to, or coarser than,
that of A(G).
2. Engaging a layer can change which circuit dominates. A layer can strengthen an
existing connection or add a new one, changing the weights accumulated around
closed routes. Within a recurrent component, the closed routes with the greatest
average connection weight are its critical cycles; a change in the gates can change
them even when the component keeps the same neurons. Because adding the same
constant to every gate shifts every cycle mean by that constant, the critical cycles
depend only on the relative engagement of the layers, αℓ(t) −αm(t).
3. Signaling kinetics set the time course of this reorganization. Fast synaptic changes
can alter which cycles dominate over short intervals, whereas sustained monoamin-
ergic, neuropeptidergic or hormonal signaling can shift their relative weights for
6


minutes or longer. As gates rise and decay, different cycles can become critical in
turn.
The spectral description extends these consequences from the critical circuits to
the rest of the network. Max-plus eigenvectors characterize the weighted pathways
connecting a critical circuit with other neurons: left eigenvectors describe its upstream
domain, the neurons from which signals can reach the circuit, and right eigenvectors its
downstream domain, the neurons the circuit can reach. Changes in the critical circuits
are therefore accompanied by changes in the neuronal domains organized around them.
Within the model, sustained configurations of these interactions could contribute to
the maintenance of internal states, and their reconfiguration to transitions between
states such as roaming and dwelling [7].
The functional signal-propagation atlas plays a distinct role in this model: it is
not a further layer, but an experimental observation of the composite operator under
particular conditions, including polysynaptic effects.
Overall, the model links the kinetics of different communication mechanisms to
the recurrent organization of the functional connectome, from its critical circuits to
their upstream and downstream domains. In the next subsections, we examine this
organization in the available datasets through independent max-plus analyses, which
preserve the biological meaning of each dataset while placing their recurrent archi-
tectures in a common mathematical framework. Finally, we compare the max-plus
description with conventional connectivity analyses of the same networks, including
linear spectral methods, community detection and rich-club analysis, to show what it
adds to their view of the connectome.
2.2 Empirical analysis
The composition proposed in Section 2.1 requires the contributions of the differ-
ent communication layers to be quantitatively comparable. To examine the available
evidence, we assembled five complementary network descriptions of the C. elegans ner-
vous system. Chemical-synapse and gap-junction connectivity were obtained from the
anatomical reconstruction of Cook et al. [3]. The monoaminergic network describes
potential signaling relationships based on neuronal transmitter identities and compat-
ible receptor expression [4, 27], while the neuropeptidergic network combines peptide
and receptor expression with experimentally characterized ligand–receptor relation-
ships and spatial constraints [5]. The functional signal-propagation network was
obtained from the optogenetic stimulation and calcium-imaging experiments of Randi
et al. [8]. Dataset versions, coverage, and preparation are described in Section 4.1.
These networks were reconstructed using different experimental approaches, and
their weights represent different biological quantities. Anatomical contact extent,
the number of supported transmitter–receptor relationships, and calcium-response
amplitude each have their own units and interpretation. Their direct combination
would therefore compare numerical values with different biological meanings. The
datasets also differ in experimental conditions and neuronal coverage, providing sep-
arate descriptions of connectivity instead of the simultaneous measurements of the
contributions required to reconstruct A(t).
7


Each network can nevertheless be characterized independently within the proposed
max-plus framework. This allows us to demonstrate its usefulness for understanding
the system-level organization of neuronal connectivity while preserving the biological
meaning of each dataset. The empirical analysis therefore examines the five available
networks individually; hormonal signaling remains part of the theoretical model only,
because no neuron-to-neuron hormonal map is yet available.
For each network, we examine the following complementary structures. Appendix A
provides a quick reading guide to their mathematical meaning and biological interpre-
tation.
• Strongly connected components (SCCs) identify maximal groups of neurons
that can be mutually reached through directed paths. Components containing cycles
delimit the regions in which recurrent interaction is structurally possible.
• The condensation graph represents each SCC as a single node, revealing how
recurrent regions and peripheral neurons are connected. It provides a compact
description of the upstream and downstream organization of the network.
• Critical cycles and critical classes identify the closed neuronal routes with the
greatest mean connection weight within a recurrent component. Their organization
reveals the specific circuits that dominate under the max-plus interpretation.
• The eigenvalue λ gives the maximum mean weight of these recurrent routes.
Its biological meaning follows the quantity encoded by the edge weights in the
corresponding dataset.
• Right and left eigenvectors extend the description of critical circuits to their
surrounding neuronal domains. Right eigenvectors characterize the downstream
organization reachable from a critical circuit, while left eigenvectors characterize
the upstream organization from which it can be reached. Their coordinates describe
these relationships in terms of weighted pathways.
Together, these structures reveal where recurrence is possible, which weighted cir-
cuits dominate within those regions, and how those circuits are connected to the wider
neuronal population. Throughout the analysis, an entry Aij represents a connection
from neuron j to neuron i, establishing the direction used to interpret upstream and
downstream relationships.
2.2.1 Gap-junction connectome
The electrical connectome provides the first view of recurrent organization across the
individual connectivity layers. Gap junctions are bidirectional and, therefore, repre-
sented as symmetric connections in the Cook et al. matrix [3]. Consequently, the right
and left eigensystems are identical and strongly connected components coincide with
ordinary connected components. There is therefore no distinct upstream–downstream
organization to recover in this layer, and separate right/left representations or a con-
densation graph would be redundant. Nevertheless, a schematic representation of the
connectome is presented in Figure 1 in order to provide a global overview of its
organization.

2.2.2 Synaptic connectome
The chemical-synapse connectome provides a markedly different view of neuronal orga-
nization from the electrical network examined above. Its directed synaptic architecture
provides a direct anatomical view of how recurrent neuronal organization emerges
from the wiring of the nervous system.
The 302 neurons separate into 11 SCCs, shown through an extended version of the
condensation digraph in Figure 2. The decomposition is highly uneven: SCC7 contains
275 neurons, approximately 91% of the complete network, SCC9 contains 18 neu-
rons, and the remaining nine SCCs consist of single neurons. This organization closely
reproduces the bow-tie architecture previously described for the chemical connectome
of C. elegans, characterized by a large strongly connected core surrounded by a much
smaller directed periphery [29]. At the same time, the decomposition recovers the
major anatomical division of the nervous system: SCC7 contains the somatic network,
whereas SCC9 contains 18 neurons of the pharyngeal nervous system and, together
with the corresponding singleton components, accounts for the established separation
into 282 somatic and 20 pharyngeal neurons [3, 30]. The condensation structure there-
fore captures both the global bow-tie topology of the synaptic connectome and the
anatomical separation between its somatic and pharyngeal systems.
SCC9 provides a particularly clear view of the organization of the pharyngeal
nervous system. It contains 18 of its 20 neurons and receives no chemical-synaptic con-
nections from any other SCC. Of its three outgoing branches, two terminate in MCL
and MCR, the bilateral MC motor neurons, thereby completing the 20-neuron pha-
ryngeal system. MC neurons are cholinergic drivers of rapid pharyngeal pumping [31]
and project extensively to non-neuronal pharyngeal targets, including the marginal
cells [30]. As these effector connections are not represented in the neuron-to-neuron
synaptic matrix, MCL and MCR appear as terminal components in the condensation
digraph. The graph therefore captures a recurrent pharyngeal core together with its
final neuronal outputs towards the structures responsible for executing feeding.
The third branch is qualitatively different because it connects the pharyngeal recur-
rent network to the large somatic SCC7. Remarkably, this transition is supported
by a single chemical edge, M1→RIPL. This result agrees with the unusual anatomi-
cal position of the RIP neurons at the interface between the pharyngeal and somatic
nervous systems. The two systems have historically been described as largely indepen-
dent and coupled primarily through RIP–pharyngeal electrical connections; the more

These two critical classes provide the anchors for interpreting the correspond-
ing eigendirections. Under the matrix convention used here, a right eigendirection
describes how the network is organized in the direction followed by outgoing connectiv-

the finite downstream domain associated with that class. Conversely, the left eigendi-
rection describes the complementary upstream domain: neurons and components from
which the recurrent class can be reached. Right and left eigenvectors thus describe
different sides of the same recurrent core.
For λ = 37, the right eigendirection contains 280 finite coordinates. These are
precisely the 275 neurons of SCC7 together with all five singleton SCCs reached
downstream from it. Starting from the RIAR–SMDVL critical loop, the right eigendi-
rection therefore expands through the entire mutually reachable somatic network
and continues into every terminal component accessible from that network. The left
eigendirection of λ = 37 provides the complementary view. It contains 295 finite neu-
rons: the complete 275-neuron somatic SCC, all 18 neurons of the pharyngeal SCC,
and the two posterior touch neurons PLML and PLMR. The seven neurons absent
from the finite left domain are the terminal components MCL, MCR, SABVR, DD4,
DD5, CANL, and CANR, all of which lie downstream of a recurrent component and
have no directed route back to the λ = 37 core. For the dominant somatic mode, the
right and left eigendirections therefore recover the downstream and upstream organi-
zation already suggested by the condensation graph, but now relative specifically to
the RIAR–SMDVL spectral core.
The λ = 15 mode exposes the hierarchy between the two recurrent systems even
more clearly. Its left eigendirection contains only the 18 neurons of SCC9, explicitly
stating that only those neurons have anatomical connections directed to the critical
class, M4. In the right eigendirection, the same 18 neurons remain finite and are joined
by MCL and MCR, giving a 20-neuron finite domain that corresponds exactly to the
pharyngeal nervous system.
The remaining neurons take infinite coordinates of two kinds. PLML and PLMR
take the value ⊥(= −∞): they are independent source components that cannot be
reached from the pharyngeal SCC. The other 280 neurons take the value ⊤(= +∞);
they are exactly the finite right domain of the higher mode λ = 37, that is, the
somatic SCC and its five terminal outputs. Through the single bridge M1→RIPL,
the pharyngeal circuit reaches a recurrent component whose own loops have a higher
mean weight (37 > 15). Under repeated propagation, the best accumulated weight
13


in the somatic component grows by 37 per step, faster than the 15 per step that
defines the pharyngeal mode, so, measured relative to that mode, it increases without
bound [24]. The ⊤coordinates therefore mark the downstream region dominated by
a stronger recurrent circuit than the one that generated the eigenvector; they encode
this hierarchy between recurrent regimes and are not predictions of unbounded neural
activity.
The synaptic connectome can consequently be read at three progressively more
specific levels. The SCC decomposition first separates the network into mutually reach-
able recurrent domains and reveals the directed relations between them. The Rmax,+
eigenvalues then distinguish two weighted recurrent regimes within those domains.
Finally, the critical classes reduce each regime to its dominant closed path: the M4
self-loop in the pharyngeal system and the RIAR–SMDVL sensorimotor feedback loop
in the somatic system. Right and left eigendirections extend these compact spec-
tral cores towards their downstream and upstream neuronal domains, respectively.
Together, these representations transform a dense 302-neuron chemical connectome
into an interpretable hierarchy of recurrent cores, directed inter-system transitions,
sensory inputs, and terminal outputs while preserving the identity of the individual
neurons supporting each structure.
2.2.3 Monoaminergic connectome
The monoaminergic layer displays a highly asymmetric recurrent organization,
markedly different from both wired connectivity layers examined above. Whereas the
chemical-synapse connectome is dominated by a large recurrent somatic core and the
gap-junction network places almost the entire nervous system within a single SCC,
the monoaminergic condensation graph contains 286 SCCs, almost all of them single-
tons. The only sizeable recurrent component is SCC268, which contains 17 neurons.
This compact core receives connections from only two upstream SCCs but projects to
267 downstream SCCs, producing a strongly divergent architecture in which a small
recurrent population broadcasts monoaminergic influence across most of the nervous
system. The remaining 16 singleton SCCs (the AFD, ASI, AWA, AWB and PHC pairs,
PQR, PDA, VC1–3 and VC6) have no monoaminergic connection to or from the rest
of the network in this representation.
The biological composition of SCC268 closely reflects the known organization
of monoaminergic signalling in the hermaphrodite nervous system. Sixteen of its
seventeen neurons correspond to the principal monoamine-releasing classes: the sero-
tonergic ADF, NSM, and HSN pairs; the dopaminergic CEP and PDE neurons; the
tyraminergic RIM pair; and the octopaminergic RIC pair [4, 27]. The remaining
neuron, I5, has been associated with less conventional and comparatively uncertain
monoaminergic, particularly serotonergic, signalling in the updated neurotransmitter
atlas [27]. SCC268 therefore concentrates almost the entire canonical monoaminergic
broadcasting system within a single recurrent component.
The two remaining dopaminergic neurons, ADEL and ADER, occupy a distinc-
tive upstream position. They form the only two singleton SCCs feeding into SCC268
and, together with the 17-neuron recurrent component, constitute the complete finite
domain of the left eigendirection. The left eigenvector consequently contains only 19
14


finite coordinates. In the opposite direction, the right eigendirection contains 284 finite
neurons: the 17 neurons of SCC268 together with the 267 downstream singleton SCCs
reached from it.

2.2.5 Functional signal-propagation network
The final layer moves from anatomically reconstructed connectivity to experimentally
measured signal propagation. Randi et al. combined single-neuron two-photon optoge-
netic stimulation with whole-brain calcium imaging to quantify how activation of an
upstream neuron affects activity in downstream neurons [8]. The resulting functional
network therefore has a different interpretation from the preceding layers: a directed
edge represents a statistically supported downstream calcium response following stimu-
lation of another neuron, and its weight is the mean post-stimulus response amplitude.
The present matrix contains 188 neurons represented in the functional atlas.
The network contains 42 strongly connected components, but only one contains
more than a single neuron. This main recurrent component comprises 147 neurons,
while the remaining 41 SCCs are singletons. Eleven singleton SCCs lie upstream of
the recurrent component and 19 lie downstream, whereas the remaining 11 fall out-
side both corresponding eigendomains. The functional network therefore combines
a large recurrent signal-propagation domain with a comparatively sparse peripheral
organization.

the recovered structures, with spectral magnitudes interpreted in the measurement
units of each dataset.
2.3 Comparison with conventional connectivity analyses
The C. elegans connectome has been characterized with a broad repertoire of network-
analysis methods, including graph-theoretic measures such as degree, path length,
efficiency, and reciprocity [20, 40], community detection [16, 17, 41–43], hub and rich-
club analyses [18, 44–46], and spectral and low-dimensional representations [15, 20, 21].
Each answers a different question about network organization. Here we ask what
the added value of the max-plus framework is with respect to all those consolidated
techniques. We compare it first with its closest conventional counterpart, a linear
spectral analysis of the same five matrices, and then with community detection and
rich-club analysis. For the latter we rely on the published literature and, as a basic
baseline applied to the same four anatomical and molecular layers, on a previous
19


bachelor’s thesis from our group [47]. We cite it explicitly as a basic baseline: it applies
standard, off-the-shelf methods in the four connectomes.
2.3.1 Linear spectral analysis
Linear and max-plus spectral analyses sit at opposite ends of the spectrum of net-
work communication models introduced above [19]. The linear eigenproblem Av = λv
adds the contributions of all incoming connections, so that (Ak)ij sums the weights
of all walks of length k from j to i, as in diffusion. The max-plus eigenproblem
keeps only the strongest one, (A ⊗k)ij, as in routing. We applied the standard linear
toolkit to each matrix, with the same orientation as in the max-plus analysis: eigen-
decomposition, eigenvector centrality (leading right and left eigenvectors, i.e. in- and
out-centrality) [48], Katz centrality [49], and spectral clustering of the symmetrized
network [50, 51].
The two analyses agree on the coarse organization. In the gap-junction network,
ALA, CANR, and CANL are the three most central neurons, matching the ALA–
CANL/R critical class (Section 2.2.1). In the chemical-synapse network, RIAR is
among the six most central neurons in both directions, and spectral clustering sepa-
rates the 20 pharyngeal neurons from the somatic network, as the SCC decomposition
does. The leading linear eigenvectors also extend over the same neurons as the max-
plus finite domains (for example, 280 and 295 neurons for the somatic synaptic mode),
because both depend on which neurons can reach which.
Beyond this, the linear description loses three kinds of information that max-plus
retains. First, it summarizes each layer through one dominant mode, so secondary
recurrent circuits disappear. The pharyngeal M4 self-loop (λ = 15) has no counter-
part among the leading linear modes: M4 ranks 285th and 292nd of 302 neurons in
in- and out-centrality, and the linear mode that weights it most is only the 18th. The
independent IL2DL–URADL electrical pair ranks 297th–298th. Second, linear central-
ity largely follows the number and strength of connections: in the neuropeptidergic
network its correlation with node strength is 0.98–0.99, and PQR ranks only 24th in
in-centrality, whereas max-plus singles out its autocrine loop as the dominant recur-
rence. In the functional network, RIH and SIBVR rank between 61st and 119th, and
their interaction appears only in the fourth linear mode. Third, beyond the leading
mode, the linear spectrum is hard to read: in the chemical-synapse and functional net-
works more than a hundred eigenvalues exceed 10% of the spectral radius, most are
complex, their eigenvectors mix many neurons with positive and negative coordinates,
and the eigenvector bases are so ill-conditioned (condition numbers above 1018 in all
four directed layers) that small changes in the data can alter them substantially [52].
The max-plus structures, by contrast, are exact objects read directly from the critical
graph and the condensation graph.
2.3.2 Biological meaning of the spectral quantities
The main difference between the two descriptions is, thus, how directly their outputs
can be read in biological terms. Because max-plus adds weights along a route and
selects between routes, every quantity it returns is expressed in the units of the original
20


data and is attained by identifiable connections. The eigenvalue is the mean weight per
connection of a specific closed route: 37 is the average of the 43 and 31 reconstructed
contacts of the RIAR–SMDVL loop, 14 is the number of supported ligand–receptor
relations of the PQR self-loop, and 0.92 is the mean calcium response propagated
around the RIH–SIBVR cycle. The critical graph names the neurons and connections
responsible for that value, and the eigenvector coordinates give the best accumulated
weight linking each neuron to the critical circuit.
The linear quantities have no such reading. The spectral radius is the growth rate
of sums of products of weights over all walks; it is not attained by any circuit, and a
product of electron-microscopy section counts, or a sum of thousands of such products,
is not a measurable quantity. Accordingly, the linear spectral radii (106.1, 716.5, 16.3,
358.2, and 1.41 for chemical synapses, gap junctions, monoamines, neuropeptides, and
functional propagation) exceed the max-plus eigenvalues by factors of about 1.5 to 26,
which reflect the density of parallel routes rather than the strength of any interaction.
Linear eigenvector coordinates are relative amplitudes of a collective mode and, beyond
the leading one, can be negative or complex, which no anatomical contact count,
receptor count, or neuronal state can be.
These differences follow from the space in which each method represents the net-
work. A linear analysis places neurons in a Euclidean vector space, where patterns
can only be combined by adding and rescaling them. To express that one part of the
network is dominated by another, or that a neuron cannot be reached from a cir-
cuit, it must use cancellations between positive and negative or complex components.
The chemical-synapse network shows this directly. The pharyngeal circuit reaches the
somatic core, whose recurrence is stronger; the linear mode closest to the pharyn-
geal circuit encodes this with a change of sign across the somatic network, whereas
the max-plus eigenvector encodes it as an order relation, assigning ⊤to the dominat-
ing region, finite values to the pharyngeal domain, and ⊥to the unreachable neurons
(Section 2.2.2). More generally, the structural results of the max-plus analysis are
statements of order or inclusion: which neurons belong to a recurrent domain, which
domains lie upstream or downstream of others, and which circuit dominates each
region. The fact that the connectivity data are described exactly and economically in
these terms, and only through cancellations in Euclidean terms, supports the proposal
that the states supported by these networks are better represented in an ordered,
lattice-structured space than in a Euclidean one.
2.3.3 Community detection and rich clubs
Community detection and rich-club analysis address the segregation and integration
of the network rather than its recurrent organization, and have recovered meaning-
ful structure in C. elegans. The most comprehensive recent analysis of the wired
connectome partitioned it into ten communities that largely correspond to sensory-
to-motor pathways and identified integrative interneuron hubs [16]; non-backtracking
spectral methods recover a similar partition [17]. Rich-club analysis identifies a densely
interconnected core of command interneurons that control locomotion [18, 45], and
the monoaminergic network shows a star-like organization around interconnected
broadcasting hubs [4].
21


Our results agree with these descriptions and add to them. RIA, one of the most
convergent premotor interneurons in [16], is part of the RIAR–SMDVL critical cycle.
The monoaminergic broadcasting hubs correspond to the 17-neuron recurrent core
identified here; in the basic baseline, 14 of the 16 neurons of the monoaminergic out-
degree rich club belong to this core [47]. Max-plus adds three things to such hub sets:
it places ADEL and ADER upstream of the core rather than within it, it adds RIC and
I5 to the core, and it identifies NSM–RIM, a characterized circuit regulating feeding
and behavioral state [38], as the dominant recurrence inside it. In the gap-junction and
neuropeptidergic layers, the baseline found either no distinct rich club or very large
ones of 30 to 50 neurons, whereas max-plus isolates a single critical circuit in each.
These comparisons with the baseline are qualitative, because it used earlier versions of
the synaptic and neuropeptidergic data and a 300-neuron connectome that excluded
CANL and CANR.
These differences again come from what each method can represent. Community
partitions are usually computed on undirected, and sometimes unweighted, graphs [16,
17], and the most complete analysis merged chemical and electrical contacts into a
single graph [16]. In the basic baseline, this grouped pharyngeal and anterior head
neurons into a single “brain” community [47], whereas the directed SCC decomposition
separates the pharyngeal and somatic recurrent systems and shows that they are
joined by a single chemical edge, M1→RIPL. Even when direction and weight are
retained [53], modularity does not say which modules are recurrent, how they are
ordered along the direction of propagation, or which connections dominate within
them. These three aspects are given by the SCC decomposition, the condensation
graph, and the critical graph, respectively.
Overall, conventional analyses and max-plus analysis agree on the coarse organi-
zation of the C. elegans connectome: the separation between pharyngeal and somatic
systems, the broadcasting character of the monoaminergic layer, the prominence of
ALA–CAN electrical coupling, and the hub role of monoamine-releasing neurons. Con-
ventional methods remain the appropriate tools for describing modules, hubs, and
global spectral properties. For identifying the recurrent circuits that organize each
communication layer and how they are embedded in the network, max-plus analysis
offers four advantages. First, and most importantly, its outputs keep a direct biological
meaning: eigenvalues, critical cycles, and eigenvector coordinates are expressed in the
units of the data and are attained by named neurons and connections, whereas the lin-
ear spectral radius and eigenvectors, centrality scores and communities, are aggregates
of many routes or neurons whose biological meaning is lost. Second, it resolves coex-
isting recurrent circuits, such as the pharyngeal and somatic loops, instead of a single
dominant mode. Third, it preserves the direction of propagation, placing each circuit
between its upstream and downstream neuronal domains. Fourth, it describes all of
these structures through relations of order and inclusion rather than through cancel-
lations between signed components, consistent with an ordered rather than Euclidean
organization of the states these networks support.

3 Discussion
A central challenge in connectomics is understanding how different mechanisms of neu-
ronal communication shape the recurrent organization through which neural activity
can persist and evolve. This study advances that goal through three connected contri-
butions: a time-dependent model of the functional connectome, a generalized max-plus
spectral method for characterizing recurrent organization, and an empirical analysis
that links the resulting structures to established neurobiology while identifying new
targets for investigation. Together, these contributions move from the description of
individual connections towards an interpretable account of the circuits and neuronal
domains that organize network-wide interactions.
The first contribution is a model in which the functional connectome emerges from
the changing contributions of superposed communication layers. Electrical coupling,
chemical synapses, monoaminergic and neuropeptidergic signaling, and potentially
other mechanisms operate over different timescales and can therefore reshape the effec-
tive network as their contributions develop or decay. Representing their composition
through a time-dependent max-plus operator connects this biological heterogeneity to
a concrete account of network reorganization: the engagement of a signaling layer may
create new routes between neuronal groups, change which feedback cycles dominate
within an existing recurrent domain, or modify the pathways linking those cycles to
the rest of the system. Importantly, critical circuits may change even when the overall
pattern of structural connectivity remains unchanged, and they depend only on the
relative engagement of the layers. The model thus provides a way to investigate how
shifts in the relative contribution of neuronal communication mechanisms could sup-
port different configurations of internal brain operation. We have presented it here in
the simplified form needed to interpret the empirical analyses; its full mathematical
development, including the formal treatment of layer composition and the organiza-
tion of the resulting state space as a hierarchy of lattices, is given in a companion
paper [26].
The second contribution is methodological. By applying generalized Rmax,+ spec-
tral analysis to directed, weighted, and potentially reducible connectomes, we obtain
a coherent description of recurrent organization at complementary scales. Strongly
connected components establish the domains of possible recurrence; the critical graph
identifies the cycles that dominate according to their mean edge weight; and fun-
damental left and right eigendirections place these recurrent structures within their
upstream and downstream neuronal domains. The all-eigenvectors construction is par-
ticularly important for neuronal networks, where distinct recurrent components may
coexist and be connected asymmetrically. Retaining their full spectral organization
yields a representation that preserves both the specific neurons participating in critical
interactions and the larger architecture within which those interactions are embedded.
This combination provides a biologically interpretable bridge between local feedback
circuits and system-level connectivity.
The third contribution is empirical, and it provides strong support for the use-
fulness of this representation. Across connectivity layers with markedly different
architectures, max-plus analysis recovers structures that correspond to well-established
23


features of the C. elegans nervous system. The synaptic decomposition reflects the sep-
aration between the somatic and pharyngeal systems, while the RIAR–SMDVL critical
cycle is consistent with the known sensorimotor feedback between RIA interneurons
and SMD motor neurons [34, 35]. In the monoaminergic layer, the restricted recurrent
core and widespread downstream organization recover the characteristic broadcasting
architecture of extrasynaptic signaling [4]; within that core, the NSM–RIM critical
interactions correspond to a previously characterized circuit involved in feeding and
behavioral-state regulation [38]. Likewise, the PQR critical self-loop in the neuropep-
tidergic network highlights a neuron already recognized for its unusually rich predicted
autocrine signaling [5]. Particularly significant is that these correspondences emerge
from the same spectral formulation despite the different biological mechanisms and
network structures involved. Their convergence with established findings demonstrates
the capacity of the method to extract meaningful neuronal organization and provides
a compelling basis for interpreting its less-characterized results.
Indeed, the framework also brings attention to recurrent relationships whose roles
are not yet comparably understood. The exceptionally prominent ALA–CANL/R elec-
trical interactions, together with the corresponding ALA–CAN connections in the
synaptic network, reveal a distinctive association across two communication layers.
In the experimentally measured functional network, the RIH–SIBVR critical cycle
identifies a strong reciprocal propagation relationship linking a neuron involved in
serotonergic regulation with a component of motor circuitry. These results are valuable
precisely because the same analysis that recovers recognized anatomical and func-
tional organization also singles out these less-explored associations. They offer focused
candidates for physiological investigation, including targeted perturbations and brain-
wide recordings to determine how their activity contributes to recurrent propagation
and behavioral-state organization.
Several limitations also point to the next steps. The anatomical and molecular lay-
ers encode potential interactions whose weights are proxies for physiological strength
and do not distinguish excitation from inhibition, which could be represented explicitly
through the dual min-plus operation. The neuropeptidergic analysis uses the conser-
vative short-range model, and no neuron-to-neuron hormonal map is yet available.
Above all, reconstructing the composite operator of Eq. (2) will require bringing the
layers onto a common scale, so that the predicted reorganization of critical circuits
can be tested against whole-brain recordings across behavioral states.
Taken together, this work proposes a different way of reading a connectome: not as
a collection of nodes to rank or modules to partition, but as a set of dominant feedback
circuits, each embedded between the neurons that can drive it and the neurons it can
reach. Max-plus analysis makes this reading exact and keeps it biological, because
every structure it returns is expressed in the units of the original measurements and
attained by identified neurons and connections. Combined with a model in which
communication mechanisms reshape these circuits over time, it turns the static wiring
diagram into a hypothesis about how internal states are sustained and switched, and it
suggests that those states are organized by order rather than by Euclidean geometry.
As connectomes and brain-wide recordings become available for more species and
conditions, a framework that preserves direction, mechanism, and biological meaning
24


will be essential to move from mapping nervous systems to understanding how they
operate.
4 Methods
4.1 Connectivity datasets
The connectivity analysis is based on five complementary network descriptions of
the adult hermaphrodite C. elegans nervous system: electrical gap junctions, chem-
ical synaptic connectivity, monoaminergic signaling, neuropeptidergic signaling, and
experimentally measured functional signal propagation. All five datasets are expressed
over identified neurons, which makes it possible to relate network-level structures back
to named biological components. They represent different aspects of neuronal interac-
tion and therefore differ in directionality, density, spatial range, experimental origin,
and interpretation of their edge weights [9, 54]. For this reason, they are retained as
separate networks throughout the analysis.
4.1.1 Electrical gap-junction connectome
Gap junctions are channels between the cytoplasms of coupled cells that allow ionic
currents to pass directly between them, supporting rapid electrical communication.
Electrical connectivity was extracted from the whole-animal adult-hermaphrodite con-
nectome reconstructed by Cook et al. [3]. The corrected July 2020 adjacency matrices
distributed through WormWiring were used [55]. These matrices incorporate correc-
tions introduced after publication and provide a standardized representation of the
adult hermaphrodite wiring diagram.
Only neuronal nodes were retained, yielding the complete set of 302 neurons. Non-
neuronal cells and neuromuscular contacts were excluded so that the resulting matrix
represents neuron-to-neuron connectivity exclusively. Gap junctions describe direct
electrical coupling between cells and are represented in the dataset as symmetric con-
nections, so that the resulting adjacency matrix is undirected at the level of graph
topology.
The weights correspond to the values reported in the corrected gap-junction adja-
cency matrix. They represent the number of serial electron-microscopy sections over
which connectivity is observed, incorporating the number and anatomical extent of
the reconstructed contacts [3]. Both rows and columns index the same neuronal set,
and the symmetry of the matrix implies that

4.1.2 Chemical synaptic connectome
The chemical-synapse network was obtained from the same corrected Cook et al.
reconstruction and WormWiring release [3, 55].
25


Chemical synapses define a directed weighted graph: an edge from neuron i to
neuron j represents a reconstructed chemical synaptic relation from the presynaptic
to the postsynaptic neuron, and its weight is given by the corresponding value in the
corrected adjacency matrix. As for the gap-junction network, only neuronal nodes
were retained, yielding a matrix over the 302 neurons of the adult hermaphrodite. The
weights describe the reconstructed anatomical contacts.
In the original Cook et al. adjacency matrices, rows correspond to presynaptic
neurons and columns to postsynaptic neurons. Thus, an entry in row i and column j
represents a weighted directed connection i →j. This orientation is explicitly taken
into account during preprocessing before the max-plus analysis.
4.1.3 Monoaminergic connectome
The monoaminergic network captures potential extrasynaptic interactions mediated
by the classical monoamines dopamine, serotonin, tyramine, and octopamine. The
layer used here was obtained through the hermaphrodite implementation provided by
the C. elegans Connectome Toolbox [56].
This implementation integrates several complementary sources. Neuronal transmit-
ter identities are informed by the updated neurotransmitter atlas of Wang et al. [27];
neuronal identities and the adult-hermaphrodite reference system are aligned with the
Cook et al. connectome; and monoaminergic receptor-expression relationships derive
from the multilayer signaling framework originally assembled by Bentley et al. [4].
A directed interaction therefore links a monoamine-producing neuron with a neuron
expressing a compatible receptor, independently of whether the two cells are connected
by a conventional synapse.
The corresponding connectivity matrices follow a source–target convention in
which rows represent the monoamine-producing, or presynaptic-equivalent, neuron
and columns represent the receptor-expressing target neuron. An entry in row i and
column j therefore represents a weighted directed connection i →j. Its weight
counts the supported monoamine–receptor relationships between the two neurons.
The resulting matrix is interpreted as an inferred transmitter–receptor signaling net-
work. Its edges describe potential routes of monoaminergic influence inferred from
transmitter and receptor identity, providing a whole-system representation of wireless
neuromodulatory communication that can be compared with the wired connectome.
4.1.4 Neuropeptidergic connectome
The neuropeptidergic network was obtained from the system-wide signaling connec-
tome reconstructed by Ripoll-S´anchez et al. [5]. This resource combines single-cell neu-
ropeptide and receptor expression with experimentally characterized peptide–receptor
relationships to infer potential signaling interactions between identified neurons.
As in the monoaminergic network, an edge represents a ligand–receptor-compatible
communication route.
The updated matrices released by the authors in February 2024 were used. These
incorporate revised single-cell expression information for dmsr-5 and npr-34. The
resource provides several spatial models that differ in the distance over which pep-
tide signaling is allowed to act. In this study, the short-range model is used as
26


the neuropeptidergic dataset because it constitutes the most spatially conservative
reconstruction. The corresponding mid- and long-range versions provide alterna-
tive representations in which progressively more distant ligand–receptor interactions
are permitted. This distinction reflects the broader spatial and temporal reach of
neuropeptide signaling [57].
The released matrices follow the same source–target orientation used for the
monoaminergic representation. Rows correspond to peptide-producing source neurons
and columns to neurons expressing compatible receptors. An entry in row i and column
j therefore represents a weighted directed connection i →j, with its weight count-
ing the supported neuropeptide–receptor relationships. The topology of this network
is consequently determined jointly by peptide expression, receptor expression, exper-
imentally characterized ligand–receptor compatibility, and the spatial assumptions of
the selected model.
4.1.5 Functional signal-propagation connectome
The functional connectivity dataset was obtained directly from the causal signal-
propagation atlas published by Randi et al. [8].
In the original experiments, identified neurons were stimulated individually using
optogenetics while responses in other neurons were simultaneously monitored through
calcium imaging. Repeating this perturbation across a large number of neuronal pairs
produced a directed map of experimentally observed influence across the nervous sys-
tem. Only interactions satisfying the false-discovery criterion q < 0.05 were retained
for the present analysis.
An edge therefore represents a statistically supported response in a recorded neu-
ron following stimulation of another identified neuron. The corresponding edge values
quantify experimentally measured changes in calcium activity, computed over the 30-
s window following stimulation and averaged across trials and animals. These values
can contain both positive and negative entries, representing increases or decreases in
the downstream response.
The orientation of this matrix differs from the preceding directed datasets. Columns
correspond to the stimulated, or pre, neuron, whereas rows correspond to the respond-
ing, or post, neuron. Thus, an entry in row i and column j represents a weighted
directed connection from the stimulated neuron j to the responding neuron i.
The functional network also differs from the other layers in its neuronal cover-
age. The matrix analyzed here contains 188 neurons represented in the perturbation
atlas, determined by the neurons that could be stimulated and recorded within the
experimental protocol. Its connectivity describes the interactions supported by this
sampling and statistical selection. Unmeasured neuronal pairs and measured pairs
without a retained response have distinct experimental interpretations, even though
neither contributes an edge to the analyzed graph.

Appendix A
Quick reading guide to max-plus
algebra and its network structures
This appendix provides an intuitive guide to the structures used in the Results. The
formal definitions and computational procedures are presented in Methods. Through-
out the guide, neurons are represented as nodes and directed interactions as weighted
edges. An entry Aij describes an interaction from neuron j to neuron i.
What does max-plus analysis measure?
Max-plus algebra replaces ordinary addition with maximization and ordinary multi-
plication with addition:
a
⊕b = max(a, b),
a
⊗b = a + b.
For a neuronal network, this means that connection weights are added along a
directed route, and alternative routes of the same length are compared by selecting the
largest accumulated weight. Recurrent routes can be compared through their mean
weight per connection.
For example, suppose that neuron P connects to neuron Q with weight 6, and Q
connects back to P with weight 4. The resulting two-edge cycle has total weight 10
and mean weight 5. A self-connection of weight 5 has the same cycle mean. Comparing
mean weights accounts for the different lengths of recurrent routes.
34


The biological interpretation follows the meaning of the original measurements. A
large weight may represent extensive anatomical contact, several supported ligand–
receptor relationships, or a large measured response. Accordingly, dominance in this
analysis refers to the weights and path-composition rule of the network under study.
Strongly connected components: where can recurrence occur?
A strongly connected component (SCC) is a maximal group of neurons in which every
neuron can reach every other neuron through directed paths. The paths can pass
through intermediate neurons, so membership in the same SCC does not require a
direct connection between every pair.
An SCC containing more than one neuron necessarily contains recurrent routes.
A singleton SCC supports recurrence when it has a self-connection. Other singleton
components can represent neurons positioned along incoming or outgoing routes, or
neurons disconnected from the rest of the network.
Biologically, SCCs identify the neuronal domains within which signals have routes
to circulate and return. Their size describes the extent of mutual reachability.
Condensation graph: how are recurrent regions arranged?
The condensation graph replaces each SCC with a single node. A directed edge between
two such nodes indicates that at least one connection links their constituent neurons
in that direction.
This representation makes the large-scale organization easier to read. It identifies
components that supply incoming routes to a recurrent region, components reached
through its outgoing routes, and connections between distinct recurrent regions. The
condensation graph is acyclic: a directed cycle between several components would
make them mutually reachable and therefore part of the same SCC.
We use upstream for neurons or components with paths leading towards a region
of interest, and downstream for those reachable from it.
Critical cycles and classes: which recurrent circuits dominate?
Within a recurrent component, a critical cycle is a closed directed route with the
greatest mean edge weight. It identifies a particular feedback circuit attaining the
maximum average weight per connection.
Several cycles can attain the same maximum. Their union forms the critical graph,
and the SCCs of this graph are called critical classes. A critical class can therefore
contain several equally dominant cycles connected through shared critical structure.
This distinguishes the full region in which recurrence is possible from the specific
circuits that dominate its weighted organization. A large recurrent component can
contain a critical class involving only one or a few neurons.

For anatomical connectivity, it describes the mean anatomical weight along a critical

Networks containing several recurrent components can have several spectral val-

Right eigenvectors: which neurons can the critical circuit
reach?
A right eigenvector describes the weighted organization extending downstream from
a critical circuit. It relates that circuit to neurons reachable through outgoing paths,
including neurons within its own recurrent component and neurons reached beyond
that component.
Biologically, this provides a description of the neuronal population towards which
the circuit has potential routes of influence. The coordinates incorporate pathway
weights relative to the spectral value associated with the circuit.
Left eigenvectors: which neurons can reach the critical circuit?
A left eigenvector provides the complementary upstream description. It relates a
critical circuit to neurons with directed paths leading towards it.
Biologically, this identifies the neuronal population that has potential routes
through which to supply signals to the circuit. Neurons in the same recurrent compo-
nent can belong to both the upstream and downstream domains. Differences between
the domains reveal the directional organization of the surrounding network.
How should finite and infinite coordinates be read?
The finite domain of an eigenvector is the set of neurons with finite coordinates. Only
differences between finite coordinates are meaningful: adding the same constant to all
of them gives the same eigenvector.
Infinite coordinates encode structure, not activity. In a right eigenvector, ⊥(=
−∞) marks neurons that cannot be reached from the critical circuit; in a left eigenvec-
tor, neurons that cannot reach it. A coordinate of ⊤(= +∞) appears when a neuron
is reachable from the critical circuit but lies in, or downstream of, another recurrent

Reading the structures together
The structures answer successive questions about the same network. SCCs locate
recurrent domains, and the condensation graph shows their directional relationships.
Critical cycles identify the dominant weighted feedback circuits within those domains,
while eigenvalues summarize their mean weights. Left and right eigenvectors then
describe how the circuits connect to upstream and downstream neuronal populations.
Together, they link specific recurrent interactions to the organization of the network
as a whole.
