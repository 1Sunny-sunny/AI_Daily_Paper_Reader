Abstract
Spiking reservoir computing combines the training efficiency of fixed recurrent
reservoirs with the energy efficiency of event-driven inference, where computa-
tion is triggered by sparse spikes rather than executed densely at every timestep.
However, existing spiking reservoirs almost exclusively rely on Leaky Integrate-
and-Fire (LIF) neurons, whose low-pass dynamics limit their ability to distinguish
information encoded at different temporal frequencies. We introduce HRF-Res,
a liquid state machine in which LIF neurons are replaced by a heterogeneous
population of Harmonic Resonate-and-Fire (HRF) neurons. These neurons exhibit
intrinsic oscillatory dynamics and selectively respond to input frequencies, enabling
the reservoir to capture frequency-specific temporal patterns. The result is more
discriminative representations and higher classification accuracy than LIF-based
reservoirs, achieved without sacrificing the energy efficiency of spiking computa-
tion, with up to 19× lower energy consumption than an equivalent non-spiking
oscillatory reservoir. Evaluated on ten benchmarks spanning time series, sequential
images, audio spike trains, and neuromorphic vision, HRF-Res achieves state-of-
the-art accuracy among spiking reservoir methods. These results demonstrate that
introducing frequency-selective dynamics into spiking reservoirs improves repre-
sentational quality and classification performance while preserving their inherent
energy efficiency.

Introduction
Processing temporal sensor streams at the edge, from industrial vibration monitoring to event-based
vision and neuromorphic audio, requires models that are both computationally efficient and capable
of operating under strict power constraints. Modern sequence models such as LSTMs [1], GRUs [2],
and Transformers [3] achieve strong performance but were not designed for this regime: they require
backpropagation through time (BPTT) to train, and perform dense computation at every timestep
during inference. As a result, their energy consumption scales with model size rather than input
activity, making them inefficient for low-power, event-driven settings where signals are sparse and
computation should ideally be triggered only when new information arrives.
Reservoir Computing (RC) addresses the training cost: a fixed, randomly initialised recurrent network
projects inputs into a rich dynamical state and only a linear readout is trained [4, 5], eliminating
BPTT entirely. The inference cost, however, remains: RC reservoirs perform dense computation at
every timestep, so energy still scales with network size rather than input activity. Spiking Neural
Networks (SNNs) offer a complementary solution. By encoding information as discrete spike events,

computation is triggered only by activity, making energy consumption proportional to firing rate rather
than network size [6, 7]. This event-driven paradigm maps naturally onto neuromorphic hardware
such as Intel Loihi [8], IBM TrueNorth [9], and SpiNNaker [10], and is particularly well suited
to event-based sensing modalities [11]. Combining SNNs with RC yields Liquid State Machines
(LSMs) [5], which inherit both training efficiency and activity-driven inference. Recent work has
shown competitive LSM performance on temporal benchmarks [12, 13, 14].
Despite this promise, existing spiking reservoirs almost exclusively rely on leaky integrate-and-fire
(LIF) neurons. A LIF neuron acts as a temporal integrator [15]: inputs that vary rapidly over time
are increasingly attenuated, resulting in an effectively low-pass response. A reservoir composed of
such units therefore encodes how much input arrives, but not at which frequencies. For signals with
multiple temporal scales, this constitutes a fundamental representational limitation [16].
Oscillatory dynamics provide a natural mechanism to overcome this lack of sensitivity to temporal
frequency structure. Subthreshold membrane oscillations have been widely observed in biological
neurons [17, 18] and are believed to play a key role in temporal coding [19, 20]. In artificial systems,
networks of oscillators form a powerful computational substrate for temporal processing [21], and
non-spiking oscillatory reservoirs such as RON [22] show that heterogeneous resonance frequencies
can improve performance and stability. Whether these benefits can be realised within a spiking
reservoir remains an open question.
In this work, we instantiate oscillatory dynamics in a spiking setting using Resonate-and-Fire neurons,
which incorporate subthreshold oscillatory behaviour within a spike-generating mechanism [23,
24]. Specifically, we employ Harmonic Resonate-and-Fire (HRF) neurons, which exhibit damped
oscillatory dynamics and intrinsic resonance frequencies. Unlike LIF neurons, HRF neurons respond
preferentially to inputs matching their resonance, effectively acting as band-pass filters. Intuitively,
a heterogeneous population of HRF neurons behaves like a bank of tuning forks, each responding
to its own frequency band, so that the reservoir state reflects not only how much input arrives but
also at which temporal frequencies. By replacing LIF units with a heterogeneous population of HRF
neurons, we introduce HRF-Res, a Liquid State Machine that combines the efficiency of spiking
computation with frequency-selective representations.
Our aim is not the neuron substitution per se, but to establish frequency selectivity as a computational
principle for spiking reservoirs. Through a controlled comparison in which neuron dynamics are the
only difference between models, we test whether band-pass behaviour survives in a full recurrent
spiking network, whether it makes reservoir representations more class-discriminative, and whether
this translates into higher accuracy at low energy cost. Our contributions are as follows:
• We show that HRF neurons act as distributed band-pass filters, producing intrinsically more
class-discriminative representations than an equivalent LIF reservoir before any readout is
trained. A controlled ablation confirms that this representational advantage translates into
improved classification accuracy across multiple datasets and modalities.
• HRF-Res achieves up to 19× lower energy consumption compared to the non-spiking RON
baseline [22], with only modest accuracy loss.
• We evaluate the proposed model on ten benchmarks spanning univariate time series, se-
quential images, audio spike trains, and neuromorphic vision, achieving state-of-the-art
performance among spiking reservoir methods.

Related Work
Reservoir computing and oscillatory dynamics. Reservoir Computing (RC) [4, 5] provides an
efficient framework for temporal processing by decoupling representation learning from training. Its
dynamical properties and practical usage have been extensively studied [25, 26]. A key factor in
RC performance is the choice of reservoir dynamics. Early work on Liquid State Machines already
suggested that neuron models with resonant or oscillatory subthreshold dynamics can yield more
separable representations than standard integrator neurons [16]. More recently, oscillatory systems
have been revisited as a powerful computational substrate, both from a theoretical perspective [21]
2


and in practice: Ceni et al. [22] showed that reservoirs composed of heterogeneous damped oscillators
significantly outperform standard RC models. However, this approach relies on continuous-valued
units, performing dense computation at every timestep, and therefore does not benefit from the energy
savings of event-driven inference. Beyond reservoir computing, oscillatory dynamics have recently
been explored as a general inductive bias in gradient-trained deep networks, from coupled-oscillator
RNNs and GNNs [27, 28, 29] to Kuramoto-oscillator units in modern architectures [30], building on
earlier work on neural synchrony via complex-valued units [31].
Spiking reservoir computing. Liquid State Machines (LSMs) [5] extend RC to spiking neural
networks, enabling event-driven computation and compatibility with neuromorphic hardware. A large
body of work has explored architectural and parametrisation improvements, including heterogeneous
time constants [12], efficient implementations and scaling strategies [13, 32], and ensemble or
receptive-field approaches [14]. Some recent works also consider hybrid models that depart from the
strict RC paradigm, such as Gaurav et al. [33], which combine non-spiking feature extractors with
trainable spiking components. Despite these advances, existing spiking reservoirs predominantly rely
on leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons, which lack intrinsic frequency selectivity.
Resonate-and-fire neurons. Resonate-and-fire (RF) neurons [23] introduce oscillatory membrane
dynamics into spiking models, enabling selective responses to input frequency. Their computational
properties have been explored in a variety of contexts, including short-time Fourier transform compu-
tation on neuromorphic hardware [34], and for converting analog signals into spike representations
for downstream SNNs [35, 36]. RF neurons have also been used as harmonic oscillators in feed-
forward SNNs for image, audio, and time-series classification, as well as related signal processing
tasks [24, 37, 34, 38]. Adaptation-based mechanisms in spiking networks offer a related, though
distinct, route to temporally structured dynamics beyond standard LIF [39]. These works highlight
the potential of RF dynamics for efficient temporal representation and frequency analysis. However,
their integration into fixed-weight reservoir computing frameworks remains largely unexplored. This
work addresses this gap by investigating oscillatory spiking neurons within the reservoir computing
paradigm.

3.1
Neuron Models
The LIF neuron is the standard building block of spiking reservoir computing. Its membrane potential
v decays towards rest at a rate controlled by the time constant τm, while integrating incoming input
I(t):

fluctuations in the input, so the membrane potential reflects a low-pass-filtered version of I(t). As
a consequence, the LIF neuron responds most strongly to low-frequency inputs, regardless of the
frequency content of the signal.
The Harmonic Resonate-and-Fire (HRF) neuron [24, 23] replaces this LIF integration with the
dynamics of a damped harmonic oscillator. Its state is described by two coupled variables, a velocity
x and a position y:

ε > 0 is the damping, which controls how quickly oscillations decay. The neuron emits a spike

Eq. (2) causes the membrane to oscillate rather than simply decay: the neuron accumulates energy
from inputs that arrive in phase with its natural frequency ω and attenuates inputs at other frequencies.
This gives the HRF neuron band-pass rather than low-pass characteristics: it is selectively sensitive to
a specific frequency band determined by γ and ε, rather than responding indiscriminately to all slow
input.

represent temporal structure at multiple timescales, improving the expressivity of the fixed dynamics
(see Appendix A.1). The timestep ∆t is treated as a hyperparameter rather than a fixed integration
step [22]. HRF-Res is implemented in Python using standard machine learning libraries, and all
hyperparameters are selected via random search on a held-out validation set (see Appendix A.4 for
the evaluation protocol and Appendix A for full parameter ranges and selected configurations).

3.3
Energy Cost Model
A central motivation for spiking reservoir computing is energy-efficient inference. To enable a
consistent comparison between spiking and non-spiking models, we quantify computational cost
using multiply-accumulate operations (MACs) for non-spiking networks and accumulate operations
(ACs) for spiking networks [41, 42]. MACs correspond to dense multiplication-and-accumulation
operations performed at every timestep over all connections, independent of neural activity. In
contrast, ACs occur only when presynaptic spikes arrive and consist of adding synaptic contributions
to the postsynaptic state, without runtime multiplication. We measure spiking computation using
synaptic operations (SOPs), defined as the number of spike-triggered synaptic transmissions, with
each SOP corresponding directly to one AC event. This enables a unified comparison of spiking
and non-spiking models under a common hardware energy model. We convert MACs and ACs into
energy using standard CMOS estimates (EMAC and EAC). Following standard practice in the SNN
literature, energy figures count synaptic operations, which scale as O(N 2) and dominate the cost
of recurrent computation; the activity-independent O(N) neuron state updates, together with full
details, are reported in Appendix A.2.
3.4
Reservoir Characterisation
A key hypothesis of this work is that oscillatory neuron dynamics in a spiking reservoir produce
frequency-selective temporal representations that are more aligned with class structure than those of
integrative dynamics, leading to improved classification. We test this through two complementary
analyses on frozen reservoirs, using a controlled baseline that isolates the effect of neuron dynamics.
As a controlled baseline we introduce LIF-Res: a model identical to HRF-Res in every respect except
that the HRF population (blue in Figure 1) is replaced by LIF neurons with heterogeneous membrane
time constants τm,i and thresholds θi, drawn per neuron from log-uniform distributions. This mirrors
the γi/εi heterogeneity of the HRF reservoir, following prior work showing that heterogeneous
time constants improve performance [12, 43]. An independent hyperparameter search is conducted
for LIF-Res on each dataset using the same protocol as for HRF-Res, ensuring the comparison
reflects the peak performance of each neuron model rather than an arbitrary parameter transfer
(see Appendix A.5). Any observed difference therefore has a single cause: the oscillatory versus
integrative nature of the reservoir dynamics. The first analysis tests whether HRF neurons behave
as band-pass filters in a full spiking reservoir, beyond the continuous-time, single-neuron regime
of Eqs. (2)–(3). This property is not guaranteed to persist under discrete-time integration, recurrent
coupling, and heterogeneous random connectivity. The second analysis evaluates whether such
frequency selectivity yields representations that are intrinsically more discriminative than those of
LIF-Res, even without readout training. This is a stricter criterion: frequency-selective responses do
not necessarily translate into features that improve classification. Together, these analyses provide
both an empirical characterisation of the HRF reservoir and evidence that this property is relevant for
downstream tasks.
Frequency tuning and Q-factor.
We measure the preferred input frequency f ∗of each reservoir
neuron by sweeping 30 log-spaced sinusoidal inputs through the full network and identifying the
frequency maximising steady-state response power via the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). Filter
sharpness is quantified by the Q-factor Q = f ∗/∆f−3 dB, where ∆f−3 dB is the half-power band-
width around the peak. A neuron is band-pass resonant (Q > 0) if both a lower and upper −3 dB
crossing exist, and integrative (Q = 0) otherwise. Being dimensionless and independent of ∆t, Q is
directly comparable across models and datasets. Full sweep parameters are provided in Appendix B,
where we also verify that applying a Hann window before the DFT, which reduces spectral leakage
that could broaden the measured peaks, leaves all results unchanged.

4.1
HRF Reservoirs Function as Distributed Band-Pass Filters
We apply the analyses of Section 3.4 to compare frequency tuning and representational discrim-
inability in HRF-Res and LIF-Res across four datasets chosen to span diverse input modalities and
temporal regimes: sMNIST [46] (sequential images, T = 784, N = 800), FordA [47] (univariate
time series, T = 500, N = 800), SHD [48] (audio spike trains, T = 250, 700 channels, N = 3,000),
and DVS Gesture [49] (event-based vision, T = 200, 2,048 channels, N = 3,000).
Frequency tuning and Q-factor (Figures 2 and 3B).
The difference between the two reservoirs
is immediate. The fraction of resonant neurons (Q > 0) is 98%, 100%, 62%, and 83% for HRF-
Res (sMNIST, FordA, SHD, DVS Gesture), compared to 23%, 9%, 15%, and 4% for LIF-Res (mean

4.2
HRF Neurons Improve Classification Across Modalities
We test whether the representational advantage of Section 4.1 translates to classification accuracy,
using the same four datasets and reservoir sizes, and the final reservoir state at time T as the readout
feature. HRF-Res outperforms LIF-Res on three of four datasets, with accuracy gains consistent
with the predictions of Section 4.1 (Table 1). The largest gap is on sMNIST (+26.6%, 93.64%
vs. 67.00%), where HRF resonance is highest (98% vs. 23%). SHD follows with +10.5 (83.48%
vs. 72.95%) and FordA with +2.6 (69.72% vs. 67.15%). DVS Gesture is the exception, where the
two models perform comparably (71.81 ± 1.59% vs. 73.48 ± 1.12%) and the overlapping standard
deviations indicate no significant difference; we discuss this case in Section 5. HRF-Res is also
more energy-efficient than LIF-Res on most datasets despite achieving higher accuracy (energy
estimated via the framework of Section 3.3), as LIF neurons appear to require higher firing rates
to encode temporal information. DVS Gesture is again the exception. These results confirm the
hypothesis put forward in Section 4.1: equipping a spiking reservoir with oscillatory HRF neurons
produces measurably richer representations than an equivalent LIF reservoir, and this representational
advantage translates directly to classification accuracy across diverse temporal data modalities.

4.4
Energy Efficiency of the Spiking Formulation
Having established that oscillatory spiking dynamics outperform integrative ones, we ask whether
the spiking formulation itself is justified relative to a non-spiking oscillatory reservoir, which is
the relevant tradeoff when targeting deployment on neuromorphic hardware. We compare HRF-
Res against RON [22], which shares the same core design but replaces LIF neurons with tanh-activated
units and HRF neurons with continuous harmonic oscillators. For RON we use the hyperparameters
reported in the original paper; for HRF-Res we use the configurations selected via the random search
described in Appendix A. Some accuracy cost is expected, as continuous activations carry more
information per timestep than binary spikes; the question is whether the energy saving, quantified
using the framework of Section 3.3, is large enough to justify the spiking formulation. Both models

4.5
Comparison to Spiking Reservoir Baselines
We evaluate HRF-Res against prior spiking reservoir methods on seven benchmarks chosen to
match those reported by each baseline [12, 13, 32, 14], enabling direct comparison: univariate time
series (FordA, FordB, Wafer, Earthquakes [47]), audio spike trains (SHD [48]), and neuromorphic
vision (DVS Gesture [49], N-MNIST [50]). Reservoir sizes are matched to each baseline where a
direct comparison exists; smaller reservoirs are used elsewhere to demonstrate efficiency. We use
the combined readout for SHD, DVS Gesture, N-MNIST, and larger-reservoir FordA; for FordB,
Wafer, and Earthquakes we use the final-state readout, which achieves competitive results with fewer
parameters.
HRF-Res achieves competitive or state-of-the-art accuracy across all modalities (Table 3). On SHD,
HRF-Res with 6,000 neurons (89.78%) surpasses the 16,000-neuron ELSM [12] at nearly 3× fewer
neurons. On FordB, Wafer, and Earthquakes, HRF-Res with only 800 neurons outperforms all Dey
et al. [13] baselines using up to 6× more neurons. On FordA, HRF-Res (large, 2,500 neurons,
82.05%) outperforms the Dey et al. baseline at matched size, while even the small 800-neuron variant
(73.91%) remains competitive. On N-MNIST, HRF-Res (97.13%) outperforms Patiño-Saucedo et
al. [32] (94.43%) and approaches Biswas et al. [14] (98.10%), who use a more complex ensemble
architecture. On DVS Gesture, HRF-Res achieves 81.06% versus 85.2% for Biswas et al. at matched
neuron count; the gap is attributable to their spatial and temporal ensemble architecture combining
multiple reservoirs, rather than neuron count alone. All HRF-Res results are averaged over 5 seeds
with standard deviation, confirming consistent performance across a simple fixed random reservoir
with no task-specific adaptations. Together, these results establish HRF-Res as a competitive spiking
reservoir model that generalises across diverse temporal modalities and input dimensionalities,
whereas individual baselines typically cover only a subset of these settings.
5
Conclusion
We introduced HRF-Res, a spiking reservoir computing model based on Harmonic Resonate-and-Fire
neurons, replacing conventional LIF dynamics with a heterogeneous population of oscillatory units.
We showed that HRF neurons act as distributed band-pass filters within the reservoir, producing
intrinsically more class-discriminative representations prior to readout training. This translates
into consistent accuracy gains across diverse temporal benchmarks (up to +26.6% over LIF-Res),
while achieving up to 19× lower inference energy than a non-spiking oscillatory baseline. Together,
these results highlight frequency-selective dynamics as a powerful and underexplored computational
principle for spiking reservoirs, enabling richer temporal representations without sacrificing efficiency.
Several limitations remain: energy estimates are model-based and not yet validated on neuromorphic
hardware, the readout is trained offline, and a rigorous theoretical characterisation of HRF reservoir
dynamics in terms of memory capacity, separation properties, and stability remains an open question.
Additionally, the representational advantage of HRF neurons is expected to be most pronounced
for signals with identifiable frequency structure; performance gains may be smaller for tasks where
class-relevant information is not encoded in temporal frequency content. This is consistent with

Experimental Details and Hyperparameter Search
This appendix is self-contained: it provides all implementation details, data preprocessing steps,
training and evaluation procedures, and hyperparameter configurations needed to reproduce the
results in the paper.
A.1
Architecture Details
Weight initialisation.
The input matrix U ∈RN×Ninp is sampled entry-wise from U(0, 1) and
scaled by the input scaling hyperparameter ν > 0; the bias b ∈RN is sampled from U(−1, 1) and
scaled by the same ν. The HRF-to-LIF feedback matrix W ∈RN×N is sampled from U(−2, 2)
and rescaled to a target spectral radius ρ > 0 [4, 26]. The LIF-to-HRF synaptic matrix V ∈RN×N
is sampled from U(−2, 2) without spectral normalisation. All matrices are fixed after initialisation
and never updated. HRF-Res is implemented in PyTorch with a logistic regression readout from
scikit-learn. Each input sequence is processed starting from a zero-initialised reservoir state.
Heterogeneous HRF parameters.
Per-neuron stiffness and damping are drawn as γi ∼U(γ −
∆γ/2, γ + ∆γ/2) and εi ∼U(ε −∆ε/2, ε + ∆ε/2), with values clamped to be strictly positive.
The natural resonance frequency of neuron i is ωi = √γi. The centres (γ, ε) and widths (∆γ, ∆ε)
reported in Tables 6–7 are treated as hyperparameters, inducing a diversity of intrinsic oscillation
frequencies across the reservoir. The timestep ∆t is treated as a hyperparameter rather than a fixed
integration step, as we are interested in the computational properties of the discrete-time system in its
own right [22].
Spike generation.
The position y is not reset after a spike, so that the subthreshold oscillation
continues undisturbed. To prevent a neuron from firing at every consecutive timestep while y remains
above threshold, the HRF threshold is made refractory: neuron i spikes when yi > θRF + ri, where ri
is incremented by one at each spike and decays as ri ←ri e−∆t/τref with τref = 0.25.
Sparse connectivity.
When the LIF→HRF connectivity pV < 1, a binary mask is sampled i.i.d.
with Pr(entry = 1) = pV and applied entry-wise to V. The HRF→LIF connectivity is kept fully
dense (pW = 1.0) in all experiments.
Sparse input projection.
For high-dimensional inputs (SHD: 700 channels; DVS Gesture: 2 048
channels; N-MNIST: 578 channels), a sparse random projection is applied to U: a binary mask is
sampled with each entry set to 1 with probability ρinp (the input density hyperparameter), and the
masked weights are rescaled by 1/√ρinp to preserve the expected norm of the input drive. At least
one active connection per neuron and per input channel is ensured by rejection sampling.
A.2
Energy Estimation Details
To quantify inference-time efficiency, we estimate computational cost using synaptic operations
(SOPs) [41, 42]. SOPs measure the number of spike-driven synaptic events and provide a hardware-
relevant proxy for energy consumption in spiking systems.
On neuromorphic hardware, each presynaptic spike crossing a synapse corresponds to a single
accumulate (AC) operation with energy cost EAC = 0.9 pJ (45 nm CMOS). In contrast, non-
spiking neural networks require multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations at every timestep, with cost
EMAC = 4.6 pJ [41].
For a two-population reservoir, the total number of synaptic operations per input sequence is given
by:
SOPs = rHRF · N · T · Ch→l + rLIF · N · T · Cl→h,
(8)
where rHRF and rLIF denote the mean firing rates of HRF and LIF populations, N is the number of
reservoir neurons, T is the sequence length, and Ch→l and Cl→h denote the number of active synaptic
connections in each direction.
This formulation reflects the event-driven nature of spiking computation: synaptic cost scales with
spike activity rather than with full connectivity. In contrast, non-spiking models perform dense matrix
14


multiplications at every timestep, leading to computation that scales with network size and sequence
length regardless of input sparsity.
The internal HRF state dynamics require O(N) operations per timestep independent of spike activity,
while recurrent synaptic communication is gated by sparse spiking events. As a result, the dominant
recurrent cost scales with effective firing activity rather than full N 2 connectivity, which is the
primary source of energy savings in spiking reservoir models.
All energy comparisons in the main paper are derived from SOP counts using the above formulation
and the hardware constants defined above.
Neuron state updates.
The SOP count above covers the event-driven synaptic cost. Neuron state
updates are instead paid at every timestep, regardless of spiking activity. With the constant factors
(1 −∆t εi), ∆t γi and (1 −∆t/τm,i) precomputed, each HRF neuron requires 3 MACs per timestep
(Eqs. (5)–(6)), as it updates two coupled state variables, while each LIF neuron requires 1 MAC
(Eq. (4)); bias additions and threshold comparisons are neglected. Per timestep, this amounts to 4N
MACs for HRF-Res (HRF reservoir and LIF encoder), 2N MACs for LIF-Res, and 3N MACs for
RON, whose oscillators follow the same update (the tanh evaluation is not counted). These costs
scale as O(N), whereas the synaptic cost scales as O(N 2).
Table 4 reports the resulting estimates for the comparison of Table 2. State updates add less than
10% to the energy of HRF-Res on all four datasets, and the reduction over RON remains 9–17×.
For the comparison of Table 1, including state updates increases the energy of HRF-Res by at most
0.12 × 10−4 J (sMNIST) and does not change the ranking between HRF-Res and LIF-Res on any
dataset.

Data Preprocessing
sMNIST / psMNIST. MNIST images (28 × 28 pixels) are read pixel-by-pixel to form sequences of
length T = 784. For psMNIST, a fixed random permutation (seed 0, independent of model seed) is
applied to the pixel order before each forward pass.
npCIFAR-10. Each 32 × 32 RGB image is read row-by-row, producing T = 32 steps of 96-
dimensional input (32 × 3 channels). Following [22], 968 steps of fixed random Gaussian noise
(drawn once, shared across all trials) are appended, giving T = 1000 total steps.
FordA / FordB / Wafer / Earthquakes. Raw univariate sequences from the UCR archive [47] are
used without modification. Sequence lengths are: FordA and FordB: T = 500; Wafer: T = 152;
Earthquakes: T = 512.
SHD. Event streams are binned into T = 250 frames over a 1.4 s window using tonic, yielding
sparse binary tensors of shape (250 × 700).
DVS Gesture. Events are binned into T = 200 frames; spatial downsampling by factor 4 reduces
128 × 128 to 32 × 32 per polarity, giving 2 048 input channels per step.
N-MNIST. Events are binned into T = 30 frames; spatial downsampling by factor 2 reduces 34 × 34
to 17 × 17 per polarity, giving 578 input channels.

A.4
Training and Evaluation Protocol
Readout.
The only trained component is the linear readout: a logistic regression classifier (scikit-
learn) fitted with the L-BFGS solver (max. 2 000 iterations) and ℓ2 regularisation strength C. For
SHD (20 classes) the multinomial objective is used; all other datasets use one-vs-rest.
Data splits.
For UCR datasets (FordA, FordB, Wafer, Earthquakes), the full provided training
partition is used for readout training. For all other datasets the standard train/test splits are used.
Hyperparameter search uses a held-out validation split of 10% of the training set (stratified); the test
set is never accessed during search.
Evaluation.
All results are mean ± std over five independent trials. Each trial uses a different
random seed controlling all fixed weight matrices (U, V, W, b) and, for neuromorphic datasets, the
sparse input projection mask. The readout is re-fitted from scratch in each trial. The same five seeds
are used for HRF-Res and LIF-Res to ensure a controlled comparison.
Hyperparameter search.
Each configuration is evaluated on the validation split with a single seed;
the best configuration is then re-evaluated with five seeds on the full train/test split. Parameters
spanning multiple orders of magnitude (∆t, γ, ε, sinp, θenc, θres, ρinp) are sampled log-uniformly;
all others uniformly. The regularisation C is sampled from a small discrete set. The number of search
trials per dataset is reported in the corresponding table caption.
Compute.
All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB). Feature extraction
per trial ranges from ∼10 s (sMNIST, N = 800) to ∼15 min (N-MNIST, N = 3600). Total compute
across all experiments (search and final evaluation) is approximately 500 GPU-hours.
A.5
LIF-Res Baseline
LIF-Res is architecturally identical to HRF-Res except that the HRF reservoir is replaced by a second
LIF population. It consists of a LIF encoder and a LIF reservoir, both of size N, connected by the
same weight matrices U, V, W with the same initialisation and sparsity conventions as HRF-Res.
The reservoir LIF neurons have heterogeneous membrane time constants τm,i and thresholds θres,i,
drawn per neuron from log-uniform distributions centred on hyperparameters τm ± ∆τm/2 and
θres ± ∆θres/2 respectively, mirroring the γi/εi heterogeneity of HRF-Res. Soft reset is used: upon
spiking, the membrane potential is decremented by the threshold rather than reset to zero. The
encoder time constant is fixed to τenc = 20 for all datasets, identical to HRF-Res. An independent
hyperparameter search is conducted for LIF-Res on each dataset using the same protocol as for
HRF-Res, with 80 trials for SHD and 100 trials for all other datasets, all using the final reservoir state
for the readout. Full search ranges and selected configurations are in Table 5.

B.1
Frequency Sweep Parameters
The frequency sweep analysis sweeps 30 log-spaced frequencies per dataset. For each frequency f, a
sinusoidal input x(t) = sin(2πf · t · ∆t) is broadcast identically across all Ninp input channels and
the network is run for Tsweep timesteps. The first half of the trajectory is discarded as transient; each
neuron’s preferred frequency f ∗is identified as the frequency maximising its steady-state response
power, estimated via the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of the second half. The same sweep
range [fmin, fmax] is applied to both HRF-Res and LIF-Res for each dataset; each model uses its own
independently tuned ∆t.

The larger Tsweep and smaller fmin for SHD and DVS Gesture reflect the slower intrinsic timescales
of these datasets: the hyperparameter search selects larger ∆t values to match the longer temporal
dependencies present in audio spike trains and event-camera streams. Using a shorter sweep or

underestimating their Q-factors.
The Q-factor is computed per neuron from the response power matrix R ∈RNfreqs×N as follows.
For each neuron j, the peak response Ppeak is identified at its preferred frequency f ∗. The half-
power bandwidth ∆f−3 dB is estimated by finding the lower and upper frequency crossings where
Rfj = Ppeak/2, using linear interpolation on the log-frequency axis. Q = f ∗/∆f−3 dB is set to
zero if either crossing does not exist, indicating purely integrative (low-pass) behaviour with no
well-defined resonance peak. We emphasise that the sweep provides a mechanistic characterisation
of each reservoir’s dynamical repertoire and does not constitute a direct model-to-model comparison
of absolute frequency scales, since the two models have independently tuned ∆t values.
Effect of spectral windowing.
The results in Section 4.1 compute the DFT on the steady-state
trajectory without an explicit window. To verify that spectral leakage does not affect our conclusions,
we repeated the analysis applying a Hann window before the DFT (Table 9). Windowing shifts
the estimates slightly but leaves every qualitative conclusion unchanged: on all four datasets, HRF-
Res has a far larger fraction of resonant neurons, higher Q-factors, and a broader distribution of
preferred frequencies than LIF-Res.

Choice of summary statistic.
CKA [45] compares fixed-length representations, so applying it to

since the HRF position yt oscillates around an equilibrium, averaging partially cancels the oscillatory
component that distinguishes HRF from LIF dynamics. Statistics that retain more of this component,
such as the RMS or the combined statistic of Section 4.3, would be expected to accentuate rather
than diminish the differences reported in Section 4.1.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist
1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The abstract and introduction state all main contributions and claims, each
directly supported by experimental results in Sections 4.1–4.5.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: Limitations are discussed in the Conclusion, covering multiple aspects includ-
ing hardware validation, theoretical characterisation, scope of evaluation, and conditions
under which the approach may underperform.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.
• While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an impor-
tant role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers
will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.
3. Theory assumptions and proofs
Question: For each theoretical result, does the paper provide the full set of assumptions and
a complete (and correct) proof?

Answer: [N/A]
Justification: The paper does not include theoretical results or proofs; all contributions are
empirical.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include theoretical results.

referenced.

4. Experimental result reproducibility
Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main ex-
perimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions
of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The architecture and neuron models are fully described in Sections 3.1–3.2,
the energy estimation framework in Section 3.3, and the analysis methods in Section 3.4.
Full hyperparameter search ranges and selected configurations for all datasets are reported in
Appendix A.6 (HRF-Res) and A.5 (LIF-Res baseline), the evaluation and training protocol in
Appendix A.4, data preprocessing in Appendix A.3, energy estimation details in Appendix
A.2, and frequency sweep and CKA implementation details in Appendices B.1–B.2. Code
is publicly available.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived
well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of
whether the code and data are provided or not.
• If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken
to make their results reproducible or verifiable.
• Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways.
For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully
might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may
be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same
dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often
one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed
instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case
of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are
appropriate to the research performed.
• While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submis-
sions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the
nature of the contribution. For example
(a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how
to reproduce that algorithm.
(b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe
the architecture clearly and fully.
(c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should
either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce
the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct
the dataset).

(d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case
authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility.
In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in
some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers
to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

7. Experiment statistical significance
Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate
information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
26


Answer: [Yes]
Justification: All results are reported as mean ± standard deviation over 5 independent trials,
where each trial uses a different random seed controlling all fixed weight matrices and, for
neuromorphic datasets, the sparse input projection mask. This is stated consistently across
all tables and figures, and described in Appendix A.4.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence
intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the
main claims of the paper.
• The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for
example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall
run with given experimental conditions).
• The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula,
call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)
• The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
• It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error
of the mean.
• It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should
preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis
of Normality of errors is not verified.
• For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or
figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative
error rates).
• If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how
they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
