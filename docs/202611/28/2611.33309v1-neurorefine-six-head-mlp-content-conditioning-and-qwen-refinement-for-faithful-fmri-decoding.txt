AND QWEN REFINEMENT FOR FAITHFUL FMRI DECODING

ABSTRACT
Reconstructing seen images from fMRI has advanced through diffusion models,
but the current state-of-the-art Brain-IT pipeline still has a lossy bottleneck: its
low-level branch outlines the figure well, yet the diffusion model that follows fre-
quently mispaints colour, object identity and layout. We move one step backward
and condition the refiner directly on the fMRI-derived low-level image, using six
one-hidden-layer MLP heads (scene, main-subject category, count, colour, object,
layout) to predict the content prompt that guides a frozen Qwen-Image-Edit-2511
model. On the 930 shared NSD concepts, scored with Brain-IT’s own metric code
on the same ground truth, NeuroRefine reaches a CLIP image cosine of 0.755
against 0.735 for Brain-IT enhanced and produces markedly sharper images, while
the saturated 2-way identification is unchanged. The low-level image supplies an
explicit spatial prior rather than a lossless layout: refinement trades some layout
agreement (0.541 against 0.638) for semantic fidelity and texture. We then show
that the standard panel is multi-factor: a high PixCorr can coexist with a com-
pletely different content, so we read the low-level and semantic factors separately
and quantify where they disagree. Finally, we trace the cortical sources of the
prompt heads across subjects and report their causal lesion profile and the fMRI
noise ceiling. Direction errors are left to future work.

INTRODUCTION
Reconstructing visual experience from brain activity measured with fMRI is a key challenge with
broad implications for both neuroscience and brain–computer interfaces (Scotti et al., 2023; Beliy
et al., 2026). Such reconstructions may provide a window into visual perception in the brain, en-
able the study of visual imagery, and assist in assessing disorders of consciousness. In a typical
decoding setting, subjects view natural images while their brain activity is recorded with functional
Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), producing paired data of images and their corresponding brain
responses; the task is to reconstruct the perceived image from new (test) fMRI signals (Fig. 1).
Early work mapped fMRI signals to handcrafted image features, which were then used for image
reconstruction.
Subsequent studies employed deep learning and, more recently, diffusion mod-
els, which substantially improved visual quality (Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023; Takagi & Nishimoto,
2023). Yet current methods often lack faithfulness to the actual seen images: they deviate in structural
aspects (e.g., position, colour) and sometimes distort the semantic content, because the reliance on
generative priors can produce realistic images even with limited guidance from brain activity (Beliy
et al., 2026).
A central limitation is the caption bottleneck. Modern pipelines first predict a semantic description
(a caption) from fMRI and then generate an image with a text-to-image model; Brain-IT additionally
refines the result with SDXL (Beliy et al., 2026). The caption, however, is lossy: it discards colour,
exact object identity and spatial layout. Whatever the caption does not express cannot be recovered
by the generator.

In this work, we introduce NeuroRefine, a refinement stage on top of Brain-IT’s pipeline. NeuroRe-
fine moves one step backward: instead of conditioning the refiner on a caption, we condition it on
the low-level image and on a content prompt predicted by six one-hidden-layer MLP heads. The
low-level image supplies an explicit spatial prior that the caption compresses away, so the frozen
generative model acts as a sharpener rather than as a scene generator.
On the 930 shared NSD concepts, NeuroRefine improves semantic fidelity and sharpness over Brain-
IT enhanced, at the cost of some layout agreement, and it replicates across subjects (Sec. 4).
Our approach starts from the coarse arrangement of the low-level initialization while restoring sharp-
ness and object identity. It reads the full fsaverage vertex vector with six one-hidden-layer MLP heads
and pairs them with a frozen Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 refiner — and we evaluate it with Brain-IT’s
own metric code on the same ground truth, so that every number is directly comparable.
We make four contributions.
Head-derived conditioning. We give the refiner an explicit spatial prior that a caption cannot carry,
by conditioning it on the low-level image derived from fMRI, and we supply the missing content with
six one-hidden-layer MLP heads. We validate this choice with a generator held fixed, so the gain is
attributable to the conditioning signal and not to a stronger model (Sec. 4.3).
A frozen Qwen refiner. We replace Brain-IT’s SDXL enhancement with a frozen Qwen-Image-
Edit-2511 (Wu et al., 2025) refiner. Scored with Brain-IT’s own metric code on identical ground
truth, this improves the semantic fidelity and the sharpness of the reconstructions while leaving the
saturated 2-way identification unchanged (table 1).
A discussion of the metric panel. We show that the standard panel is multi-factor: a high PixCorr
can coexist with a completely different content. We therefore measure the relation between the low-
level and semantic factors, quantify where they disagree, and report each factor separately instead of
collapsing them into one ranking (Sec. 5.1).
A neurophysiological account. We characterise the representations produced by these six MLP
content heads on the fsaverage surface: which cortical networks carry each property (scene, main-
subject category, count, colour, object, layout), and how far the attribution is causally load-bearing.
The account combines surface maps, a causal lesion test, the repeat-trial noise ceiling, and a spin-null
test of cross-subject agreement, so that the semantic readout is not a black box (Sec. 5.2).

RELATED WORK
Reconstruction quality has improved rapidly since deep generative models were introduced. Early
decoding mapped fMRI to handcrafted image features and then inverted them, and was limited to
simple stimuli. Deep networks replaced the handcrafted features: MindEye (Scotti et al., 2023)
predicts CLIP and VAE embeddings and decodes with a diffusion prior, Brain-Diffuser (Ozcelik &
VanRullen, 2023) pairs a low-level and a semantic pathway, and Takagi & Nishimoto (Takagi &
Nishimoto, 2023) map voxels to Stable Diffusion latents. Later work adds structure and semantics in
different ways: DREAM (Xia et al., 2024a) reverses the visual system hierarchically, UMBRAE (Xia
et al., 2024b) unifies decoding across subjects and tasks, NeuroVLA (Shen et al., 2024) couples
reconstruction with language interaction, MindBridge (Wang et al., 2024) transfers across subjects
with a shared encoder, NeuroPictor (Huo et al., 2024) conditions diffusion on multiple fMRI-derived
controls, MindEye2 (Scotti et al., 2024) shares a subject-invariant backbone, and MindTuner (Gong
et al., 2025) tunes the generator with a visual-fingerprint prior. Brain-IT (Beliy et al., 2026) maps
functionally similar voxels into clusters shared across subjects, predicts localised features, and adds
an SDXL refinement stage; table 1 lists their reported numbers next to ours. Across this literature the
pattern in table 1 is consistent: PixCorr is highest for the method with the blurriest output, the 2-way
identification metrics saturate for every strong model, and the remaining headroom is in semantic
and perceptual fidelity. Our contribution is orthogonal to all of these methods — we change what
the refinement stage is conditioned on, which leaves the semantic stage interchangeable.
Generative priors and refinement.
Diffusion priors improved realism but can also reduce faith-
fulness, because a powerful generator produces a plausible image even from weak guidance. Two
remedies appear in the literature: more guidance (extra labels, extra subjects, better captions) and
stronger conditioning (image-to-image rather than text-to-image). Brain-IT takes the first route when
it refines with SDXL conditioned on its own reconstruction, but the SDXL stage they release is text-
free image-to-image with a weak preservation bias, and its metric effect is negligible (table 1). We
take the second route with a stronger editing model and an explicit preservation instruction, and we
show with a fixed generator that the gain is attributable to the conditioning signal (Sec. 4.3).
Semantic decoding and the caption bottleneck.
Most pipelines compress all voxels into a sin-
gle global embedding (Scotti et al., 2023; Beliy et al., 2026), although visual information is dis-
tributed across distinct but interconnected regions; spatial voxel groupings (Brain-IT clusters, Mind-
Eye2 subject-invariant backbones) mitigate this but still predict a global representation. Whatever
the representation, the text-to-image step that follows it is lossy: a caption cannot carry position,
exact colour or object identity, and the generator fills the gap with its own prior. Our result is a
direct measurement of that loss: with the same generator and the same semantic content, replacing
the caption by the image raises CLIP image cosine and improves layout agreement relative to the
caption-conditioned arm (Fig. 4 and table 2).
Evaluation.
The standard panel has two known failure modes: pixel metrics reward blur, and 2-way
identification saturates for every strong model (Beliy et al., 2026; Scotti et al., 2023). We demonstrate
both effects with controlled perturbations in Sec. 5.1 and therefore report CLIP image cosine as the
headline content metric. This is not a new metric — it is the quantity that the reported 2-way decision
thresholds — but reporting it explicitly makes the differences between strong methods visible.
Cross-subject transfer and data eﬀiciency.
Sharing components across subjects improves data
eﬀiciency; Brain-IT reports meaningful reconstructions with about one hour of subject data. We
separate the two axes: the readout (which needs subject data) and the generator (which does not,
because it is frozen). Table 6 shows that the “1 h” regime is reproduced only when the readout
is initialised from other subjects: from scratch, 250 images sit below the trivial mean-image prior,
whereas with transfer they already exceed it.

METHOD
Our NeuroRefine pipeline reconstructs an image from fMRI in two stages (Fig. 2). The first stage
produces a low-level image that provides a coarse spatial prior, together with a compact content
description. The second stage refines the image using a frozen editor conditioned on both signals.
The low-level branch follows Brain-IT (Beliy et al., 2026); the editor is Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Wu

3.1
LOW-LEVEL BRANCH
Voxel input.
The input is the betas of 39,548 fsaverage surface vertices (lh 19,004, rh 20,544),
z-scored per vertex, from the same preprocessed NSD release that Brain-IT uses (Allen et al., 2022;
Beliy et al., 2026); we do not resample or select voxels, so our readout sees exactly the information
their readout sees.
VGG prediction and DIP inversion.
Following Brain-IT (Beliy et al., 2026), we train a readout
to predict multi-layer VGG features with an InfoNCE loss (van den Oord et al., 2018), treating each
position in each layer as a separate token. At inference, the predicted features are inverted by a Deep
Image Prior (Ulyanov et al., 2018): a convolutional generator is optimized so that its VGG activations
match the predicted features under an L2 loss. The result is a low-level image that carries the coarse
layout but no reliable semantics; it is the input to our refiner. We use this branch unchanged, so any
improvement below is due to the refiner, not to a different low-level model.
3.2
SIX-HEAD MLP CONTENT PREDICTOR
Six heads.
A bank of six one-hidden-layer MLPs (1024 hidden units, GELU, dropout 0.3) maps
the same voxel vector to structured fields — scene, main-subject category, count, colour, object
and layout. The heads read the full 39,548-dimensional vertex vector directly; Brain-IT’s voxel-to-
cluster mapping is used only by the low-level branch, so there is no cluster-attention or transformer
block between the betas and the heads. Scene, main-subject category and count are trained with
categorical cross-entropy (softmax), while colour, object and layout are multi-label fields trained with
independent binary cross-entropy on sigmoid logits. Every head is deterministic given the betas. The
predicted fields are assembled into a short content prompt that names the scene, the main subject, the
4


object count, the dominant colours and the layout. Because the heads are deterministic, the attribution
analysis of Sec. 5.2 reflects the trained weights, not sampling noise.
Semantic alternatives.
We compare the heads against two other ways of supplying content, which
we keep as ablations rather than the main pipeline. The first is Brain-IT’s unCLIP semantic de-
coder (Beliy et al., 2026; Scotti et al., 2023), which maps a predicted CLIP embedding through a
diffusion prior. The second is retrieval: a linear readout predicts the CLIP-L image embedding, and
we return the nearest photograph from a gallery of 66,841 NSD images with the shared 1,000 re-
moved. Retrieval gives high identity but imports the arrangement of another photograph. Both are
reported in Sec. 4.
3.3
IMAGE-CONDITIONED EDIT REFINER
A frozen editor.
The refiner is Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Wu et al., 2025), used frozen at 1024 ×
1024 for 20 denoising steps with guidance 4.0. It takes the low-level image and the content prompt
and returns the reconstruction. The instruction is instruction-first: “follow the low-level layout and
render the content below”, followed by the head output. Because the editor is frozen, it adds no
subject-specific parameters; a new subject needs only the readout and the heads.
Why the low-level image, not a caption.
Brain-IT conditions its SDXL refiner on a caption (Beliy
et al., 2026); a caption cannot carry position or exact colour, so the refiner has to re-infer the layout.
Conditioning on the low-level image gives the refiner an explicit spatial prior, and the content prompt
only supplies what the low-level branch lacks. The editor is not required to copy that prior exactly
— it trades part of it for texture and semantics (Sec. 4.2) — but it starts from the input arrangement
instead of inventing one. This is the “one step backward” of the title.
3.4
DESIGNS WE COMPARED
We kept three directions separate and evaluate them on the same concepts and ground truth (Sec. 4).
Text-to-image conditions Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 on the prompt alone; it produces clean images but
re-invents the scene. Low-level + edit is the pipeline above. Retrieval + edit refines a retrieved pho-
tograph; it has the highest identity but the lowest layout. The ablations are decided on five diagnostic
concepts, chosen to cover an easy scene, a cluttered scene, a counting failure, an object substitution
and a hard animal case; they are not the final pipeline, so a full 930 rerun would not change their
conclusion.
3.5
METRICS
We call Brain-IT’s released compute_metrics verbatim, so every number in this paper can be
reproduced by running their code on our arrays. The panel contains PixCorr (Pearson correlation
of pixels), SSIM (structural similarity), AlexNet(2) and AlexNet(5) two-way identification, Incep-
tion two-way identification, CLIP two-way identification, and the EffNet-B and SwAV correlation
distances (lower is better). Because the two-way metrics saturate for strong models, we additionally
report CLIP image cosine between reconstruction and ground truth; unlike the 2-way decision it does
not saturate. Note that a 2-way trial compares sim(ˆxi, xi) with the similarity to a random distractor
xj, so its accuracy depends on both terms, and CLIP cosine is only one of them (we return to this in
Sec. G). We also report a sharpness proxy (mean gradient magnitude at 224×224); it measures edge
energy, not perceptual quality, and over-sharpening or edge artefacts can raise it, so we never read it
as image quality on its own. Finally we decompose content into colour, structure, layout, background
and detail agreement, so that a method that wins on colour but loses on layout is visible. LPIPS is
not computed, exactly as in the released Brain-IT code.

4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
Dataset and ground truth.
We use the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) (Allen et al., 2022), a 7 T
dataset in which eight subjects viewed natural photographs drawn from COCO. We evaluate on the
930 concepts shared by all subjects, with the shared 1,000 images as the test set; subject 1 is used
unless stated otherwise. The dataset is scene-centric: most images contain a small number of salient
objects in a natural layout, which is what the low-level branch can outline, while fine texture and
exact instance identity are limited by the 7 T resolution and by single-trial noise. That asymmetry is
why a content signal is needed on top of the low-level image. All arms are normalised to 224 × 224
before scoring, as in Brain-IT, and the betas are the 39,548 fsaverage surface vertices used by Brain-
IT (Beliy et al., 2026).
Metrics.
We call Brain-IT’s released compute_metrics (Beliy et al., 2026) verbatim, so num-
bers are directly comparable to table 1. The panel is deliberately two-level: PixCorr, SSIM and
AlexNet(2) measure low-level structure; AlexNet(5) is intermediate; Inception, CLIP 2-way, EffNet-
B and SwAV are semantic. Because the 2-way metrics saturate for strong models, we additionally
report CLIP image cosine between reconstruction and ground truth; it is one term of the 2-way trial,
not a threshold on it (Sec. G). We report the panel factor-wise rather than as a single ranking; Sec. 5.1
quantifies where the factors disagree. LPIPS is not computed, exactly as in the released Brain-IT
code.

CROSS-SUBJECT REPLICATION
We retrain only the per-subject readout and keep the frozen generator, so the comparison is subject-
specific end to end. The CLIP-cosine gain over Brain-IT enhanced replicates on all three subjects
we ran, while 2-way identification stays tied (table 3, Fig. 6). Because the generator is frozen, no
additional subject data is needed for the refiner; the per-subject cost is the readout only, and a data-
eﬀiciency curve for the readout (with and without cross-subject transfer) is reported in Sec. 4.7.

WHAT DOES “ONE HOUR” REQUIRE?
Brain-IT reports meaningful reconstructions with about one hour of subject data; that claim must be
read as one hour with cross-subject initialisation, not as one hour from scratch, and we make the
distinction explicit here. Since our generator is frozen, the only component that consumes subject
data is the readout, so we can separate the two axes. Training the readout from scratch on subject 1

5.1

The panel is multi-factor, not a single ranking.
The standard panel is deliberately two-level:
PixCorr, SSIM and AlexNet(2) measure low-level structure; AlexNet(5) is intermediate; Inception,
CLIP 2-way, EffNet-B and SwAV are semantic. These factors are not redundant. Across the 930 con-
cepts the per-concept ranks of the low-level factor (mean z of PixCorr and SSIM) and of the semantic
factor (CLIP image cosine) are only weakly related: Spearman ρ = 0.150 (95% CI [0.083, 0.214]),
and on 183/930 concepts (19.7%) the two factors point in opposite directions with both |z| > 0.5.

A PCA over the eight per-concept axes gives one dominant structural factor (42.7% of variance; Pix-
Corr 0.45, detail 0.46, background 0.44, layout 0.41, SSIM 0.39), then a colour-versus-layout axis
(14.0%) and a semantic axis dominated by CLIP cosine (12.1%). The panel is therefore multi-factor
rather than two neat factors, so a single aggregate or a low-level metric alone hides the disagreement;
we report it factor-wise and add two summaries: CLIP image cosine for the semantic factor and a
layout correlation for the structural factor (Fig. 8).
Controlled perturbation is a definition check, not a ranking proof.
Blurring the ground truth
raises PixCorr and lowers CLIP cosine, and recolouring does the opposite (table 5). This is exactly
what the definitions imply, but it is a synthetic manipulation: it does not by itself prove that real
reconstructions are misranked. On the real arms the same decoupling appears: across the 930 con-
cepts PixCorr and CLIP cosine correlate at ρ = 0.117 (95% CI [0.055, 0.177]) for NeuroRefine and
ρ = 0.068 (CI [0.000, 0.137]) for Brain-IT, which is why we read the two factors separately rather
than declaring one metric invalid.
Where the factors conflict.
A paired bootstrap over concepts puts an effect size on the dis-
agreement: relative to Brain-IT enhanced, NeuroRefine gains CLIP cosine by +0.0202 (95% CI
[0.0174, 0.0231], paired t p = 1.6 × 10−39, dz = 0.45) while losing PixCorr by −0.0885 (CI
[−0.102, −0.0749]) and layout by −0.0977 (CI [−0.1154, −0.0807]); the 19.7% conflict set above
is exactly where these two movements separate. We illustrate them with four case studies rather
than a single anecdote: the airplane, where a head-conditioned version renders the belly while the
seen image and Brain-IT are a horizontal seaplane; the cow, where Brain-IT is weak and the content
prompt helps; the bird, where the head says “field” but the seen image is a bird over blue water; and
the soccer scene, where the low-level+edit arm genuinely improves. These are case studies, not a
population estimate, and we say so.
Recommendation.
Report the panel factor-wise, with CLIP image cosine as the semantic summary
and a layout measure as the structural summary, and state the trade-off explicitly. We do not claim
that PixCorr or SSIM are invalid; they measure a different factor.
5.2
WHERE DO THE PROMPT HEADS READ?
All heads are visually dominant.
The content prompt is driven by six one-hidden-layer MLPs
(scene, main-subject category, count, colour, object, layout) that read the same vertices. We attribute
each head to cortex in two independent ways: the read-out weight energy ∥W1[:, v]∥2, and an input
saliency obtained analytically by back-propagating the arg-max class through the single hidden layer,
averaged over 200 images. Both give the same picture: mapped to the inflated fsaverage surface and
parcellated with Schaefer-2018, the attribution mass sits in the visual network for every head, with
the expected spread into dorsal and ventral streams (Fig. 9). We can therefore say nothing about
non-visual contributions of these heads, and we do not.

The attribution is causally load-bearing.
Zeroing the most salient vertices changes the head logits
several-fold more than zeroing a random set, and more than zeroing the least salient set (Fig. 10); the
effect is strongest for the heads that need spatial and numerical structure. Two ceiling measurements
frame the interpretation. The split-half reliability of the repeat trials is near one for the head outputs,
which only says that the heads are deterministic functions of their input; the reliability of the fMRI
signal itself per vertex is far lower and is the real ceiling for any readout trained on three repeats
(table 7). Cross-subject agreement of the attribution maps is small but above a spatial spin null
for every head (table 8): there is a shared, weakly-localised substrate, and we do not claim stable
individual differences beyond it.
5.3
FAILURE MODES
Our pipeline is faithful to its input: when the content prompt is wrong, the editor sharpens the mistake
rather than repairing it (Fig. 11). The failures are of four kinds. Object substitution: a bird becomes
an airplane, a giraffe becomes a zebra. Scene substitution: a fruit basket becomes an indoor scene.
Count and instance errors: a crowded race field becomes a single rider, two vases become a room
with two people. Direction: facing and mirroring are frequently wrong because the shared CLIP-like
representation is largely invariant to reflection. These are limits of the content predictor and of the
representation, not of the editor; we report them explicitly rather than hiding them in an aggregate.
6
DISCUSSION
What the refiner is and is not.
The refiner is a faithful sharpener, not a second decoder. The
low-level branch supplies a spatial prior, and the six MLP content heads supply the content; the
frozen editor then restores texture and colour on top of that layout. When the content head is wrong,
the refiner reproduces the error with high fidelity (Sec. 5.3); when the conditioning is text alone,
it re-composes the scene (Sec. 4.3). Both observations point to the same conclusion: the useful
information for a refinement stage is the image plus a compact content signal, and the ceiling of the
method is set by the content predictor, not by the low-level contour.
Sharpness is not quality.
Our outputs are much sharper than Brain-IT’s (mean gradient magnitude
1.85 against 0.27), but that number measures edge energy: over-sharpening and edge artefacts also
raise it, and no LPIPS or human preference study is available here. We therefore use sharpness only
to show that the refiner restores texture the low-level branch lacks, never as a standalone claim of
better perceptual quality.
Why the metrics disagree.
The panel is multi-factor: PixCorr/SSIM/Alex(2) measure low-level
structure, while Inception/CLIP/EffNet/SwAV are semantic. A method can be high on one and low on
the other, and a high PixCorr can coexist with a different content; we quantify the conflicts in Sec. 5.1.
We therefore report the panel factor-wise, with CLIP image cosine and layout as summaries. We do
not claim that the low-level metrics are invalid — they measure a different factor — but a single
aggregate hides the trade-off.
Direction.
Facing and mirroring are the one failure that is not inherited from the content head but
from the representation both pipelines share. A CLIP-like embedding is approximately invariant

initialisation (Sec. 4.7); a single subject trained from scratch at that budget stays below the trivial
mean-image prior, so the “1 h” regime should always be quoted with that qualifier. Whether the

same holds on a different scanner or dataset is an open question: our external check on BOLD5000
(Sec. 4.6) shows that a frozen NSD readout transfers only partially, and that the refiner amplifies
whatever bias the readout brings.
LIMITATIONS
(i) The content predictor is the bottleneck: when it is wrong the editor sharpens the error, and im-
proving it is future work. The refinement stage also trades layout agreement for semantic fidelity,
so we do not claim lossless preservation of the low-level arrangement. (ii) LPIPS is not computed,
following the released Brain-IT code. (iii) Cross-subject replication covers three subjects. (iv) The
human audit is a curated set, not a random sample. (v) The low-level metrics (PixCorr/SSIM) are
lower for sharper outputs by construction; we report them as one factor, not as a failure. (vi) The
external BOLD5000 check is preliminary and not directly comparable to the NSD numbers.

D
FAILURE CASES
Our editor sharpens and preserves what the content prompt gives it, so its errors are inherited rather
than invented: when the content predictor confuses objects (a bird becomes an airplane, a giraffe
becomes a zebra) or invents a scene (a fruit basket becomes an indoor scene), the editor faithfully
sharpens the wrong content. Count and instance errors follow the same route: a crowded race field
becomes a single rider, two vases become a room with two people (Fig. 11). Direction and object
facing are frequently wrong, because both our pipeline and the baselines pass through a CLIP-like
embedding that is largely invariant to mirroring; a global mirror test fixes only a small fraction of the
direction failures, and a dedicated direction readout is future work. These are limits of the content
predictor and of the representation, not of the editor.
E
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
The refiner is Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 at 1024 × 1024, 20 steps, guidance 4.0, used in instruction
mode with the instruction “follow the low-level layout and render the content below”. The frozen
generator is shared by every subject and every arm; only the readout and the six heads are subject-
specific. The retrieval gallery is the union of the NSD images over subjects (66,841 images) minus the
shared 1,000, and we verified that the test set and the gallery are disjoint. All metrics are computed at
224 × 224 with Brain-IT’s compute_metrics; 2-way identification uses a fixed random permu-
tation and is stable to ±0.002 across reruns. Sharpness is the mean gradient magnitude, computed
identically for every arm.
Prompt construction across subjects.
The content prompt is built from the six head outputs with
one shared template: the colour field contributes its top three colours above a per-class threshold, and
the scene and object fields contribute their predicted labels. For 71 of the 930 concepts the original
pipeline leaves the subject/object fields blank (the annotation carries no confident main subject);
subjects 2 and 5 inherit exactly the same rule, so their prompts differ from subject 1 only through
the per-subject head predictions. The low-level input is subject-independent: it is Brain-IT’s fused
low-level image with a fixed unsharp/gamma post-process, used unchanged for every subject.
F
CONTENT-HEAD LABEL SPACES
All six heads are trained on labels parsed from Qwen3-VL annotations of the training images only;
the 930 shared test concepts are never used for supervision, so no label leaks into evaluation. The
heads read the same full 39,548-dimensional vertex vector independently — they are six separate
one-hidden-layer MLPs, not a transformer and not an attention block, and Brain-IT’s voxel-to-cluster
mapping is used only by the low-level branch — so their only interaction is through the prompt
string that concatenates their outputs. Three fields are single-label and use categorical cross-entropy
on softmax logits (scene, main-subject category, count); three are multi-label and use independent
binary cross-entropy on sigmoid logits (colour, object, layout). The label spaces are:
• scene: 38-way softmax over canonical scenes (market, kitchen, ocean, beach, street, city, field,
forest, mountain, airport, train station, stadium, zoo, …) with an other fallback; the class is
assigned by ordered keyword rules on the annotation’s scene and caption fields, most specific rule
first.
• main-subject category: 20-way softmax over the image’s main subject (person, people, man,
woman, child, dog, cat, bird, horse, cow, bear, sheep, elephant, giraffe, zebra, fish, airplane, train,
car, food). This is a category of the depicted content, not an NSD participant.
• count: 3-way (1 / 2 / ≥3), taken from the largest number word or digit in the annotation.
• colour: 12 independent binary labels (white, black, red, blue, green, yellow, brown, orange, pink,
grey, purple, gold). It is multi-label: one image can carry several colours, and each head output
is thresholded independently rather than normalised across classes.
• object: 150 independent binary labels over a canonical object vocabulary built from the training
annotations. Multiple objects per image are allowed; the head predicts presence, not a single
arg-max class.
21


• layout: 8 independent binary labels (left, right, front, behind, above, below, centre, on), parsed
from the relation clauses of the annotation. They describe where the main subject sits relative to
other objects; they are not a dense spatial map, which is why the low-level image, not this head,
carries position.
Because colour, object and layout are multi-label with independent binary cross-entropy, we report
their accuracy as per-label average precision rather than top-1, and we never compare them numeri-
cally with the single-label scene, main-subject and count heads (which use categorical cross-entropy).

METRIC DEFINITIONS
2-way identification: for image i and a random other image j, the trial succeeds if sim(ˆxi, xi) >
sim(ˆxi, xj); we report accuracy over all images (chance 0.5). n-way: top-1 of ˆxi among all n ground-
truth features. CLIP cosine: cosine between CLIP-L image embeddings of ˆxi and xi. It is one term of
the 2-way trial above, not its threshold: the trial also depends on sim(ˆxi, xj) for a random distractor,
so a model can have a high CLIP cosine and still lose trials when the distractor pool happens to
be close. We report it as the semantic summary because, unlike the 2-way accuracy, it does not
saturate near 1. PixCorr: Pearson correlation of the two images’ pixels. SSIM: structural similarity.
EffNet/SwAV: L2 distance in the corresponding feature space (lower is better).
