Abstract 13 

Local field potentials (LFPs) are widely interpreted as readouts of population synaptic activity, an 14 

assumption derived almost entirely from  cortical recor dings . Whether these principles extend to 15 

subcortical structures remains unclear. We address this  question  in the subthalamic nucleus (STN), 16 

where LFPs are routinely recorded  and  used to guide adaptive  deep brain stimulation  for 17 

Parkinson's disease , using a biophysically detailed population model benchmarked against patient 18 

microelectrode recordings. As in the cortex, STN  extracellular potentials were dominated by 19 

synaptic currents.  Differently from the  cortex , however , LFPs could not be reliably predicted from 20 

these currents or other average population quantities.  This dissociation  arises  from the STN's 21 

symmetric neuronal morphology and lack of recurrent connectivity, which promote destructive 22 

interference among single -neuron contributions,  decoupl ing  the LFP  from  population -level 23 

dynamics.  This decoupling  was not absolute: pathological beta synchrony restored a robust 24 

synapse -LFP relationship by  consistent  underlying dynamics , while the aperiodic slope of the 25 

power spectral density tracked STN neuronal morphology, firing rate, and excitatory -inhibitory 26 

balance.  Together, t hese findings challenge the prevailing view of LFPs as universal readouts of 27 

population activity . Our results show that the interpretability of extracellular signals depends 28 

critically on neuronal morphology and synchronization state,  and provide a mechanistic 29 

framework for the use of STN LFPs as biomarkers in adaptive deep brain stimulation for 30 

Parkinson’s disease. 31

Introduction 33 

34 

Extracellular potentials (ECPs) are among the most widely used electrophysiological signals in 35 

neuroscience 1. They reflect neuronal transmembrane currents  in the vicinity of the recording 36 

electrode, including contributions from spiking and synaptic activity 2,3 . Owing to their rich 37 

informative content, ECPs are  both  used in basic and clinical neuroscience as biomarkers of 38 

neurological disorders 4–6.39 

The physiological mechanisms underlying ECP generation have been extensively investigated in 40 

cortical circuits 7–12 , where  local field potentials (LFPs), i.e., low -pass filtered ECPs,  primarily 41 

reflect synaptic inputs onto spatially aligned pyramidal neurons. In contrast, LFPs in subcortical 42 

structures remain poorly understood. This gap between cortical and subcortical 43 

electrophysiological features limits a mechanistic interpretation of experimental recordings and 44 

the development of computational models of subcortical networks. 45 

These limitations are particularly relevant for the subthalamic nucleus (STN), a key structure in 46 

Parkinson’s disease (PD) and the primary target of deep brain stimulation (DBS) 13 –15 . STN LFPs 47 

are routinely recorded to characterize pathological network dynamics and guide adaptive DBS 48 

strategies 16 –18 . However, despite numerous studies linking LFP features to PD symptoms 14 –17 ,49 

linking pathophysiological mechanisms with STN electrophysiological signals remains a major 50 

challenge. 51 

Computational modelling offers a powerful approach to address these questions 19,20 . However, 52 

most PD computational studies have focused on the mechanisms underlying pathological 53 

synchronization of firing dynamics inside the basal ganglia, and in particular within beta 21,22  and 54 

gamma 23  bands.  The few computational studies that have explicitly incorporated neuronal 55 

morphology (a necessary component to accurately estimate ECPs) have primarily examined the 56 

effects of DBS within the STN 25,26 , rather than systematically investigating how morphology, 57 

synaptic inputs, and firing dynamics interact to shape ECPs. 58 

Here, we address this issue leveraging a biophysical model of the STN incorporating detailed 59 

neuronal morphology, anatomically constrained synaptic connectivity, and realistic population 60 

architecture. The resulting ECPs were benchmarked against microelectrode recordings from PD 61 

patients. We show that synaptic currents contribute substantially to STN ECPs, as in the cortex. 62 

However, unlike cortical ECPs, STN ECPs cannot be reliably predicted from synaptic currents. 63 

We identify neuronal morphology and synaptic input dynamics as the main determinants of this 64 

dissociation, and we show that pathological beta oscillations can restore this coupling. Finally, we 65 

demonstrate that neuronal morphology and excitatory/inhibitory balance shape the STN spectral 66 

aperiodic slope. 67 

Together, these results establish a mechanistic link between neuronal architecture, synaptic 68 

dynamics, and subthalamic extracellular signals, providing a foundation for the interpretation of 69 

STN recordings in health and disease .70

Validation of synaptic parameters against biological constraints 76 

Building upon a previously developed multicompartmental model of an STN neuron 27,28 , we tuned 77 

synaptic features 25  (Fig. 1A) to match literature -reported findings. We placed peri -somatic cortical 78 

(i.e., excitatory) and dendritic -targeting pallidal (i.e., inhibitory) synapses and performed voltage -79 

clamp simulations in response to individual input spikes. We analyzed the amplitude of the 80 

simulated post -synaptic currents and identified the range of synaptic conductances reproducing 81 

experimentally reported values (excitatory currents: 105 ± 5 pA 29 ; i nhibitory conductances: 0.24 82 

± 0.06 nS 30 , gray bar in Fig. 1B). Excitatory synapses required larger conductances  and showed 83 

larger variability , yet produced  amplitudes  comparable in amplitude to inhibitory synapses, 84 

indicating a higher efficacy of peri -somatic inhibition.  Within  the selected  ranges, we next 85 

investigated which combinations of excitatory and inhibitory conductances reproduced the firing 86 

statistics of STN neurons recorded in vivo 31 –33 . Simulations used literature -based estimates of 87 

synaptic numbers and input firing rates for cortical and pallidal populations. This analysis revealed 88 

a well -defined region of parameter space in which the model produced physiological firing rates 89 

(Fig. 1C, black dots) with mean value between 30 to 40 spikes/s and coefficients of variation close 90 

to 1.0 . Outside this region, the model exhibited two distinct failure regimes. Insufficient excitatory 91 

drive produced low -rate or absent firing (Fig. 1C). Conversely, excessive excitation produced 92 

sustained depolarization and loss of physiological firing (Fig . 1C  white region).  Based on these 93 

constraints, we selected inhibitory and excitatory synaptic conductances of 0.28 nS and 15 nS, 94 

respectively (dashed lines in Fig. 1B, C; Methods), which reproduced the  experimentally  observed 95 

firing properties. Under these synaptic parameters, the model transitioned from the regular 96 

spontaneous firing observed in the absence of synaptic inputs (Fig . 1D top) to an irregular firing 97 

regime consistent with in vivo STN activity (Fig. 1D bottom ).98

Symmetric morphology and uncorrelated activity limit STN LFP reconstruction 166 

In cortical circuits, LFPs are largely generated by synaptic activity 1,7 . Importantly, this relationship 167 

can be inverted, allowing LFPs to be reconstructed from a linear combination of excitatory and 168 

inhibitory population synaptic currents 8. Having shown that STN ECPs are likewise dominated by 169 

synaptic currents (Fig. 2I), we next asked whether the same synaptic -to -ECP relationship extends 170 

to the STN (Fig. 3A). 171 

We first validated this approach in a cortical network composed of simplified ball -and -stick 172 

excitatory and inhibitory neurons (Fig. 3A, right; Methods). Consistent with previous studies 8,173 

cortical LFPs were accurately reconstructed from the sum of the absolute excitatory and inhibitory 174 

synaptic currents (R² = 0.94; Fig. 3B, right). Applying the same approximation to the STN model 175 

yielded almost no correspondence between synaptic currents and the ground -truth LFP (R² = 0.03; 176 

Fig. 3B, left), indicating that STN LFPs cannot be linearly reconstructed from population synaptic 177 

activity. Similar results were obtained using population firing rates, average membrane potentials 178 

(Supplementary Fig.  2A) or kernel -based approaches 9,38  (Supplementary Fig.  2B-C), suggesting 179 

that STN LFPs cannot be reliably inferred from standard population -level measures of network 180 

activity. The divergence between cortical and STN LFP generation became evident as larger 181 

fractions of the neuronal population were included in the extracellular signal reconstruction (Fig. 182 

3C). In the cortical model, reconstruction accuracy improved with additional neurons, consistent 183 

with population averaging reducing single -neuron variability. In contrast, reconstruction 184 

performance deteriorated in the STN as more neurons were included, indicating that population 185 

summation does not average out single -cell variability in this structure. This effect became evident 186 

when neuronal dynamics were preserved but somatic positions were randomized (Fig. 3D). 187 

Cortical excitatory LFPs were largely unchanged, with correlations between original and permuted 188 

signals remaining near 1. In contrast, STN LFPs were highly sensitive to neuronal arrangement, 189 

with correlations approaching 0 after permutation. Cortical inhibitory populations showed an 190 

intermediate response (R² = 0.44). Together, these results indicate that STN extracellular signals 191 

remain strongly shaped by randomness in the spatial organization, whereas cortical LFPs primarily 192 

reflect population -level activity. 193 

This intermediate behavior of cortical inhibitory populations suggested that neuronal symmetry, a 194 

feature that makes inhibitory neurons more STN -like than cortical excitatory neurons, can only 195 

partially account for the observations in the STN.  We therefore tested the role of recurrent 196 

connectivity, a hallmark of cortical circuits that is absent in the feedforward STN network, by 197 

replacing recurrent cortical inputs with Poisson spike trains matched for mean firing rate, thereby 198 

disrupting coordinated synaptic activity generated by local network interactions.  This 199 

manipulation substantially reduced the goodness -of -fit of the synaptic LFP approximation for 200 

excitatory neurons, lowering R² from 0.94 to 0.47 (Fig. 3E). The effect was even stronger for 201 

inhibitory neurons, where R² decreased from 0.45 to 0.09, reaching values comparable to those 202 

observed in the STN. Similar effects were obtained when reconstructing the LFP from other 203 

population -level descriptors, including average somatic membrane potential and firing rate (Fig. 204 

3E). 205

Pathological beta synchrony restores  LFP  interpretability 235 

The results described above indicate that STN LFPs are strongly shaped by cancellation effects, 236 

weakly related to standard population descriptors, and highly sensitive to recording geometry (Fig. 237 

3), suggesting a noise -dominated signal with limited information content. This contrasts with 238 

extensive experimental and clinical evidence showing that STN LFPs exhibit structured, disease -239 

related spectral features, including beta -band oscillations 18,39  and the aperiodic slope 40  of the power 240 

spectrum, both well -established biomarkers in Parkinson’s disease. We therefore asked whether 241 

structured network synchronization could restore  the information  content  of STN LFPs. 242 

To address this question, we introduced beta -oscillatory cortical and pallidal inputs into the model, 243 

independently varying their amplitudes and relative phase shift (Fig . 3A) . Model parameters were 244 

constrained using the same STN microelectrode 37  recordings employed in previous analyses by 245 

matching spike -triggered beta -band (13 –30 Hz) LFPs . The best -fitting solutions consistently 246 

exhibited a cortical –pallidal phase shift between 0 and π/2 (≈11 ms; Fig. 4A) , together with a 247 

dominant cortical oscillatory drive (A exc  = 3 spike/s , A inh  = 7.5 spike/s ). The corresponding best -248 

fitting solution accurately reproduced the experimental LFPs (Fig. 4B –C), indicating that beta -249 

band spike –field coupling is primarily governed by cortical oscillatory input and its phase 250 

relationship with pallidal activity. 251 

The introduction of beta oscillations qualitatively transformed the relationship between population 252 

activity and the LFP. Whereas synaptic currents and membrane potentials poorly predicted STN 253 

LFP in the absence of beta (Fig. 3), both quantities accurately  followed  beta -band LFP fluctuations 254 

under oscillatory conditions (Fig. 4D). Thus, pathological synchronization restores a low -255 

dimensional relationship between population activity and the STN LFP that is otherwise  absent 256 

from the asynchronous regime .257

STN aperiodic slope reflects neuronal morphology, E/I balance, and average STN firing rate 261 

Having characterized the rhythmic components of the STN LFP, we next investigated its aperiodic 262 

spectral component. The aperiodic exponent simulated in out model (1.13) fell within the range 263 

observed in the dataset of experimental MER recordings (Fig. 4E, mean  ± STD=1.00  ± 0.49) and 264 

was substantially lower than values typically reported for cortical recordings (approximately 2.5  -265 

3.0 41 ). To investigate the origin of this difference, we examined how neuronal morphology shapes 266 

the PSD aperiodic slope using morphologically diverse neurons from the Allen Institute visual 267 

cortex database 42  (see Methods). Across cell types, the aperiodic exponent increased 268 

monotonically with morphological asymmetry (r = 0.78, p=0.02 Fig. 4E): neurons with elongated, 269 

pyramidal -like morphologies produced steeper spectral slopes, whereas more symmetric inhibitory 270 

interneurons exhibited shallower exponents. STN neurons fell on the same relationship, consistent 271 

with their relatively symmetric morphology. Interestingly, applying this relationship to a272 

reconstructed human layer 5 pyramidal neuron yielded a predicted PSD slope of 2.7, in quantitative 273 

agreement with human cortical recordings 41 . These results indicate that the aperiodic exponent is 274 

strongly constrained by neuronal morphology and that a simple morphological relationship can 275 

account for regional differences in LFP spectral slopes. While neuronal morphology explained the 276 

lower aperiodic slopes of the STN relative to cortex, STN network dynamics also contributed to 277 

shaping the aperiodic exponent. The PSD slope was also strongly related to the STN mean firing 278 

rate (r =  -0.81, p << 0.001; Fig. 4F) and to the balance between excitatory and inhibitory inputs, 279 

with stronger inhibition associated with steeper slopes (r =  -0.81, p << 0.001; Fig. 4F). The 280 

aperiodic slope therefore inherits STN sensitivity to firing rate through its dependence on the E/I 281 

drive ratio, linking an arrhythmic spectral feature of the LFPs to the spiking regime of the network. 282 

Together, these findings show that STN LFPs, despite their limited  relations with  population 283 

quantities  under asynchronous conditions, can retain information about network dynamics:  the 284 

emergence of  beta  synchronization (observed in PD)  restores a direct link with synaptic activity, 285 

whereas the aperiodic slope reflects the underlying firing  rate  and excitatory –inhibitory balance. 286

Discussion 310 

Local field potentials are widely considered indicators of population -level neural activity, but their 311 

interpretation has largely been derived from cortical regions. Our results show that these 312 

assumptions do not generalize to the subthalamic nucleus. Using a biophysically detailed STN 313 

model validated against microelectrode recordings from Parkinson’s disease patients, we 314 

demonstrate that, although STN ECPs are primarily generated by synaptic currents, they cannot 315 

be reliably reconstructed from those currents. This dissociation differs fundamentally from cortical 316 

regions and arises from two key properties: the symmetric morphology of STN neurons and the 317 

absence of recurrent connectivity, which reduces correlated synaptic activity. Together, these 318 

features promote destructive interference when summing single -neuron contribution to field 319 

potentials, making STN LFPs highly sensitive to  recording  position . Nevertheless,  STN LFPs  can 320 

retain meaningful information about network dynamics. In particular, the aperiodic spectral slope 321 

reflects the balance between excitatory and inhibitory drive and the resulting firing regime, 322 

suggesting a potential biomarker of network state. Moreover, under conditions of (pathological) 323 

beta synchrony, the relationship between synaptic currents and the LFP is substantially 324 

strengthened. 325 

Together, these results reveal a fundamental divergence in how  extracellular signals  reflect 326 

population activity across cortical and subcortical circuits, calling for a circuit -specific framework 327 

for interpreting field potentials. 328

Comparison with previous modeling studies 330 

Previous computational studies of STN LFPs have typically extended cortical models, assuming a 331 

direct, geometry -independent mapping between population synaptic currents and LFPs, modeled 332 

as their summed network activity 43 –45 . Our results challenge this assumption by showing that 333 

neuronal morphology, spatial organization, and recording  positioning  strongly influence STN 334 

ECPs . Other studies have incorporated detailed STN morphology and detailed DBS electrode 335 

modeling 25,26,46,47 . In comparison, our model focuses on a more systematic characterization of 336 

synaptic mechanisms, including dynamics, conductance values, and synapse number per neuron, 337 

while exploring how different cortical and pallidal input configurations shape STN extracellular 338 

signals. In this sense, our work is complementary to previous approaches: whereas prior studies 339 

have primarily addressed electrode properties and the effects of DBS, we investigate the 340 

relationship between spiking activity, synaptic dynamics, and extracellular fields in the STN. 341 

Importantly, our results delineate conditions under which STN LFPs do not reliably reflect 342 

underlying population synaptic activity, providing a theoretical framework to guide the 343 

interpretation of STN recordings. 344

STN LFPs do not directly reflect population synaptic activity 360 

Standard population descriptors that successfully approximate cortical LFPs 8,11,52,53 , including 361 

synaptic currents, firing rates, and average membrane potentials, fail to capture STN LFPs. Our 362 

results suggest that this limitation arises from how individual neuronal contributions combine to 363 

generate the population field. In cortical circuits, correlated synaptic inputs and aligned current 364 

dipoles promote constructive interference, establishing a robust relationship between extracellular 365 

signals and population -level variables. As increasingly larger neuronal populations contribute to 366 

the field, the correspondence between synaptic currents and LFPs improves. 367 

In the STN, however, sparse recurrent connectivity and heterogeneous cortical and pallidal 368 

inputs 54,55  produce weak correlations between the extracellular contributions of individual neurons. 369 

Their summation therefore promotes cancellation rather than amplification, preventing reliable 370 

recovery of the underlying population activity from the LFP. 371 

Additionally, our simulations reveal a strong dependence of STN LFPs on  electrodes placement .372 

Identical population activity can generate markedly different extracellular signals depending on 373 

the spatial arrangement of neurons relative to the recording contact. This observation has important 374 

implications for DBS, where electrode position varies both across patients and across contacts on 375 

the same lead 56,57 . Consequently, differences in LFP amplitude or spectral features may reflect 376 

recording geometry rather than underlying physiological differences, warranting caution when 377 

interpreting these signals in adaptive DBS applications 18,58 .378 

379 

Beta oscillations  and aperiodic slope  restore interpretability  of  local  field  potentials 380 

Although asynchronous STN activity produces cancellation -prone signals, beta oscillations 381 

fundamentally alter this scenario. By introducing correlated synaptic drive, beta -oscillating inputs 382 

restore a strong linear relationship between synaptic currents and the extracellular signal.  Under 383 

these  conditions, LFPs  provide a faithful readout of  synchronized subthreshold dynamics. 384 

The aperiodic slope of the STN power spectrum has also emerged as a clinically relevant 385 

biomarker in PD 40,59,60 . Our results provide a mechanistic explanation for both its baseline value 386 

and its state dependence. The relatively shallower STN slope with respect to cortical recordings 41

arises directly from neuronal morphology and can be predicted from the degree of morphological 388 

asymmetry of the neurons generating the field potentials. In addition, we found the slope to reflect 389

Conclusions 394 

These findings argue against a universal interpretation of LFP . The relationship between synaptic 395 

activity and extracellular fields critically depends on neuronal morphology, synchrony, and 396 

recording geometry. In cortex, these factors support a faithful representation of synaptic dynamics. 397 

In the STN, they instead lead to cancellation, strong geometry dependence,  and a weak and indirect 398 

relationship to population activity. Consequently, STN LFPs should not be interpreted as a direct 399 

readout of neuronal population activity in the same sense as cortical LFPs, but rather as signals 400 

whose meaning is strongly context dependent . This redefines how subcortical extracellular signals 401 

should be interpreted and sets constraints for the development  of  adaptive  DBS biomarkers. 402

STN single neuron 406 

To construct the subthalamic nucleus  population model, we first developed a biophysically 407 

detailed single -neuron model (Fig. 1). We adopted a previously validated morphological and 408 

electrophysiological model of an STN neuron 28  and extended it by incorporating excitatory cortical 409 

and inhibitory pallidal synaptic inputs.  Synaptic inputs were spatially distributed according to 410 

anatomical evidence 25 : inhibitory pallidal synapses were placed proximally (<100 μm from the 411 

soma), whereas excitatory cortical synapses were distributed across more distal dendritic 412 

compartments (Fig. 1A). Pallidal synapses were modeled with rise and decay time constants of 1.1 413 

ms and 7.8 ms, respectively  62,63 , whereas cortical synapses exhibited faster kinetics, with a rise 414 

time of 0.1 ms and a decay time of 3 ms 64 . Reversal potentials were set to −80 mV for inhibitory 415 

synapses 65  and 0 mV for excitatory synapses. 416 

Synaptic conductances were calibrated to reproduce experimentally observed postsynaptic 417 

currents and physiologically realistic firing dynamics. To constrain synaptic efficacy, we 418 

performed in silico voltage -clamp experiments  (Fig. 1B) . The somatic membrane potential was 419 

held at −70 mV for excitatory inputs 30  and −80 mV for inhibitory inputs 29 , consistent with  the 420 

experimental protocols. For each synapse type, single synaptic events were delivered at 200 421 

randomly sampled locations drawn from the prescribed spatial distributions, and the resulting 422 

postsynaptic currents were recorded. The amplitude distributions of excitatory and inhibitory 423 

currents were then compared with experimentally reported ranges 29,30  (Fig. 1B). Because pallidal 424 

synapses exhibit strong short -term depression, inhibitory conductances were tuned using their 425 

experimental  steady -state synaptic efficacy rather than peak transient amplitudes .426 

The model was subsequently extended to include full synaptic populations. The number of pallidal 427 

afferents was set to 883 based on anatomical estimates 66  whereas cortical inputs were included 428 

using an 8:3 ratio relative to pallidal inputs, consistent with previous computational stud y67 .429

Decoupling of synaptic and spiking contributions to local field potentials 488 

We disentangled the contributions of spiking and synaptic activity to the local field potential using 489 

two independent approaches  (Fig.  2F ). In both cases, we started from the complete network 490 

simulation and computed the LFP as the sum of the contributions from all individual neurons. To 491 

quantify the contribution of each component to the total LFP, we computed the coefficient of 492 

determination (R 2, computed as squared correlation coefficient) between the reconstructed signals 493 

and the LFP obtained from the complete simulation.  To isolate the contribution of synaptic activity 494 

(Fig. 2F, left) , we replayed the same synaptic inputs from the complete simulation onto the same 495 

neuronal population, while removing the active membrane mechanisms responsible for action 496 

potential generation (i.e., fast sodium channels, persistent sodium channels, delayed rectifier 497 

potassium channels, Kv3.1 potassium channels, T -type calcium channels , high -voltage -activated 498 

calcium channels, small -conductance calcium -activated potassium channels, hyperpolarization -499 

activated cation channels, intracellular calcium accumulation mechanisms). This procedure 500 

preserved synaptic currents while preventing neurons from producing spikes. 501 

To estimate the contribution of spiking activity  (Fig. 2F, right) , we first extracted spike times from 502 

the full network simulation by thresholding the membrane potential at −10 mV. We then computed 503 

extracellular action potential templates for each neuron at the electrode position, preserving the 504 

original neuronal positions and rotations used in the  full  population simulation. Finally, we 505 

convolved each neuron’s spike train with its corresponding extracellular template to reconstruct 506 

the single -neuron contributions to the LFP. 507

Cortical model 509 

Cortical extracellular field potentials were simulated using the simplified ball -and -stick 510 

microcircuit model  75  (Fig. 3A ).  Excitatory and inhibitory neurons were represented by stylized 511

dynamics. The network received external Poisson -distributed excitatory inputs and included 517

Detailed descriptions of channel kinetics, connectivity, and model parameters are provided in  75 .519 

Simulations generated two principal outputs: extracellular potential estimates and synaptic 520
