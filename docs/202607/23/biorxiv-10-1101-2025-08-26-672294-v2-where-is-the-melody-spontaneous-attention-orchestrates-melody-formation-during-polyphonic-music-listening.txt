# Abstract 45 

Humans seamlessly process multi -voice music into a coherent perceptual whole. Yet the 46 

neural strategies supporting this experience remain unclear. One fundamental component of 47 

this process is the formation of melody, a core structural element of music. Previous work on 48 

monophonic listening has provided strong evidence for the neurophysiological basis of 49 

melody processing, for example indicating predictive processing as a foundational 50 

mechanism underlying melody encoding. However, considerable uncertainty remains about 51 

how melodies are formed during polyphonic music listening, as existing theories (e.g., 52 

divided attention, figure –ground model, stream integration) fail to unify the full range of 53 

empirical findings. Here, we combined behavio ural measures with non -invasive 54 

electroencephalography (EEG) to probe spontaneous attentional bias and melodic 55 

expectation while participants listened to two -voice classical excerpts. Our uninstructed 56 

listening paradigm eliminated a major experimental constraint, creating a more ecologically 57 

valid setting. We found that attention bias was significantly influenced by both the high -voice 58 

superiority effect and intrinsic melodic statistics. We then employed transformer -based 59 

models to generate next -note expectation profiles and test competing theories of polyphonic 60 

perception. Drawing on our findings, we propose a weighted ‑integration framework in which 61 

attentional bias calibrates the overall degree of integration of the competing streams. In 62 

doing so, the proposed framework reconciles previous divergent accounts by showing that, 63 

even under free ‑listening conditions, melodies emerge through an attention -guided statistical 64 

integration mechanism. 65

# Introduction 66 

Music from many cultures and traditions, including Western tonal music, typically leads to the 67 

perception of melody, which is a monophonic sequence of notes that a listener would sing, 68 

whistle, or hum back to identify the music (1, 2). While certain types of music are monophonic 69 

by nature (e.g., Sean -nós singing, nursery rhymes), leaving little doubt as to what the melody 70 

is, music typically involves multiple sound streams, or voices. A variety of factors, some 71 

acoustic (e.g., the particular instrument used, pitch distance) and others musical (e.g., 72 

meaningful melodic progressions), have been identified as key contributors to the perception 73 

of distinct sound streams in music. However, there remains considerable uncertainty on how 74 

those streams are processed by our brain into a coherent whole; and while melody has been 75 

proposed as a key structural element of music (3), the neural strategies underpinning its 76 

emergence from multi -stream compositions remains unclear. 77 

While many theories have been proposed, one point of agreement is that attention 78 

mechanisms have a main role in polyphonic music perception. Much of the debate has centred 79 

around whether our brains are capable of dividing auditory attention across multiple auditory 80 

streams. In contrast with the widely studied multi -talker speech scenario, where attention can 81 

only be directed to one speaker or conversation at a time (4, 5), music is built so that distinct 82 

streams have strong relationships (e.g., harmonic) that can make them be perceived as 83 

coherent elements of a unified percept. Theories of polyphonic music perception have been 84 

formulated, for example explaining this phenomenon as the result of a divided attention 85 

between the streams (6, 7). A second view proposes that the attentional focus is directed to a 86 

single “foreground” stream, while also processing its harmonic relationship with the 87 

“background” (figure -ground model (8, 9)). As music unfolds, listeners may switch their 88 

attention to different streams, changing what is regarded as foreground and background. A 89 

third view proposes that our brains merge multiple music streams into a single complex 90 

melody, which then becomes the focus of attention (integration model (9)). 91 

Tailored experiments have been used to test the validity of these models, often involving tasks 92 

such as error detection in which participants are asked to click a button when they hear 93 

anomalies in pitch, rhythm, or harmony (10). That work led to a wealth of knowledge on what 94 

our brains “can” do. However, these tailored tasks come at a cost, such as imposing tasks 95 

leading to neural processing strategies different from uninstructed listening. For example, error 96 

detection tasks may include the explicit instruction of focussing on one or all streams, leading 97 

to processing strategies (e.g., selective attention behaviour, rapid attention switching) that 98 

might or might not be employed during uninstructed listening. As such, while that work can 99 

certainly inform us on what our brains are capable of doing, it is less clear if that reflects the 100 

typical neural functioning during uninstructed listening. 101 

Here, we investigate how the human brain processes two -stream polyphonic music during 102 

uninstructed listening by combining a behavioural task and non -invasive 103 

electroencephalography measurements (EEG). Participants were presented with classical 104 

music pieces synthesised with a high -quality virtual piano instrument, where each of the two 105

Where is the attention? 118 

First, we decode the attention bias in the listeners’ brains in the polyphonic listening conditions. 119 

In doing so, we answer the question: Where is the attended melody? To that end, we derived 120 

two attention bias scores. The first score is behavioural, and it reflects which of the two sound 121 

streams the listener sung back during a listen and repeat task in a dedicated block 122 

(behavioural attention bias score; Figure 1 ). The second score was derived from the EEG 123 

signal through an attention decoding procedure (neural attention bias score). Recent 124 

methodological developments provide us with tools to carry out that decoding from EEG 125 

signals based on regression methods (15). Specifically, we fit attention decoders on the EEG 126 

data recorded during the monophonic condition, which contained melodies selected from high 127 

and low pitch streams, and main and support streams. That way, the resulting decoders were 128 

unbiased with regard to pitch height and voice statistics, and were then applied to decode 129 

attention on the polyphonic conditions. 130 

We hypothesised both music streams to be robustly encoded in the human cortex during 131 

polyphonic music listening, primarily reflecting the cortical encoding of low -level acoustic 132 

properties (e.g., sound envelope). With regard to auditory attention, the high -voice superiority 133 

effect and melody statistics are the two possible contributors to salience – timbre was not a 134 

factor in this experiment, as the sound stimuli were synthesised using MIDI sounds from a 135 

single instrument (see Methods ). In the PolyOrig condition, both contributors were expected 136 

to primarily steer attention toward the high pitch stream by construction. Avoiding 137 

generalisations, the melodic material that characterises the main motif or primary thematic 138 

element, which is distinguished by its more intriguing and expressive features, is often 139 

constructed in the more exposed higher registers. For good practice, the main motif is 140 

supported by a harmonic and rhythmic structure constructed in order to enhance the attention 141 

on the main thematic material. 142 

We designed the PolyInv condition to disentangle the two key contributors to saliency – high -143 

voice superiority and melody statistics – by placing the main motif in the low -pitch voice. The 144 

pieces in this experiment, primarily double -counterpoint compositions, were selected 145 

specifically as the inversion of voices does not compromise their structural integrity. For 146

What is the melody? 157 

After having established the attention bias of each participant and music piece, we present a 158 

second analysis to determine how the perceived melody is built by our brains. To that end, we 159 

rely on the known sensitivity of EEG signals to melodic expectations (10, 16 -19). A music note 160 

may be more or less expected based on its prior context. Monophonic music listening leaves 161 

little doubt as to what that local context is, and models have been built that estimate next -note 162 

expectations, for example based on variable -order Markov Chains (20) and deep -learning 163 

architectures (21 -23). Here, we use state -of -the -art transformer -based computational models 164 

of music to generate numerical hypotheses for our brains’ next -note expectations, matching 165 

different theories of polyphonic music perception. We then relate these simulations with neural 166 

signals recorded during the polyphonic conditions, determining which music processing 167 

strategy is most neurophysiologically plausible for uninstructed listening, and how attention 168 

bias relates with the selected strategy. 169 

We considered a divided attention (6) and an integration model (9). The divided attention 170 

model would predict distinct melodic surprise for the two polyphonic streams, with equal 171 

strength. Measuring different strengths, in this two -stream listening scenario, would instead 172 

be compatible with a figure -ground model (7, 9), where the listening focus is directed to one 173 

particular stream (horizontal melody), which may alternate as the music unfolds, while the 174 

other is processed in function of the attended stream (vertical harmony). The other possibility 175 

that we considered was the integration model, according to which next -note expectations 176 

would be based on a single melody combining the two streams. Stream integration is in line 177 

with previous findings (9, 24) and compatible with the intuition that our vocal tract can only 178 

produce monophonic sequences. An auditory -motor neural pathway, condensing the auditory 179 

input into monophonic vocal motor commands, would be in line with the definition of melody 180 

itself (i.e., a monophonic sequence that a listener would hum back after hearing a music 181 

piece). Note that integration is not in contrast with the processing of other properties, such as 182 

vertical harmony, for example via a distinct neural pathway. 183 

With that premise, our analysis aims to arbitrate among those three models. Measuring 184 

differences in the melody expectation encoding for the two streams in PolyOrig would contrast 185 

with the divided attention model. With regard to PolyInv, we had distinct hypotheses for the 186 

other two models. Specifically, the Figure -ground model PolyInv to impair the integration 187

# Results 212 

Neural signals were recorded with EEG from 31 participants (16 males) during the 213 

uninstructed listening of polyphonic compositions or their monophonic components ( listening 214 

block; Fig. 1A ). The listening block involved the listening of monophonic and polyphonic 215 

pieces. In the second part of the experiment, participants undertook a melody identification 216 

task in a dedicated block, where short polyphonic music segments (~4 -8 seconds) were 217 

presented to the participants, who were asked to sing back the melody, and to indicate if they 218 

sung the high or low pitch stream on a five -point scale (see Methods ). The analyses that 219 

follow integrate behavio ural ( Fig. 1B ) and neural data ( Fig. 1C ) to address the following key 220 

questions: (a) Where was the focus of attention, and what factors influenced it? (b) What 221 

melody is encoded in the human brain during uninstructed listening to polyphonic music? 222

Where is the attention? Investigating attention bias and its contributors using 224 

behavioural and neural measurements 225 

In PolyOrig, the listeners’ attention was expected to focus on the high pitch stream by 226 

construction. PolyInv was built to disentangle the high -pitch superiority effect and the motif 227 

attractiveness. Measuring a strong attention bias toward the high -pitch stream in PolyInv 228 

would reflect a dominance of the high -pitch superiority effect (Hp0). Conversely, an attention 229 

bias toward the low -pitch stream in PolyInv would indicate a dominance of the motif 230 

attractiveness (Hp2). Our expectation was that both factors would contribute to the attention 231 

bias after the pitch inversion, thus leading to a reduced attention bias compared with PolyOrig 232 

(Hp1; Fig. 2A ). 233

pitch bias ( t-test, p = 6.6*10 -4, d = 0.80). Crucially, while the attention bias remained toward 236 

the high -pitch voice, its magnitude decreased with the voice inversion (paired t-test: p = 1.2*10 -

Next, we tested if the neural data also pointed to the same hypothesis. Neural measurements 239 

of attention bias were derived via an attention decoding analysis. Attention decoders were fit 240 

on the low -frequency EEG data (1 -8 Hz) from the monophonic condition using lagged ridge 241 

regression, with a methodology referred to as backward temporal response function (TRF; 242 

(25 -27)). The model fit identifies a linear combination of all EEG channels that produces an 243 

optimal reconstruction of the sound envelope. Since the monophonic listening condition only 244 

involved one music stream, participants could only attend to that stream, meaning that the 245 

resulting TRF model serves as an attention decoder. Sound envelope reconstructions were 246 

derived using that model on the EEG data from the polyphonic conditions. Pearson’s 247 

correlations were calculated between the reconstructed signal and the envelope of both 248 

polyphonic streams. The neural attention bias index was derived as the difference between 249 

those correlations ( Fig. 1C ). 250

What is the melody? Investigating melody formation by probing melodic prediction 290 

mechanisms in the listener’s brain 291 

When asked to sing back a polyphonic piece, listeners produce a melody that is either one of 292 

the streams (segregation) or a mixture of the two (integration). The analysis that follows aimed 293 

at determining what the listeners’ brain regards as the melody. To that end, we relied on 294 

measurements of melodic expectations, which were previously validated in the context of 295 

monophonic music listening (16, 28, 29). Three models in the literature would predict different 296 

outcomes for this analysis: (a) the divided attention model predicts a simultaneous encoding 297 

of melodic expectations calculated on two streams as independent monophonic streams; (b) 298 

the figure -ground model would predict the encoding of melodic expectations for the main motif; 299 

and (c) the integration model would correspond to the encoding of melodic expectations for a 300 

melody that combines the two streams. Here, estimates of melodic expectations (surprise and 301 

entropy) for each melody stream were derived from an Anticipatory Music Transformer (23). 302 

As a first step, we obtained a neural measurement of melodic expectation encoding on the 303 

monophonic condition, aiming to replicate previous findings in the literature (16, 30). The 304 

mapping between stimulus features and EEG is multivariate -to -multivariate, making methods 305 

such as the TRF (which is multivariate -to -univariate) suboptimal. Here we used Canonical 306 

Correlation Analysis (CCA) instead, because it accounts for all stimulus features and all EEG 307 

channels simultaneously. When comparing two expectation models, the difference in 308 

explained variance is typically very small, with the two models capturing essentially the same 309 

time -latencies and neural sources. As such, we opted for the method with the highest 310 

sensitivity in terms of explained variance, at the cost of model interpretability. 311 

We measured match -vs -mismatch classification (Fig. 3A; (31, 32)), which informs on the EEG 312 

sensitivity to the music features, where higher classification scores indicat e a stronger 313 

encoding of a given stimulus feature -set. Here, we compared the CCA mapping between two 314 

feature sets, one including only acoustic features ( A: note onset, envelope, envelope 315 

derivative) and the other combining acoustic and melodic expectation features ( AM , where M 316 

includes note onsets amplitude -modulated by pitch surprise and note onsets amplitude -317 

modulated by pitch entropy for all streams in the stimulus). CCA models were fit for A and AM 318 

separately. As the match -vs -mismatch evaluation is a randomised procedure , we run 250 319 

repetitions of that procedure to obtain a distribution of the participants average classification 320 

score for each model (A and AM) . A statistically significant cortical encoding of melodic 321 

expectations was measured in the monophonic condition (AM > A, Wilcoxon rank sum: p <322 

10 -30 ; Fig. 3B -top ), consistent with previous EEG, MEG, and intracranial EEG work with Music 323 

Transformers and Markov models (16). 324

using the monophonic music transformer ( Fig. 3A, B ). A statistically significant encoding of 326

responses to PolyInv stimuli than the monophonic model, which is in line with our hypothesis. 349

# Discussion 377 

This study investigated the neural processing of melody during polyphonic music listening. 378 

Previous research indicated that listeners can detect changes or errors in simultaneous music 379 

streams in some contexts (6, 8) but not others (9), challenging the possibility that our brains 380 

can truly divide attention between simultaneous melodic streams. Theories like the figure -381 

ground model and stream integration (9) have been proposed as perceptual strategies that 382 

can compensate for the difficulty of truly dividing attention. While that work offers precious 383 

insights into what our brains “can” do, the tasks used previously for that research alter the 384 

listening experience, raising doubts with regard to how those models impact uninstructed 385 

listening. Here, we fill that gap by measuring the spontaneous attention bias in the listeners’ 386 

brains during the uninstructed listening of polyphonic music. First, we found that salience is 387 

affected by both the high -voice superiority effect and the statistical properties of the melodic 388 

lines ( Fig. 2 ), providing a quantitative approach to disentangle the two effects. Second, we 389 

provide evidence for a weighted integration strategy, with the attention bias altering the stream 390 

integration rate ( Fig. 3 ). Altogether, these results shed new light on the processing of melody 391 

during the uninstructed listening of polyphonic music, pointing to an inter -relationship between 392 

attention and statistical processing mechanisms, with attention orchestrating the way melody 393 

is formed. 394 

395 

Investigating the contributors to attention during uninstructed polyphonic music 396 

listening 397 

Attention is potentially the key mechanism for understanding how melody is processed during 398 

polyphonic music listening. To that end, we designed a paradigm where the attention focus 399 

would change across conditions, without the need for instructing the participants on where to 400 

direct their attentional focus. We opted for using double -counterpoint compositions involving 401 

two monophonic piano melodic lines, primarily from J.S. Bach ( Table 1 ). As discussed by P.A. 402 

Scholes, such compositions involve melodic lines that “move in apparent independence and 403 

freedom though fitting together harmonically” (33), creating a scenario where both streams 404 

present attractive melodic progressions. As a counter example, let’s consider a scenario 405 

involving polyphonic music with two streams, where the support stream has infrequent notes 406 

with little melodic variation. That scenario would make the pitch -line inversion less effective, 407 

as the new high -pitch stream would not stand on its own as the main melody. 408 

Previous studies demonstrated that the focus of selective auditory attention can be reliably 409 

decoded from EEG signals during sustained attention tasks (27) and, more recently, by 410 

studying instructed attention switches (34, 35). While that work was initially carried out on 411 

speech listening tasks in multi -talker scenarios (27, 36, 37), EEG attention decoding was also 412 

shown to be effective when considering polyphonic music during instructed selective attention 413 

tasks (15). Hausfeld and colleagues (38) went one step beyond by comparing an instructed 414 

selective attention task, where attention was steered to a given music stream, with a divided 415 

attention task, where participants were asked to focus on two music streams simultaneously. 416 

EEG attention decoding showed a preference toward the attended stream in the former, while 417

no preference was measured in the latter task. Altogether, those results strongly support the 418 

reliability of decoding attention from EEG. However, that work focused on instructed listening, 419 

thus not informing us on how polyphonic music listening unfolds naturally. Other design 420 

choices further complicate the interpretation of those results, such as the use of different 421 

instruments for the distinct streams, which introduce another possible bias that, although 422 

interesting, was considered unnecessary to answer the research question in the present 423 

investigation. For that reason, our experiment only included virtual piano emulations 424 

generated from MIDI scores. 425 

Our attention decoding analysis indicates that both high -pitch and melody statistics contribute 426 

to music salience. The present study teases apart two key factors influencing uninstructed 427 

attention by utilising two conditions involving the same type of music, with the sole difference 428 

that the pitch lines were inverted. Using behavioural ( Fig. 2B ) and EEG decoding indices ( Fig. 429 

2D ), we found that the listeners’ steer their attention toward the high -pitch stream in both 430 

conditions, even though there was a substantially weaker bias in PolyInv. These results, 431 

together with the forward TRF results ( Fig. 2E ), are in line with our initial hypothesis that two 432 

key contributors are at play, and that the high -voice superiority effect is, in this case, stronger 433 

than the bias due to the melody attractiveness. It should be noted that our EEG results refer 434 

to the average attention bias throughout a piece, and that the behavioural index is only derived 435 

on a subsample of the music material. As such, that result would be compatible with different 436 

attention dynamics, such as a weaker attention bias throughout the piece, an increase in 437 

attention -switching between the voices, or their integration. Further to this , neural and 438 

behavioural measurement of attention bias were derived on different tasks, involving long 439 

(~ 1.5 minutes) and short (~4 -8 seconds) music segments, with the latter happening as the 440 

second stage of the experiment, hence involving portions of stimuli that were heard in the first 441 

stage. As such, while we found consistent neural and behavioural attention bias 442 

measurements on average, further research should assess if differences in stimulus length, 443 

repetition, and task might affect the attention bias at more fine -grained levels (e.g., single 444 

participant and music piece). 445 

Another limitation was the focus on a single music culture and style, which was a design choice 446 

allowing us to compare attention bias across two conditions that were minimally different. 447 

Indeed, different cultures are characterised by unique musical features. For example, some 448 

traditions employ distinct systems of note organi sation, such as Makams , which incorporate 449 

additional pitches like quarter tones — which are not commonly found in Western music, 450 

except in certain avant -garde applications. Likewise, the inclusion of different musical styles 451 

would introduce varying melodic features, as they are constructed using diverse techniques. 452 

Future research should consider generalising the results of this research using a more diverse 453 

set of stimuli that can capture these interesting variabilities across music style and culture, as 454 

well as investigating the impact of other related factors, such as subjective biases (e.g., 455 

personal taste) and musical training. 456 

This experiment involved two -stream stimuli where the main melody is primarily on the high 457 

pitch line, while both streams could stand on their own. The pitch inversion in PolyInv allowed 458 

us to study the neural encoding of music in conditions with different attention biases, while 459

lines results in an inversion of the voicing, which affects the perception of the sound but not 461

interchangeability, avoiding harmonic issues. Since a chord is typically defined by three notes, 463 

the absence of a third voice does not compromise harmonic clarity. In particular, many of these 464

the lines are inverted, the resulting music retains coherence and stability, despite the absence 469

results of this study to other music styles requires further work, our choice of using double -471 

counterpoint pieces enabled manipulations that were key for testing our hypotheses. 472

Stream integration mechanisms contribute to melody formation 474 

Numerous studies reported that neural responses to monophonic melodies reflect the 475 

expectation of a note, leading to patterns of neural activations that are compatible with a 476 

Bayesian view of the human brain, fitting frameworks such as predictive processing ((39); but 477 

see (21)). However, the relevance of those findings to the uninstructed listening of polyphonic 478 

music processing remains unclear. Listeners can identify a music piece by singing back its 479 

melody, indicating that part of polyphonic music processing involves transforming the 480 

incoming sound into a monophonic melody. This melody might correspond to a given stream 481 

or result from the integration of two or more streams. Previous work proposed theories on how 482 

that melody might be extracted. Here, we worked under the assumption that our brains 483 

process the resulting melody according to the predictive processing framework, where melodic 484 

expectations have been shown to be measurable with EEG (16). Using music transformers, 485 

we built numerical hypotheses on those melodic expectations, in one case assuming the 486 

processing of each monophonic stream (monophonic model), while the other model 487 

implements an integration of the two polyphonic streams (polyphonic model; Fig. 3 ). 488 

Differently from existing theories, our results suggest that the human brain processes multiple 489 

music streams via a dynamic strategy. Specifically, a strong attention bias toward a dominant 490 

stream corresponded with a melody processing consistent with a monophonic processing of 491 

that stream. A weak attention bias, instead, corresponded with an integration of the two 492 

streams , as evidenced by a stronger alignment with the polyphonic model of melody 493 

expectations. In light of this result, we propose a weighted -integration model , in which attention 494 

modulates how strongly the two streams are integrated : with a strong bias, next -note 495 

prediction for the main stream are effectively monophonic (no integration); with a weaker bias, 496 

the support stream contributes proportionally (weighted integration) . It should be noted that 497 

our results only speak to melody formation and melody prediction , and they do not address 498 

the important issue of how support stream s are processed . While the figure -ground model 499

offers a possible explanation , our data does not address that question nor exclude other 500 

possibilities, such as the divided attention model or one of its variations. 501 

With the premise that attention might orchestrate music stream integration, one might observe 502 

that PolyOrig is more closely related with everyday listening of, for example, Western music, 503 

which typically involve s a dominant melody. As a result, everyday music listening might be 504 

consistent with how the corresponding melodies would be processed in a monophonic context. 505 

This is a tantalising possibility, as it would make previous findings on monophonic melody 506 

perception directly relevant to more typical everyday multi -stream music listening. It should 507 

also be noted that our results involved averaging attention bias measurements throughout 508 

entire music pieces, while that bias likely changes within each piece. As such, the rate of 509 

integration might change dynamically at a more fine -grained level than when assessed here, 510 

calling for further investigation. 511 

Our findings complement the past literature on simultaneous pitch sequences. Previous work 512 

using odd -ball paradigm s measured mismatch -negativity responses to deviant notes in high 513 

or low pitch streams (40). That line of work , however, involves simplified paradigms including 514 

constraints making the task different from the typical music listening experience . We are 515 

especially referring to the use of deviant notes, very short stimuli, and isochronous note timing. 516 

Indeed, such previous work is valid and offers solid evidence of what our brains can do under 517 

those conditions. Our goal here was, instead, to determine how our brains operate in 518 

conditions one step closer to realistic music listening . As a result, we provide data confirm ing 519 

that melodic expectations can be measured in listening scenarios involving two -voice 520 

compositions , with results indicating that stream integration can naturally occur in 521 

correspondence of a weak attention bias. 522 

Conclusions 523 

Additional research is necessary to determine if the inter -relationship between attention and 524 

melody formation is specific to this specific music style or if that is a general mechanism. Our 525 

methodology could be replicated on two -stream polyphonic music from other styles and 526 

cultures, with one caveat: the choice of an appropriate computational model of music must 527 

take into account the music culture or style of the stimuli and listener. That might constitute a 528 

limiting factor for under -represented music styles, even though the rapid developments in 529 

music transformer research are promising in that regard and may close that gap in the near 530 

future. Further work should also explore factors such as repetition, musical training, and music 531 

preference, which are expected to be particularly important for uninstructed listening tasks. 532 

Repetition, for example, may alter the attention bias and has been put forward as a possible 533 

way for disentangling the figure -ground and integration accounts of polyphonic music 534 

processing (9). With regard to musical training, while our results did not show any effects, our 535 

definition of musical training was quite varied in this sample. Future work aiming to shed light 536 

on that specific phenomenon should consider larger and more homogeneous samples. 537 

In sum, we demonstrate that polyphonic music processing can be studied with EEG with an 538 

uninstructed listening task. Our findings include novel insights into the inter -relationship 539 

between attention and melody statistical processing. Based on these results, we propose an 540

Participants 556 

40 participants were recruited for this study. Data from nine participants were excluded due to 557 

technical issues with the synchronisation triggers in the EEG recording , leading to a dataset 558 

with 31 participants overall (16 males, aged between 19 and 30, mean = 23. 2, std = 2.3), 8 of 559 

whom have had formal musical training or at least two years of professional musical 560 

experience. All participants gave their written informed consent to participate in this study. The 561 

study was undertaken in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by 562 

the ethics committee in the School of Psychology of Trinity College Dublin. Data collection 563 

was carried out between September 2022 and January 2024. 564

Experimental design 566 

Testing took place in a dark room. The experiment was organised into two experimental 567 

blocks: a listening block and a melody identification block (Figure 1), collecting EEG and 568 

behavioural measurements respectively. First, participants were presented to monophonic 569 

and polyphonic music pieces, with the sole instruction of listening to the music while looking 570 

at a fixation cross and minimising motor movement. After the listening block, the second part 571 

of the experiment involved a melody identification task, aided by a listen and repeat exercise. 572 

Specifically, participants were presented with short (4 -8 seconds) segments of polyphonic 573 

music, extracted from previously presented PolyOrig or PolyInv pieces. After hearing a 574 

segment, participants were asked to sing it back. Then, they were presented with the two 575 

melodies as separate monophonic pieces with the order randomised and asked: “Which of the 576 

2 melodies were you attempting to sing? 1: only the first melody; 2: mostly the first melody; 3: 577 

an equal mix of both; 4: mostly the second melody; 5: only the second melody”. EEG signals 578 

were not recorded during the second block and the recorded singing was not analysed. 579 

Importantly, participants sang back the melody before being presented with the monophonic 580 

streams, encouraging them to base their selection on their own melody extraction during 581 

polyphonic listening and reducing potential task -related influences, including presentation 582 

bias. 583 

Neurobs Presentation software was used for coding the experiment 584 

(http://www.neurobs.com ), which was carried out in a single session for all participants. Audio 585 

stimuli were presented at a sampling rate of 44,100 Hz and played through Sennheiser HD 586 

280 Pro headphones. EEG data was simultaneously acquired with a sampling rate of 250 Hz, 587 

from twenty -four electrode positions using an mBrainTrain Smarting wireless system. 588

Canonical Correlation Analysis 644 

The comparison between the polyphonic expectation models was carried out using Canonical 645 

Correlation Analysis (CCA), which implements a multivariate -to -multivariate mapping between 646 

the stimulus features and the EEG signal. The main advantage over TRFs is that all stimulus 647 

feature s and EEG dimensions are related by applying linear transformations to both stimulus 648 

and EEG dimensions, projecting them onto a shared canonical subspace, leading to higher 649 

correlation values. 650 

In its more general definition, given two sets of multichannel data X and Y of size T × J 1 and 651 

T × J 2, CCA finds linear transformations of both that make them maximally correlated. 652 

Specifically, CCA produces the transformation matrices W 1 and W 2 (sizes J 1 × J 0 and J 2 × J 0,653 

where J 0 < min(J 1,J 2)) that maximise the correlation between pairs of columns of XW 1 and 654 

YW 2, while making the columns of each transformed data matrix mutually uncorrelated. The 655 

first pair of canonical components (CC) is the linear combination of X and Y with highest 656 

possible correlation. The next pair of CCs are the most highly correlated combinations 657 

orthogonal to the first, and so -on. When X and Y represent the stimulus features and neural 658 

data respectively, CCA captures their instantaneous relationship. As such, similar to the TRF 659 

analysis, CCA can be used to study time -lagged relationships i.e., where the neural reaction 660 

to a given sound input unfolds with a non -zero delay and duration. Previous research 661 

determined that this modelling approach is effective at measuring sound -EEG mappings, 662 

providing different ways to include time -lags in the CCA modelling. Here we have done so by 663 

including time -lagged versions of both stimulus and EEG in X and Y respectively (please refer 664 

to the CCA -2 implementation in (36) ), including a lag window of 0 to 500 ms. This leads to a 665 

substantial expansion of the X and Y matrices. To prevent overfitting, both X and Y were 666 

reduced to 24 dimensions via principal component analyses. The CCA results were evaluated 667 

with leave -one -out cross validation to control for overfitting, exploring input -output shifts over 668 

a window of -500 to 500ms. 669 

The neural encoding of a given set of features was evaluated with the match -vs -mismatch 670 

accuracy metric. Stimulus and EEG time -series are partitioned into 20 -second segments. 671 

Each segment is projected onto the canonical space. Correlations are calculated between the 672 

stimulus features and two EEG segments in the canonical space: the corresponding EEG 673 

segment (match) and a randomly selected one (mismatch). A binary classification accuracy 674 

score is derived by counting the proportion of match > mismatch correlations across all 675 

segments ( 31 ), providing a measurement of stimulus feature sensitivity for the EEG signal of 676 

a given participant. This metric is adopted in the analysis in Figure 3 to determine if 677

Anticipatory Music Transformer 680 

AMT is a pretrained causal transformer model for symbolic music. Transformer architectures 681 

internally build probability distribution functions for each upcoming note based on long term 682 

memory (training set) and short term memory (context). We used the stanford -crfm/music -683 

large -800k checkpoint, which was pretrained on large -scale music corpora including Lakh 684 

MIDI, MetaMidi, FMA transcriptions, and approximately 450,000 commercial recordings 685 

transcribed automatically. AMT represents symbolic music as sequentially ordered tokens 686 

encoding pitch and onset of each note individually and was trained to predict each upcoming 687 

token given the preceding context. Thus notes that occur simultaneously are encoded as 688 

separate MIDI events that share the same onset. The released model uses a context length 689 

of 1024 tokens. 690

Stimuli feature extraction 692 

Acoustic and melodic features were extracted from the audio files. Acoustic features included 693 

the sound envelope ( Env ), which was extracted from the Hilbert transform of the acoustic 694 

waveform. Second, we included the halfway rectified envelope derivative ( Env’ ), which was 695 

calculated on the Hilbert envelope at the original sampling rate (44,100 Hz), before 696 

downsampling both Env and Env’ . Melodic features were derived using AMT . The melodic 697 

features used were the surprise and entropy of the pitch of the notes ( S, H), extracted from 698 

the model's conditional probability mass function over the set of possible notes . Surprise is 699 

the information content of the note. 700
