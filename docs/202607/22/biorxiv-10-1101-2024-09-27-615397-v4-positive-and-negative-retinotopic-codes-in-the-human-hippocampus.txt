# Abstract 1

The hippocampus sits at the apex of the visual hierarchy, yet little is known about the visual 2

properties of this core memory structure. Recent work suggests that a latent, bivalent retinotopic 3

code persists in large-scale memory networks at the cortical apex, scaffolding interactions with 4

sensory networks. Here, we tested whether a bivalent retinotopic code also persists within the 5

hippocampus. To do this, we leveraged high-resolution 7T functional MRI along with voxel-scale 6

visual population receptive field (pRF) modeling in 7 densely-sampled individuals (5 female). 7

Our findings reveal a robust, voxel-scale retinotopic code broadly distributed across subfields 8

and along the long axis of the human hippocampus, comprised of roughly equal proportions of 9

pRFs with positive and negative amplitude responses to visual stimulation. Hippocampal pRFs 10 

displayed canonical visual properties, including stable valence and visual field preferences 11 

across runs and a contralateral bias. Retinotopic structure also persisted at rest: hippocampal 12 

voxels with similar pRF locations were more strongly correlated than voxels representing 13 

different visual field locations. Finally, across the ventral visual stream, the prevalence of 14 

negative-amplitude pRFs increased with mnemonic involvement, culminating in the balanced, 15 

bivalent organization within the hippocampus. These findings support the view that sensory and 16 

mnemonic systems are coupled through a shared retinotopic code at the apex of the visual 17 

hierarchy. 18 

# Significance Statement 19 

The hippocampus is closely coupled to the visual system, and recent work has challenged the 20

in the human hippocampus, consisting of both typical positive and atypical negative responses. 23

# Introduction 28 

The hippocampus serves as a critical hub for sensory-mnemonic interactions, integrating 29 

sensory information with memory processing to support the encoding and retrieval of episodic 30 

memories (Scoville and Milner, 1957; Cohen and Squire, 1980; Cohen and Eichenbaum, 1993). 31 

As such, hippocampal representations essentially rely on input from sensory systems, including 32 

the visual system (Vinogradova, 2001; Jeffery, 2007; Pereira et al., 2007). Yet relatively little is 33 

known about the visual coding properties of this core memory structure. 34 

Classic models of cortical organization place the hippocampus at the apex of the visual 35 

hierarchy (Felleman and Van Essen, 1991) where it is thought to construct abstract, internal 36 

models of the external world. Canonically, hippocampal coding schemes are thought to be 37 

primarily allocentric and context-dependent (Scoville and Milner, 1957; O’Keefe and Dostrovsky, 38 

1971; Cohen and Squire, 1980; Cohen and Eichenbaum, 1993; Eichenbaum and Cohen, 2014; 39 

Whittington et al., 2020), fundamentally distinct from sensory-driven retinotopic codes like those 40 

found in early visual areas (Sereno and Allman, 1991; Tootell et al., 1998). However, recent 41 

findings challenge this view. Work in rodents and nonhuman primates has shown that a 42 

significant proportion of hippocampal neurons exhibit egocentric visual coding (Acharya et al., 43 

2016; Gulli et al., 2020; Purandare et al., 2022), and human fMRI research has identified visual 44 

field preferences in hippocampal voxels (Knapen, 2021; Silson et al., 2021). Thus, contrary to 45 

the classic view, these cross-species findings suggest the hippocampus may demonstrate 46

egocentric visual coding, but evidence from cerebral cortex indicates these descriptions may be 47 

incomplete. 48 

Recent studies of retinotopic coding in memory-related cortical regions, like the Default 49 

Network (DN), have observed atypical retinotopic coding alongside classic mnemonic 50 

responses. Specifically, many of the DN’s retinotopic responses exhibit a negative valence — 51 

that is, decreasing activity in response to visual presentation (Szinte and Knapen, 2020; Klink et 52 

al., 2021; Knapen, 2021; Steel et al., 2024b) — in contrast to the typical positive valence found 53 

in visual areas. This bivalent (positive and negative) retinotopic code structures a voxel-scale 54 

push-pull dynamic between perceptual and mnemonic cortical regions and networks (Steel et 55 

al., 2024b, 2024a). One hypothesis is that a similar bivalent retinotopic code might exist in the 56 

hippocampus, given the structure’s well-characterized mnemonic properties and connectivity 57 

with the DN (Greicius et al., 2004; Vincent et al., 2006; Kahn et al., 2008). 58 

In this study, we used high-resolution 7T functional MRI from visual mapping tasks to test 59 

whether a bivalent retinotopic code is present within the human hippocampus. In brief, our 60 

results show that the human hippocampus contains a robust retinotopic code, with a balanced 61 

proportion of positive and negative population receptive fields (pRFs), evenly distributed 62 

throughout the hippocampus. Importantly, this code is reliable: pRF amplitude and valence are 63 

consistent across runs of the retinotopic mapping task. Both positive and negative pRFs 64 

demonstrate a bias towards the contralateral visual field, and we found the proportion of 65 

negative pRFs increases moving up the ventral visual stream hierarchy, parallelling mnemonic 66 

involvement. Finally, during resting-state fixation, retinotopic preferences strongly influence the 67 

correlation of activity among hippocampal pRFs, suggesting that this bivalent retinotopic code 68 

may be broadly relevant for mnemonic information processing in the hippocampus. 69

# Methods 71 

Overview 72 

We used data made publicly available through the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD, Allen et al., 73 

2022) that included 8 participants scanned extensively at 7T over the course of several days. 74 

The high resolution of the data allowed for detailed explorations of hippocampal activity. 75 

Relevant to the present study, each participant completed both three runs of sweeping bar 76 

retinotopic mapping task and at least 14 runs of a resting-state fixation task (range: 14 – 34 77 

runs). Retinotopy and fixation data were minimally preprocessed using manual ICA denoising, 78 

and all subsequent analyses were performed in participant-specific volume space. To allow for 79 

the identification of negative retinotopic responses, population receptive field (pRF) modeling 80 

was performed using AFNI’s mapping procedure (3dNLfim). We did not regress global signal, 81 

and data were left unsmoothed. 82

Task Paradigms 109 

The retinotopic mapping paradigm (adapted from the Human Connectome Project Retinotopy 110 

Dataset; Benson et al., 2018, RETBAR1 and RETBAR2) consisted of a bar aperture slowly 111 

moving across the visual field, revealing objects at multiple scales (i.e. faces, houses, and 112 

objects) superimposed on a pink-noise background (Fig. 1A). Both the apertures and textures 113 

updated at a rate of 15 Hz, and covered a circular region with a diameter of 8.4°. For each 300 114 

second run, participants were instructed to fixate on a small (0.2° x 0.2°) semi-transparent dot in 115 

the center of the screen, and indicate with a button press whenever the color of the dot changed 116 

(which occurred every 1 – 5 sec). Three runs of this task were collected, for a total of 15 117 

minutes of data. Additional wedge/ring runs were also acquired as part of the NSD, but 118

Quality Control and Preprocessing 128 

All data was assessed for quality prior to inclusion in the dataset as described in the original 129 

data release (Allen et al., 2022). Motivated by the sensitivity of resting-state functional 130 

connectivity to data quality, particularly motion, we performed additional quality control 131 

measures on the fixation data. We assessed the quality of resting-state fixation data using 132 

AFNI’s quality control report creation tool (APQC, Taylor et al., 2024), and excluded any fixation 133 

run with a maximum displacement of greater than 1.8 mm, or a framewise displacement greater 134 

than 0.12 mm. Runs with a maximum displacement between 1 mm and 1.8 mm, and a 135 

framewise displacement of between 0.1 mm and 0.12 mm, were assessed on a case-by-case 136 

basis, and excluded if the movement was the result of large shifts (approach similar to Du et al., 137 

2024). For functional connectivity analyses we removed the first 25 TRs (40s) of every fixation 138 

run, leaving 260 sec (4.25 min) of data, to remove artifacts associated with the instructed deep 139 

breath during the second fixation run in each session. After these exclusions, one participant 140 

(subj03) had only a single fixation run remaining (4.25 min), and so was excluded from the 141 

present study’s analyses. The other 7 participants had between 8 and 24 runs (mean: 14, sd: 142 

6.13, 34 – 102 min) of high-quality fixation data. 143

Retinotopic Mapping 156 

Population Receptive Field Modeling 157 

To perform population receptive field (pRF) modeling, we average all three denoised and 158 

normalized retinotopy runs together to produce an average retinotopy time series, to which we 159 

applied the AFNI modeling procedure using 3dNLfim as described in Silson et al. (2015). The 160 

model calculated 4 million possible 2D Gaussian receptive fields with a given center position 161 

and size based on the timing of the presented sweeping bar (resampled to the temporal 162 

resolution of the fMRI acquisition, TR = 1.333s). These ideal Gaussian responses were 163 

convolved with an a priori hemodynamic response function chosen to mirror a previous 164 

characterization of hippocampal activity during a visual oddball task (Angeli et al., 2025; peak 165 

activity at 4 s, FWHM ≈ 3.33 s). For each voxel, the best of these predicted time series was 166 

chosen after the application of a flexible amplitude scalar, using a combination of Simplex and 167 

Powell optimization algorithms, minimizing the least-squares error between the acquired BOLD 168

Reliability and Stability of pRF Modeling 174 

To establish the reliability of our pRF model in the hippocampus, we compared task-derived 175 

model fits to those of a control null distribution for each participant. To generate this control null 176 

distribution of model fits, we applied the retinotopic model to hippocampal activity during a task 177 

that would not be expected to have systematic retinotopic visual field stimulation: resting-state 178 

fixation. For each participant, we first averaged the time course of all resting-state fixation runs 179 

prior to trimming, which were matched in length to the retinotopy task runs (300s). Then, we 180 

applied the retinotopic model to the average hippocampal resting state activity to derive model 181 

fits for each hippocampal voxel. Critically, because participants did not see the sweeping bar 182 

stimulus during resting-state fixation, this produces a null distribution of model parameters. We 183 

used this null distribution to determine an empirically-derived noise floor within the 184 

hippocampus. This was important because previous pRF research has applied R 2 cutoffs based 185 

on data in visual areas, and it is not a given that the hippocampus would have a comparable 186 

noise-floor to visual areas. We chose a noise floor corresponding to the 95 th percentile R 2 value 187 

of the null model (0.115), and only considered voxels with fits that exceeded this floor to be 188 

retinotopic. For consistency, and because null fits remained largely stable across regions (Fig. 189 

S4), reliability estimates in cortical regions were calculated using the same noise floor. 190 

In addition to requiring fits above the noise floor, we conservatively screened voxels based on 191 

model estimates of size (sigma) and eccentricity, guided by the size of the presented stimulus 192 

(diameter 8.4°, bar width 1.05°). Specifically, we excluded any voxels with a size estimate 193

Correlation of Anatomical Location and Visual Field Location 254 

To explore the possibility that hippocampal retinotopy is organized in a sparse topographic 255 

map, we tested the relationship between hippocampal pRF’s preferred visual field location and 256 

that voxels distance from other significant hippocampal pRFs. To do so, we paired every 257 

hippocampal pRF with every other hippocampal pRF in the same hemisphere. We did both 258 

valence agnositic pairings (“All”) and valence-specific either within a valence (i.e. positive pRFs 259 

paired with only positive pRFs, and vice versa) or explicitly cross-valence (i.e. positive pRFs 260 

paired with only negative pRFs, and vice versa). For each pair, we then calculated the 261 

Euclidean distance between their pRF center positions and the Euclidian distance between their 262 

locations in the brain, and correlated those values across voxels within a participant. 263

Influence of Retinotopic Alignment 275 

To better understand the influence of retinotopic features beyond valence on functional 276 

connectivity, we investigated differences in functional connectivity between pairs of pRFs 277 

sensitive to either similar or different portions of the visual field, which we term retinotopic 278 

alignment. To assess the influence of retinotopic alignment within the hippocampus, we took 279 

each hippocampal pRF sequentially as a source and sorted hippocampal pRFs within the same 280 

hemisphere of the same or opposite valence according to the Euclidian distance between that 281 

pRF’s center and the source pRF center. We defined the pRF with the smallest difference in 282 

Euclidian distance to be retinotopically aligned (“Matched”), and randomly sampled 1 voxel from 283 

the 1/3 of voxels with the largest distance 1000 times to generate a group of non-aligned voxels 284 

(“Random”). We then calculated the run-level partial correlation between the source voxel, the 285 

matched voxel, and each of the random voxels, controlling for the average activity of non-286 

significant hippocampal voxels. Finally, these values were Fisher z-transformed, averaged 287 

across runs, and the random voxels further averaged to generate a single correlation value. 288 

After completing this process for every source voxel, we averaged the matched and random 289 

correlation values according to the valence of the source and target pRFs to generate 4 290 

participant-level estimates for retinotopic alignment: 1) matched, same valence 2) matched, 291 

opposite valence 3) random, same valence 4) random, opposite valence. 292

Statistical Tests 294 

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2). Specific tests are described in 295 

the relevant portions of the Results and Methods. In summary: run-level significance for 296 

retinotopic modeling R 2 was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test while other feature 297 

reliability measures (center position, valence) were z-scored relative to estimates sampled from 298 

all hippocampal voxels. We used a linear mixed effects model to assess the contribution of brain 299 

region to the proportion of −pRFs, controlling for mean R 2. All other analyses were assessed 300 

using t-tests. 301

# Results 303 

We first determined the presence of retinotopic coding in the human hippocampus. To do this, 304 

we performed voxel-scale population receptive field (pRF) modeling (Dumoulin and Wandell, 305 

2008, for implementation see Cox, 1996, 2012) on high-resolution 7T fMRI data from the 306 

hippocampus during a visual retinotopic mapping task (Fig. 1A). 307

applying the retinotopic model to hippocampal activity during independent runs of resting-state 310 

fixation data from the same participants (see Methods). Task-derived model fits were 311 

significantly better than the null model fits (within participants: paired t-test, t(6) = 4.023, p < 312 

0.01; across participants: asymptotic two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test, H a: task < null, D −

As an additional validation, we assessed whether the retinotopic features of hippocampal 358

showed strong reliability across identical runs (Fig. 2A). Across pRF voxels, both center position 360 

estimates (i.e., preferred visual field location) and response valence (i.e., positive vs. negative 361

To provide functional context for the hippocampal retinotopic code, we explored whether pRF 448 

prevalence and valence follows the pattern established by visual regions lower in the hierarchy 449 

— where −pRFs become more prevalent towards mnemonic regions at the cortical apex, in line 450 

with their putative functional role in mnemonic processing (Steel et al., 2021a, 2023, 2024b) 451 

(Fig. 4). In early visual cortex, 52.4% (se: 1.7%) of voxels are retinotopic and only 14.3% (se: 452 

3.1%) of those have a negative valence. Along the ventromedial visual pathway to the 453 

hippocampus (Rolls, 2025), the proportion of retinotopic voxels decreases while the proportion 454 

of negative voxels increases towards a maximum of 51.7% (se: 6.6%) in the hippocampus (Fig. 455 

4). Parahippocampal cortex is of particular interest because previous work has identified a 456 

functional split between the perceptual parahippocampal place area (PPA) and mnemonic 457 

ventral place memory area (vPMA), found in the posterior and anterior portions, respectively, of 458 

parahippocampal cortex (Steel et al. 2021a, 2023). Mirroring this functional divide, 17.4% of 459 

pRFs in the PPA have a negative amplitude (se: 5.9%), while the immediately anterior vPMA 460 

has a much higher proportion of −pRFs (28.9% +/− 4.2%). To ensure that the relatively high 461 

proportion of negative pRFs in higher-order regions and the hippocampus was not driven by 462 

changes in the average model fit across regions (see Fig. S4), we fit a linear mixed-effects 463 

model predicting −pRF prevalence from region and mean R². Only region identity predicted 464 

−pRF prevalence (region: F(3, 18.305) = 8.825, p < 0.001; mean R 2: F(1, 22.831) = 0.193, p =465 

0.665).Thus, consistent with previous work in cortex (Szinte and Knapen, 2020; Steel et al., 466 

2024b), these results indicate that the proportion of −pRFs increases ascending the perceptual-467 

mnemonic hierarchy, independent of retinotopic model fit, supporting the interpretation that +/− 468 

pRFs differentially relate to perceptual and mnemonic processing. 469

# Discussion 532 

Here, we identified a bivalent retinotopic code in the human hippocampus, with roughly equal 533 

proportions of positive and negative visually-evoked responses (+/−pRFs). These populations 534 

exhibit consistent valence and visual field preferences, show a contralateral bias, and are 535 

broadly distributed throughout the hippocampus. Critically, this organization extends beyond 536 

retinotopic mapping tasks: shared visual field preference – estimated during retinotopic mapping 537 

– continues to predict intrinsic functional coupling at rest. Together, these findings suggest that 538 

retinotopy in the hippocampus reflects an intrinsic organizational principle, rather than simply a 539 

consequence of visual stimulation, potentially serving to link perceptual and mnemonic 540 

processing at the apex of the visual hierarchy. 541 

Challenging Conventional Models of the Visual Hierarchy 542 

Our findings challenge conventional views which propose that low-level sensory codes like 543 

retinotopy are shed as information ascends the visual hierarchy towards the hippocampus 544 

(Jeffery, 2007; Whitlock et al., 2008; Vann et al., 2009; Clark et al., 2018; Alexander et al., 545 

2023). These models suggest that the hippocampus represents abstract, allocentric internal 546 

models of the external world at the expense of fine-grained sensory details present in early 547 

visual areas. However, our study joins previous work (Knapen, 2021; Silson et al., 2021) in 548 

demonstrating that low-level visual coding persists in the hippocampus, and further expands 549 

upon that work by characterizing the retinotopic code as bivalent in nature and persistent at rest. 550

Hippocampal pRFs remain constrained by classic sensory features such as contralateral 551 

visual field bias, as in visual (Hemond et al., 2007; Wandell et al., 2007; Chan et al., 2010; 552 

Groen et al., 2022a) and higher order cortex (Szinte and Knapen, 2020; Knapen, 2021; Silson et 553 

al., 2021; Steel et al., 2024b). This reinforces that visual field preferences are encoded in higher 554 

order, mnemonic cortex. Unlike in perceptual regions — where most retinotopic voxels are 555 

+pRFs — the hippocampus contains a balanced mixture of positive and negative retinotopic 556 

populations distributed throughout, with no topographic organization. Rather than reflecting a 557 

progressive transformation to purely mnemonic coding within the hippocampus, this bivalent 558 

organization indicates that perceptual and mnemonic representations are intermixed. This 559 

balanced architecture suggests a close linkage between the hippocampus and vision (Nau et 560 

al., 2018; Turk-Browne, 2019), while also underscoring the importance of negative retinotopic 561 

responses in mnemonic circuitry (Szinte and Knapen, 2020; Steel et al., 2024a, 2024b). 562 

Together, these findings support the view that bivalent retinotopy is a broader organizational 563 

principle in higher-order mnemonic regions, structuring persistent interactions with visual cortex. 564 

Integration with Other Hippocampal Neural Codes 565 

The presence of a bivalent retinotopic code in the human hippocampus initially seems at odds 566 

with canonical descriptions of allocentric, spatial selectivity often described in hippocampal 567 

electrophysiology studies (O’Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971; Moser et al., 2008). However, recent 568 

work suggests a complex, multi-faceted view of hippocampal activity that accommodates both 569 

response types — particularly in non-human primates (Piza et al., 2024). In freely moving 570 

macaques and marmosets, canonical place responses are rarely pure representations of spatial 571 

location (Gulli et al., 2020; Mao et al., 2021; Piza et al., 2024). Instead, hippocampal neurons 572 

most commonly present a multiplexed representation of allocentric space alongside egocentric 573 

features including head direction, facing location, or gaze. 574

Hippocampal egocentric visual codes are thought to be more prevalent in primates than in 575 

rodents due to differences in the spatial exploration strategy between species (Piza et al., 576 

2024). Rodents are nocturnal, and supplement relatively coarse visual information with local 577 

whisking and olfaction while moving (Piza et al., 2024). In contrast, diurnal primates rely more 578 

on long-range visual information, with spatial exploration largely occurring while stationary (Piza 579 

et al., 2024). Yet even rodents display significant hippocampal mixed-selectivity to visual 580 

information (Acharya et al., 2016), with visual stimulation alone being sufficient to drive a subset 581 

of place cells in mice (Chen et al., 2013) and significant overlap (78%) between hippocampal 582 

neurons sensitive to egocentric vision and allocentric position (Purandare et al., 2022). 583 

Within this broader framework of mixed selectivity, the relatively sparse retinotopic code we 584 

observe here (~9% of hippocampal voxels) likely co-exists with spatiotopic codes of place and 585 

grid cells. Indeed, computational models of hippocampal dynamics suggest that place and grid 586 

cells could still serve as the dominant structure for information processing in the hippocampus, 587 

providing pre-structured states to support memory encoding in both spatial and non-spatial 588 

tasks, with sensory information applied to the scaffold of these pre-structured states (Chandra et 589 

al., 2025). Future research should explore whether the bivalent retinotopic code detected in this 590 

study is overlaid on top of place and grid cell representations, or whether it constitutes an 591 

independent contributing code that interacts with these spatial representations. 592 

Functional Relevance of Bivalent Hippocampal Retinotopy 593 

Recent work in cortex suggests that bivalent retinotopy at the apex of the cortical hierarchy 594 

structures a push-pull dynamic between perceptual and mnemonic systems (Steel et al., 2024b, 595 

2024a). We observe a similar oppositional relationship within the hippocampus, where pRFs of 596 

opposite valence are anti-correlated. Critically, the present findings demonstrate this 597 

organization within a single mnemonic structure, rather than across specialized regions (Steel et 598 

al., 2024b) or networks (Steel et al., 2024a), For example, a recent study found the externally-599

oriented Dorsal Attention Network and internally-oriented Default Network, classically 600 

considered functionally independent, show retinotopically-structured resting-state interactions 601

intraregional scale. 603 

One possible functional role for a bivalent code is supporting the coordination of perceptual 604 

encoding and mnemonic retrieval. In this context, opposing retinotopic populations could 605 

provide a spatial mechanism for separating concurrent encoding and retrieval-related processes 606 

while preserving a shared visuospatial reference frame, akin to the proposed role of 607 

hippocampal theta (Hasselmo et al., 2002; Kunec et al., 2005; Hasselmo and Stern, 2014; 608 

Kerrén et al., 2018). Notably, eye-movements provide a compelling link between visual input, 609 

theta oscillations (Hoffman et al., 2013; Jutras et al., 2013; Doucet et al., 2020), and 610 

hippocampally-dependent behavior (Wynn et al., 2020b, 2020a; Favila and Aly, 2023; Setton et 611 

al., 2024). Visual input (via retinotopy and theta) may thus allow the hippocampus to coordinate 612 

perceptual and mnemonic functions while maintaining functional separation. 613 

Alternatively, the bivalent retinotopic code may reflect predictive coding processes (Steel et 614 

al., 2024b, Wirth, 2023). Negative pRFs could encode top-down sensory predictions, whereas 615 

positive pRFs reflect bottom-up sensory drive. Their antagonistic interaction would enable rapid 616 

detection of mismatches between expected and observed input, thereby supporting mnemonic 617 

updating. Future work should investigate whether valence-specific populations differentially 618 

track prediction errors or encode predictive templates during memory-guided perception. 619 

Open Questions and Future Directions 620 

Several open questions lie beyond the scope of the present study. While we have shown that 621 

a pRF model robustly fits hippocampal responses during retinotopic mapping, these responses 622 

may not be fundamentally retinotopic in nature. Notably, work describing hippocampal view-623 

selective cells (Rolls and O’Mara, 1995; Liu et al., 2017; Corrigan et al., 2023; Piza et al., 2024) 624

suggests an alternative spatiotopic explanation in which hippocampal responses are linked to 625 

locations within a visual view, rather than locations of stimulation on the retina. This study is 626 

unable to disentangle these possibilities because participants were instructed to maintain 627 

fixation. Similarly, the temporal continuity of the moving bar stimulus makes it difficult to assess 628 

the potential impact of predictive responses in visually-evoked hippocampal activity. 629 

The present data also leave unresolved questions about temporal dynamics, as the temporal 630 

resolution of fMRI (1.33s) precludes precise characterization of timing. As noted above, the 631 

hippocampal theta oscillation is implicated in sensory-mnemonic processing (Hasselmo et al., 632 

2002; Kunec et al., 2005; Hoffman et al., 2013; Jutras et al., 2013; Hasselmo and Stern, 2014; 633 

Doucet et al., 2020), and bivalent retinotopy may be shaped by oscillatory dynamics. Further 634 

studies exploring hippocampal retinotopic responses with greater temporal resolution, such as 635 

with MEG or intracranial recordings, are needed to resolve this interaction and achieve 636 

descriptive parity with early visual retinotopic codes (Groen et al., 2022b; Yuasa et al., 2023). 637 

Finally, there are limitations to the current work stemming from the type and quantity of 638 

collected tasks. First, only three runs of the sweeping bar task were acquired for each 639 

participant, and more data would likely improve hippocampal model fits. Second, because only 640 

resting-state fixation lacked dynamic visual stimulation, we used it for two analyses, creating 641 

some apparent tension. Rest was first used to define a non-visual null model floor, and later 642 

used for testing whether a retinotopic voxel’s amplitude and center position predicted intrinsic 643 

functional coupling. Because these properties were estimated from retinotopic mapping, rather 644 

than rest, this dual use should not bias our results. Nevertheless, future studies should consider 645 

using distinct non-visual tasks for these purposes. 646 

To summarize, we identified a bivalent retinotopic code in the human hippocampus, which 647 

structures voxel-wise interactions within this mnemonic structure. This bivalent retinotopic 648 

scaffold may allow for the segregation and integration of perceptual and mnemonic information. 649
