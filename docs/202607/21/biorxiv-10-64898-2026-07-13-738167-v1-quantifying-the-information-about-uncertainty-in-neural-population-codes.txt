## Abstract 1

The activity of neural populations typically encodes more information about sensory or motor variables than 2

can be captured by point estimates of the variables. We present and compare two approaches to quantifying 3

this additional or ancillary information and its relationship to uncertainty: the mutual information between 4

activity and estimation error, and the Fisher information loss, which can be interpreted in terms of curvature in 5

information geometry. We show that deviations from Gaussianity of estimation errors, including the long tails 6

frequently observed in human behavioural tasks, are an expected corollary of the presence of ancillary information. 7

However, populations with similar distributions of estimation error can differ substantially in their ancillary 8

information content depending on the noise characteristics. For a given population tuning and noise model, our 9

results quantify an upper bound on the information about uncertainty that can be obtained from population 10 

activity alone: behaviour demonstrating knowledge in excess of this bound would indicate access to a separate 11 

source of information about uncertainty. Finally, we contrast the effects of external noise and decreasing internal 12 

signal strength on ancillary information and the Gaussianity of errors. Our work directly relates knowledge 13 

about uncertainty to non-Gaussianity in sensory estimates, and establishes a coherent theoretical foundation for 14 

investigating the basis of metacognition in neural population activity. 15 

## Author summary 16 

The brain processes sensory evidence about the external world via inherently noisy neural activity. As a re-17 

sult, behavioural judgments – such as estimating the direction of a moving object – are fundamentally uncertain. 18 

While animals, including humans, routinely use uncertainty to guide decisions under risk, how neural populations 19 

represent this uncertainty remains unclear. In this work, we show how the same neural activity used to decode 20 

a sensory variable can also provide information about the estimate’s reliability. We introduce a mathemati-21 

cal framework to quantify this ”ancillary information” directly from a neural population’s encoding model. We 22 

demonstrate that ancillary information predicts non-Gaussianity in estimation errors and sets an upper bound on 23 

metacognitive sensitivity (how accurately subjective confidence tracks performance). Crucially, we show that neu-24 

ral populations with distinct noise characteristics can yield near-identical estimation errors while providing very 25 

different degrees of uncertainty information. This highlights the importance of evaluating ancillary information, 26 

not just error patterns, when comparing competing models of sensory coding. 27 

## 1 Introduction 28 

Studies in humans and non-human primates indicate that the brain can access information about the uncertainty 29 

of internal representations to support adaptive behaviour (Bays et al., 2024; Cortese, 2022; Goris et al., 2025; Ma 30 

& Jazayeri, 2014), using it to integrate different sources of information appropriately (C.-H. S. Lin & Garrido, 31 

2022; Murai & Yotsumoto, 2018; Wolpert & Landy, 2012) and choosing between risky actions (Bach & Dolan, 32 

2012; Johnson & Busemeyer, 2010). For example, a driver preparing to change lanes must estimate the distance 33 

and speed of an approaching car. In daylight, when viewing conditions are good, this estimate is relatively 34 

precise, and the driver may feel the gap is sufficient and proceed. At night or in fog, the same driver may judge 35 

their estimate to be unreliable and choose to wait. In such real-world situations, decisions rely on not only an 36 

estimate of the external state, but also on information about the uncertainty of that estimate. Behavioural studies 37

Some uncertainty is inherent in the information available to our senses (e.g. one typically cannot see what 40 

is behind one’s head), but in cases where the sensory signal is informative but weak (like seeing in fog) the 41 

uncertainty reflects internal sources of noise. A range of models have been proposed to link subjective uncertainty 42 

or confidence to statistical uncertainty in internal representations (Boundy-Singer et al., 2023; Denison et al., 43 

2018; Li & Ma, 2020; Sanders et al., 2016). In the framework of signal detection theory (SDT), confidence in a 44 

categorical decision can be derived from the distance of a scalar internal signal from a decision boundary (Fleming 45 

& Lau, 2014; Maniscalco & Lau, 2012). However, for the continuous variables that are more frequently the focus 46 

of real-world decisions, confidence is more appropriately represented by the width or spread of the likelihood 47 

function (or the posterior distribution in a Bayesian setting) over the space of possible values. 48 

One account of estimation for continuous variables, the variable precision (VP) model, postulates that encod-49 

ing precision fluctuates randomly over objects and time because of doubly stochastic internal noise (van den Berg 50 

et al., 2012). While originally formulated to explain the presence of long tails (deviations from Gaussianity) in 51 

estimation errors in recall from visual working memory, the VP model has also been used to model confidence 52 

judgements, given the additional assumption that observers have subjective access to the encoding precision for 53 

each observation (van den Berg et al., 2017) (see also Fougnie et al. (2012)). However, the causes of precision 54 

variation are not identified by these descriptive models. 55 

In the brain, stochasticity in neuronal spiking contributes to variability in internal representations, which 56 

gives rise to uncertainty in downstream decoding systems. However, the same activity can also carry information 57 

about uncertainty. Ma et al. (2006) showed that for Poisson spiking variability (and a broader class of “Poisson-58 

like” noise characteristics) the summed amplitude of activity in a neural population could be used as a source of 59 

information about the uncertainty in a decoded estimate, with more activity corresponding to less uncertainty. 60 

Bays (2016) showed that patterns of error and subjective confidence in perceptual estimation of a low-contrast 61 

orientation stimulus could be simultaneously captured by a population coding model with Poisson noise charac-62 

teristics. This model had previously been shown to account for long tails and the effects of memory set size and 63 

prioritization on recall errors (Bays, 2014). Schneegans et al. (2020) united the neural and descriptive accounts, 64 

showing that the VP model provides an approximation in the continuous limit to predictions of a population 65 

coding model with Poisson noise. These results suggest that, along with access to uncertainty, the long-tailed 66 

error pattern observed in continuous estimation tasks can emerge naturally from population codes. 67 

A recent study presented an alternative, geometric perspective on such deviations from normality, describing 68 

long tails as a consequence of curvature of the encoding manifold embedded in high-dimensional neural activity 69 

space (Wei & Woodford, 2025). The underlying framework was equivalent to a population coding model with 70 

Gaussian noise (Schneegans & Bays, 2017; Schurgin et al., 2020; Tomi´ c & Bays, 2024b), but information about 71 

uncertainty was not directly addressed in this geometric account. 72 

Here, we present a general framework in which to situate the information about uncertainty contained in a 73 

population code, and an interpretation of it from a geometric perspective. We consider the maximum likelihood 74 

estimate (MLE) as a point estimator of a continuous variable based on activity in an idealized neural population. 75 

Information about uncertainty in an estimate is contained in ancillary statistics, components of the neural activity 76 

that are probabilistically independent of the estimated variable. We quantify this ancillary information using 77 

two complementary approaches: an entropy-based measure MI e, defined as the mutual information (MI) between 78 

neural activity and error in the MLE, and a measure based on Fisher information (FI), Iloss , calculated as the 79 

difference between FI for the neural activity versus the MLE. The latter measure is closely related to curvature 80 

in information geometry, revealing a formal relationship between encoding of uncertainty and deviations from 81 

Gaussianity in behavioural error. We investigate how the ancillary information in a population code depends on 82 

the combination of noise and tuning characteristics. 83

2.4 Relationship between the ancillary information measures 183 

The information loss Iloss (Eq. 17) and the mutual information MI e (Eq. 10) represent two alternative measures 184 

of ancillary information, i.e. the information contained in an observation about reliability of the MLE. Both 185

for the FI-based Iloss , reliability is quantified as sensitivity to small changes in the true parameter, while for the 190 

entropy-based MI e, reliability reflects the probability of mistaking any one parameter value for another. 191

A geometric perspective on statistical estimation can further illuminate the relationship between estimation error 201 

and information about uncertainty. As a simple illustration, consider a game of boules (Fig. 2a), in which a heavy 202 

ball is thrown with the intention of landing as close as possible to a smaller target ball, the “jack”. Imagine that 203 

the heavy ball’s landing point has isotropic Gaussian variability centred on the jack, and consider an observer 204 

who knows the jack is at unit distance from the thrower but doesn’t know in which direction. Based on the heavy 205 

ball’s landing point, this observer’s maximum likelihood estimate of the direction of the jack from the thrower is 206 

the direction of the ball from the thrower. However, the reliability of this estimate increases the further the ball 207 

lands from the thrower: the distance is an ancillary complement. 208 

To make this more concrete, two different landing points ( x1 and x2) are shown along with their corresponding 209 

likelihood functions, i.e. the probability of the landing point as a function of the direction of the jack, p(x|θ). 210 

The direction with maximum likelihood (the MLE, ˆθx) is the same for both of the illustrated landing points, so 211 

the estimation error is identical. However the likelihood functions differ in width, with the farther landing point 212 

having narrower likelihood, indicating less uncertainty about the jack’s direction: informally, this landing point 213 

is compatible with a smaller range of possible directions. 214 

The statistical model here is equivalent to Fisher’s circle model (R. Fisher, 1955; described in Efron, 2022, 215 

Sec.4.6) for a circle with unit radius. Estimation certainty can be quantified by the Observed FI, which in this 216 

setting is exactly equal to the distance D,217 

Jˆθ (x) = D. (18) A landing point at the “critical point” C, at distance zero, would be equiprobable for all possible locations of 218 

the jack on the circle, and thus provide no information (uniform likelihood, Jˆθ = 0). As distance of x from C219 

increases, its projection onto the unit circle becomes increasingly sensitive to small changes in θ, so estimation 220 

certainty increases. The distance D from the critical point is ancillary, as it is statistically independent of the 221 

direction, and in this case it is the ancillary complement to the MLE ˆθx, because together with the MLE it fully 222 

identifies the likelihood function, which is von Mises (circular normal) with circular mean ˆθx and concentration 223 

parameter equal to D.224 

The circle model is of the translation-type due to its rotational symmetry. As a result (Section 2.1) the 225 

distribution of the MLE can be predicted exactly from the form of the likelihood functions. Specifically, for a 226 

given distance D the MLE is von Mises distributed about the true location of the jack with concentration D,227 

so the marginal error distribution is a scale mixture of von Mises with concentrations distributed identically to 228 

distances (Fig. 2a, inset). The mixture has heavier tails than a von Mises distribution due to the variation in 229 

width of the component distributions. In this case, the mixture distribution does not have a closed-form PDF; to 230 

further elucidate the relationship between ancillary information and tailedness, Section 2.6 describes a synthetic 231 

model with Gaussian likelihoods in which the marginal estimation error has a location-scale t-distribution. 232 

The two-dimensional circle model provides a starting point for modelling estimation of a scalar stimulus value 233 

θ from a neural response r that lives in an M -dimensional space R, where each dimension corresponds to the 234 

observed activity of one neuron (Fig. 2b). The circle (which consisted of the mean landing points for every 235 

different possible direction of the jack) can be generalized to a one-dimensional manifold M of arbitrary shape 236 

embedded in R consisting of the mean neural responses for every possible value of the stimulus, μθ = ¯r(θ). 237 

Assuming the noise in neural activity belongs to the broad class of exponential family distributions, the 238 

statistical model takes the form of a one-parameter curved exponential family, with a well-characterized geometric 239 

interpretation (Efron, 1975). All observations r that yield the same MLE ˆθr lie in a flat ( M − 1)-dimensional 240 

hyperplane Aˆθ (Efron, 2022). This ancillary subspace intersects with M at μˆθ (Amari, 1982). 241 

The critical point C now becomes an ( M − 2)-dimensional critical subspace containing responses that are 242 

equiprobable for all values of the stimulus θ. Within the ancillary subspace corresponding to a particular MLE, 243 

the Observed FI Jˆθ (r) increases linearly with Euclidean distance measured orthogonally from C in the direction 244 

of ¯r(ˆθr), i.e. parallel to the vector labelled λˆθ . Variation in r within Aˆθ orthogonal to this line corresponds to 245 

observations that have the same MLE and Observed FI but differ in other aspects of the shape of the likelihood 246 

function. 247

previous section. It arises because of the curvature of the statistical manifold defined by the curved exponential 249 

family. In the simplest case of unit isotropic Gaussian noise, the “statistical curvature” γθ is identical to the 250 

geometric curvature of the one-dimensional manifold M in the response space R. If the manifold were a straight 251 

line, the likelihood function and hence the Observed FI would be the same for all observations r. For translation-252

## 3 Discussion 493 

We have presented a new framework relating principles of neural coding to information about uncertainty and 494 

non-Gaussianity in sensory estimates. We showed that ancillary information – by which we mean, the information 495 

about a stimulus contained in population activity additional to that expressed in the MLE – manifests as vari-496 

ability in the width and shape of the likelihood function. This variability naturally gives rise to non-Gaussianity 497 

16 

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license made available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 20, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.13.738167 doi: bioRxiv preprint

of error distributions based on the MLE, even when individual likelihoods are Gaussian functions, and provides 498 

a basis for uncertainty to be informative about estimation error, a property known as metacognitive sensitiv-499 

ity (Fleming, 2024). Our framework connects statistical inference in neural population codes to metacognition 500 

without making explicit assumptions about how uncertainty maps onto subjective confidence, and it quantifies a 501 

theoretical upper bound on metacognitive sensitivity attainable from population activity alone. 502 

In human estimation tasks, the non-Gaussianity of error distributions, and in particular the long tails observed 503 

in association with higher variability, has been a source of considerable debate (Bays & Husain, 2008; Bays, 2014; 504 

Fougnie et al., 2012; Schneegans et al., 2020; Schurgin et al., 2020; Taylor & Bays, 2020; van den Berg et al., 2012; 505 

Wei & Woodford, 2025; Wilken & Ma, 2004; Zhang & Luck, 2008). Previous studies have demonstrated that 506 

models based on population coding can emulate the long tailed error distributions and also reproduce variation 507 

in subjective confidence (Bays, 2014, 2016; van den Berg et al., 2017), but the connection between the two has 508 

not previously been formalized. Here, we provide a principled account of this relationship in terms of divergence 509 

of likelihood functions which, in a translation-type model, also comprise mixture components of the estimation 510 

error distribution. We presented two complementary measures of this divergence, Iloss and MI ε.511 

The quantity Iloss is a measure of ancillary information that captures variability of the likelihood function 512 

in terms of Fisher information. As demonstrated by a synthetic model with Gaussian likelihoods, increasing 513 

Iloss yields heavier tails in the marginal error distribution (see Section. 2.6). Its asymptotic limit, I∞ 

> loss

can 514 

be interpreted in terms of curvature in information geometry. Specifically, for translation-type models, Efron’s 515 

statistical curvature (Efron, 1975) is equal to the ratio between the asymptotic Fisher information loss and 516 

the Expected Fisher information (Eq. 20), establishing curvature as a formal statistical measure in a statistical 517 

submanifold. 518 

In a recent study, Wei and Woodford (2025) emphasized the role of representational geometry in shaping error 519 

distributions, attributing deviations from Gaussianity to curvature in the neural manifold. In that framework, 520 

curvature is characterized in a more descriptive sense (Kriegeskorte & Wei, 2021), based on representational 521 

dissimilarity and low-dimensional embeddings such as principal component analysis (PCA), rather than as a 522 

formal statistical quantity. Representational dissimilarity in this framework is conceptually analogous to the 523 

“psychological scaling function” proposed to underlie error distributions in the target confusability competition 524 

(TCC) model (Schurgin et al., 2020). Note that both models incorporate Gaussian noise: the Wei and Woodford 525 

(2025) model is mathematically equivalent to the flat Gaussian noise model considered here, while the TCC 526 

model can be viewed as an approximation of it (Tomi´ c & Bays, 2024b; Wei & Woodford, 2025). 527 

While these models predict long-tailed behavioural error distributions, they have not connected them with 528 

information about uncertainty or addressed metacognitive sensitivity. Moreover, they assume noise-independent 529 

representational structure. In particular, the representational geometry study (Wei & Woodford, 2025) proposed 530 

that the geometry is determined by tuning functions of the neural population, largely disregarding the role of 531 

noise structure. In contrast, by formulating the statistical model as a curved exponential family, we derived 532 

analytic expressions for the curvature of the corresponding statistical submanifold and show that curvature is 533 

jointly determined by tuning and noise characteristics. 534 

An alternative proposal for analyzing the geometry of non-Gaussian noise models is to apply an appropriate 535 

transformation to the responses that makes the variability approximately isotropic and Gaussian (Kriegeskorte & 536 

Wei, 2021; Wei & Woodford, 2025). In this study, we compared a Poisson noise model with an isotropic Gaussian 537 

noise model that approximates it via a variance-stabilizing transformation. We confirmed that the Gaussian 538 

approximation produced similar error distributions to the Poisson model, and further showed that the Expected 539 

FI was precisely equated in the two models. However, the Gaussian approximation displayed consistently lower 540 

ancillary information than the Poisson model, as measured by Iloss or MI e. This suggests that the approach 541 

of transforming non-Gaussian noise models for analysis within the representational geometry framework may 542 

be sufficient for approximating first-order properties (e.g. estimation error) but could give misleading results for 543 

higher order properties (e.g. information about uncertainty). In comparison, the statistical curvature described 544 

by information geometry predicts asymptotic information loss with analytic precision, but this provides only a 545 

qualitative guide to the information loss in finite samples. 546 

We further examined estimation and ancillary information in a Poisson population with shared fluctuations 547 

in gain, which has been proposed to describe activity in the brain more accurately (Goris et al., 2014; I.-C. Lin 548 

et al., 2015). With mean activity held constant, gain modulation had no effect on Expected FI and only a small 549 

effect on estimation error patterns, but dramatically altered ancillary information (see Section 2.7.1). Ancillary 550 

information, measured either by Iloss or MI e, increased monotonically with increases in gain modulation. 551 

We presented as an alternative measure of ancillary information the entropy-based quantity MI e, which char-552 

acterizes variability in the likelihood in information-theoretic terms. MI e corresponds to the mutual information 553 

between estimation error and neural activity. Because mutual information is invariant under reparametriza-554 

tion, this measure provides a principled bridge between ancillary information encoded in population activity and 555 

behavioural measures of metacognitive sensitivity. From this perspective, metacognitive sensitivity in human 556 

observers can be naturally quantified by the mutual information between estimation errors ε and confidence 557 

reports c, i.e. MI( ε; c). Specifically, when confidence reports c are generated as a transformation of the neural 558 

activity that retains all of its ancillary information, then MI( ε; c) will equal MI e; otherwise MI( ε; c) will be less 559 

than MI e. For a given population code, MI e therefore quantifies an upper bound on metacognitive sensitivity 560 

attainable from the same neural representation used to generate the perceptual estimate. 561

Metacognitive sensitivity below this bound would imply imperfect access to uncertainty, potentially due 562 

to “metacognitive noise” (Bang et al., 2019; De Martino et al., 2013; Fleming & Daw, 2017; Guggenmos, 2022; 563 

Mamassian & de Gardelle, 2022; Maniscalco & Lau, 2014, 2016; Mueller & Weidemann, 2008; Shekhar & Rahnev, 564 

2021; van den Berg et al., 2017), heuristic use of evidence (Maniscalco et al., 2016; Peters et al., 2017; Zylberberg 565 

et al., 2012), inference from suboptimal internal models (Herce Casta˜ n´ on et al., 2019), or changes in evidence 566 

distributions at perceptual level (Rahnev et al., 2012; Shekhar & Rahnev, 2024; Zylberberg et al., 2016). As we 567 

further demonstrated (see Section. 2.7.2), reduced metacognitive performance can also arise from external noise, 568 

which corrupts internal representations without contributing ancillary information. External noise could arise in 569 

sensory transduction or neural transmission upstream of the activity providing the confidence signal, or it could 570 

accumulate through recurrent connections that sustain activity over time. Note however that recurrency would 571 

also be expected to induce specific patterns of noise correlation, the consequences of which for metacognitive 572 

sensitivity remain an important subject for future research. 573 

Alternatively, metacognitive sensitivity exceeding the bound set by MI e would imply access to a separate 574 

source of information about uncertainty, such as post-decisional information after a decision has been made 575 

(Fleming & Daw, 2017; Schulz et al., 2023). Dayan (2023) referred to the cases of metacognitive sensitivity being 576 

less or greater than MI e as hypo- and hyper-sensitivity, respectively. The quantity MI( ε; c) can be viewed as a 577 

continuous analogue of the information-theoretic measure meta-I (Dayan, 2023), which was originally introduced 578 

to assess metacognitive sensitivity in binary discrimination tasks. Meyen et al. (2025) further interpreted meta-I579 

as ’surplus transmitted information’, namely the additional information transmitted by a classifier’s response 580 

beyond the minimum possible transmitted information for the Type-1 performance (classification accuracy), 581 

and proposed relative metainformation (RMI) as a normalized information-theoretic measure of metacognitive 582 

sensitivity in binary tasks. Our proposed information-theoretic measure MI e generalizes the concept behind 583 

meta-I and RMI to the domain of continuous estimation, and we have extended its application to the study of 584 

ancillary information in neural population codes. 585 

We observed an inverted-U shape relationship between the two measures of ancillary information examined 586 

in this study (Fig. 5e). Assuming a fixed non-zero Expected FI ( I), MI e approaches zero at both extremes of 587 

Iloss (i.e. Iloss → 0 or Iloss → I ). When Iloss → 0, the Observed FI becomes constant across observations. 588 

In this limit, the ancillary complement represented by the Observed FI contains no variability, so conditioning 589 

on it does not reduce uncertainty in the estimation error and sensitivity thus drops to zero. Conversely, as 590 

Iloss approaches I, the likelihoods corresponding to different observations diverge strongly, with rare, very high 591 

certainty observations intermixed with frequent, very uncertain observations; the latter dominates the entropy-592 

based measure MI e which falls to zero. Although all noise models exhibit similar inverted-U dependence between 593 

the two measures of ancillary information, they can differ substantially in the entropy-based ancillary information 594 

MI e even when matched for both Fisher information and FI-based ancillary information Iloss .595 

These results highlight that, for a given noise model, estimation error patterns uniquely determine the an-596 

cillary information – and thus the metacognitive sensitivity attainable for an ideal observer. However, across 597 

models, similar error patterns can arise from different noise structures that afford different amounts of ancil-598 

lary information. Consequently, combining estimation errors and empirical measures of metacognitive sensitivity 599 

could help distinguish between neural models that have been considered interchangeable in previous work. 600 

Although our framework is formulated in terms of ML estimation under an assumption of translation in-601 

variance, it admits a natural extension to Bayesian inference. For translation-type models, Fisher information 602 

is constant across parameter space, and Bayesian inference based on the uninformative Jeffreys prior (Robert 603 

et al., 2009), pJ (θ) ∝ √I(θ), reduces to ML estimation; in this case, the Jeffreys prior is uniform. Translation 604 

invariance may be violated in more ecological settings with non-uniform stimulus statistics. In such cases, one 605 

established approach is to apply an invertible transformation of the stimulus into a sensory space where Fisher 606 

Information is uniform and the assumption of translation invariance applies (Wei & Stocker, 2015). A specific 607 

case is estimation of a scale parameter, for which the Jeffreys prior takes the form pJ (σ) ∝ 1/σ (Robert et al., 608 

2009), corresponding to a uniform prior on the log scale (i.e. translation invariance in log σ) – a special case 609 

of the approach described by Wei and Stocker (2015) with logarithmic transformation. Extending the present 610 

framework to more general Bayesian settings that relax translational invariance and incorporate heterogeneous 611 

coding schemes will be an important direction for future work. 612 

In summary, this work provides a principled framework relating metacognitive sensitivity to population coding 613 

of sensory evidence, offering a path toward unifying theories of perceptual processing and metacognitive compu-614 

tations. By formalizing how ancillary information arises from the probabilistic representation of neural codes, 615 

our approach makes concrete predictions about when and why metacognitive sensitivity diverges depending on 616 

behavioural performance, and how such divergences depend on the underlying coding characteristics. These pre-617 

dictions can be tested in behavioural and neurophysiological studies that concurrently collect estimation errors 618 

and confidence under controlled manipulations of set size, sensory uncertainty, prior expectations, etc. Cru-619 

cially, the ability to quantify information about uncertainty directly from encoding models establishes a coherent 620 

theoretical foundation for understanding the basis of metacognition within a single formalism. 621
