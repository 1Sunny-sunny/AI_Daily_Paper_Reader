# Abstract 10 

Controlling specific neur on al dynamics with electrical stimulation is critical for therapeutic neuromodulation , yet 11 

deriving optimal control policies remains challenging due to the complex and non -stationary nature of biological 12 

neur on al networks . While reinforcement learning (RL) offers a powerful closed -loop control framework , its reliance on 13 

prolonged stimulus -driven exploration is difficult to reconcile with the physiological limits of living tissue . Here , we 14 

demonstrate an in silico -to -in vitro transfer strategy that achieves efficient state -dependent control of network bursting 15 

in cultured neurons. The transferred policy outperforms heuristic controls , while maintaining constrained stimulation 16 

usage . Concurrent calcium imaging reveals the mechanistic basis of the learned polic y: the agent optimizes stimulation 17 

spatially and temporally , exploiting local network topology and intrinsic physiological temporal dynamics . These results 18 

establish in vitro brain -on -chip cultures as a tractable stepping stone for RL -based neuromodulation and demonstrate that 19 

effective control policies can be derived in biophysically calibrated digital twins and transferred directly to living 20 

networks. 21

# Introduction 22 

The quest to precisely control neur on al activity is an enduring challenge of modern neuroscience, with implications for 23 

understanding brain function and developing next -generation therapeutic interventions like deep brain stimulation and 24 

brain -machine interfaces 1,2 . Effective neuronal modulation can be pursued using either open -loop or closed -loop control 25 

strategies . Open -loop controllers operate on fixed -parameter protocols, delivering stimuli without regard to the system's 26 

ongoing state. While simple to implement, this strategy is fundamentally limited when interacting with a complex, 27 

adaptive system like the brain, whose response to input is state -dependent 3,4 . In contrast, closed -loop (or feedback) 28 

controllers dynamically adjust their stimulation parameters based on real -time readouts of neur on al activity. 29 

Theoretically, such state -dependency offers the potential for more robust, efficient, and personalized neuromodulation 1,5 .30 

However , translating this theoretical promise into practice in biological neuronal networks (BNNs) presents a formidable 31 

challenge. BNNs are not static circuits; they are seemingly stochastic (identical inputs can produce divergent responses )32 

and non -stationar y, due to ongoing changes in synaptic connectivity 6,7 . While this set of behaviours is essential for their 33 

computational power, it also means that traditional control methods are ineffective , since they are often designed for 34 

systems with fixed, predictable dynamics and struggle with large action spaces and non -linear relationships 8. To mitigate 35 

the dimensionality challenge, some closed -loop pipelines restrict the action space a priori : they use initial observation 36 

periods to identify and select read -out and stimulating electrodes 9-12 . Although this approach has achieved some success, 37 

it is limited by the fact that the initial electrode selection can become outdated due to the plasticity and non -stationarity 38 

of BNNs 6,13 .39 

This translation challenge has sparked interest in more flexible control strategies. Reinforcement learning (RL), a 40 

machine learning framework for solving sequential decision -making problems 14 , offers a particularly promising and 41 

scalable solution. An RL agent can learn an optimal closed -loop policy - that is, a set of rules that determines the best 42 

action to take based on the current observed state - through repeated interaction with an environment , without requiring 43 

an explicit system model . This makes RL a conceptually attractive framework for adaptive neuromodulation. Yet, a 44 

critical gap persists: while RL -based controllers ha ve achieved encouraging results in silico within models designed to 45 

emulate in vivo circuitry 15 -18 , their application to actual biological systems has yet to be demonstrated .46

A possible step toward closing this gap is to validate RL agents on living neuronal systems that are complex enough to 47 

be methodologically relevant, yet simple enough to be experimentally tractable. In vitro cultures have historically played 48 

this role 19,20 . Under appropriate conditions, t hese cultures reproduce key features of in vivo dynamics - including 49 

nonlinearity, adaptability, and non -stationarity - and can exhibit both physiological and pathological activity patterns at 50 

the population level . This dynamical fidelity, combined with experimental and ethical accessibility, makes them a 51 

practical target for closed -loop control studies 6,21 . In particular, the ability to reliably elicit and disrupt synchronised 52 

network dynamics in vitro can be directly mapped onto two clinically relevant control objectives: initiating coordinated 53 

burst activity, as required when promoting thalamocortical spindles for sleep -dependent memory consolidation 22 , and 54 

suppressing pathological synchrony, as pursued in adaptive deep brain stimulation targeting beta oscillations in 55 

Parkinson's disease 1. Despite this translational potential , the use of RL in BNNs has occurred almost exclusively in vitro 56 

- and even within this domain , the framework remains largely unexplored. Except for few studies implementing formal 57 

RL schemes 23,24 , existing approaches have predominantly relied on stateless parameter optimization (such as adapting 58 

electrode selection probabilities based on past performance) 25,26 rather than learning state -dependent control policies. 59 

These limitations in existing RL -based approaches are not incidental - training RL controllers directly in BNNs poses 60 

practical challenges. Defining meaningful state representations from high -dimensional neuronal activity is itself non -61 

trivial 24 ; and once training begins, learning reliable control polic ies requires long training sessions with hundreds to 62 

thousands of stimulus repetitions 25,27 . Importantly, prolonged interaction can compromise cell viability, induce stimulus -63 

specific adaptation 10,28 and drive network drift 29,30 . Together, these facto rs produce a non -stationary environment where 64 

the optimal control policy becomes a “moving target”, progressively devaluing data collected earlier in training .65 

Therefore, the critical challenge is not merely to bring RL to BNNs, but to do so in a way that is compatible with their 66 

physiological constraints . To this end, we present a hybrid pipeline in which an RL agent is first trained in silico , using 67 

biophysically detailed models calibrated to reproduce the emergent dynamical properties and biological variability of in 68 

vitro cultures . This training phase allows the agent to explore the policy space under statistically stationary and 69 

reproducible conditions before being transferred to living networks . We then demonstrate that the resulting state -70 

dependent control polic y generalise s to in vitro BNNs, successfully eliciting network bursts on demand. Finally, calcium 71

# Results 74 

## Biophysically detailed in silico model reproduces key activity dynamics of in vitro cultures 75 

We developed a biophysically detailed computational model to recreate the activity of primary hippocampal neuronal 76 

networks (Fig. 1a). The model comprised 200 single -compartment Hodgkin -Huxley (HH) neurons, with a composition 77 

of 95% pyramidal neurons and 5% parvalbumin -positive (PV+) interneurons. Each neuron featured voltage -gated sodium 78 

and potassium channels, along with leak currents representing the intrinsic membrane permeability. Neurons were 79 

randomly spatially distributed at a density of 35,000 cells/cm², matching experimental conditions . Synaptic connectivity 80 

was implemented with sparse, probabilistic connections, incorporating AMPA, NMDA, and GABA A receptor dynamics, 81 

and including short -term plasticity (STP) mechanisms , namely depression and facilitation. To enable direct comparison 82 

with experimental recordings, we simulated "virtual" electrodes that captured extracellular spiking activity. Individual 83 

neuron and synapse parameters were fixed based on literature -derived experimental data, whereas the network's 84 

connectivity parameters were tuned to reproduce the characteristic dynamics of our in vitro preparations - embryonic 85 

(E18) rat hippocampal neurons cultured on microelectrode array (MEA) chips (Fig. 1b). 86 

In vitro cultures showed highly reproducible and well -established activity patterns across preparations, consisting of 87 

individual spikes and bursts (brief periods of high -frequency firing) that spontaneously organized into network bursts 88 

(NBs) detectable across multiple electrodes 7,31 . Our in silico model successfully recapitulated these spatiotemporal 89 

dynamics, with NBs emerging spontaneously across the array of “virtual” electrodes (Fig. 1c). Notably, the in silico 90 

networks exhibited significantly less non -stationarity than the in vitro cultures, maintaining stable network inter -burst 91 

interval values over time (Fig. 1g; similar results for other metrics are not shown). Such relative stability is anticipated; 92 

although the model incorporates STP to capture rapid firing dynamics, the underlying structural connectivity and synaptic 93 

weights remain fixed, providing a stationary baseline compared to the stochastic drift of living tissue. 94

## Reinforcement learning agent generalizes network burst control in silico 116 

After the calibration of the simulated model, we designed a reinforcement learning pipeline to maximize the immediate 117 

probability of eliciting a network burst (NB) while minimizing stimulation usage. This objective was chosen because 118 

NBs represent well -defined, temporally localized events of maximal network coordination, providing a natural and 119 

explicit reward signal , and a stringent test of whether the agent has learned to exploit the network's internal excitability 120 

state. This framework for state -dependent control was further motivated by clinical contexts in which targeted promotion 121 

of synchronised activity is desirable 22 .122 

Reliable generalisation across network simulations required grounding the agent's decisions in observable 123 

electrophysiological features that reflect excitability in a simulation -invariant manner. Stimulation efficacy depends 124 

nonlinearly on both temporal context (e.g., recovery from previous NB) and spatial context (e.g., electrode -specific 125 

network coupling) . We therefore designed a state representation integrating both dimensions (see Methods: State 126 

representation ). Each episode consisted of sequential 200 ms stimulation steps (Fig. 2a). Episodes terminated upon NB 127 

detection and resumed only after a 200 ms quiescent period, ensuring the end of the previous NB. 128 

To achieve robust performance across different networks, we used a two -stage training strategy (Fig. 2b). First, we turned 129 

to online learning, where individual agents were trained from scratch using Proximal Policy Optimization 32 (PPO) on 130 

multiple simulated networks, generating environment -specific (hereafter termed Specialist ) stimulation policies. 131 

Specialists showed rapid learning progression, with cumulative rewards and stimulation usage (fraction of actions where 132 

stimulation was used) reaching asymptotic performance within 250 training episodes (Fig. 2f,h). The state -action 133 

distributions from these agents were aggregated for policy distillation (Fig. 2b). Then, an agent was trained on these 134 

state -probability pairs to produce a Generalist policy that minimized the Kullback -Leibler (KL) divergence of the 135 

outputted probabilities across all training environments. 136 

We then tested the Generalist policy on a subset of simulated networks (excluded from policy distillation) and compared 137 

its performance to the respective Specialists . Consistent with the premise that fundamental excitability dynamics are 138 

conserved across networks, the Generalist agent’s action probabilities often matched those of network -specific 139

## Online learning induces network fatigue in vitro without improving control efficacy over pre -180 

## trained Generalist agent 181 

To compare our approach against standard online learning, we first assessed the feasibility of training Specialist agents 182 

from scratch directly on in vitro networks ( Protocol 1, Fig . 3a). While technically achievable, online learning highlight ed 183 

an inherent challenge in biological R L: the balance between policy convergence and network stability . Stabilizing an 184 

effective policy typically required a comparable number of episodes to in silico training (Fig. 3b) , corresponding to 185 

approximately five minutes of stimulation (Fig. 3c) . However, this training period was already sufficient to trigger a186 

measurable degradation in network activity, which we henceforth define as “network fatigue” . Specifically, most 187 

networks reduced spontaneous burst frequency during the final baseline (Fig. 3h,i). This fatigue caused a confounding 188 

order effect: Generalist policies tested after a Specialist training phase showed longer network inter -burst intervals 189 

(NIBIs) compared to Generalist policies tested first (Fig. 3g). A n alternative comparison between online learning and 190 

our proposed approach would likely require a reduction in stimulation density to minimize network fatigue - an 191 

adjustment that would, in turn, significantly extend the time required to reach policy convergence. 192 

We also noted that initial training trajectories deviated from in silico predictions during early training phases (Fig. 3b). 193 

Specifically, we observed an initial increase in median cumulative reward driven by less responsive networks; in these 194 

cases, extended periods of network quiescence allowed the agent to accumulate positive rewards for withholding 195 

stimulation, thereby inflating the cumulative signal prior to policy convergence (see Methods: Reward function ). 196 

Expectedly, these networks were also significantly more prone to failing convergence criteria. 197

## The pre -trained Generalist agent outperforms heuristic controls in evoking in vitro network 220 

## bursts 221 

Despite the clear advantages of using a pre -trained Generalist agent over na ive online learning, we still sought to 222 

determine if it was approaching the maximum possible performance of our control task . To this end, we compared the 223 

Generalist against two heuristic controls (Protocol 2, Fig. 4a), each deliberately depriv ing a key aspect of the agent's 224 

decision -making. The " Without Best " policy restricted stimulation to all electrodes except the one empirically identified 225 

as highest yielding for that network, removing the agent's ability to exploit its single most successful action. The 226 

"Random " policy applied spatially randomized stimulation at the same overall stimulation rate (stims/min) as the 227 

Generalist , removing any learned spatial selectivity while preserving the total stimulation budget. Together, these 228 

controls probe the Generalist from two directions: whether its advantage stems from knowing which electrode to 229 

stimulate , or from the overall rate of stimulation .230 

All stimulation policies successfully reduced the mean network inter -burst interval (NIBI) relative to spontaneous 231 

baseline activity. However, the Generalist agent achieved greatest reduction in NIBI (Fig. 4b) while maintaining 232 

comparable stimulation usage (Fig. 4c ). Pairwise comparisons further underscored this superiority, demonstrating that 233 

the Generalist agent significantly outperformed its heuristic counterparts on a n experiment -by -experiment basis (Fig. 234 

4e ). 235 

We next investigated whether the "network fatigue" observed during naive online learning (Protocol 1) persisted under 236 

this shorter evaluation scheme . While Protocol 2 showed a trend toward post -stimulation increase in NIBI (p = 0. 052 ), 237 

the magnitude of this effect was drastically reduced compared to Protocol 1. Specifically, Protocol 1 induced severe 238 

exhaustion after 11 minutes of active stimulation, whereas Protocol 2 produced only mild drift after 3 minutes (d = 1.04 239 

vs. d = 0.39 ). This confirms that sustained, high -dose stimulation is the primary driver of network fatigue . To ensure this 240 

mild drift did not systematically bias the evaluation of the subsequent heuristic controls, we conducted a validation 241

A striking feature of these cultures is the sheer variance in their spontaneous baseline activity, which spans a full order 245 

of magnitude (hence the log -scale representation in the NIBI plots). Such variability poses a natural question: do these 246 

differences in spontaneous NIBI statistics dictate how excitable the networks are , or can the Generalist agent simply 247 

overpower them? To answer this, we first correlated each network's spontaneous baseline NIBI with its performance 248 

under each policy (Fig. 4f) and found consistent moderate positive associations across all thr ee (τ = 0.38 –0.47): networks 249 

with shorter spontaneous inter -burst intervals tended to achieve shorter intervals under closed -loop control as well. We 250 

also compared the performance ranks of the different stimulation policies across individual networks and observed 251 

similarly strong cross -policy correlations (τ = 0.64 –0.71), indicating that no policy can substantially reorder networks 252 

relative to the others. Taken together, these results suggest that network identity (rather than policy choice) is the 253 

dominant determinant of NB elicitation performance, with each preparation's intrinsic properties setting an effective 254 

ceiling on NIBI reduction. This ceiling likely reflects a combination of the network's spontaneous refractory -like 255 

dynamics and preparation -specific factors such as electrode -network coupling via the MEA interface. 256

# Discussion 322 

Our in silico framework was designed to re capitulate key activity dynamics observed in vitro - such as bursting statistics 323 

and response probabilities - within a biophysically grounded yet computationally tractable environment. Unlike 324 

biological neuronal networks (BNNs), which are characterized by non -stationarity and network drift over extended 325

timescales 24,34 , the simulated environment maintains statistical stationarity. Such stability provides the clean signal 326 

required for robust policy learning . It insulates the agent from confounding physiological drift, ensuring that training 327 

optimizes for genuine causal dependencies rather than overfitting to transient excitability states . Consequently, the model 328 

functions as a standardized benchmark, enabling the rigorous training and validation of agents under reproducible 329 

conditions prior to their deployment in BNNs. 330 

We conceptualized the closed -loop neuromodulation task as a nonlinear contextual bandit problem, where the objective 331 

is to map an instantaneous network state to the optimal stimulation target that maximizes the immediate probability of a 332 

network burst while penalizing stimulation usage . This framing is biologically appropriate for two reasons. First, the 333 

control goal is inherently 'greedy' - prioritizing immediate responsiveness over long -horizon planning. Second, our data 334 

confirms that electrode efficacy is not static but varies nonlinearly with the network’s recovery status (i.e., whether the 335 

network is refractory or excitable) . However, while the theoretical formulation aligns with the bandit framework, the 336 

standard algorithmic solutions are inadequate for real -time implementation. Nonlinear bandit algorithms (e.g., 337 

Neural UCB 35 ) typically rely on explicit uncertainty quantification , continuously calculating confidence bounds to guide 338 

exploration. This introduces a computational overhead that grows with experimental duration, often exceeding the sub -339 

millisecond latency desirable for precise feedback. Furthermore, these methods may struggle with the non -stationarity 340 

of biological tissues 36 . To resolve this conflict between theoretical framing and practical latency, we used an adapted 341 

version of the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm . This choice offers two main advantages . First, PPO learns 342 

a reactive policy capable of low -latency inference (single forward pass), effectively solving the contextual bandit 343 

challenge within the strict timing constraints of the biological loop. Second, unlike specialized bandit algorithms, PPO 344 

provides a unified framework that naturally generalizes beyond the greedy setting: it allows us to solve the immediate 345 

bandit problem (discount factor  𝛾 = 0) while retaining the architectural flexibility to incorporate long -horizon credit 346 

assignment ( 𝛾 > 0) should the control objectives evolve .347 

It is also worth noting that the success of this algorithm relies on a state representation explicitly designed to capture 348 

instantaneous network excitability. By encoding the recent history of network burst ing activity , the state vector provides 349 

the agent with a proxy for both recovery status and NB spatial propagation . This design choice is validated by the strong 350

Actor -Critic framework 541 

We adapted Proximal Policy Optimization 32 (PPO) by modifying the critic to estimate  𝑄 (𝑠 , 𝑎 ) rather than  𝑉 (𝑠 ) (Supp. 542 

Algorithm 1). This modification was motivated by our contextual bandit framework, where stimulation decisions are 543 

treated as independent and sparse NB evocations require precise credit assignment. Whereas a state -value critic would 544 

conflate the effects of all electrode choices, our Q -value critic directly models action -specific outcomes, enabling clearer 545 

identification of which actions resulted in successful NB evocation. We used batched training with 256 samples per 546 

batch, performing policy updates over 10 epochs using 64 -sample mini -batches with advantage normalization to improve 547 

learning stability. The architecture consisted of single 32 -unit layer MLP s for both the actor (policy) and critic (Q -value) 548 

networks, with the policy network outputting a categorical distribution (softmax activation) over the possible actions .549

Convergence criteria 552 

To quantitatively determine when an online -trained agent stabilized upon an effective control policy, we established a 553 

dual -condition convergence threshold based on episodic cumulative reward. Raw cumulative rewards were first 554 

smoothed using a centred rolling median filter (window size = 30 episodes). Policy convergence was defined as the first 555 

episode where the trailing window of median -smoothed rewards simultaneously satisfied two criteria: (1) absolute 556 

efficacy, requiring all values within the window to exceed a minimum reward threshold of 1.5 ( which is roughly 557 

equivalent to waiting at least two steps more than the number of stimulations applied, and successfully eliciting a NB ); 558 

and (2) behavioural stability, requiring the standard deviation of the rewards within the window to remain below a strict 559 

tolerance of 0.2. The exact time and step count to reach this conservative convergence point were recorded to evaluate 560 

learning speed. Networks that failed to satisfy both criteria prior to the termination of the training phase were labelled as 561 

non -converging. 562

Protocol 1: Transfer vs. Online Learning ( Generalist vs. Specialist) 579 

To benchmark the performance of the pre -trained Generalist policy against the online learning capabilities of the 580 

Specialist policy , we implemented a counterbalanced crossover design. Following the initial baseline, the Generalist 581

To dissect the efficacy of the Generalist policy, we compared it against two heuristic controls : Without Best (which 585 

excluded the single highest -performing electrode identified during the Generalist phase) and Random (which maintained 586 

the Generalist policy's stimulation usage but randomized stimulation timing and electrode selection). Following the 587 

initial baseline, the Generalist policy (1 minute) was applied first to establish the target metrics. This was followed by 588 

the two control policies (1 minute each), applied in a randomized order. This sequential design ensured that control 589 

policies were conditioned on the specific active electrodes identified by the Generalist agent. The Without Best policy 590 

specifically tested the contribution of high -performing electrodes, whereas the Random policy served as a stochastic 591 

control, together providing complementary insights into policy -dependent network responses. 592
