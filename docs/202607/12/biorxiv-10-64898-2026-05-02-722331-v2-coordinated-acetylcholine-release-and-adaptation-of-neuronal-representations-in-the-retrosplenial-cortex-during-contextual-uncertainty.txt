Abstract 45 

Accurate learning underpins optimal predictions and requires the brain to navigate uncertainty, 46 

identifying important information and distinguishing between expected and unexpected 47 

uncertainties . Theoretical models propose that the neuromodulator acetylcholine positively 48 

correlates with expected uncertainty , priming neuronal networks for learning . We tested this 49 

hypothesis by measuring acetylcholine release and neuronal representations in the retrosplenial 50 

cortex of mice whilst challenging them with expected and unexpected uncertainties of reward 51 

location. Acetylcholine release did not directly correlate with expected or unexpected 52 

uncertainty but instead increased with changes in expected uncertainty. In tandem, increased 53 

expected uncertainty shifted neuronal representations from a position al reference frame to 54 

focus on salient landmarks , such as reward location. Transition s in expected uncertainty also 55 

accelerated remapping of neuronal representations and behavioural adaptation when 56 

unexpected uncertainty wa s experienced. Thus, we demonstrate acetylcholine release in 57 

retrosplenial cortex discerns types of uncertainty and correlates with learning speed in 58 

uncertain environments. 59

Introduction 66 

Plasticity is a fundamental and vital feature of brain function enabling learning and behavioural 67 

adaptation. New information needs to be rapidly incorporated into existing estimates and 68 

predictions that are encoded within neuronal networks , but in real world situations this updating 69 

depends on uncertainty. For example, if we find our favourite ice cream van parked on the 70 

same road every day there can be some variation in the exact location , but this is expected 71 

uncertainty and , once we have learnt the range of variation , does not require any great 72 

adjustment of predictions to find our ice cream . However, if one day the ice cream van has 73 

parked at the far end of the street or on the next block , this is outside the boundaries of our 74 

predictions and is therefore unexpected uncertainty , requiring an update in estimates of the 75 

van ’s future location and adjustment of behavior, such as budgeting extra time to find the van 76 

or selecting a more reliable vendor . Importantly, there is a dynamic interaction between 77 

expected and unexpected uncertaint ies , since the degree of expected uncertainty dictate s the 78 

threshold for detectin g unexpected uncertainty , and conversely , an unexpected event will 79 

increase the level of expected uncertainty 1,2 .80 

To distinguish between these forms of uncertainty and engage accurate learning the brain must 81 

provide signals that link the uncertainty condition to learning mechanisms. Longstanding 82 

evidence shows that neuromodulator systems provide signals associated with expectation, 83 

error , and uncertainty, with their release reconfiguring neuronal circuits to enable the update 84 

of estimates and memories 3,4 . This suggests neuromodulators determine the importance and 85 

impact of new information in a manner that incorporates estimates of uncertainty .86 

Acetylcholine has a particular prominence in this regard since its release promote s plasticity 87 

and learning 5,6 and is associated with arousal, attention , and expected uncertainty 7,8 . Along 88 

with other neuro modulators such as noradrenaline and dopamine , acetylcholine is also 89 

associated with surpris e or unexpected uncertainty that necessitate s a shift in inference about 90 

the current environmental state 9-12 .91 

Cha nges in expected uncertainty are predicted to alter the environmental context , as knowledge 92 

about stimulus -outcome contingencies become s un reliable for guid ing effective behaviour. 93 

This process is proposed to be accompanied by acetylcholine release , which facilitate s94 

plasticity to support learning of revised contingencies 1,4 . Changes in expected uncertainty can 95 

arise when the reliability of known environmental features shift within an otherwise stable 96 

context . However, unexpected uncertainty also necessitates learning of revised contingencies 97

Expected and unexpected uncertainty of reward location. 137 

To investigate the role of acetylcholine in regulating plasticity of neuronal dRSC 138

In each daily session , mice r an multiple traversals of a 3 m track with a reward delivered at a 141 

single location on each traversal . A t the end of each traversal , the mice were teleported back to 142 

the start after a 3 s timeout which mark ed the end of the previous trial and the start of the next 143 

one . The URT ask is designed to manipulate the degree of expected and unexpected uncertainty 144

Acetylcholine release and neuronal plasticity in response to a novel environment 204 

Acetylcholine release in the hippocampus increases when mice are exposed to a novel 205 

environment 38 , whi le exposure to novel environments also causes remapping of neuronal 206 

representations in both the hippocampus and dRSC 32,38 . Encountering a novel environment 207 

also constitutes a large contextual change that generates unexpected uncertainty and an increase 208 

in expected uncertainty . To test the relationship between acetylcholine release and neuronal 209 

plasticity in dRSC , we first measured acetylcholine release and recorded the activity of 210 

neuronal populations in dRSC in response to exposure to a novel environment. 211

jGCaMP 8m 38,40 in dRSC neurons and monitored their dynamics using two -photon microscopy 214 

whilst mice ran laps of the virtual reality track (Fig. 2A -C). 215

Having established th e sensitivity of acetylcholine release and neuronal representations in 323 

dRSC to unexpected and high expected uncertainty elicited by a novel environment , we next 324 

tested cholinergic and neuronal population response s to specifically altering the level of 325 

expected uncertainty of reward location in the URTask (Fig. 1A -C) . Increasing expected 326 

uncertainty is predicted to increase acetylcholine release and promote learning of the alter ed 327 

context ual state (e.g., cue reliability) , but not to otherwise change neuronal representations of328 

features of the context that remain stable 1,7 . In a familiar environment, mice were first trained 329 

under low uncertainty , with a single predictable reward delivered within a 10 cm wide zone . In 330 

the test session , mice completed at least 20 laps in the low uncertainty condition before the 331 

reward location contingency was abruptly changed so that the single reward on any given lap 332 

was delivered within a 1 m wide zone , still centred at the same position along the track (Fig. 333 

3A) . Thus, the only manipulation wa s a decrease in the predictability of reward location ,334 

experience d as an increase in expected uncertainty once the new reward location contingency 335 

wa s learnt. 336 

Because the manipulation increas ed the reward location distribution without shifting the 337 

average reward location , we observed limited behavioral adaptation . Mice exhibited a small 338 

decrease in anticipatory licking , with anticipato ry deceleration remaining consistent (Fig. 1C ,339 

3B ). Due to the random variability of the reward location after the reward contingency switch, 340

predicted increase in expected uncertainty as the more variable reward location is learnt (Fig. 345 

1A). 346

Acetylcholine signals a state change in expected uncertainty 652 

We examined acetylcholine release in the dRSC in response to manipulations of environmental 653 

context designed to selectively alter expected or unexpected uncertainty, either independently 654 

or in combination . Acetylcholine release was associated with increased expected uncertainty 655 

induced by reducing the reliability of reward location around a fixed position within a familiar 656 

environment (Fig. 3) . In contrast , acetylcholine release was not observed when unexpected 657 

uncert ainty was in troduced in a n otherwise stable context by a bruptly relocating the reward to 658 

a different re gion of a familiar environment (Fig. 4) . Notably , acetylcholine releas e was 659 

observed following exposure to a novel environment (Fig. 2), a manipulation that constitutes a660 

large contextual shift predicted to increase both unexpected uncertainty and , importantly ,661 

expected uncertaint y as new con tingenci es are learned. Thus, these initial findings broadly 662 

support theoretical models proposing that acetylcholine signals expected, rather than 663 

unexpected, uncertainty 1,7 .664 

We a lso observed increase d acetylcholine release when reward delivery shifted from an 665 

unreliable , b road zone to a reliable , narrow zone in a di stinct region of the track – a666 

manipulation that introduce d unexpected uncertainty against a background of high expected 667 

uncertainty (Fig. 5) . However , because acetylcholine release was selectively modulated by 668 

manipulations of expected uncertainty but not unexpected uncertainty in other experiments , we 669 

consider it unlikely that unexpected uncertainty accounted for the observed increase in 670 

cholinergic tone . Instead , we propose that th is increase in acetylcholine release reflect s a671 

transition between distinct states of expected uncertainty , specifically, a shift from high 672 

expected uncertainty ( broad reward zone) to low expected uncertainty (narrow reward zone) .673 

The interpretation that acetylcholine is sensitive to state transitions in reward reliability is 674 

consistent with emerging work showing that acetylcholine release scale s with uncertainty of 675 

reward outcome and can predic t adjustment of behavioural strategy 49 . However, our data 676 

further indicate that , at least in dRSC, acetylcholine release does not simply signal the absolute 677 

magnitude of expected uncertainty 1. Rather , acetylcholine appears to signal changes in reward 678
