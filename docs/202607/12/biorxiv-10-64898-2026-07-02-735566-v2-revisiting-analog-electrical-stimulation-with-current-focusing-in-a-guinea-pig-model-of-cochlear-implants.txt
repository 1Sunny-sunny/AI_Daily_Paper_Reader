Abstract: Modern cochlear implants (CIs) use pulsatile stimulation to restore hearing for 14 

individuals with severe hearing loss. CIs provide robust speech recognition in quiet but poorly 15 

represent temporal fine structure (TFS) , needed for challenging listening situations . Analog 16 

stimulation preserves the acoustic waveform and may better encode TFS , yet it has not been 17 

evaluated combin ed with modern current -focusing strategies. We compared neural responses in 18 

the inferior colliculus (IC) evoked by CI stimuli consisting of 100 pulses/s biphasic pulse trains 19 

and 100 cycles/s sinusoidal analog stimulation with monopolar, bipolar, and tripolar electrode 20 

configurations in urethane -anesthetized guinea pigs. Following cochlear implantation, multiunit 21 

activity was recorded from the tonotopic axis of the central nucleus of the IC using 16 -channel 22 

silicon probes. Detection thresholds, spread of excitation, vector strength, sustained response 23 

percentage, and temporal response properties were quantified. Analog stimulation consistently 24 

evoked significantly lower activation thresholds than pulsatile stimulation while maintaining 25 

comparable or sometimes narrower spatial selectivity across stimulation modes. In contrast, analog 26 

stimulation generated lower vector strength, larger tonic response components, and a pronounced 27 

level -dependent polarity effect. At low stimulus levels, responses were dominated by the cathodic 28 

phase of the sinusoidal waveform, whereas increasing stimulus level responses were elic ited by 29 

both phases, producing synchronization at twice the stimulus frequency. These findings 30 

demonstrate that stimulation waveform strongly influences temporal coding while having 31 

relatively little effect on the spatial distribution of neural activation. These results provide a 32 

physiological basis for reexamining analog stimulation as an alternative strategy for cochlear 33 

implant sound coding. 34

Introduction 37 

Cochlear implants (CIs) are widely regarded as one of the most successful neural prostheses. 38 

Average CI user s achieve 70 –80% accuracy in open -set sentence recognition in quiet environments 39 

(1) , but challenges persist regarding understanding speech in nois e, pitch perception (2,3) , and 40 

music appreciation (4,5) . Hearing restoration through CIs relies on two core aspects : how the 41 

acoustic signal is translated into electrical patterns (coding strategy) , and how those patterns are 42 

spatially distributed within the cochlea through the electrode array (electrode configuration) .43 

CIs process sound frequency through a series of bandpass filters that selectively deliver stimulation 44 

to the multiple, tonotopically arranged electrodes. The most common type of sound coding strategy 45 

used in modern CI s employs p ulsatile stimulation and some version of the Continuous Interleaved 46 

Sampling (CIS ) (6) strategy that temporally interleav es stimulation pulses across electrodes so that 47 

only a single electrode is active at a time , thereby prevent ing electrical field summation and likely 48 

redu cing channel interaction. This pulsatile strategy has proven highly effective for speech 49 

perception in quiet ; however, it provides only coarse (if any) representation s of intricate sound 50 

features such as temporal fine structure (TF S), which limit s performance in noisy environments or 51 

for complex tasks . In normal acoustic hearing, TFS cues are preserved for frequencies up to 52 

approximately 1.5kHz, and are represented by the synchronized spiking of auditory nerve fibers 53 

in response to pure tones or amplitude -modulated tones (7) . In CI hearing with the CIS -based 54 

strategy, TSF cues are not preserved because of the use of a fixed carrier rate (of at least 500 55 

pulses /s (pps) ). Only slow changes in envelope amplitude are available. Over the last decade, 56 

alternative sound coding strate gies have been developed to represent some aspects of TFS, but 57 

their efficacy for complex stimul us perception has not been demonstrated (8,9) and the lack of TFS 58

An alternative sound coding strategy based on Analog Stimulation (AS) , also called Simultaneous 61 

Analog Stimulation or SAS (13,14) , more faithfully represent s the acoustic waveform by 62 

delivering a continuous electrical signal (15) . In this strategy the envelope and all aspects of the 63 

TF S are encoded directly in the stimulus waveform , preserving the instantaneous amplitude 64 

variations of the filtered signal by translating them directly into a time -varying electrical 65 

waveform . This theoretically enables the auditory nerve to respond to the stimulus in a manner 66 

analogous to normal hearing. 67 

For a time, a subset of CI systems offered both CIS and SAS to recipients, allowing for direct 68 

clinical comparisons between the two strategies . A p lethora of early psychoacoustic studies found 69 

that SAS yielded overall compar ed speech perception performanc e similar to CIS alone (as 70 

summarized in Stupak et al. 201 8 (16) ) and a meaningful subset of users performed better with, 71 

and preferred, the analog stimulation strategy . For example, Koch et al. reported that one third of 72 

patients preferred to use the SAS strategy after three months of implant experience ; although mean 73 

sentence scores were similar between groups, word recognition scores were significantly higher 74 

for SAS users than for CIS users (17) . Similarly, another study found that half of subjects selected 75 

SAS as their preferred strategy, with the use of SAS result ing in higher overall patient performance 76 

(18) .77

intracochlear electrodes in the linear array of the cochlear implant to spatially restrict the electrical 80

Animals 104 

Eleven Hartley guinea pigs (Cavia Porcellus ) were used in this study. At the time of surgery, age 105 

ranged from 4 to 10 months , weight from 350 to 850 g and both sexes were used . Procurement, 106 

housing and all experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the National Institutes 107 

of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals as well as the approved animal care 108 

and use protocols at Mass Eye and Ear, Boston, MA. All animals used in this study had normal 109 

hearing confirmed with neurophysiological responses (see below (19,24,25) ).110

Electric stimulation 155 

This study reports the evoked responses obtained with pulsatile and analog stimulation combined 156 

with current focusing strategies: mono -, bi- and tripolar pulse trains (MP -P, BP -P and TP -P) and 157 

their equivalents with AS (MP -A, BP -A, and TP -A). All stimuli were charge -balanced to avoid 158 

ionic imbalance that could damage spiral ganglion neurons. All three configurations of stimulation 159 

described belo w have been widely used in the literature (e.g. (28,29) ). Specifically, the monopolar 160 

configuration uses one intra -cochlear active electrode and the extra -cochlear electrode as return 161 

electrode . Bipolar and tripolar also use one intra -cochlear active electrode but use respectively one 162 

or two neighboring electrodes for return ing current. For bipolar, return was always the basal 163 

neighbor while tripolar used the two flanking neighbors (one apical and one basal) as return s, and 164 

shared current equally (Fig. 1B ). 165

Inferior Colliculus Multi -Unit Activity (MUA) recordings and analysis. 174 

MUA were recorded in a similar fashion as in McInturff et al. 2023 , 2022 (30,31) . Briefly, r aw 175 

voltage signals from the IC recording channels were sampled at 25 kHz. An a verage of activity 176 

across all 16 recording channels was used as a global reference and subtracted from each single 177 

channel waveform to remove large artifacts present across channels ( e.g. heartbeat, breathing ). 178 

Waveforms were then stimulus -aligned. For pulsatile stimulation, s timulus artifacts were also 179 

minimized by zeroing the first 0. 7 ms following each pulse. For each channel, the average of the 180 

aligned waveform was then used as a local reference and subtracted from each individual 181 

waveform to remove local field potential ( LFP ) components still present in the signal. The 182 

resulting waveforms were then digitally filtered (5th order, Butterworth bandpass filter, 300 Hz to 183 

3 kHz). 184 

Multiunit response latencies were determined as the timestamps of local maxima above a voltage 185 

threshold . A 0.8 ms dead time was implemented following each event (32) to avoid counting event s186 

that are not biologic . Voltage thresholds for spike triggering were calculated separately for each 187 

channel as 4 x the standard deviation of voltage traces during the stimulus -off period (0.5 s). Post -188 

stimulus time histograms (PSTHs) with bin widths of 0. 6 ms to appropriately capture the sub -189 

millisecond temporal precision of IC neurons (33,34) , were constructed to study MUA offline, 190 

post -experiment .191

Data analysis 192 

D-prime : Offline analyses started with the characterization of thresholds and saturation levels, and 193 

“stimulation tuning curves” (STCs). We then conducted cumulative d-prime analysis of the PSTHs 194 

(d’ ) as previously described in Arenberg, Bierer and Middlebrooks 2010 (35) . Briefly, for each 195 

stimulus -level combination , a receiver -operating characteristic (ROC) curve was formed from the 196 

trial -by -trial PSTHs. For each channel , the average PSTH at a given level was considered the signal 197 

condition while the background condition consisted of the PSTH for the level below . The area 198 

under the ROC curve (AUC) yielded the proportion of trials in which the current level was 199 

discriminable from the previous level . That proportion was z-score d and multiplied by √2 to give 200 

the discrimination index ( d′). Cumulative d’ was finally defined by the su mmation of the current 201 

d’ and d’ -1 level. A log -linear correction was applied to avoid any extreme AUC value that could 202 

bias the analysis (36) . Detection threshold was taken as the interpolated stimulus level at 203 

which cumulative d′ = 2 for each recording site .204 

Spread of Excitation : The spread of activation along the tonotopic axis of the IC was represented 205 

by stimulation tuning curves ( STCs ), w ith a response contour of d′ = 2 as a function of the depth 206 

along the recording probe (vertical axis) and stimulus current level (horizontal axis) . This iso -rate 207 

curve was then used to calculate t he Spread of Excitation (SoE) as follow s. The recording channel 208 

with the lowest level to achieve d’ >2 served as the tip of the STC, then the SoE width was 209 

calculated as the distance (channel span) between this iso -rate curve boundary at 3 dB above 210 

response threshold .211

Responses to both pulsatile and analog stimulation showed strong temporal synchrony to the 287 

stimulation (Fig. 3). However, responses to analog stimulation (Fig. 3, right) have a strong, level -288 

dependent polarity effect (PE) that was not observed with pulse -train stimulation Fig. 3, left). This 289 

effect was seen for individual channels (Fig. 3A,B) and for average responses for all channels (Fig. 290

Discussion 365 

The present study evaluated for the first time the coupling of sinusoidal analog stimulation with 366 

current focus ing modes (bipolar, tripolar) with the objective of comparing IC responses to analog 367 

with more conventional pulsatile stimulation . Results replicated previous findings related to 368 

electrode configuration, namely that spread of activation narrowed (19,29) , and threshold of 369 

activation increas ed systematically with more focused mode s of stimulation (from monopolar, to 370 

bipolar, to tripolar ) (28,42) . Within mode of stimulation, analog stimulation always elicited 371 

responses with lower thresholds but similar spread of activation, apart from bipolar , which was 372 

narrower with analog stimulation . Last ly , IC responses to analog stimulation differed from 373 

pulsatile in three ways: 1) lower synchrony, 2) higher sustained/tonic activity, and 3) a level -374 

dependent polarity effect. 375

compared sinusoidally amplitude -modulated biphasic pulse trains stimulation to unmodulated 380 

pulse trains (43) . Note that stimuli used in that study consisted of longer phase duration pulses of 381 

200 μs compared to the 40 μs used in the present study. Th ese methodological difference s make 382 

results of this previous study difficult to compare with t he present ones .383 

In the present study, thresholds for pulsatile stimulation were, on average, 17 dB higher than for 384 

sinusoidal stimulation. Psychoacoustic studies have demonstrated that detection thresholds 385 

decrease systematically with increasing stimulus duration, reflecting the integration of stimulus 386

III.  Polarity -Dependent Responses, Apparent Frequency Doubling, and Implications for 477 

Pitch Coding 478 

A mechanistically informative finding of the present study is the strong polarity dependence of IC 479 

responses to analog stimulation and its interaction with stimulation level . At low stimulation levels, 480 

neural responses were predominantly evoked during the cathodic phase of the waveform, 481 

consistent with classical biophysical models showing that cathodic current preferentially 482 

depolarizes peripheral processes of AN fibers (55,77,78) . At higher stimulation levels, however, 483 

we observed robust spiking during both cathodic and anodic phases, leading to neural 484 

synchronization at twice the nominal stimulus frequency (i.e., an apparent 200 Hz response to a 485 

100 Hz sinusoidal waveform). This polarity dominance has been repeatedly observed in animal 486 

models (79 –81) . Briefly, it is known that SGNs peripheral processes are more sensitive to cathodic 487 

currents whereas their somas are preferentially depolarized by anodic currents. Since peripheral 488 

processes usually sit closer to the CI array, cathodic -first pulses are associated with lower 489 

thresholds. Those results prompted the idea that the difference in thresholds for cathodic and 490 

anodic stimuli, referred to as the polarity effect (PE) could be used to probe the presence or absence 491 

of peripheral processes (82) . Similarly, polarity -dependent recruitment shifts with increasing 492 

levels have been reported in the studies cited above, where higher current amplitudes reduce the 493 

asymmetry between anodic and cathodic excitation by recruiting additional neural elements, 494 

including central axons and nodes of Ranvier. 495 

Hair cells do not show synaptic activation to both phases of a pure tone, with only one direction 496 

of the cilia ry deflection being excitatory. Thus, sound -evoked neural responses only arise from 497 

one of the two phases of pure tone s (83,84) . On the other hand, it is well established that electric 498

IV.  Implications and future directions 522 

The present findings carry important implications for both auditory neuroscience and CI 523 

engineering and development . It is worth noting that the translation of focused stimulation to 524 

functional performance gains in CI users has been inconsistent. For example, bipolar or tripolar 525 

mode with pulsatile stimulation requires higher current to reach threshold and therefore more 526 

battery consumption (91) . A compounding issue is that focused stimulation is not necessarily 527 

beneficial at all electrode locations due to irregular local nerve survival patterns (92,93) . The 528 

observation that analog stimulation coupled with current focusing configurations , achieve d lower 529 

thresholds of neural activation suggests that it could improve stimulation efficiency, potentially 530 

reduce power consumption, channel interaction, and current spread, while preserving the temporal 531 

representation of sound . Current CI systems rely almost exclusively on brief biphasic pulses, 532 

largely for safety and technical simplicity. However, this design inherently sacrifices temporal 533 

energy integration efficiency and the encoding of temporal fine structure. Our results suggest that 534 

alternative stimulation paradigms employing analog waveforms may achieve equivalent neural 535 

activation with significantly lower peak currents, consistent with prior modeling and physiological 536 

studies (61,70) . Furthermore, the invariance of spatial tuning width with tripolar , and possible 537 

sharpening with bipolar and monopolar , indicates that such strategies need not compromise 538 

sp ectral resolution, a critical consideration for speech and music perception in CI users. 539 

Collectively, these findings reveal potential trade -offs between waveform structure, synchrony, 540 

and coding fidelity . Pulsatile stimulation ensures strong, temporally precise neural activation but 541 

promotes hypersynchrony. Analog stimulation reduces hypersynchrony and introduces level -542 

depend ent sustained dynamics that may more closely approximate acoustic activation patterns, yet 543 

it can generate complex nonlinear response transformations at higher levels that are still not fully 544
