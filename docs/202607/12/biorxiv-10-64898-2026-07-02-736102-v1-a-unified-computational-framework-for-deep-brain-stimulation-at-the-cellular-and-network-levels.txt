# Abstract 

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has been demonstrated to be a successful therapeutic intervention for neurological disorders, yet the mechanisms underlying its effects on neuronal circuits remain incompletely understood. In this study, we propose a comprehensive phenomenological computational model that accounts for the impact of electrical stimulation parameters on neuronal circuits while incorporating experimentally-validated synaptic and cellular constraints. We investigate how DBS pulses modulate spiking activity in populations of homogeneous neurons representing stimulated nuclei, systematically examining the influence of circuitry architecture, including synaptic connectivity strength (weak vs. strong) and organization (sparse vs. rich). To characterize how DBS-modulated neuronal activity propagates through downstream networks, we develop a simple encoder that reveals distinct encoding patterns arising from different architectural configurations of stimulated nuclei. Furthermore, by connecting stimulated nuclei to recurrently connected neuronal populations, we examine the propagation of DBS-modulated neuronal synchrony across various circuit motifs. Our results demonstrate that three critical factors shape DBS-modulated neuronal activity: (a) the intrinsic synaptic and cellular properties of stimulated nuclei, (b) the architectural organization of stimulated nuclei in terms of synaptic strength and connectivity density, and (c) the circuit motifs formed by postsynaptic targets of stimulated nuclei. This unified model provides a mechanistic framework for understanding DBS representation and propagation in neuronal networks, offering insights that may inform optimization of stimulation parameters for clinical applications. 

# Author summary 

Computational models of deep brain stimulation have proven to be supremely useful in disentangling the clinical benefits and adverse effects observed in the treatment of a variety of conditions. Despite this, the capacity for many of the existent computational models to account for micro/meso-circuit activation remains limited, as the major techniques rely on detailed characterization of tracts surrounding the DBS electrode, or depend on an non-physiologically constrained injected current intending to mimic the influence of electrical stimulation. # Introduction 1

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a neuromodulatory therapy used for a myriad of 2

movement disorders [1–3], with a growing application to other psychiatric and 3

neurological conditions [4 – 6]. Different hypotheses have been suggested to describe the 4

method of action of DBS [7–9] and various computational models were developed to 5

support those hypotheses [10 –12]. Where, discounting non-neuronal effects, the primary 6

effect is considered to be axonal activation. These axonal interactions include 7

antidromic engagement, encapsulating a more global response to DBS. The localized 8

impact of deep brain stimulation is also heterogeneous, dependent on the target 9

nuclei [12], as well as the intranuclear location [13], which are thought to be driven by 10 

the specific organization of afferents as well as meso-network architecture. Deep brain 11 

stimulation also elicits different forms of short and long term synaptic plasticity [12, 14]. 12 

The complexities of the response to deep brain stimulation is what makes the versatility 13 

of computational models so useful as an exploratory and explanatory tool. The 14 

implementation of deep brain stimulation within computational models varies widely, 15 

we categorized DBS computational models in the literature based on two levels of 16 

descriptions, (1) detailed biophysical models of (2) abstract models. Detailed biophysical 17 

models of DBS were developed to study how physical characteristics of electrodes, 18 

stimulation parameters, and tissue substrates contribute to neural activities [10, 15, 16]. 19 

These methods allow for high accuracy characterization of neuronal engagement, 20 

accounting for non-faradaic/non-linear interactions, and the pulse waveform, which have 21 

been shown to substantially influence the resulting neural recruitment [11, 17, 18]. 22 

However, these methods depend on the accuracy and existence of a 3D representation of 23 

the neuronal structure [19], and these models have an immense computational burden, 24 

which makes it prohibitive to study how DBS modulates dynamics of a large population 25 

of neurons. Abstract models, consider the impact of DBS by some simplification, such 26 

as the injection of supra-threshold current [20, 21], or of some modified rate function [22]. 27 

The simplicity of this method, and lack of physical dependence, allows abstract methods 28 

to more thoroughly explore the response of large networks, and the range of responses in 29 

a more computationally tractable manner. Although abstract models can describe some 30 

aspects of DBS-induced changes (even at the firing rate level [22]), these abstractions 31 

can miss important effects such as antidromic activation [22], somatic-axonal 32 

decoupling [20, 21], or the spatial engagement shaped by stimulation settings. In this 33 

work we intend do develop a technique that can account for the varying impacts of deep 34 

brain stimulation, without loss of generalizability to more detailed or abstract systems. 35 

Building on our previous computational models of DBS that capture various 36 

temporal dynamics of instantaneous firing rates of stimulated neurons in different 37 

sub-cortical regions (DBS targets) [12], we propose a unifying algorithmic framework 38 

that enables modelling the impact of DBS in any spiking neural network, conceptually 39 

described in [23] and implemented initially in [13]. Specifically, inspired by a previous 40 

work [24], we model the impact of DBS using direct synapse activation, or when missing 41

# Discussion 222 

In this work we described a comprehensive method by which one can implement the 223 

impact of DBS in any spiking neural network. By directly on afferent and efferent 224 

synapse populations we are able to account for a variety of features thought to 225 

contribute to the impact of stimulation, such as recruitment probability, and latency of 226 

activation (Figure 2). To showcase how the effects of short term plasticity interact with 227 

DBS we constructed both a feed-forward and recurrent network, and stimulated at 228 

different frequencies and intensities to probe the response, both in the spiking activity 229 

and proxy for local field potential. Within this we demonstrate how low intensity DBS 230 

may fail to recruit the efferent populations at low frequencies when under the influence 231 

of short-term facilitation at the higher frequencies. Similarly, how at high intensity and 232 

high frequency the recurrent recruitment in the stimulated population may be lost from 233 

short term depression. Prior methods of simulating the interaction of DBS with short 234 

term plasticity did so by introducing a time-varying current that would be injected to 235 

simulate the effect of DBS [21, 22]. By contrast, our method does not necessitate the 236 

creation of currents specific to DBS, as it acts solely on existent synapses, thereby 237 

inheriting their synaptic dynamics. 238

Since deep brain stimulation is often thought to act as an information lesion, we 239 

investigated how DBS impacts different motifs on the ability encode information. As 240 

illustrated in Figure 3-5 the capacity for the circuit to support the propagation of DBS, 241 

and in turn how readily it is able to lesion information processing is highly sensitive to 242 

both parameters and motif. In the simplest feed forward case (Figure 4) we see that how 243 

DBS effects efferent nuclei, and when DBS optimally interferes, or replaces the encoded 244 

information depends greatly on the modality of encoding. We depict three different 245 

encoding schemes, temporal precision, rate coding, and normalized rate coding. For 246 

temporal precision and normalized rate encoding there is a non-monotonic relationship 247 

to encoding with respect to connectivity strength and probability. Intuitively, the pure 248 

rate encoding scheme follows a monotonic relationship, as in a feed forward network the 249 

increase in connectivity directly corresponds to an increased drive by DBS. When 250 

comparing the temporal precision across motifs however, we diverge from [26], as 251 

inhibition stabilizes the spike propagation, especially in the lateral inhibition case [33]. 252 

The intent of these simulations is to showcase that the effect of DBS is shaped strongly 253 

by the properties of the network being stimulated, for which this method provides easy 254 

interrogation. An exemplary interrogation is of a recurrent network representing part of 255 

the basal ganglia, as it pertains to features such as Evoked Recurrent Neural Activity 256 

(ERNA) [13] and Delayed Local Evoked Potentials [15] known to be related to clinical 257 

improvement in conditions such as Parkinson’s disease and Dystonia [13, 34]. These 258 

responses are observed as an oscillatory response to stimulation in the local field 259 

potential, which grows with frequency and intensity. For Figures 6-7 the recurrent 260 

circuit has short term facilitation between the nuclei, and we can directly see the 261 

interplay of the plasticity dynamics, wherein the apparent oscillation is strengthened by 262 

the facilitation at higher frequencies and the intensity of stimulation. 263 

Computational models of deep brain stimulation have been growing in popularity for 264 

fibre recruitment, especially with utilization of volume conductor models and similar to 265 

estimate the volume of activated tissue (VAT) [35,36]. These techniques function depend 266 

on a detailed characterization of the intersecting fibres in order to estimate what fibres 267 

might interact with each DBS pulse. While many trajectories have been characterized 268 

in good detail, such as the hyperdirect pathway [11], many tracts such as those between 269 

the basal ganglia nuclei are only roughly estimated by probabilistic tractography [37, 38]. 270 

These probabilistic estimates of connectivity within the basal ganglia are unable to 271 

meaningfully distinguish subthalamic nucleus-globus pallidus externus (STN-GPe) and 272 

STN-globus pallidus internus (STN-GPi), which are thought to play an important role, 273 

especially the STN-GPe connectivity in the clinical efficacy of treatment of Parkinson’s 274 

disease and Dystonia [13, 34]. To allow for investigating the differential fibre recruitment 275 

profiles of these precise fibres we set out to develop this framework. 276 

Limitations 277 

While this method allows for a drop-in implementation of DBS into existing networks, 278 

there are many factors that it does not account for without careful consideration. The 279 

calculation of the number of recruited efferent, and afferent fibres is dependent on the 280 

user to determine, from methods such as the volume of activated tissue. This method 281 

also does not account for any direct sub-threshold changes to dynamics that DBS may 282 

induce, which may interact with the soma leaving it in a more or less excitable state 283 

which this method fails to capture. Further, this method can introduce a great degree of 284 

computational complexity in certain simulators, such as NEST and Brian, as all 285 

connections become interposed with parrot neurons, requiring then more spike 286 

propagation, which can substantially slow simulation time. 287

# Conclusion 288 

With the growing efforts to optimize and better understand the therapeutic mechanism 289 

of DBS, fibre specific recruitment is thought to play an integral role. Herein we present 290 

a standard computational framework to interrogate fibre specific activations, in a 291 

physiologically constrained computationally tractable manner. The model allows for the 292 

integration of known physiological properties, such as those of activation latencies and 293 

dose responses, while allowing for exploration of fibre recruitment properties for less 294 

defined fibres, e.g. intra-basal-ganglia fibres. The model of stimulation generalizes to 295 

any spiking network stimulation by injecting spike times directly at synaptic elements. 296 

This direct integration allows for features such as short term synaptic plasticity to be 297 

included in the effect of DBS. To showcase the tractability and generalization of this 298 

approach we generated circuits of different structural and temporal motifs and showed 299 

how differentially DBS affected these networks. 300
