Abstract 36 

Motor learning is a lifelong process, from infancy through old age. Acquiring new motor 37 

actions through repetitive practice requires adjusting motor output in response to sensory 38 

input and integrating them to facilitate learning. For this to occur, the central nervous system 39 

must flexibly predict and adapt to the dynamic interplay between sensory inputs and motor 40 

outputs. Although overall brain function changes with age, it remains unclear how flexible 41 

brain networks, reflecting moment-to-moment reconfiguration of large-scale brain networks, 42 

influence motor learning ability with aging. To address this, we designed a visuomotor learning 43 

task involving both younger and older adults and quantitatively assessed brain network 44 

flexibility, leveraging multichannel electroencephalography (EEG) in humans. We found age-45 

group differences in motor learning properties, brain network flexibility, and their neural 46 

relationships. In younger adults, the learning aftereffect was associated with brain network 47 

flexibility during motor preparation in the learning task. However, this association was not 48 

observed in older adults. Together, our findings suggest that brain network flexibility during 49 

motor preparation was critical for acquiring and maintaining new motor actions in younger 50 

adults, but that this coupling was attenuated with aging. 51

Introduction 62 

More recently, many people live well beyond the age of 60 and maintain good health. In 63 

our current reality, various types of motor and cognitive learning occur throughout a lifetime, 64 

spanning from early infancy to old age. Notably, learning new motor skills, such as playing 65 

musical instruments, remains feasible even in older adulthood. The definition of skill 66 

emphasizes reducing variability in action outcomes, and this variability progressively 67 

decreases throughout the learning process. However, the roles of movement variability and 68 

neural flexibility in motor learning remain subjects of ongoing debate. Although movement 69 

variability (i.e., exploratory behavior) is thought to facilitate motor learning (Ranganathan and 70 

Newell, 2010; Uehara et al., 2019; Ranganathan et al., 2020), and greater motor exploration 71 

has been linked to better learning outcomes (Wu et al., 2014), neural relationships remain 72 

unclear. A previous human electroencephalogram (EEG) study demonstrated that overall brain 73 

network flexibility reflects key neural processes involved in skill formation and predicts musical 74 

performance skill in proficient brass players. Notably, brain network flexibility primarily 75 

contributed to feedback control, rather than feedforward control, during skilled musical 76 

performance (Uehara et al., 2023). This study also utilized the brain network flexibility, which 77 

is defined as a quantitative index of node flexibility, capturing the frequency with which nodes 78 

change their module assignments over a given period. Here, nodes represent brain regions, 79 

and a module represents a group of brain regions that are more strongly interconnected with 80 

each other. This finding sheds new light on the crucial role of brain network flexibility in 81 

sensorimotor integration and the regulation of consolidated motor function. Although this 82 

analytical approach, namely brain network flexibility, is highly mathematical and initially 83 

appeared to be disconnected from biological mechanisms, accumulating empirical evidence 84 

suggests that it reliably captures fundamental properties of brain function (Braun et al., 2015; 85 

Betzel et al., 2017; Reddy et al., 2018; Monteiro et al., 2019; Uehara et al., 2023). Building on 86 

this body of evidence, we argue that brain network flexibility, reflecting moment-to-moment 87

From the perspective of aging, considering the age-related alterations in the neural 90 

system, as is widely accepted, age-related neural changes result in a gradual decline in 91 

sensorimotor function and learning ability (Vercillo et al., 2017; Hehl et al., 2020; Pauelsen et 92 

al., 2020). This decline is associated with reductions in white and gray matter volumes 93 

(Courchesne et al., 2000; Good et al., 2001; Jernigan et al., 2001) and alterations in white 94 

matter microstructural organization (Cox et al., 2016; Beck et al., 2021), changes in brain 95 

activity, and network dynamics (Garrett et al., 2013; Monteiro et al., 2019; Frolov et al., 2020; 96 

Goelman et al., 2023), and declines in dopaminergic and gamma-aminobutyric acid 97 

neurotransmission (Kaasinen and Rinne, 2002; Guitart-Masip et al., 2016; Hermans et al., 98 

2018) even in healthy aging. These neural factors may individually or collectively disrupt the 99 

coordinated operation of brain networks across multiple levels, ultimately leading to behavioral 100 

changes (Marstaller et al., 2015; Shafiei et al., 2019; Yoshimura et al., 2020). There is 101 

substantial literature supporting theories of age-related changes in brain function, including 102 

findings of increased neural recruitment in the form of overactivation, increased connectivity, 103 

and frontal compensation. Of note, the Hemispheric Asymmetry Reduction in Older Adults 104 

(HAROLD) model, Posterior-Anterior Shift in Aging (PASA) theory, the Compensation-Related 105 

Utilization of Neural Circuits Hypothesis (CRUNCH), and the Scaffolding Theory of Aging and 106 

Cognition (STAC) serve as conceptual frameworks (Cabeza, 2002; Davis et al., 2008; Reuter-107 

Lorenz and Cappell, 2008; Reuter-Lorenz and Park, 2014; Son et al., 2024). 108 

Over the past decade, the concept of brain network flexibility has also emerged as an 109 

important framework for understanding age-related changes. For example, a human functional 110 

magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study has demonstrated that the strength of brain network 111 

flexibility was reduced in older adults, and the flexibility of the brain area in the prefrontal 112 

cortex was associated with motor skills including lower error during bimanual motor 113

whether age-group changes in brain network flexibility could hinder motor learning. 115 

Here, we aimed to investigate whether age-group changes in brain network flexibility 116 

affect motor learning ability in both young adults (YA) and older adults (OA). We utilized 117 

multichannel scalp electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings while participants from both YA 118 

and OA groups engaged in learning an error-based visuomotor adaptation task. In the present 119 

study, we hypothesized that entire brain network flexibility would increase with aging due to 120 

excessive neural noise (Garrett et al., 2010) and compensatory reorganization (Cabeza, 121 

2002). This increased flexibility becomes functionally ineffective in supporting motor learning 122 

in older adults. This is because higher brain network flexibility in older adults may reflect 123 

compensatory recruitment of neural resources rather than efficient network reconfiguration. 124 

Consequently, age-related neural deterioration may weaken the link between flexibility and the 125 

stabilization of motor memories, leading to a decoupling between neural flexibility and motor 126 

learning. We hypothesized that frameworks such as CRUNCH and STAC may provide a more 127 

compelling explanation than alternative frameworks, as mentioned above. Viewed holistically, 128 

focusing on whole-brain dynamics in terms of brain network flexibility is essential for further 129 

elucidating neural mechanisms of motor learning ability in the context of aging. 130 

Moreover, an additional aim of this study was to determine how brain network flexibility 131 

changes over time during motor adaptation and to clarify its functional role in motor learning. 132 

Previous EEG studies have suggested that the suppression of trial-by-trial neural variability, 133 

as indexed by event-related potentials, was closely associated with enhanced perceptual 134 

abilities (Arazi et al., 2017; Daniel and Dinstein, 2021). Based on these findings, we 135 

hypothesized that the reduction in brain network flexibility during the motor learning process 136 

contributes to the stabilization of motor learning in young adults. Finally, we postulated that 137 

early consolidation of motor adaptation via wakeful rest (i.e., offline learning) may explain how 138 

brain network flexibility underpins a newly formed internal model of motor adaptation 139

Materials and Methods 145 

We utilized an error-based visuomotor adaptation task to assess individual motor 146 

learning abilities. To estimate the number of participants required to reliably assess motor 147 

learning effects, we conducted an a priori power analysis using the G*Power program 148 

(Erdfelder et al., 2009) with the effect size set at 0.49 based on our prior data collection from 149 

seven healthy right-handed young individuals (26.4 ± 5.5 yrs, range: 22 -35 yrs, two females). 150 

Using the same experimental protocol described later (see Experimental task and protocols ), 151 

the effect size was determined assuming changes in motor performance (i.e., the degree of 152 

the deviation of cursor position) between the early, middle, and late phases of adaptation. The 153 

statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed) for a one-way repeated measures 154 

analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a within-subject factor (adaptation phase). Despite the 155 

sample size calculation being conducted only for the younger group, this a priori power 156 

analysis estimation showed that at least 25 participants were required for one group, and 157 

effect sizes were reported for each statistic to confirm the robustness of the results. Based on 158 

this procedure, fifty-four healthy participants were recruited and divided into two groups, each 159 

consisting of 27 participants. The groups were categorized as younger-aged (YA) (27.6 ± 5.8 160 

yrs, range: 20 - 38 yrs, 13 females) and older-aged (OA) (66.8 ± 7.4 yrs, range: 58 - 80 years 161 

old, 12 females). All participants were naïve concerning our visuomotor adaptation task, had a 162 

normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no history of musculoskeletal or neurological disorders, 163 

and were right-handed according to the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield, 1971). 164 

Before data collection, all participants in the OA group underwent a Mini-Mental State 165

Experimental task and protocols 177 

Experimental protocol, trial structure, and apparatus are illustrated in Figure 1. The 178 

electromagnetically shielded experimental room used in this study was completely darkened 179 

to minimize the availability of any visual information that was not projected on a PC monitor. 180 

Participants were seated 120 cm in front of a 24.5-inch (1920 x 1080 pixels resolution) 181 

computer display comfortably positioned on a chair with the right forearm supported by an 182 

armrest and a chin resting comfortably on a chin rest to ensure a consistent position 183 

throughout the experimental session. 184 

Prior to all data collection, participants underwent a 3-min resting-state EEG recording 185 

during which they remained at rest with their eyes open and were instructed not to focus on 186 

any particular thoughts. During the resting-state data collection, participants were instructed to 187 

see the fixation cross placed at the center of the monitor. Following this, participants 188 

performed three sustained maximal voluntary contractions (MVCs) of the right index and little 189 

fingers, respectively. The maximum force was defined as an MVC value and used to 190 

determine cursor control during the task. 191

On each trial, the target moved straight ahead at a constant speed of 10.8 cm/s in one of 192 

five directions (10, 11, 12, 1, or 2 o’clock) in a randomized order within each session. 193 

Participants were instructed to track a moving target on the screen by controlling the cursor as 194 

accurately as possible. Our experimental system allowed participants to freely control cursor 195 

speed and direction by pressing two force-torque transducers (USL06-H5, Tec Gihan, Co. 196 

Ltd., Japan) using their right index and little fingertips (Figure 1A). Our task design was 197 

inspired by previous literature (O’Sullivan et al., 2009), which demonstrated that the 198 

distribution of coordinated force made by the index and little fingers is greater when 199 

heteronymous effectors (e.g., index and little fingers) are used together than when 200 

homonymous combinations are used. This finding implies that the nervous system must 201 

adaptively coordinate the muscles by integrating both effort and variability. By employing a 202 

non-habitual effector like the little finger, we investigated how the brain flexibly learns force 203 

mapping in unfamiliar coordination contexts. The cursor direction controlled by a participant 204 

was determined using a composite vector related to the force exerted in the z-direction output 205 

from the index and little fingertips. In other words, by modulating the forces exerted by the 206 

index finger and little finger, participants could freely move the cursor in the left-right direction. 207 

Related to this, the cursor velocity was modulated according to the force applied in the z-208 

direction of the force sensors, such that greater force resulted in faster cursor movement. 209 

Force signals were amplified via an amplifier device (GDA-06B, Tec Gihan, Co., Ltd., Japan) 210 

and then stored on a computer via a NI-DAQ device (USB-6002, National Instruments, United 211 

States) at a sampling frequency of 1 kHz with analog-to-digital conversion. The index finger 212 

controlled leftward cursor movement, while the little finger was responsible for rightward 213 

cursor movement. Therefore, the cursor direction was determined by the vector component 214 

calculated from the force applied by the index and little fingers. Cursor velocity was also 215 

controlled by varying the pressure applied to the force-torque transducers. 216

For the experimental protocol, participants were asked to learn the force mapping in 221 

their right index and little fingers through an error-based visuomotor adaptation task. The task 222 

began with a familiarization period consisting of 25 null movement trials performed without any 223 

added sensor perturbation. After the familiarization, we asked participants to complete a total 224 

of 200 trials. The experiment consisted of three conditions: baseline (unperturbed), adaptation 225 

(perturbed), and aftereffect. As shown in Figure 1B, at the beginning of each trial, the target 226 

circle appeared after a 5-second preparation period, signaling the “Go Cue”. Participants were 227 

asked to accurately track the target by controlling the cursor. At the end of each trial, they 228 

received binary feedback on their performance, with green or red squares indicating success 229 

or failure, respectively. Figure 1D illustrates the sequence of all trials. In the baseline condition 230 

consisting of 50 trials, participants tracked the moving cursor with a force sensor sensitivity 231 

ratio of 1:1 between the index and little fingers. During the adaptation phase, participants 232 

completed 100 trials with the force sensor 233 

sensitivity ratio of 1:3 between the index and little fingers, requiring them to adapt to the de 234 

novo force mapping. To investigate the relationship between brain network flexibility and early 235 

consolidation of motor adaptation induced by neural replay, we asked participants to complete 236 

an additional session without the sensor sensitivity changes after a 10-minute break. Previous 237 

motor adaptation studies have commonly employed retention tests after delays of several 238 

minutes (e.g., 10 min) to evaluate the stability of newly acquired motor memories (Bönstrup et 239 

al., 2019). From a molecular perspective, the 10-min rest, i.e., wakeful rest, likely captured the 240 

earliest stage of offline memory processing, during which consolidation-related molecular 241 

cascades may have already been initiated (Dudai, 2004; Parra et al., 2026). To do this, the 242 

aftereffect was assessed in a 50-trial session with sensor sensitivity returned to baseline after 243

Data recording 253 

Behavioral data. Time series of the two-dimensional target and cursor positions (x- and 254 

y-axes) on the screen were continuously acquired at a sampling rate of 100 Hz using the 255 

dedicated software package as described above. 256 

Electroencephalography. To record neural activity during data collection, we utilized a 257 

multi-channel EEG amplifier system (actiCHamp, Brain Products GmbH, Germany). EEG 258 

signals were continuously recorded from 63 electrodes positioned on the scalp following the 259 

international 10/10 system, using active electrodes embedded in a wearable elastic cap 260

was placed at AFz. During the data recording, all electrodes were referenced to the right 262 

earlobe, while the left earlobe was also recorded as an electrode for re-referencing. 263 

Electrooculography (EOG) signals are widely used to detect activities of eye movements and 264 

are available to remove eye movement-related noises from the EEG signals. EOG signals 265 

were recorded using electrodes placed above and below the left eye for monitoring vertical 266 

eye movements and below the left and right eyes for monitoring horizontal eye movements. 267 

EEG and EOG signals were amplified and digitized with 24-bit resolution at a sampling rate of 268 

1 kHz. These signals were continuously stored on a personal computer via BrainVision 269

To further determine how brain network flexibility relates to the ability to maintain and recall a 317

To quantify functional brain networks and their network flexibility, we used the 338 

preprocessed EEG data obtained from 63 channels and computed phase-based measures of 339 

neural oscillatory activity (Lachaux et al., 1999; Varela et al., 2001; van Diessen et al., 2015). 340 

This approach aligns with the dynamical systems observed in the brain (Lachaux et al., 1999), 341 

where phase synchrony of neural activity significantly contributes to information processing 342

For the behavioral findings, YA and OA groups were able to adapt sufficiently to changes 682 

in the novel force mapping (i.e., previously unexperienced mapping). Although older adults 683 

had less precise cursor control compared to younger adults at the baseline (Supplementary 684 

Figure 1), they exhibited motor learning effects similar to those of younger adults as they 685 

could adapt to the novel mapping (from the baseline of 1:1 to the adaptation of 1:3) (Figure 2). 686 

As an overview of the motor learning task and definitions of the behavioral parameters, we 687 

considered the learning slope to reflect the magnitude of trial-by-trial changes in motor 688 

performance in response to experienced errors, whereas the aftereffect reflected the 689 

maintenance and recall of the internal model for future use. (Heuer and Hegele, 2008; Hegele 690 

and Heuer, 2010; Shadmehr et al., 2010; Krakauer and Mazzoni, 2011; Krakauer et al., 2019; 691 

Leech et al., 2022). From a neural perspective, previous studies suggest that neural activity 692 

emerging during the post-learning rest period is interpreted as the replay or reactivation of 693 

recently acquired motor memories (Buch et al., 2021; Griffin et al., 2025; Sjøgård et al., 2025). 694 

Accordingly, evaluating motor performance following a 10-min rest period may provide 695 

valuable insight into early offline processes that contribute to the stabilization and 696 

maintenance of internal models acquired from motor adaptation. We found that older adults 697 

exhibited a significantly steeper learning slope than younger adults (Figure 2B), whereas no 698 

age-related differences were observed in the aftereffect (Figure 2C). It can be interpreted as a 699 

scenario in which the rapid reduction in performance errors observed in older adults was 700

constraint on learning efficiency in older adults. This finding aligns with a previous human EEG 724

recruitment of alternative neural resources and reorganization of functional networks 743

We further discuss perspectives on the frequency-specific contributions of brain network 755 

flexibility to motor learning properties. Despite the complexity of interpreting frequency-specific 756 

effects, identifiable roles emerge from our findings. Our LASSO regression analyses revealed 757 

that the aftereffect was positively associated with alpha-band flexibility and negatively 758 

associated with theta- and beta-band flexibility in young adults. It is well established that alpha 759 

oscillations are ubiquitous in the brain (Berger, 1929), yet their functional significance remains 760 

incompletely understood. Recently, alpha oscillations are thought to play a central role in 761 

regulating network information flow (Sadaghiani et al., 2012; Scheeringa et al., 2012) and 762 

attention (Romei et al., 2012; Herring et al., 2015; Peylo et al., 2021). Rather than directly 763 

reflecting motor output, alpha-band dynamics have been linked to the establishment of task-764 

ready network configurations and the gating of neural resources (Klimesch et al., 2007; Ai and 765 

Ro, 2014; Babiloni et al., 2014; Brickwedde et al., 2019; Borra et al., 2023). Therefore, the 766 

reduction in alpha-band flexibility observed in this study may reflect the stabilization of 767 

preparatory network states as learning progresses, reducing the need for continual large-scale 768 

network reconfiguration. Consistent with this interpretation, our predictive models using 769 

LASSO identified alpha-band brain network flexibility during motor preparation as a key 770 

predictor of the learning aftereffect (Figure 6). 771 

As a final point, interpreting LZC as an index of neural complexity offers additional 772 

insight into our findings. We examined LZC because understanding local signal complexity is 773 

necessary for a more comprehensive understanding of brain network flexibility. The present 774 

study found a dissociation between resting-state LZC and brain network flexibility across the 775 

age groups, with higher LZC in older adults but comparable brain network flexibility (Figure 3). 776 

Drawing on previous evidence and our findings, we postulate that LZC is likely a sensitive 777 

indicator of local neural dynamics (Schartner et al., 2015; Medel et al., 2023) and clearly 778

brain network flexibility may saturate owing to constraints on network reconfiguration, possibly 780 

because resilience mechanisms maintain large-scale network organization throughout the 781 

lifespan (Heisz et al., 2015; Gonzalez-Escamilla et al., 2018; Neudorf et al., 2024). This 782 

resilience mechanism and sensitivity to aging may lead to the resting-state dissociation. 783 

Another noteworthy finding is that the age-related elevations in LZC and preserved brain 784 

network flexibility were confined to the resting state and motor preparation, disappearing 785 

during motor execution. This selective manifestation suggests that the aging brain employs a 786 

proactive neural strategy characterized by hyper-complex idling. By maintaining a broader 787 

repertoire of dynamic states and high network reconfigurability before movement, older adults 788 

may create a functional buffer that compensates for age-related neurobiological declines 789 

(Goble et al., 2009; Coxon et al., 2014; Serbruyns et al., 2015; Frolov et al., 2020; Zapparoli et 790 

al., 2022; Goelman et al., 2023). This heightened readiness may facilitate the successful 791 

transition into a stabilized, task-optimal state during motor action, where motor performance 792 

becomes indistinguishable from that of the younger adults. Moreover, we statistically 793 

confirmed the significant positive correlations between LZC and brain network flexibility, as 794 

summarized in Table 1. This implies that both measures may be governed by a shared 795 

dynamical mechanism spanning local and global neural dynamics. It should be noted that the 796 

observed reductions in LZC for alpha- and beta-frequency bands might be partially attributed 797 

to the well-established event-related desynchronization (ERD) observed during motor 798 

behavior (Pfurtscheller and Lopes Da Silva, 1999). While ERD is conventionally characterized 799 

as a reduction in oscillatory power, complexity measures quantify the temporal irregularity of 800 

neural activity. Therefore, although ERD-related power changes and complexity changes may 801 

be linked, they do not necessarily reflect the same underlying neural phenomenon. Rather, 802 

they likely provide complementary information about cortical dynamics. 803

cerebellum plays a key role in the regulation of motor learning, including adaptation (Krakauer 806 

and Mazzoni, 2011; Tsay et al., 2024). However, non-invasive measurement of cerebellar 807 

electrophysiology is hindered by the limited spatial resolution of human scalp EEG recordings 808 

(Andersen et al., 2020). In principle, studies employing EEG to delineate functional networks 809

said, the cerebellum is connected to broader cortical regions, including the motor, frontal, 811

understanding motor learning. Second, the present study compared only momentary 815 

differences between the two specific age groups. To account for the entire lifespan, the next 816 

step should be to employ a cross-sectional study design that assesses age-related changes 817 

along a continuum from infancy to old age. 818 

In conclusion, the present study revealed dissociated roles of brain network flexibility in 819 

motor learning across the different age groups. Brain network flexibility during motor 820 

preparation, rather than motor execution, was critical for maintaining new motor action in 821 

younger adults, but this coupling was attenuated in older adults aged approximately 60 years 822 

and above. Understanding brain network flexibility and its state could facilitate the 823 

development of novel tools for enhancing motor learning and mitigating age-related learning 824 

limitations. 825
