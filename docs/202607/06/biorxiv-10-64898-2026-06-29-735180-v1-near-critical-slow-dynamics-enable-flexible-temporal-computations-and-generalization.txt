## Abstract 

Although neural activity often evolves along low-dimensional manifolds, such descriptions do not 1

explain the dynamical mechanisms that generate, constrain, and stabilize computation. Identifying 2

these mechanisms is essential for predicting responses to perturbations, understanding generalization 3

to untrained signals, and explaining how similar computations arise from distinct circuit implementa-4

tions. Here we use recurrent neural networks trained on an interval timing task as a model system 5

to uncover the dynamical mechanisms of neural computation. We show that, despite converging 6

to highly diverse attractor architectures, trained networks share a conserved transient dynamics. 7

During learning, networks self-organize near dynamical bifurcations, forming structured ghost sets 8

of slow points characterized by graded spectra of near-zero eigenvalues. These slow sets form a 9

dynamical scaffold that constrains trajectory evolution. Inputs transiently reconfigure the vector 10 

field and reposition activity within this scaffold, while the underlying slow set governs subsequent 11 

dynamics. As a result, temporal computation is implemented through structured transient evolution 12 

rather than convergence to fixed points or persistent activity states. The extent of the slow sets 13 

predicts generalization to unseen temporal intervals, and networks lacking such organization fail to 14 

extrapolate reliably. To test sufficiency, we construct a minimal dynamical system endowed with 15 

analogous slow set geometry that reproduces interval timing without learning, providing a benchmark 16 

for identifying the essential dynamical ingredients of temporal computation. Together, these results 17 

identify structured slow transients as a candidate dynamical mechanism for temporal computation, 18 

provide a mechanistic interpretation of slow low-dimensional manifolds as emergent consequences 19 

of underlying state-space structure, and suggest that computational capacity in near-critical systems 20 

arises from the organization of transient flow rather than attractor states alone. 21 

Keywords slow manifolds | criticality | generalization | timing 22

## Introduction 23 

How neural populations perform computations through their dynamics remains a fundamental challenge in neuroscience. 24 

Across perception, memory, decision-making and action, behavior emerges from the transformation of information 25 

through precisely organized patterns of population activity evolving over time. Understanding neural computation 26 

therefore requires identifying the dynamical principles that generate, constrain and control these activity trajectories. 27 

Recent work has shown that neural population activity often evolves along low-dimensional trajectories Khona and 28 

Fiete (2022); Langdon et al. (2023); Perich and Gallego (2025). In interval timing tasks, in particular, neural dynamics 29 

occupy low-dimensional manifolds whose geometry and traversal speed correlate with behavioral output (Jazayeri and 30 

Afraz, 2017; Wang et al., 2018; Remington et al., 2018; Egger et al., 2019; Saxena and Cunningham, 2019; Bi and 31 

Zhou, 2020; Jazayeri and Ostojic, 2021; Beiran et al., 2023). Similar low-dimensional structures emerge in recurrent 32 

neural networks trained on analogous tasks, suggesting that they reflect general principles of neuronal computation 33 

(Wang et al., 2018; Remington et al., 2018; Beiran et al., 2023). These observations have motivated a geometric view in 34 

which computation is associated with low-dimensional manifolds, with task outputs encoded by positions or velocities 35 

along the manifold. 36 

However, this framework leaves a fundamental mechanistic question unresolved: what dynamical structures generate 37 

and regulate these trajectories? Identifying a manifold describes where neural activity lies, but not the mechanisms 38 

that constrain the temporal evolution and determine its timescales. This distinction is important because the same 39 

low-dimensional trajectory can, in principle, arise from fundamentally different underlying dynamics, leading to 40 

different predictions about robustness, perturbation responses and generalization. Understanding computation therefore 41 

requires moving beyond geometric descriptions of neural activity to identify the dynamical mechanisms that generate it. 42 

This challenge is particularly acute for temporal computations, which are inherently transient Buonomano and Laje 43 

(2010); Zhou et al. (2022). Rather than converging to stationary attractors, neural populations must transform inputs 44 

through precisely organized patterns of activity that unfold over time. The central question is therefore not simply which 45 

low-dimensional manifolds exist, but which state-space structures organize transient evolution and thereby implement 46 

the computation itself. 47 

Here we address this question by analyzing recurrent neural networks trained on an interval reproduction task 48 

and tracking the evolution of their dynamics during learning. We find that networks achieving similar behavioral 49 

performance converge to highly diverse attractor architectures, yet consistently develop a common transient dynamical 50 

organization. Across solutions, learning positions network dynamics near bifurcations, generating structured ghost-like 51 

sets of slow points (Strogatz and Westervelt, 1989) characterized by graded spectra of near-zero eigenvalues (Koch et al., 52 

2024). These slow sets constrain the flow of the activity trajectories and organize their temporal evolution, providing a 53 

mechanism for reliable transient computation. Thus, while solutions are degenerate at the level of attractors, they are 54 

conserved at the level of transient dynamical organization. We further show that the computation emerges from the 55 

interaction between these structured slow sets and input-dependent reconfiguration of the high-dimensional vector field. 56 

Inputs transiently redirect trajectories through state space, whereas the slow sets constrain and temporally organize their 57 

subsequent evolution, enabling interval encoding, memory and reproduction. The extent of the slow sets determines 58 

generalization beyond the training range, and minimal dynamical systems endowed with analogous geometry reproduce 59 

the essential features of interval timing. Together, our findings identify structured slow transients as the dynamical 60

(Figure 1b; Supp. Fig. S1c). Despite exhibiting nearly identical performance and similar low-dimensional trajectories, 77

To characterize this diversity formally, we analyzed ten independently trained 3-unit GRU networks (Supp. Fig. 80 

S1b) as tractable representative examples. We systematically identified their bifurcation structure, tracking how the 81

2a; Supp. Fig. S2a). Thus, successful interval reproduction was compatible with a wide range of attractor architectures, 85 

demonstrating that learning does not converge onto a unique attractor solution. Task-relevant trajectories however 86 

revealed a second, more striking feature. During interval encoding, memory and reproduction, trajectories did not reside 87

We next explain how the temporal evolution of the trajectory is regulated on the representative network poised near 105 

a saddle-node bifurcation (Fig. 2a, top). The first stimulus, S1, transiently stabilized an input-dependent fixed point 106 

towards which the flow was transiently redirected. Although the stimulus was too brief for convergence to the attractor, 107 

following the stimulus removal, the activity trajectory was displaced along the organized slow set. Hence, between the 108 

stimuli the activity evolved along this slow scaffold rather than collapsing toward the baseline attractor, such that the 109 

encoded interval T corresponded to the distance traversed through the slow region(Fig. 2c, left and middle). The second 110 

stimulus, S2, again transiently reconfigured the vector field and its removal resulted in repositioning the activity to a 111 

different location on the same slow set (Fig. 2c, right; Supp. Fig. S2b). Because the slow set exhibits a graded spectrum 112 

of near-zero eigenvalues, the parts of the trajectory initiated from different locations evolved with appropriately matched 113 

temporal profiles. Memory was therefore maintained as a transversal along the structured slow set, similarly to the T114 

encoding, rather than as persistence at a stationary state. Following the Go cue, activity continued to evolve along the 115 

same scaffold and eventually leaving it, reproducing the stored interval without requiring convergence to an attractor 116 

(Supp. Fig. S2b). 117

by the trajectories across task conditions (Supp. Fig. S3a). Consistent with previous studies, the population activity 119 

remained confined to low-dimensional manifolds. However, our analysis indicates that these manifolds only describe 120 

where the activity lies, but do not mechanistically explain the computation. Computation emerged from the interaction 121

could be performed, this reorganization coincided with the emergence of SN bifurcations and the positioning of the 142 

network’s parameters in their immediate vicinity (Fig. 3a; additional example shown in Supp. Fig. S4a,b). Thus, 143 

learning progressively transformed attractor-dominated dynamics into organized transient flow in state space. 144 

This observation closely matches a canonical phenomenon from dynamical systems theory. Systems positioned 145 

near saddle-node bifurcations generate extended regions of slow dynamics, termed ghost sets, with graded spectra 146 

of near-zero eigenvalues (Strogatz and Westervelt, 1989; Koch et al., 2024; Koch and Nandan, 2026). The learned 147 

slow set structures exhibit the same signatures, including flow slowing and directional funneling (Fig. 2b, 3b, bottom 148 

row). Hence, learning did not encode temporal intervals through stable states, but by creating state-space geometries 149 

that support structured transient evolution of the population activity. Although in many networks the bifurcations 150 

were induced by the external inputs, in some of the trained examples similar near-critical organization emerged with 151 

respect to the internal network parameters (i.e. the bias parameter bh 12 , example shown in Fig. 3c; Supp. Fig. S4c -152 

middle column). This observation enabled us to directly test the functional importance of near-critical organization 153 

by perturbing a single bifurcation parameter while keeping all remaining parameters fixed. Timing performance was 154 

maximal in the immediate vicinity of the bifurcation and deteriorated as the system moved away from the critical regime 155 

(Fig. 3). This loss of performance coincided with the reorganization or disappearance of the ghost set (Supp. Fig. S4c). 156 

In training examples where the parameter responsible for critical organization could not be identified due to the high 157 

dimensionality of the system, we nevertheless identified slow sets characterized by an equivalent eigenvalue structure 158 

(examples in Supp. Fig.S2c,d). Although we cannot exclude the possibility that different mechanisms may give rise to 159 

these structures, the existing literature has so far associated them primarily with ghost sets Koch et al. (2024); Koch 160 

and Nandan (2026). These observations therefore indicate that organized slow transients are functionally necessary 161 

for temporal computation rather than epiphenomenal consequences of training. Furthermore, they also imply that 162 

positioning in a critical vicinity of the bifurcation where the ghost set exists, after the coalescence of the stable and 163 

saddle nodes, is crucial for controlling the temporal evolution of the trajectories during task evolution. 164 

The extent of the slow sets determines generalization to untrained intervals 165 

We next asked whether the extent of the slow sets determines the networks’ ability to generalize. Here, we define 166 

generalization as the ability of a trained network to accurately perform task conditions that were never encountered 167 

during training. Because the ghost slow sets not only generate long timescales but also funnel trajectories along 168 

task-relevant directions, we hypothesized that their extent constrains the range of computations that can be realized. To 169 

test this idea, we trained a 3-unit GRU network to memorize and reproduce a single interval ( T = 80 a.u.; corresponding 170 

Tdelay = 50 a.u.). As expected, when evaluated using a different interval ( T = 60 a.u.), the network failed to reproduce 171 

the target duration accurately ( ∆T = −18 a.u.; Figure 4a), indicating that successful extrapolation does not arise 172 

trivially from training. Nevertheless, the trained network exhibited organization near a SN bifurcation and an associated 173 

ghost slow set (Fig. 4b; Supp. Fig. S6a,b). Our previous analysis suggests that different intervals correspond to different 174

## Discussion 210 

Flexible behavior requires neural activity to evolve through precisely organized transient dynamics. Although low-211 

dimensional neural manifolds have been widely observed across cognitive tasks Remington et al. (2018); Wang et al. 212 

(2018); Egger et al. (2019); Langdon et al. (2023); Perich and Gallego (2025), the dynamical mechanisms that generate 213 

and constrain the evolution of the trajectories along these manifolds have remained unclear. Here we show that recurrent 214 

neural networks trained on interval timing converge to diverse attractor configurations, however, they exhibit a conserved 215 

organization at the level of transient dynamics. Across independently trained solutions, the temporal evolution of the 216 

trajectories is consistently organized by structured ghost sets characterized by graded spectra of near-zero eigenvalues 217 

that constrain and channel the population activity flow. These structures emerge during learning through positioning 218 

the networks in a critical vicinity of saddle-node bifurcations. Recent work has shown that near-critical dynamics in 219 

neuronal and artificial networks exhibit long timescales Levina et al. (2007); Deco and Jirsa (2012); Shew and Plenz 220 

(2013); Ma et al. (2019); Kelso (2023); Goto et al. (2025); Rossi et al. (2025); Hancock et al. (2025), high-dimensional 221 

global modes, and abrupt learning transitions Pachitariu et al. (2026); Dinc et al. (2026), suggesting a link between 222 

criticality and computational capability. We have extended these findings by identifying structured slow sets as explicit 223 

geometric objects that not only organize temporal computations, but their extent also predicts the generalization 224 

to unseen intervals. Our results also provide a mechanistic reinterpretation of low-dimensional neural manifolds. 225 

Although neural activity remains confined to low-dimensional trajectories during task execution Wang et al. (2018); 226 

Remington et al. (2018); Egger et al. (2019); Jazayeri and Ostojic (2021), we demonstrate here that these manifolds 227 

do not themselves determine computation. Instead, computation emerges from the interaction between input-driven 228 

reconfiguration of the high-dimensional vector field and the existing slow set that governs the subsequent evolution of 229 

the population dynamics. Different temporal intervals correspond to distinct traversals of a shared slow scaffold, whose 230 

eigenvalue structure determines the rate of progression through state space. Thus, low-dimensional slow manifolds 231 

reflect constraints imposed by dynamical organization rather than serving as primary computational substrates. 232 

A central implication of this view is that task-relevant information is encoded and transformed through evolution 233 

along the structured slow regions, rather than by position on a stable attractor. We further demonstrate the sufficiency of 234 

this mechanism using a minimal dynamical system endowed with analogous slow set geometry. Without learning or 235 

parameter optimization, the system reproduces interval timing across broad intervals range via traversal of ghost-like 236 

slow regions whose spatial extent governs the duration of transient evolution. This establishes structured slow sets as a 237 

minimal dynamical ingredient for temporal computation. Importantly, our findings suggest a constructive principle 238 

for designing efficient and minimal artificial neural networks: embedding extended slow set regions Schmidt et al. 239 

(2021); Durstewitz et al. (2025); Dinc et al. (2026) should improve robustness and generalization in temporal tasks. 240 

Finally, this framework yields experimentally testable predictions. During learning, neural population activity should 241 

progressively align with slow modes corresponding to task variables. Perturbations would induce rapid relaxation along 242 

fast directions while preserving constrained evolution within the slow scaffold. Moreover, the spatial extent of the slow 243

Identifying state-space structures 284 

The attractor landscapes of the trained networks were identified for the continuous-time ordinary differential equations 285 

of the trained networks using the XPPAUT bifurcation analysis package (Ermentrout and Mahajan, 2003). The results 286 

were additionally verified using the BifurcationKit package in Julia (Veltz, 2020). For all the randomly initialized 287 

trajectories depicted as grey lines in the figures, the trained networks were numerically integrated using the Euler 288 

scheme in Python. 289 

Slow points in state space were identified by minimizing the scalar function q Sussillo and Barak (2013) using 290 

SciPy’s minimise package with Newton’s method and a conjugate gradient solver. The trajectories of the RNN while 291 

performing the task were used as initial conditions for the identification of the slow points so that the solutions remain 292 

bounded within the state space. A qthreshold of 0.0001 was generally used for the identification of the slow point. In the 293 

regions where the norm of a dynamical system is close to zero q(x) < q thresh , local linear expansion of the dynamics 294 

is still valid. Thereby the local eigenvalues were calculated by numerically evaluating the Jacobian at these points. For 295 

more details refer to (Koch et al., 2024). 296
