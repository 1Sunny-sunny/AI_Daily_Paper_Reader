Abstract 16 

Hedonic valence , the intrinsic pleasantness or unpleasantness of an experience, is  fundamental to human 17 

psychological functioning.  Yet , how  valence  is represented in the brain  remains an  open question . Functional  MRI 18 

studies have demonstrated  that the brain encodes both positive and negative valence,  but this evidence largely stems 19 

from experiments using simplified,  controlled stimuli, such as images, sounds, or words . As a result, it remains unclear 20 

how  valence  is processed  duri ng  rich,  naturalistic  experiences that more closely reflect  real  life . In addition , most 21 

studies adopt a single statistical model, raising concerns about the robustness of  their  findings. This study used a 22 

formal voxel -wise Bayesian model selection approach to test  alternative statistical models supporting  Bipolarity, Va-23 

lence -General, and Bivalence hypotheses to identify the most optimal model of valence representation during narrative 24 

listening. Our results  provide  evidence for  the  Bipolar model . We identified distributed  brain  regions  that  selectively 25 

encod e valence as a bipolar continuum (negative to positive) during narrative comprehension , including classical 26 

emotion -related  hubs such as ventromedial prefrontal cortex, as well as  regions  not traditionally associated with emo-27 

tion processing , such as  inferior  occipital  cortex,  supramarginal  cortex, inferior frontal cortex, and middle cingulate. 28 

Regions selectively encoding arousal and those broadly responsive to both valence and arousal were also identified. 29 

These findings highlight the  importanc e of  using  formal model comparison and naturalistic paradigms in affective 30 

neuroscience,  advancing our  understanding of how  valence  is  represented in the brain during real -world experiences .31

Hedonic valence refers to the affective quality of pleasure or displeasure. It modulates  nearly  every aspect of 35 

psychological functioning , including perception, memory, judgment, language, social interaction, and well -being .36 

Empirical studies using exploratory factor analysis and multidimensional scaling consistently  identify  valence as the 37 

most fundamental dimension of core affect and discrete emotions (Russell, 1980; Barrett & Bliss‐Moreau, 2009).  A38 

deeper  understanding  of  its  neural  representation  is  essential for advancing research in  emotion, cognition, and mental 39 

health. 40 

Valence  is  typically  conceptualized as  a continuum  ranging from negative to positive affect . However, a 41 

long -standing debate  persists  over how valence is represented in the brain , whether positive and negative affect are 42 

encoded by the same or separate  neural  systems.  Two primary theoretical accounts have been proposed  (Figure  1A).43

The Bipolarity  account  posits that valence is  represented along  a single continuum from negativ e to positiv e affect 44 

(Russell, 2003; Russell & Barrett, 1999) , with neural activity  monotonically increas ing  or decreas ing  along this  axis .45 

Empirical support  includes  linear modulation of BOLD signals by valence in regions such as  the orbit ofrontal cortex 46 

(OFC) , inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), superior  and middle  temporal gyr i (STG , MTG ), and insula  in response to  affec-47 

tive  stimuli  (Altmann et al., 2012; Anderson et al., 2003; Cunningham et al., 2004; Heinzel et al., 2005; Tseng et al., 48 

2016) .49 

In contrast, t he Bivalence  account  argues that although the behavioral manifestation of valence is bipolar, the 50 

underlying evaluation may not adhere to a bipolar structure  (Cacioppo et al., 1997, 1999; Cacioppo & Berntson, 1994) .51 

Instead,  positive and negative affect  are thought to arise from  distinct evaluative processes , encoded  by  independent 52 

neural  systems . Although  some studies  report dissociable brain regions  for each polarity, findings are inconsistent, 53 

with overlapping or divergent regions  observed  across tasks  (Chikazoe et al., 2014; Lewis et al., 2006; Viinikainen et 54 

al., 2010) . These inconsistencies raise questions about whether apparent selectivity reflects true neural specialization 55 

or limitations of classical statistical inference , as the absence of  statistical  significance does not confirm the absence 56 

of an effect .57 

In light of these mixed findings, some studies have instead reported a valence -general  response profile in 58 

which neural activity increases  with the  magnitude of valence ( i.e., equivalently  for both positive and negative stimuli ;59 

(Bestelmeyer et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2006; Viinikainen et al., 2010, 2012; Winston et al., 2003, 60 

2005) . This pattern is typically observed in studies employing conjunction analyses, or when valence is parameterized 61 

as intensity (i.e., the absolute value of bipolar valence) or modeled with a quadratic term, yielding a U -shaped or V -62 

shaped relationship between BOLD signal and valence  (Figure 1A ). This pattern has been observed in a distributed 63 

network  including the OFC,  ventromedial prefrontal cortex ( vmFPC ), dorsomedial prefrontal cortex ( dmPFC ), ante-64 

rior cingulate cortex ( ACC ), middle cingulate cortex (MCC), IFG, STG, MTG, insula, hippocampus,  parahippocampal 65 

gyrus  (PHG), and  amygdala  (Benelli et al., 2012; Bestelmeyer et al., 2017; Chikazoe et al., 2014; Lewis et al., 2006; 66 

Lindquist et al., 2016; Tseng et al., 2016; Viinikainen et al., 2010, 2012) . However,  an alternative explanation is that 67 

increased  neural responses to both positive and negative stimuli  compared to neutral  may  not reflect  valence but 68 

conceptually distinct processes, such as  arousal  (i. e., the  experienced physiological activation , such as being in  a calm 69 

vs. excited  state ) or  motivational  salience  (i.e.,  the significance of stimuli in relation to homeostatic needs  and  goal -70 

directed attention ; Lindquist et al., 2012, 2016; Viinikainen et al., 2012) .71

Despite extensive research, several methodological issues remain that may hinder a clear understanding of 72 

the neural representation of valence. First, most studies have examined valence using a single statistical model,  with 73 

conclusions typically drawn from patterns of significant activation. However,  a significant effect under one model 74 

does not  exclusively  imply that  the data  provides the best  support for the underlying theoretical  account . Similarly, it 75 

does  not  preclude alternative models from explaining the same data better when parameterized differentl y, which may 76 

thus support a different theoretical account . As such, inferences about valence may depend critically on the choice of 77 

the  statistical model rather than  the  underlying neural organization.  Second, applying a single model uniformly across 78 

the whole brain implicitly assumes that all regions encode valence in the same manner. It is plausible that different 79 

brain regions encode valence using distinct response profiles , for example, some regions may exhibit monotonic  re-80 

sponses, whereas others may show  valence -general  responses  (Kim et al., 2020) . Ignoring such heterogeneity may 81 

obscure region -specific encoding patterns and contribute to inconsistent findings across studies.  Only  few  studies have 82 

evaluated multiple alternative  modelling approaches  within the same dataset  (Kim et al., 2017, 2020; Lewis et al., 83 

2006; Lindquist et al., 2016; Viinikainen et al., 2010, 2012) , y et even these have primarily relied on  the significance 84 

of neural  activation  for each model  rather than formal model comparison. This is problematic because  a region may 85 

show  a linear  profile  when fit with a linear model  and  a valence -general profile when fit with a quadratic model ,86 

making it difficult to determine which  one of these alternatives  provides the most parsimonious explanation  for the 87 

observed  neural responses. 88 

In addition, much of the existing literature relies on highly controlled stimuli (e.g., emotional images, faces, 89 

sounds, and words). While such stimuli provide experimental control and allow researchers to equate potential con-90 

founds, they are typically presented as isolated emotional events and therefore do not approximate everyday  dynami-91 

cally -unfolding  and highly context -dependent  emotional experience s. In contrast, listening to a narrative or watching 92 

a movie more closely approximates real -world experience. Neural responses under such naturalistic paradigms have 93 

been shown to be more reliable and reproducible than those elicited by controlled stimuli  (Hasson et al., 2010; 94 

Saarimä ki, 2021) . Consistent with this, r ecent  studies  demonstrate  that neural activity  within  the emotional network 95 

of different participants  exposed to  the same movies or narratives can  become synchronized , reflecting shared pro-96 

cessing of unfolding emotional dynamics and providing an ecologically valid framework for examining the neural 97 

representation of valence across individuals  (Nanni‐Zepeda et al., 2024; Nummenmaa et al., 2014; Savard et al., 2024; 98 

Tan et al., 2022; Vaccaro et al., 2024) .99

To address these limitations, the present study adopts a formal model comparison framework to characterize 100 

how valence -related neural responses are best captured during naturalistic narrative listening. Rather than inferring 101 

conclusions from activation patterns under a single model, we directly compare alternative statistical  models  of va-102 

lence that differ in their assumed response profiles (e.g.,  bipolar, valence -general, and bivalen t). Model comparison 103 

approaches have been widely used in psychology and behavioral sciences but remain underutilized in cognitive neu-104 

roscience. Recent developments have enabled Bayesian model comparison within the general linear model (GLM) 105 

framework for fMRI data, allowing voxel -wise evaluation of model evidence and selection of the most parsimonious 106 

model across participants  (Soch et al., 2016, 2017; Soch & Allefeld, 2018) . Applying this framework provides a 107 

principled approach to dissociate competing statistical accounts of valence representation and to identify potential 108 

regional heterogeneity in how valence -related signals are encoded in the brain. 109 

Method 110 

Description and preprocessing of fMRI  data 111 

We leveraged two datasets from the  Narrative s fMRI data collection  (Nastase et al., 2021) . Young adults 112 

passively listened to four narratives in an MRI scanner. Forty -six participants listened to the narratives  I Knew You 113 

Were Black  (Black ) and  The Man Who Forgot Ray Bradbury  (Forgot ) in separate sessions,  and 19 participants listened 114 

to narratives  Slumlord  and  Reach for the Stars One Small Step at a Time  (Reach ) in single sessions  (see Table 1 for 115 

the details). One participant was excluded based on the recommendations from the original paper, resulting in a total 116 

sample size of 64 people ( Mage  = 22.67,  SD age  = 6.57, 41 females, 23 males). 117 

We used the preprocessed fMRI data provided in the  Narratives  dataset , which  was collected using a 3T 118 

scanner with a 1.5s repetition time. For the initial preprocessing steps, the raw fMRI data were temporally and spatially 119 

corrected, then normalized to MNI space using fMRIPrep  20.0.5  (Esteban et al., 2019, 2020) . The  3dBlurToFWH M120 

program in AFNI  21. 1. 01  (Cox, 1996)  was used  to  appl y a  6mm FWHM Gaussian filter  to smooth the data. Con-121 

founding variables, including head motion, signals in  cerebrospinal fluid  and white matter, and time drifts were re-122 

gressed out  (Nastase et al., 2021) . The residuals were used as the “clean” fMRI data for our analyses.  We resliced  all 123 

preprocessed  images  to a matching spatial resolution of  3×3×4 mm 3 voxels  using the 3dResample program in AFNI. 124

Subjective Ratings 129 

To capture the variation in valence within narratives and increase the sampling rate of subjective ratings, 130 

each sentence was divided into segments following general grammatical rules. Two research assistants independently 131 

segmented the stories, extracted the onset and offset timestamps for each segment from the audio recordings, and then 132 

conferred to ensure that each segment adhered to the segmentation guidelines, conveyed complete meanings, and 133 

followed natural transition s. The raw audio recordings were split into separate audio clips which were then used as 134 

stimuli in the behavioral rating experiment. 135 

To obtain the subjective valence and arousal ratings for each segment, four online experiments were con-136 

ducted with separate samples (total sample size across experiments:  N = 175 ; see  Table  2).  Participants were first 137 

presented with instructions and examples of how to use a valence -by -arousal grid ( Figure 1A ) to rate their feelings 138 

during narrative listening. Then,  the  audio clip s for each segment  of the narrative  were  automatically played , one at a 139 

time . A rating screen was shown immediately after each narrative segment,  with the instruction “ please rate how you 140 

felt about the stimulus ”.  All segments were played in the ir  original  sequence  in the narrative . After completing the 141 

listening and rating tasks, participants answered  four to five multiple -choice  comprehension questions  (depending on 142 

the narrative)  to assess understanding and engagement . Nineteen participants were excluded due to low comprehen-143 

sion accuracy (less than 60%), resulting in a final sample of 15 6 participants across the four experiments ( Mage  = 20.75, 144 

SD age  = 4.01, 132 females, 2 4 males). 145 

To evaluate interrater reliability of valence and arousal ratings, we computed intraclass correlation coeffi-146 

cients (ICC) using a two -way random -effects model with absolute agreement, separately for each narrative. We report 147 

both single -measure ICC2 and average -measures ICC2k. The former reflects the reliability of an individual rater, 148

Although only participants  demonstrating  sufficient  narrative comprehension (accuracy >  60% ) were in-154 

cluded,  substantial  individual differences in valence and arousal perception  remained  (Figure  1E and  Table  3). To 155 

obtain representative valence and arousal ratings at the group level, we  used a well -established method to  compute 156 

the weighted average based on the similarity of the valence and arousal ratings across the participants listening to the 157 

same narrative  (Abdi et al., 2012) . Conceptually, this approach downweighs the influence of participants with idio-158 

syncratic ratings.  Specifically, we first standardized and normalized each participant’s valence and arousal ratings. 159 

Next, we calculated the Hilbert -Schmidt inner product as a similarity measure for every pair of participants. We then 160 

applied  a principal  component analysis  (PCA)  based on the RV coefficient (i.e., a multivariate generalization of the 161 

squared Pearson correlation coefficient)  to the resulting inner product matrix and used its first eigenvector to determine 162 

participant weights  (for details, see Abdi et al., 2012) . The weighted average valence and arousal ratings were used 163 

for the subsequent fMRI data analyses .164

Model comparison  within participants 178 

We performed  within -participant  voxel -wise  GLM  regressions in SPM 12 (http://fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) to 179 

examine whether BOLD response s varie d as 1) a linear function of valence (Bipolar); 2) a quadratic function of va-180 

lence (Valence -general, U -shaped); 3) a linear function of absolute -transformed valence (Valence -general, V -shape); 181 

or 4) a linear function of positivity and negativity separately (Bivalence).  The Bipolar and valence -general (V -shaped 182 

and U -shaped) models each included a single boxcar function marking the onset and duration of each narrative seg-183 

ment. The Bivalence model included two boxcar functions: one for positive -valence segments and one for negative -184 

valence segments. All models also included the additional modulators described below  (Figure  2A).185 

Bipolar model.  This model include d two  additional  parametric modulators: one for arousal and one for va-186 

lence. 187 

Valence -general, U -shaped model.  This model include d additional  linear parametric modulators for arousal 188 

and valence as the Bipolar model, plus an additional parametric modulator representing the quadratic (squared) term 189 

of the valence ratings to capture U -shaped effects. 190 

Valence -general, V -shaped model.  This model  include d an additional  linear arousal parametric modulator 191 

(as in the Bipolar model), and an absolute -value -transformed valence modulator to model V -shaped effects. 192 

Bivalence model.  This model was similar to the Bipolar model  but modeled positive - and negative -valence 193 

segments separately.  It included  four  additional  modulators : an arousal parametric modulator and a valence parametric 194

To determine which model fits the data best  for  each voxel, we applied cross -validated Bayesian model 214 

comparison (cvBMS) using the SPM -based MACS toolbox  (Soch & Allefeld, 2018) . For  each  voxel  and each  partic-215 

ipant , this method calculates a cross -validated version of the log model evidence (cvLME) across fMRI sessions .216 

Specifically,  for the narratives  Black  and  Forgot  that were acquired in two separate sessions,  one  session  was used  to 217 

estimate posterior distributions, which were then used as informative priors for the other session to compute out -of -218 

sample log model evidence. This procedure was repeated across sessions and the resulting values were summed to 219 

obtain the cvLME  values  for each  of  the four models  (Model Family 1) . For the  narratives  Slumlord  and  Reach  that 220 

were acquired in a single session, half -split  cross -validation  was applied  (Soch et al., 2016) . These cvLME maps were 221 

then converted into posterior model probabilit y maps, and the model with the highest posterior probability  was se-222 

lected as the most optimal model for that voxel and participant  (Figure  2A).223 

Model selection across participants 224 

Because different voxels and participants may favor different models, we applied a  between -participant 225 

model selection analysis using the MACS toolbox. For each voxel,  we applied r andom -effects Bayesian model selec-226 

tion (RFX BMS) , allowing for the possibility that different participants have different optimal models.  We computed 227 

the  likeliest  frequency with which each model was selected as  the most  optimal  model over the other  three models 228 

across participants, resulting in four model frequency maps. We then constructed a  between -participants  model selec-229 

tion map by assigning each voxel to the model with the highest selection frequency  (Figure  2A).230 

Identifying the voxels modulated by valence 231 

While cvBMS identifies the best -fitting model, it does not indicate whether valence significantly modulates 232 

BOLD activity. To address this, we conducted voxel -wise  within -participant  t-tests (for Bipolar, Valence -general V -233 

shaped, and Bivalence models) and F -tests (for the Valence -general U -shaped model) on the  parametric modulators 234 

of  valence.  The F -tests allow  us to examine the overall effect of both linear and  quadratic  terms  in the Valence -general 235 

U-shaped model.  For the Bivalence model,  the effects of  positive and negative valence were tested separately.  Be-236 

tween -participant  analyses were then conducted to identify regions where valence modulation effects were consistent 237 

across participants. Statistical maps were thresholded using voxel -wise  p < .001 and cluster -size correction  using 238 

SPM12.  To isolate regions where valence significantly modulated neural activity under the most appropriate model, 239 

we binarized the group -level model selection maps and applied these masks to the thresholded  group -level statistical 240 

maps. 241

Localizing the regions broadly activated when listening to narratives 242 

To identify the brain activation response s to narrative  listening  independent ly  of  the affective content,  we 243 

conducted a  group -level analysis based on the effect of the first boxcar function  parametric modulator  in the Bipolar 244 

model . Statistical maps were thresholded  as previously described .245 

Localizing the regions selectively modulated by arousal after controlling for valence 246 

To examine the unique effect of arousal, we fitted a separate GLM model  using the same model as for  the 247 

Valence  Bipolar model , except  that we reversed the order  in which  the arousal and valence regressors  were entered 248 

into the model , allowing  us to examine the linear relationship between BOLD and arousal after removing the variance 249 

explained by valence.  Group -level analysis was conducted at  voxel -wise  p < .001  with cluster -size correction , simi-250 

larly to  how we examined valence modulation effect for  the  Bipolar  model .251 

Conjunction analysis 252 

To isolate regions sensitive only to valence, only to arousal, or to both valence and arousal , we conducted a 253 

conjunction analysis.  We first generated a binary mask containing the voxels that were significantly correlated with 254 

valence after controlling for arousal in the  Bipolar  model, without differentiating the direction of the correlation. A 255 

similar mask was generated for the effect of arousal after  controlling  for  valence.  Then , we applied a  voxel -wise 256 

conjunction analysis to identify the voxels that  were  broadly sensitive to both valence and arousal, as well as those 257 

uniquely responsive to one  affective  dimension. 258

Results 260 

Normative affective ratings 261 

For valence, interrater reliability of the aggregated ratings was consistently high across narratives (mean 262 

ICC2k = 0.94, range = 0.93 –0.96). In contrast, reliability at the level of individual raters was low (mean ICC2 = 0.31, 263 

range = 0.25 –0.41), indicating a robust shared hedonic structure during narrative listening alongside substantial indi-264 

vidual variability. For arousal, reliability of the aggregated ratings was moderate to high (mean ICC2k = 0.81, range 265 

= 0.66 –0.90). In contrast, reliability at the level of individual raters was low (mean ICC2 = 0.12, range = 0.05 –0.18), 266 

indicating that although a shared arousal structure is present at the group level, individual differences in arousal per-267 

ception are substantial. 268

Model comparison 282 

The cvBMS results showed that the Bipolar model had the highest model selection frequency across the 283 

sample compared to the other three models for most of the brain voxels ( Figure  2B). The Valence -general V -shaped 284 

model  was most frequently  select ed  only in several clusters in the parieto -occipital lobe, including bilateral inferior 285 

parietal lobule (IPL), angular gyrus (AG), inferior occipital gyrus (IOG), lateral occipital gyrus (LOG), calcarine  sul-286 

cus , and precuneus  (PreCun) . Neither t he  Valence -general U -shaped  nor the  Bivalence model  was  selected as the 287 

group -level optimal model for any brain voxels .288 

Considering that the cvBMS framework selects models based on a trade -off between model accuracy and 289 

complexity, we cannot rule out the possibility that the Bivalence model was not selected due to its higher complexity 290 

(i.e., six parameters, compared to three parameters in the Bipolar and Valence -general V -shaped models, and four 291 

parameters in the Valence -general U -shaped model). To address this concern, we reconstruct ed  the Bivalence model 292 

with reduced complexity.  Specifically, rather than splitting ratings into mutually exclusive positive - and negative -293 

valence segments, we applied a n additional  STATIS  procedure  to derive  group -level  ratings  of positive valence and 294 

negative valence , separately . For positive valence, negative ratings were set to zero for each participant prior to ap-295 

plying STATIS to compute a weighted average across participants. For negative valence,  positive values were set to 296 

zero , the sign of the ratings was reversed, and the same STATIS procedure was applied to obtain synthesized negative 297 

valence ratings. This approach yielded continuous estimates of both positive and negative valence for each segment. 298 

Using these reconstructed regressors, we defined  a reduced -complexity version of the Bivalence model  with four 299 

parameters , including onset, arousal, positive valence, and negative valence  regressors 2. This  model  was  combined 300 

with the original three models (Bipolar, Valence -general V -shaped, and Valence -general U -shaped) to form  a new 301 

model comparison family  (Model Family 2) . Multicollinearity  was acceptable for the reconstructed  model (VIF  ≤ 5; 302

365  Discussion 366 

Our study  combined a  formal Bayesian model comparison framework  with parametric modulation models  to 367 

examine how hedonic valence is represented in the brain  under an ecologically valid context  during narrative listening. 368 

By comparing multiple statistical models of valence within the same dataset, we evaluated  which model provides the 369 

best fit  for  characterizing  affect -related neural responses. Across most brain voxels and participants, a linear model 370 

corresponding to the bipolar representation of valence was consistently selected as the optimal model. 371 

It is important to distinguish between model selection and statistical inference on individual regressors. The 372 

cross -validated Bayesian model comparison framework identifies the model that best explains the overall BOLD sig-373 

nal in each voxel, taking into account both goodness -of -fit and model complexity , weighing on the tradeoff between 374 

prediction accuracy and  overfit ting . However, selection of a given model does not necessarily imply that the paramet-375 

ric modulator of interest (e.g., valence) exhibits a statistically significant effect.  Alternative models,  including  repa-376 

rameteriz ing  bipolar valence as “valence intensity” (Valence -general V -shaped), using  additional quadratic term to 377 

delineate the non -linear shape (Valence -general U -shaped), and  reconstruct bipolar valence as bivalent  scales  (Biva-378 

lence) , were selected in  some  voxels  when compared  within  different model famil ies.  However,  when we further 379 

examin ed the  neural activity modulated by valence, those voxels showed  no or little correspondence with valence .380 

This suggests that although these models captured more variance, the voxels did not reliably encode valence in the 381 

hypothesized manner.  Importantly,  for the voxels  selected by the four -parameter Bivalence model  in bilateral  Heschl’s 382 

gyrus , their BOLD signals  significantly correlated with both positive and negative valence,  largely resembl ing  a va-383 

lence -general pattern in primary auditory cortex  associated with auditory processing in the narrative listening task, 384 

rather than reflecting independent encoding of positive and negative valence  per se .385 

Interestingly, our findings  supporting t he Bipolar representation of valence contrast with previous  studies 386 

directly comparing statistical models within the same data , which  have  more often supported the Valence -general or 387

often relied on activation -based approaches, interpreting any significant activation as support for a given model. How-389 

ever, statistical significance alone does not indicate good model fit, nor does its absence imply poor fit. Null results 390 

for the Bipolar model in earlier work may stem from low statistical power (N < 20) rather than the absence of a true 391 

effect. In our data,  the neural activity  in bilateral STG /STS  was  significantly  modulated  by both bipolar valence  (Bi-392 

polar model)  and absolute -transformed valence  (Valence -general V -shaped model) , creating ambiguity about the un-393 

derlying valence –BOLD relationship. However, model comparison using cvBMS showed that the Bipolar model pro-394 

vided a significantly better fit than the V -shaped model for most participants at the group level. This demonstrates that 395 

cvBMS can resolve such ambiguity and underscores the importance of jointly considering overall model fit and the 396 

statistical significance of the regressor of interest. 397 

Second, previous studies often  used  highly  controlled  emotional  words  (Lewis et al., 2006) , static pictures 398 

(Viinikainen et al., 2010)  and sounds  (Viinikainen et al., 2012)  or  meta -analysis based on studies using  factorial de-399 

signs  that treat valence as  categorical variables  (Lindquist et al., 2016) . Th e rapid presentation of  isolated static stimuli 400 

may  not reflect how emotions unfold in real -life. The observed brain activation in response to both positive and neg-401 

ative stimuli in these studies may  alternatively  reflect  difference  between emotional and neutral stimuli  driven by 402 

salience in a laboratory setting , rather than affective processing  representative of  day -to -day life  experiences . Also, 403 

treating valence as categorical variables  (e.g., contrasting positive vs.  neutral  or  negative  vs. neutral conditions)  may 404 

not capture the  full range  of  BOLD -valence  relationship s,  considering that valence is conceptualized as a continuous 405 

spectrum.  In contrast, in our study,  examining how BOLD  is modulated by continuous valence ratings during  immer-406 

sive narrative listening allows affective states to unfold more dynamically,  providing a more ecologically valid context 407 

to examine the neural representation of valence . Aligning with  this assumption and consistent with our findings, using 408 

naturalistic movie -watching, Kim et al.  (2020)  reported engagement of STS, MFG , and mPFC in predicting signed 409 

bipolar  valence. Similarly, using a narrative -listening paradigm, Wallentin et al. (2011)  found  valence -related activa-410 

tion in the bilateral MTG, left IFG, left temporoparietal junction, PreCun, and left vmPFC . Although they  found no 411 

effect for the Bipolar model,  this  may  be attributed to the  high familiarity  of  the narrative (“ The Ugly  Duckling”)  or 412 

the method used to extract  valence ratings  at the sentence level , which may not capture  more time -resolved valence 413 

changes that typical ly  happen between clauses and within sentence s.414

Our findings provide novel insights into the neural representation of affective experience during n arrative 415 

listening. BOLD signal  varied  linear ly  with  bipolar valence in distributed frontal -temporo -parietal regions , midline 416 

regions , as well as  subcortical structures like the amygdala and hippocampus.  Many of these regions , including the 417 

bilateral ST G, STS , MCC,  amygdala, hippocampus, and  PreCun , were also modulated by arousal.  While t he Circum-418 

plex Model of Affect  (Russell, 1980)  posits valence and arousal  as  independent  affective  dimensions , accumulating 419 

empirical evidence suggests  they  are not strictly independent, as highly positive or negative affective states tend to 420 

exhibit higher arousal. Prior studies have shown that regions such as the bilateral STG, MTG, and amygdala encode 421 

both  dimensions  (Baucom et al., 2012; Bestelmeyer et al., 2017; Mourã o-Miranda, 2003; Wallentin et al., 2011) ,422 

indicating a convergence between our findings and prior studies using more tightly controlled, short -duration stimuli .423 

At the same time, the involvement of these regions should not be taken as evidence of valence -specific coding 424 

per se . In particular, the amygdala has been variably characterized as preferentially responsive to negative valence, 425 

broadly responsive across valence, or more fundamentally tuned to affective salience and motivational relevance 426 

(Costafreda et al., 2008; Lindquist et al., 2012, 2016; Pessoa, 2010; Sergerie et al., 2008) . Consistent with these per-427 

spectives, the co -modulation of these regions by both valence and arousal in the present study suggests that their 428 

activity may reflect more general processes such as salience  and novelty  detection. From this perspective,  rather than 429 

reflecting stable  and directional  encoding of  positive and negative  valence,  the observed direction of the bipolar va-430 

lence association in these regions may be context -dependent, shaped by the distribution of salient events within the 431 

narrative and the extent to which positive versus negative moments capture attention or motivational relevance. 432 

Focusing on regions uniquely sensitive to valence  while  controlling for arousal, our findings showing nega-433 

tive correlations with valence ratings largely replicate those reported  by Nummenmaa et al. (2014), who used both 434 

GLM analyses and a model -free inter -subject correlation approach  during a similar narrative listening task . As valence 435 

shifted from positive to negative, neural activity increased in bilateral SMG and PCC, and became more temporally 436 

synchronized across participants in dACC, MCC, PCC, and somatosensory regions during narrative listening. Similar 437 

increases in synchronization associated with negative valence in midline regions have also been observed during 438 

movie watching  (Nummenmaa et al., 2012) . Functionally, the SMG has been implicated in empathy and mentalizing 439 

(Mar, 2011; Silani et al., 2013) , while the dACC and MCC are key nodes of the salience network, supporting the 440 

detection of motivationally relevant stimuli and the coordination of adaptive responses  (Seeley et al., 2007) . In contrast, 441 

the PCC is a core node in the default mode network, associated with episodic, contextual, and internally oriented 442

the processing of increasingly negative information may reflect a distinct cognitive –affective state, in which listeners 444

distress. 446 

In contrast, regions showing positive correlations with valence ratings provide both replication and extension 447 

of prior findings.  Consistent with previous work, we observed increased activation in the bilateral vmPFC  associated 448 

with increased positive valence ratings.  This aligns with a meta -analysis demonstrating that the vmPFC uniquely 449 

supports the Bipolarity hypothesis, with BOLD signals particularly sensitive to positive valence  (Lindquist et al., 450 

2016) . Extensive research in humans and animals has identified the medial prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortices as 451 

critical hubs for hedonic experience and pleasure coding  (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015; Kringelbach, 2005) , high-452 

lighting the vmPFC’s role in integrating positive affect and evaluating hedonic stimuli in naturalistic contexts.  At the 453 

same time,  we also observed increased activation associated with higher positive valence ratings in regions not tradi-454 

tionally linked to valence processing, including bilateral IF J and IOG, typically involved in semantic representation 455 

and cognitive control  (Ralph -Lambon et al., 2017) . Growing evidence suggests these regions may modulate affective 456 

experience, particularly in naturalistic settings. For example, Nummenmaa et al.  (2014)  reported enhanced neural 457 

synchronization in IFG and mPFC with higher positive valence, while Thye et al.  (2023)  found heightened activation 458

et al.  (2024)  showed that the  occipital -temporal cortex  encodes both affective content and semantic categories in 460 

response to natural images. Emotion regulation studies further show IFG is engaged when actively regulating emo-461 

tional intensity through cognitive reappraisal  (McRae et al., 2012; Otto et al., 2014) . Together, these findings suggest 462 

that positive affect in naturalistic contexts may involve greater integration with semantic and conceptual processes, 463 

potentially supporting the interpretation of affective meaning. 464 

Interestingly, some regions sensitive to valence in our narrative listening task , such as the IFG, PCC, and 465 

precuneus , have not been consistently reported in prior studies using highly controlled stimuli. One possible explana-466 

tion relates to differences in temporal receptive windows (TRWs) across cortical regions. Work using naturalistic 467 

paradigms, including narrative listening and movie watching, has shown that higher -order regions such as the default 468 

mode network and lateral prefrontal cortex integrate information over longer timescales, accumulating contextual and 469 

semantic information across extended periods  (Baldassano et al., 2018; Hasson et al., 2008; Lerner et al., 2014) . In 470

study may reflect the gradual construction of affective meaning as information unfolds over time, a process that is 473

There are several methodological limitations that warrant further discussion. These limitations not only con-475 

strain the interpretation of our findings but also reflect broader, long -standing challenges in affective neuroscience. 476 

First, while our model selection  approach supports  the Bipolarity hypothesis,  the use of  BOLD  signals  restricts  our 477 

analysis to the  voxel  level . Some evidence suggests that at the neuronal level, valence representation may align more 478 

closely with the Bivalence hypothesis  (Belova et al., 2008; Morrison & Salzman, 2009 ; also see Lindquist et al., 2016 479 

for a discussion ). Accordingly , o ur findings do not necessarily contradict the affective workspace hypothesis  (Barrett 480 

& Bliss‐Moreau, 2009; Lindquist et al., 2012, 2016) , which  proposes that neural populations flexibly participate in 481 

representing affective states, such that the same regions may contribute to both positive and negative experiences 482 

depending on context.  In addition,  although we explored multiple ways of specifying the Bivalence model, the fre-483 

quentist framework (e.g., parametric modulation within a GLM) cannot provide direct support for the Bivalence the-484 

oretical account. This limitation arises because the Bivalence account is defined by two criteria: for a voxel to uniquely 485 

encode positive valence, (1) BOLD activity should be significantly associated with positivity, and (2) BOLD activity 486 

should not be associated with negativity. While standard GLM approaches can test the first criterion by rejecting a 487 

null hypothesis, they are not well suited to provide evidence in favor of the second criterion, which requires support 488 

for the absence of an effect. Accordingly, future work may benefit from Bayesian frameworks that can more directly 489 

evaluate evidence for both the presence and absence of effects. 490 

Second,  we used  group -level synthesized affective ratings from  an independent  sample to localize  neural 491 

correlates of valence  during narrative listening.  This  approach has been successfully applied in prior work using sim-492 

ilar naturalistic paradigms  (Kim et al., 2020; Wallentin et al., 2011) . Although  inter -rater  reliability was high  at the 493 

group  level  (ICC2k  > 0.9 for valence  and >  0.8  for arousal ), we also observed substantial individual  variability (ICC2 494 

≈ 0.3), indicating meaningful  differences  in  subjective affective  experience . These  individual  differences  are unlikely 495 

to reflect  low engagement , as  raters with poor  comprehension  were excluded.  Instead, they  may be  related  to prior 496 

experience -sampling  findings suggesting that individuals can exhibit idiosyncratic  profiles  of  core affect  (Barrett & 497 

Bliss‐Moreau, 2009) . Our approach,  which focuses  on  a representativ e affective experience  derived from a  weighted 498

Third,  although  narrative  listening offers high ecological validity, the correlational nature of our analysis 504 

limits causal inference , especially  considering that the normative affective ratings may correlate  with  other  confound-505 

ing  features  embed ded  in the narrative  stimuli.  A related limitation is that our method cannot fully dissociate affective 506 

processing from semantic knowledge of valence, sometimes referred to as “affective valence” versus “semantic va-507 

lence”  (Itkes et al., 2017; Itkes & Kron, 2019) . This distinction is particularly relevant in a narrative listening context, 508 

which necessarily engages auditory and language networks. For example, regions such as the inferior frontal gyrus 509 

and angular gyrus, implicated in our results, are also well known for their roles in semantic processing and control 510 

(Binder et al., 2009; Ralph -Lambon et al., 2017) . More broadly, acoustic, prosodic, semantic, and affective features 511 

of naturalistic stimuli are inherently intercorrelated, making it difficult to isolate their unique contributions using the 512 

present approach. Addressing this challenge will require methodological frameworks that can better accommodate 513 

such correlated, high -dimensional predictors.  At the same time, more controlled experimental paradigms  also  remain 514 

valuable  for isolating specific mechanisms through targeted manipulation. 515 

Finally, our samples were  predominantly  young adult females , and prior research  suggest s affective pro-516

Conclusions 519 

In sum, we present the first study to apply a cross -validated Bayesian model comparison framework within 520 

a naturalistic narrative listening paradigm to formally evaluate which statistical model best characterizes valence rep-521 

resentation under ecologically valid conditions.  Our findings suggest  that hedonic valence is best characterized by a 522 

bipolar representation  for BOLD signals . While valence -related signals were distributed across cortical and subcortical 523 

regions, many areas (e.g., amygdala, STG, hippocampus) were jointly modulated by arousal, suggesting sensitivity to 524 

affective salience and motivational relevance rather than valence -specific coding, with effects that may vary depend-525 

ing on contextual dynamics within the narrative. In contrast, regions such as  vmPFC  was associated with  positive 526
