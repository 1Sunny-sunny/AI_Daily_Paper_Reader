Abstract 20 Cochlear implants (CIs) restore hearing by stimulating auditory neurons to encode 21 amplitude envelopes across frequency bands, providing essential cues for speech 22 recognition. This study investigated how stimulation pulse rate constrains temporal 23 envelope processing and speech cue perception in ten post-lingually deaf CI users by 24 evaluating amplitude modulation (AM) detection thresholds and consonant identification 25 performance across pulse rates. The effects of pulse rate on temporal processing and 26 speech perception were examined using both standard clinical multi-channel strategies 27 and single-channel strategies designed to isolate within-channel envelope 28 representations. Results revealed a significant decline in AM detection and consonant 29 recognition performance at the lowest tested pulse rate of 125 pulses per second (pps), 30 consistent with perceptual constraints on temporal processing at low carrier rates, rather 31 than inadequate envelope sampling. At the highest pulse rate of 4000pps, a non-32 significant reduction in AM detection was observed which may be consistent with 33 previously reported reductions in amplitude discrimination at high pulse rates. 34 Consonant recognition performance remained stable across clinically relevant pulse 35 rates (250–2000pps), though listener-specific pulse rate effects were observed. Notably, 36 significant correlations were found between single-channel and multi-channel 37 performance in AM detection and consonant recognition tasks. These findings support 38 an important contribution of within-electrode temporal envelope processing to multi-39 channel speech perception and highlight the clinical relevance of individual variability in 40 pulse rate effects. 41 Keywords: Cochlear implant; Pulse rate; Envelope processing; Modulation detection 42

1. Introduction 43 Cochlear implant (CI) devices restore hearing by electrically stimulating auditory 44 neurons within the cochlea. The functionality of CIs relies on sound coding strategies 45 that transform environmental sounds into electrical pulses, delivered via intra-cochlear 46 electrodes. These strategies are designed to primarily encode amplitude envelopes 47 across frequency bands, as these envelopes convey critical temporal cues for speech 48 recognition. Typical CI strategies represent amplitude envelopes using constant-rate 49 carrier pulses, often sacrificing fine temporal detail. 50 CI performance depends on accurate encoding of temporal envelope cues, which are 51 critical for speech perception. The ability to perceive these cues can be hindered by 52 inadequate stimulation parameters or listener-specific auditory processing limitations. 53 The repetition rate of carrier pulses likely plays a key role in determining access to 54 speech temporal envelope information. While low carrier pulse rates are expected to 55 distort envelope modulations and impair speech perception, higher pulse rates should, 56 in principle, enhance temporal modulation precision. Despite these theoretical 57 predictions, studies investigating the impact of pulse rate on speech perception have 58 yielded inconsistent results. Some studies demonstrate benefits from moderate-to-high 59 pulse rates (Kiefer et al., 2001; Loizou et al., 2000; Nie et al., 2006; Verschuur, 2005), 60 and some others report either no discernible advantage across pulse rates (Berg et al., 61 2023; Friesen et al., 2005; Plant et al., 2007; Shannon et al., 2011) or benefits at lower 62 pulse rates (Brochier et al., 2017; Vandali et al., 2000). Furthermore, several studies 63 have identified individually optimal pulse rates, which vary among CI users with no 64 consistent pattern (Plant et al., 2007; Shader et al., 2020; Weber et al., 2007). 2.4. Experiment 2: Amplitude Modulation (AM) Detection 182 Stimuli for AM detection consisted of sinusoidally amplitude-modulated noise bands 183 processed using experimental ACE strategy conditions that varied in channel rate and 184 number of channels, as described in section 2.3. The carrier was random noise low-185 pass filtered below 6 kHz with a 6th-order Butterworth filter. The carrier noise bands 186 were regenerated for each presentation at full scale between -1 and 1 and then scaled 187 to prevent clipping after AM. This scaling ensured that 100% AM depth remained within 188 the full-scale range, eliminating the risk of distortion in the AM stimuli. AM stimuli were 189 created by modulating noise-band carriers with 25 Hz sinusoids at random phases. This 190 AM frequency was at least five times smaller than all carrier rates, meeting the criteria 191 for accurate envelope sampling as established by modulation discrimination tasks. 192 Performance in these tasks remained accurate when the carrier rate was at least four 193 times the modulation frequency (McKay et al., 1994). Furthermore, the 25Hz AM 194 frequency represents the frequency range of critical speech envelope cues, which are 195 predominantly below 20Hz (Sinha, Azadpour, 2024; Souza, Rosen, 2009). 196 The detection threshold for AM depth was measured in an adaptive 3-interval 3-197 alternative forced choice task. In each trial, two intervals contained non-AM noise, while 198 one interval, presented in random order, contained AM noise. Participants were tasked 199 with identifying the interval containing the AM noise. The duration of the stimulus in 200 each interval was 500ms and the inter-stimulus interval was 800ms. Statistical Analysis 209 Repeated measures ANOVA analyses were used to evaluate the effects of pulse rate on 210 different outcome measures, including T and C levels, AM detection thresholds and 211 consonant recognition performance. The within-subject effects of pulse rate were 213 analyzed using a conservative within-subject approach, where significant effects were 214 determined based on non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals. The correlations 215 between different outcome measures and between single-channel and multi-channel 216 measures were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. 217 218

We evaluated the role of within-electrode temporal transmission in 372 multi-channel outcomes by evaluating the correlation between single-channel and multi-373 channel results. The correlation between single-channel and multi-channel performance 374 was evaluated for AM detection and consonant recognition tests with data being 375 averaged across the tested pulse rates. Averaging across pulse rates provided a more 376 reliable composite estimate for each measure and allowed us to perform a single 377 correlation for each test as opposed to multiple correlations for each pair of rates. 378 Figure 6A illustrates the relationship between AM detection thresholds for single-379 channel and multi-channel strategies across 10 subjects. Pearson correlation analysis 380 revealed a strong relationship between single-channel and multi-channel AM detection 381 performance (r=0.89, n=10, p<0.001; 95% confidence interval: [0.59, 0.97]). These 382 findings suggest that detection of AM presented to a single cochlear site in the middle of 383

4. Discussion 417 

4.1. Effect of pulse rate and electrode location on T and C levels 418 The average T and C levels across the electrode array decreased as pulse rate 419 increased, with T levels showing a larger and more systematic reduction compared to C 420 levels, consistent with previous findings (Kreft et al., 2004; Zhou et al., 2012). The 421 pronounced impact of pulse rate on T levels may be explained by the facilitative effects 422 of pulse interactions at lower current amplitudes (Boulet et al., 2016; Middlebrooks, 423 2004). Analysis of T and C levels across the array revealed significantly higher values 424 for mid-array electrodes compared to apical electrodes at low pulse rates (125pps and 425 250pps). This observation is consistent with previous reports on T level patterns 426

It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 29, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.24.734273 doi: bioRxiv preprint

20 obtained with perimodiolar electrode array designs (Gordon, Papsin, 2013) and may 427 reflect the larger electrode-to-modiolus distances in the mid-cochlear region for these 428 electrode arrays (Long et al., 2014). Notably, the across-array differences in T and C 429 levels diminished at higher pulse rates. It has been proposed that T levels provide 430 insights into the local electrode-neural interface quality at each electrode site (Bierer, 431 2010; Long et al., 2014; Pfingst, Xu, 2004). The observed reduction in spatial variations 432 in T levels at higher stimulation rates may result from temporal pulse interactions 433 broadening the spatial spread of neural excitation and reducing local specificity 434 (Middlebrooks, 2004; Zhou, 2016). This suggests that low-rate stimulation may be more 435 effective for identifying local electrode-neural interface quality across CI arrays. 436 

4.2. Effect of pulse rate on consonant recognition 437 Consonant recognition scores were similar across mid to high pulse rates between 250 438 and 2000pps. This range includes the lowest and highest pulse rates that are typically 439 used in clinical CI strategies. However, consonant recognition significantly declined at 440 125pps for both single-channel and multi-channel ACE strategies, demonstrating a 441 consistent pattern of reduced speech perception at low pulse rates. This pattern was 442 also observed for information transmission of consonant features, where transmission of 443 manner of articulation feature was consistently poorer for 125pps compared to higher 444 rates. The decline at 125pps is consistent with perceptual constrains on temporal 445 envelope processing at low carrier rates, rather than inadequate sampling of the 446 acoustic envelope. Critical speech envelope variations are predominantly below 20Hz 447 (Sinha, Azadpour, 2024; Souza, Rosen, 2009), and should be precisely sampled at 448

It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 29, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.24.734273 doi: bioRxiv preprint

21 125pps carrier rate (McKay et al., 1994). The poorer performance at 125pps likely 449 reflects perceptual inefficiencies at lower pulse rates rather than encoding limitations. 450 

4.3. Variability in pulse rate effects across individuals 451 Individual subject results revealed significant variability in the effects of pulse rate on 452 consonant recognition and information transfer of articulation features. This observation 453 is consistent with previous indications that optimal pulse rates can vary across 454 individuals (Plant et al., 2007; Shader et al., 2020; Weber et al., 2007). The results of 455 this study show that pulse rate can differentially affect the transmission of consonant 456 articulation features across individuals, meaning that optimal pulse rate may be different 457 for different consonant features within an individual. This may explain the overall flat 458 pattern of consonant recognition scores as a function of pulse rate. Additionally, pulse 459 rate can affect single-channel and multi-channel outcomes differently. The inconsistency 460 between single-channel and multi-channel strategies reveals that pulse rate influences 461 within-channel and cross-channel speech cues differently. These observations suggest 462 that pulse rate benefits depend on individual auditory profiles that determine the 463 transmission of temporal and spectral cues. 464 

4.4. AM detection and partial support for inverse U-shaped pattern 465 AM detection results were partially consistent with an inverse U-shaped function of 466 pulse rate. The decline at 125 pps was statistically robust, while the decline at 4000 467 pps, though descriptively present, did not reach significance. These results provide 468 partial support for the hypothesis that very high pulse rates impair modulation sensitivity, 469 but larger samples with greater statistical power are needed to confirm the high-rate 470 effect. The poorer AM detection at 125pps is likely due to psychophysical limitations in 471

22 modulation sensitivity rather than imprecise envelope sampling, as the 25Hz modulation 472 frequency used in this study meets the minimum criteria for accurate sampling at 473 125pps (McKay et al., 1994). Notably, poorer AM detection at 125pps is consistent with 474 poorer consonant recognition at this rate, suggesting that reduced modulation 475 transmission at low pulse rates may underlie poorer speech perception at these rates. 476 The non-significant reduction in single-channel AM detection performance at 4000pps 477 aligns with previous findings that high pulse rates impair sensitivity to amplitude 478 variations (Galvin, Fu, 2005; Galvin, Fu, 2009), and may result from increased loudness 479 variability (Azadpour et al., 2018). However, the decline at 4000pps was not statistically 480 significant, suggesting that perceptual effects at high rates may be inconsistent and 481 subject to individual variability. 482 AM detection performance with single-channel strategies was significantly better than 483 with multi-channel strategies. This is a counterintuitive observation given the 484 expectation that multi-channel stimulation across the cochlea would enhance the 485 representation of amplitude modulations. Electrodogram analyses revealed that multi-486 channel strategies result in greater fluctuations in current amplitudes and lower overall 487 stimulation levels compared to single-channel strategies. Envelope fluctuations in 488 narrow band channels and reduced channel-specific current levels may explain the 489 poorer AM detection performance observed for multi-channel approaches (Fraser, 490 McKay, 2012; Galvin, Fu, 2009). 491 

4.5. Role of single-channel temporal envelope in multi-channel outcomes 492 The correlations between single-channel and multi-channel performance emphasize the 493 importance of temporal envelopes at individual cochlear sites in supporting broader 494

AM detection thresholds with single-channel strategies strongly 495 predicted AM thresholds with multi-channel strategies (r=0.89), demonstrating that 496 temporal cue transmission at a single electrode plays a critical role in multi-channel 497 responses. Similarly, single-channel speech recognition performance moderately 498 correlated with multi-channel outcomes (r=0.64), with variability in single-electrode 499 envelope transmission accounting for approximately 40% of the variance in multi-500 channel speech recognition results. 501 These findings support an important contribution of within-channel temporal envelope 502 processing to multi-channel speech perception outcomes in cochlear implant users. 503 Single-channel measures provide a valuable approach for evaluating envelope 504 transmission at individual cochlear sites, allowing researchers and clinicians to isolate 505 and assess temporal processing capabilities independent of cross-channel spectral 506 representations. Single-channel speech assessments used in this study may offer a 507 means of identifying electrode-specific contributions to speech cue transmission in CIs. 508 

4.6. Absence of significant correlation between AM detection and consonant 509 

recognition 510 In contrast to some previous studies (Fu, 2002; Won et al., 2011), this study didn’t find a 511 significant correlation between AM detection thresholds and consonant identification 512 scores for either single-channel or multi-channel strategies. This absence of correlation 513 may be attributable to the relatively low modulation frequency of 25 Hz used for AM 514 stimuli in the present study, compared to 100Hz or larger modulation frequencies used 515 by Fu (2002) and Won et all (2011). Although 25 Hz falls within the range of speech-516 critical envelope frequencies (Sinha, Azadpour, 2024; Souza, Rosen, 2009), these 517

The reasons why significant correlations have been 520 reported at higher modulation frequencies, beyond those considered critical for speech 521 envelope encoding, warrants further investigation. 522 

4.7. Limitations and future directions 523 This study has several limitations that should be considered. The modest sample size (n 524 = 10) consisted exclusively of post-lingually deafened adults using Cochlear Nucleus 525 devices with perimodiolar electrode arrays, which limits the generalizability of findings to 526 other CI systems and patient populations. The multi-channel experimental strategies 527 employed a fixed maxima of 6, differing from the standard clinical configurations. 528 Additionally, single-channel testing was conducted using a single mid-array electrode 529 (electrode 12), and the findings may not generalize to apical or basal regions of the 530 cochlea. Only one AM frequency (25 Hz) was tested; evaluating a broader range of 531 modulation frequencies could provide a more comprehensive understanding of temporal 532 processing across pulse rates. While T and C levels were measured at each rate to 533 control for rate-dependent loudness differences, residual confounds related to rate-534 dependent changes in loudness growth cannot be entirely ruled out. 535 Despite these limitations, the findings of this study highlight the complex relationship 536 between pulse rate, temporal envelope processing, and consonant recognition in CI 537 users. Mid-range pulse rates generally produced consistent performance, but the 538 variability observed across individuals underscores the importance of personalized 539 programming strategies to optimize CI outcomes.
