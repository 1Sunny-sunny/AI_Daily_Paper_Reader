Abstract 13 

Episodic memory requires reconstructing the position of the self within a remembered 14 

environment, yet whether and how memory engrams incorporate self-referenced spatial 15 

information remains unknown. Using activity-dependent engram tagging, longitudinal 16 

calcium imaging and optogenetic perturbation in the retrosplenial cortex, we found that 17 

cortical memory engrams are enriched for egocentric and boundary-coding neurons that 18 

encode self-position relative to environmental boundaries. Longitudinal imaging revealed that 19 

future engram neurons are preferentially recruited from a pre-existing spatial scaffold rather 20 

than generated de novo during learning. During memory retrieval, scaffold populations 21 

underwent coordinated refinement and became transiently reorganized into a scaffold– 22 

engram network state whose dynamics tracked memory expression. Silencing scaffold 23 

neurons reduced memory expression while preserving recall dynamics, whereas engram 24 

silencing abolished recall dynamics while maintaining a stable low-memory state. Together, 25 

these findings identify a scaffold–engram architecture through which episodic memories are 26 

reconstructed by reinstating the self-referenced spatial representations within remembered 27 

space. 28

Introduction 29 

Every episodic memory carries a spatial perspective — the position and orientation of the self 30 

within the environment where an experience occurred 1-3 . This first-person perspective 31 

distinguishes episodic recollection from general semantic knowledge: to remember is not 32 

merely to know what happened, but to reconstruct where one was situated when it happened 33  

> 2,3

. How the brain represents this self-referenced spatial perspective within the cellular 34 

architecture of cortical memory remains unknown. 35 

Memory engrams — ensembles of neurons activated during learning and reactivated during 36 

recall — provide the cellular substrate for long-term memory storage and retrieval 4-9 .37 

Engram studies have established which neurons are activated during memory encoding and 38 

that their reactivation is sufficient to drive recall 4,7 . Recent work has shown that hippocampal 39 

engram neurons preferentially encode stable allocentric place fields 10 linking engram identity 40 

to the allocentric place cell code. However, allocentric representations — including the place 41 

cells, grid cells, and boundary representations 11-14 — describe environmental structure 42 

independently of the observer and do not specify the remembered position of the self within 43 

that environment. Whether and how memory engrams incorporate self-referenced spatial 44 

information has not been examined. 45 

Egocentric spatial representations have increasingly been identified across distributed cortical 46 

and hippocampal networks involved in memory and navigation, including retrosplenial, 47 

parietal, entorhinal, hippocampal, and prefrontal circuits 15-24 . These representations encode 48 

the position and orientation of the body relative to environmental structure, providing a 49 

potential framework through which experiences may be organized relative to the self. Among 50 

these regions, the retrosplenial cortex (RSC) is particularly well positioned to mediate 51 

transformations between allocentric environmental representations and body-centered spatial 52

frameworks 25-29 . Computational and theoretical models have proposed that RSC integrates 53 

contextual and self-referenced information required for episodic memory reconstruction 1,30 .54 

Consistent with this role, RSC disruption impairs both spatial orientation and contextual 55 

memory in rodents and humans 31-35 . Together, these findings suggest that RSC may provide 56 

a cortical substrate through which remembered experiences are reconstructed from a first-57 

person spatial perspective. 58 

Here we show that cortical memory engrams in RSC are not randomly organized neural 59 

ensembles but are embedded within a pre-existing egocentric spatial scaffold composed 60 

predominantly of egocentric and boundary-coding populations. This scaffold pre-exists 61 

memory formation, undergoes coordinated global refinement during recall, and becomes 62 

transiently reorganized into a coordinated network state during memory retrieval. 63 

Optogenetic dissection reveals dissociable causal contributions of scaffold and engram 64 

populations: Scaffold silencing reduced memory expression while preserving the temporal 65 

decay dynamics characteristic of contextual fear memory retrieval, indicating that the 66 

scaffold contributes to the magnitude of memory expression but does not drive retrieval 67 

dynamics. In contrast, engram silencing abolished the normal temporal evolution of recall 68 

while maintaining a stable low-memory state, indicating that the engram drives the active 69 

dynamics of memory retrieval within the scaffold framework. Together, these findings 70 

identify a scaffold–engram architecture that organizes cortical memory around self-71 

referenced spatial representations. 72

Cortical memory engrams preferentially encode self-referenced spatial representations 74 

To determine what information cortical memory engrams encode, we performed activity-75 

dependent engram tagging combined with longitudinal Ca 2+ imaging in RSC during 76 

contextual fear conditioning (CFC) and recall. AAV-cFos-tTA, AAV-CaMKII-Cre, and 77 

AAV-TRE-DIO-GCaMP6s were injected into RSC to express GCaMP6s in CaMKII-positive 78 

excitatory neurons under c-Fos-tTA/TRE control (Fig. 1a). Mice underwent a three-day CFC 79 

protocol (Fig. 1b). During habituation (Day 1), mice freely explored the conditioning 80 

chamber for 30 min under the DOX-on condition, preventing activity-dependent labeling. 81 

During conditioning (Day 2), mice were transitioned to the DOX-off condition to permit c-82 

Fos-dependent GCaMP6s expression in excitatory neurons active during memory encoding. 83 

Immediately following conditioning, mice were returned to the DOX-on condition to restrict 84 

labeling to the conditioning epoch. During recall (Day 3), mice were re-exposed to the 85 

conditioned context under the DOX-on condition, and tagged engram neurons were imaged 86 

during memory retrieval (Fig. 1b, c). Histological analysis confirmed recruitment of RSC 87 

excitatory neurons during CFC, as indicated by c-Fos expression (Extended Data Fig. 1). c-88 

Fos-tagged GCaMP6s-expressing engram neurons were imaged during re-exposure to the 89 

conditioned context, yielding Ca²   traces from which spikes were deconvolved (Fig. 1d). 90 

Animals exhibited robust freezing during recall, confirming successful memory retrieval (Fig. 91 

1e). 92

boundaries relative to the self. The predominant population exhibited egocentric boundary 94 

tuning, firing selectively at specific egocentric bearing ( θego ), and egocentric distance ( Dego )95

comprised the non-engram population (59.8 %, n = 453 in 10 mice; Fig. 2f). 117

Using this classification, we retrospectively examined the spatial tuning of SR and SNR 118

SNR EBCs exhibited selective firing at preferred θego and Dego during habituation that 121 

became sharper during recall, reflected by increased peak firing rates and increased 122 

boundary-proximal firing (Fig. 2g, h, Extended Data Fig. 3). Similarly, representative SR and 123 

SNR BCs exhibited stronger firing proximal to environmental boundaries during recall 124

egocentric and contextual boundary representations are refined during memory retrieval 126 

across both engram and non-engram populations. 127 

To quantify scaffold composition across learning, EBCs and BCs were classified using 128 

thresholds derived from habituation-session shuffle distributions (95 th percentile), and the 129 

same thresholds were applied to recall data to enable direct comparison across sessions. 130 

During habituation, SR and SNR populations exhibited indistinguishable spatial cell type 131 

compositions, with no significant differences in EBC or BC proportions (Fig. 2k), 132 

demonstrating that future engram neurons are recruited from the same pre-existing spatial 133 

coding population as non-engram neurons. During recall, the proportions of both EBCs and 134 

BCs increased significantly relative to habituation in SR and SNR populations alike (Fig. 2k), 135 

indicating that increased recruitment of scaffold populations occurs globally across the 136 

network rather than selectively within engram neurons. 137 

To assess within-neuron tuning refinement, egocentric tuning and boundary coding properties 138 

were quantified for habituation-classified neurons during recall using the same habituation-139 

derived thresholds. Vector length increased significantly from habituation to recall in both SR 140 

and SNR EBC populations (Fig. 2l), accompanied by significantly more dispersed θego 141

Discussion 260 

Episodic memory requires reconstructing the position of the self within a remembered 261 

environment, yet how cortical circuits embed the self-referenced spatial information within 262 

memory representations remains unknown. Our results show that cortical engrams in the RSC 263 

are organized around an egocentric spatial scaffold that encodes the position of the body 264 

relative to environmental boundaries. This organization anchors memory to a representation 265 

of the self relative to environmental structure. Whereas hippocampal representations provide 266 

allocentric maps of space, our findings identify a distinct cortical framework in which 267 

memory is structured around the egocentric position of the self, providing the first-person 268 

spatial perspective that defines episodic recall. 269 

The prevailing engram framework has established which neurons are activated during 270 

memory encoding and that their reactivation drives recall 4,7,36,38-40 , but has not addressed how 271 

memory is structured relative to the self. Fos-expressing hippocampal neurons preferentially 272 

encode stable allocentric place fields 10 , linking engram identity to the allocentric place cell 273 

code. However, allocentric representations describe environmental structure independently of 274 

the observer and therefore do not specify where the self was situated within the remembered 275 

environment. Our findings reveal a distinct cortical organization in which RSC engrams are 276 

embedded within a spatial scaffold composed predominantly of egocentric boundary cells 277 

and boundary-coding populations that together encode self-position relative to environmental 278 

geometry. This organization is consistent with theoretical models proposing that RSC 279 

mediates transformations between allocentric maps and body-centered reference frames 280

A central question raised by this organization is whether the spatial scaffold emerges during 282 

memory formation or pre-exists as its substrate. Our longitudinal analyses demonstrate that 283 

future engram neurons are recruited from a pre-existing population that already encodes self-284 

position relative to environmental geometry. The scaffold therefore constitutes a coordinate 285 

framework that is recruited into memory rather than generated by it. This finding has 286 

important implications for theories of engram formation: memory encoding may selectively 287 

recruit neurons from an existing spatial scaffold rather than constructing entirely new 288 

representations de novo. Such an architecture may help explain why RSC disruption produces 289 

profound contextual memory deficits 9,41-43 : without the scaffold, the coordinate framework 290 

within which memories are organized is disrupted from the outset. 291 

Although the scaffold pre-exists memory formation, it does not remain static. During recall, 292 

egocentric and boundary-related representations transiently strengthened across both engram 293 

and non-engram populations. This global refinement indicates that the scaffold is 294 

dynamically modulated during memory retrieval, providing an enhanced coordinate 295 

framework during active memory expression. Importantly, this refinement was distributed 296 

across both engram and non-engram populations, indicating that scaffold sharpening is a 297 

network-level property rather than an engram-specific plasticity mechanism. 298 

Consistent with this interpretation, memory retrieval transiently reorganized the scaffold into 299 

a coordinated network state. Spatial scaffold populations showed preferential learning-300 

dependent coordination with engram neurons during recall, a pattern absent during 301 

habituation and therefore specific to memory retrieval. Previous studies have reported 302 

increased synchrony between engram and non-engram neurons during memory retrieval 36,37 ,303 

but the coordination observed here was spatially structured: engram neurons preferentially 304 

interacted with egocentric and boundary-coding populations rather than with non-spatial 305

These findings raise important questions about how egocentric cortical engrams interact with 338 

allocentric hippocampal engrams during episodic memory retrieval. Hippocampal engrams 339 

encode contextual experience and allocentric spatial information 10,44 , whereas RSC engrams 340 

encode the egocentric position of the self within that same environment. These systems 341 

therefore appear complementary: the hippocampus represents environmental structure 342 

through allocentric place, grid cell, and boundary representations 11-14 , whereas the RSC 343 

prominently represents the position of the self within that structure. This functional division 344 

aligns with theoretical distinctions between allocentric and egocentric reference frames that 345 

are central to models of episodic memory 1,3,30 .346 

Egocentric spatial representations are increasingly being identified across multiple brain 347 

regions classically associated with memory and navigation, including medial entorhinal 348 

cortex, hippocampus, posterior parietal cortex, and prefrontal cortex 15-21,23,24 . Although these 349 

signals have largely been studied in the context of spatial navigation and decision-making, 350 

their presence across distributed memory-related circuits raises the possibility that self-351 

referenced spatial organization may represent a broader principle of memory processing 352 

rather than a specialization unique to RSC. Within this framework, different memory systems 353 

may encode complementary aspects of episodic experience across distinct reference frames, 354

These findings also have implications for memory disorders. Early RSC dysfunction in 360 

Alzheimer’s disease precedes substantial hippocampal degeneration 45,46 and may disrupt the 361

which memories are organized. 367

scaffold that is refined during recall, reorganized into coordinated network dynamics during 369 

memory retrieval, and required for memory expression during recall. Together, these findings 370 

identify a principle of cortical memory organization in which past experience is reconstructed 371 

by placing the self within the spatial structure of the remembered experience. 372
