# Abstract 11 

Speech perception requires transforming acoustic input into neural representations that support 12 

linguistic understanding, yet its underlying computational principles remain unclear. Classical 13 

efficient coding theories posit optimal compression of sensory input, whereas alternative accounts 14 

propose that neural systems preferentially encode information that supports prediction. A key open 15 

question is whether such predictive en coding operates on fixed inputs or on flexible internal 16 

representations . We instantiated three hypothesis models of speech processing : (i) optimal 17 

compression with deep autoencoders, (ii) predictive reconstruction with predictive autoencoders, 18 

and (iii) predictive information representation via latent -space prediction using contrastive learning .19 

We compared resulting speech latent representations to electroencephalograph ic (EEG) activity 20 

during speech listening . Representations learned under the predictive information objective best 21 

explained neural latents . Crucially, only representations that selectively compressed predictive 22 

information predicted behavioral performance, suggesting that neural speech representations are 23 

structured to encode predictive information in latent space rather than to maximize compression or 24 

input prediction. 25 

# Main 26 

The ultimate goal of perception is explanation —the ability to infer representations of the world from 27 

sensory inputs that are useful for prediction and behavior. 1 As an example, speech perception is a 28 

task in which successful comprehension requires extracting relevant acoustic information from 29 

signals in which informative and irrelevant components are nonlinearly mixed 2. Classic sensory 30 

coding theories argue for the importance of information maximization about the input stimulus under 31 

channel capacity constraints. 3–6 Alternatively, the nervous system may follow a predictive encoding 32 

principle, producing efficient representations of predictive information. 7–9 Although both principles 33 

implement forms of efficient coding, they differ fundamentally: the former lacks an explicit notion of 34 

relevant information within signal variability and therefore assumes that no information should be 35 

discarded, whereas the latter selectively prioritizes information that supports prediction. 36 

Prediction is a fundamental function of the brain for guiding future actions. For instance, when 37 

catching a ball, sensory inputs must inform future movements that are inherently delayed due to 38 

biological constraints such as sensory transduction and muscle activation 10 . From another 39 

perspective, actions can be viewed as means to reach an expected sensory state 11,12 , such as a40 

proprioceptive state indicating that the ball has been successfully caught . In both cases , a 41 

representation of the trajectory of the ball is useful only insofar as it allows predicting its evolution 42 

sufficiently far in to the future. This idea that the neural system should learn an efficient ‘ small -scale 43 

model ’ from which to generate predictions dates back to Craik (1943) , who first proposed such 44 

predictions should occur in a learnable symbolic (latent) space. However, whether and how these 45 

theoretical principles map onto computations during speech perception remains largely unknown. 46 

M/EEG studies have often circumvented this problem by focusing on specific, a priori features of the 47 

speech signal known to drive cortical activity, such as the envelope in delta/theta bands 13 –16

how the brain autonomously extracts relevant information from the full sensory input. More recently, 51 

deep learning models developed for automatic speech recognition (ASR) ,19,20 and text generation 52

These representations have enabled hypothesis testing about the nature of internal representations 54 

during naturalistic listening 21,22 23 –26 . For example, Caucheteux and colleagues (2022) 27 used the 55 

deep language model GPT -2 to show how the brain makes simultaneous predictions at different 56 

hierarchical levels of the linguistic structure, from frontoparietal to temporal cortices. 57 

However, none of these approaches explicitly tests how the neural system constructs efficient 58 

internal models of speech. Specifically, during speech listening, neural activity could be optimized to 59 

follow (i) an information -maximization principle to produce efficient codes of the speech stimulus via 60 

optimal compression, (ii) a predictive reconstruction principle through prediction error minimization 61 

about the future speech input, or (iii) a predictive information principle via prediction in a learnable 62 

latent space. To test these hypotheses, we first extract task -relevant information using a state -of -63 

the -art speech recognition model (wav2vec 2.0), thereby operating in a high -performance speech 64 

representation space. We then instantiate these principles into alternative deep neural networks 65 

(DNNs) by modifying their architecture and objective function. We train these models and compare 66 

their latent speech representations with EEG activity of 26 participants listening to the same 67 

sentences while performing a speech recognition task (Fig 1a). 68 

To implement the proposed hypotheses in DNN models we trained (i) a deep autoencoder (deep -69 

AE) that learns optimal nonlinear compression of speech features using a decoder trained with a 70 

reconstruction loss, (ii) a deep predictive autoencoder (deep -pAE) that learns optimal nonlinear 71 

compression of speech features with a decoder that minimizes the prediction error about the future 72 

input and (iii) a contrastive learning model that learns a representation of the predictive information 73 

by optimizing a loss in the latent space with no reconstruction (CL) 28 . Among these models, CL 74 

representations best explained neural activity . Furthermore, when CL was trained to compress 75 

predictive information using an output bottleneck, neural encoding of resulting speech 76 

representations correlated with participants’ speech recognition performance . This selective 77 

alignment suggests that a key principle of neural information processing during speech perception 78 

is the extraction of predictive information through prediction in latent space, enabling the system to 79 

discard irrelevant signal details . Beyond providing insight into the neural computations underlying 80 

speech perception, our results offer a principled framework for designing DNN -based hypotheses of 81 

speech perception whose representations can be directly compared with neural latent 82 

representations measured in human EEG. 83 

# Results 84 

Speech recognition performance 85 

We analyzed the EEG activity of 26 healthy Italian participants listening to short English sentences 86 

(n=100; from 2.9 to 4.5 seconds duration). All participants were native Italian speakers who self -87 

reported English as their second language (the use of a second language ensures greater 88 

intersubject variability and avoids ceiling effects in comprehension performances). Stimuli were 89 

selected from the USC -Timit dataset 29 which provides recordings of American English speakers, 90 

along with their transcripts. Each sentence was presented three times in a randomized order during 91 

the experiment, resulting in a total of 300 trials per participant, divided in three blocks. Each sentence 92 

was followed by a speech recognition task (Fig. 1a bottom). The task consisted in the visual 93 

presentation of one word at the end of the played audio which could either rhyme or not rhyme with 94 

any of the words in the heard sentence. This task implicitly forces participants to perform an internal 95 

transcription of the words to be compared with the one presented at screen. Participants were asked 96 

to indicate whether the word rhymed or not by pressing a button. To avoid response bias, rhyming 97 

and non -rhyming words were matched for their average frequency of use (~6e -05). Participants’ 98 

success rate in the blocks ranged from 57 % to 87 % (Fig 1b). Average success rate in the three 99 

blocks was not significantly different (Fig. 1c), thus ensuring compatible results across the whole 100 

session. To verify that the designed rhyming task is a good proxy of speech -to -text skills, we 101

Neural encoding of wav2vec 2.0 features does not correlate with participants’ speech 124 

recognition performance 125 

We first transformed speech signal s using the wav2vec 2.0 (Extended Data Fig. 1a), which is the 126 

current state -of -the -art for speech to text tasks 19 . Features extracted by wav2vec 2.0 are expected 127 

to retain primarily the information necessary to perform speech recognition. Moreover, wav2vec 2.0 128 

features are more effective when decoding M/EEG signals than simple spectrogram features (Mel 129 

Spectrogram) and features learnt end -to -end together with M/EEG data (Deep Mel) 30 .130 

To check that wav2vec 2.0 features are relevant for participants during speech listening , we 131 

computed mutual information (MI) between the participants’ EEG activity and the output wav2vec 132 

2.0 latents of the same speech signals using the Gaussian Copula Mutual Information (GCMI) 133 

estimator 31 . We expected this to be encoded in the listeners’ activity with a reasonable lag and to 134 

show a meaningful topography of information encoding. At the same time, we hypothesized that the 135 

strength of this encoding would not predict participants’ behavioral performance in the speech 136 

recognition task. Indeed, unlike biological systems, wav2vec 2.0 is optimized solely for recognition 137 

performance, whereas neural representations are shaped by efficiency constraints that prioritize 138 

task -relevant information under biological limitations. 139 

In line with previous findings 22,25 , we found that wav2vec 2.0 features are significantly encoded in 140 

the listeners’ neural activity ( p = 2.00e -04; one side cluster -based statistics 32 against the mean of 141 

100 surrogate data; Extended Data Fig 1b ). This encoding was primarily localized over central and 142 

temporal electrod es ( Extended Data Fig 1b) . However, in line with our hypothesis, the strength of 143 

encoding of the features did not correlate with participants’ speech recognition performance ( r =144 

0.09, p = 6.44e -01; Extended Data Fig. 1c). 145 

To our knowledge, this is the first evidence of such a dissociation: although wav2vec 2.0 146 

representations are significantly encoded in neural activity, they do not appear to isolate the subset 147 

of information that is behaviorally relevant for human speech recognition . Together, these results 148 

indicate that wav2vec 2.0 features provide a strong and neurally grounded representational basis 149 

yet remain insufficient to fully capture the computations underlying human behavior. 150 

Linear dimensionality reduction does not isolate relevant information 151 

To make sure that linear dimensionality reduction cannot fill the missing correlation with behavior, 152 

we ran a principal component analysis (PCA) 33 , that, beyond visualization, is often employed in 153 

neuroscience to reduce dimensionality while preserving relevant information 34 –36 . In a similar way, 154 

we, here, try to isolate the behaviorally relevant information in the dataset using PCA on wav2vec 155 

2.0 features. Because PCA orders components by explained variance, components with small 156 

eigenvalues are often treated as noise and discarded. However, this heuristic can obscure 157 

structured, task -relevant information that resides in low -variance dimensions, for example, when 158 

solving image recognition with deep neural networks 37 .159 

For this reason, w e applied PCA to the wav2vec 2.0 output and separated it into top - and bottom -160 

variance subspaces (Fig. 2b, left). Top variance subspaces are obtained by cumulating explained 161 

variance ratio starting from the most informative feature (PC1) to reach a targeted value, conversely 162 

bottom variance subspaces are generated to reach a targeted explained variance value starting from 163 

the least informative feature (the last PC; Fig. 2a). Even subspaces explaining as little as 0.1% of 164 

variance retained time -specific information and spectral content consistent with top subspaces (Fig. 165 

2b, right). Sentence correlation matrices revealed lower correlations in bottom subspaces, 166 

suggesting they encode more sentence -specific information (Fig. 2c). 167 

Decoding analyses confirmed this: when retaining increasingly large fractions of variance in the top 168 

subspaces (25 – 99.99 %), decoding accuracy improved up to ~90% explained variance, after which 169 

gains plateaued (Fig. 2d). In contrast, when high -variance components were excluded, decoding 170 

accuracy peaked in the bottom 1% variance subspace (75.3%), indicating that low -variance PCs 171 

carry meaningful information often overlooked by standard PCA practice. Global and local nuclear 172 

norm analyses supported these results. In practice, global nuclear norm is a proxy of linear 173 

separability of the classes (the higher the better), while local nuclear norm is a measure of the 174 

compactness of the data (the higher the less compact). Bottom subspaces exhibited higher global 175

Predictive information is best encoded in neural activity and its compression explains 213 

participants’ speech recognition performance 214 

We now want to test alternative encoding hypotheses on speech perception. To do so we need to 215 

move beyond PCA and constrain speech features using more complex nonlinear models. To our 216 

knowledge, no previous work has tried to test speech perception hypotheses by changing the 217 

architecture and the objective function of DNN models and comparing the resulting speech latents 218 

with human neural latents (Fig. 3a -b) 219 

We first verified that our three models produce sufficiently distinct speech latents reflecting the initial 220 

hypotheses: (i) optimal compression via a deep autoencoder (deep -AE) optimizing nonlinear 221 

reconstruction of wav2vec 2.0 features, (ii) predictive information in signal space with a predictive 222 

deep autoencoder (deep -pAE) minimizing future prediction error, and (iii) predictive information in 223 

latent space using a contrastive learning model (CL) optimizing latent -space prediction via InfoNCE 224 

loss (Extended Data Fig. 3a –b). CL representations showed the highest consistency across training 225 

runs, remaining reproducible even at very low dimensionalities 28 (Fig. 3c), and exhibited strong 226 

temporal predictability with consistent spectral structure across dimensions (Fig. 3d). Deep -AE and 227 

deep -pAE performed well on reconstruction but showed limited predictive accuracy at low 228 

dimensionality, whereas CL supported superior temporal prediction even with three latent 229 

dimensions , consistent with compact spaces favoring isolation of predictive information. 230 

Dimensionality seven was chosen as an effective bottleneck, above which predictive information 231 

extraction improves by no more than 1% (Extended Data Fig. 3c). CL representations also remained 232 

more temporally predictive than deep -pAE latents at larger offsets, despite no explicit multi -step 233 

training (Extended Data Fig. 4b –d). 234 

To determine which hypothesis best accounts for neural computations, we quantified EEG encoding 235 

of each model's speech latents and tested whether encoding strength correlated with speech 236 

recognition performance. The best -fitting hypothesis should (i) yield the most strongly neurally 237 

encoded features and (ii) show a positive encoding strength –performance correlation. 238 

To this end, we computed mutual information between speech latents and neural latents (Fig. 3a) .239 

Across all output dimensionalities, CL latents were significantly more strongly encoded in the EEG 240 

than both autoencoder model s (Fig. 3e ; CL vs p -AE: p = 1.81e -02, p = 3.04e -05, p = 1.57e -05, p =241

persisted in time -resolved analyses (Extended Data Fig. 5a), where CL consistently showed broader 244

Therefore, producing speech representations through predictive information maximization via 246 

prediction in latent space better explained neural activity. Crucially, only the average encoding of 247

Speech neural encoding parallels non redundant predictive information 283 

If the brain follows a similar predictive encoding strategy, we should observe a comparable temporal 284 

pattern of predictive information accumulation in both speech and neural latents. To test this, we 285 

compared how the CL model accumulates predictive speech information at inference time (Fig. 4a) 286 

with how neural latents encode predictive information during speech listening. Although the model 287 

is trained on next -sample prediction (20 ms ahead), its predictive and memorization abilities are 288 

evaluated over a larger window spanning 600 ms before to 800 ms after speech sample 289 

presentation, consistent with previous EEG encoding analyses. A dominant temporal regularity in 290 

speech , and a prominent feature of EEG speech tracking , is the syllable, whose duration across 291 

languages, including English, is approximately 200 ms. We therefore examined the accumulation of 292 

predictive information ( ΔI) in the 200 ms preceding speech sample presentation (Fig. 4b). At each 293 

20 ms step prior to presentation, the model acquires predictive information about future speech 294 

beyond what was already accumulated ( δi, Fig. 4a) , a quantity we term non -redundant predictive 295 

information , as it is, to a first -order approximation, predictive information not redundant with that 296 

acquired in the past. 297 

We split speech samples into two classes (Fig. 4c, 4i): those easily predictable 200 ms in advance 298 

(low -Δ-information: L ΔI) and those that are not (high -Δ-information: H ΔI) (Fig. 4d, 4j). Importantly, 299 

the two classes do not differ in predictability at the moment of presentation , the sole distinction lies 300 

in how much predictive information was accumulated beforehand. Non -redundant predictive 301 

information ( δ-information), defined as the temporal derivative of  ΔI, was consistently higher for H ΔI302 

samples during this window, reflecting the greater accumulation required to compensate for their 303 

initially lower  ΔI (Fig. 4e, 4k). 304 

EEG encoding computed separately for H ΔI and L ΔI samples revealed the expected parallel: H ΔI305 

neural encoding was initially lower but rose rapidly to exceed L ΔI within ~100 ms of onset (Fig. 4f, 306 

4l; dim 3: p = 4.00e –02, 3.40e –03; dim 7: p = 1.60e –03, 3.44e –02), and the cross -correlation 307 

between  δ-information and EEG encoding peaked at biologically plausible delays (Fig. 4g, 4m). 308 

Crucially, only the neural encoding of H ΔI samples during the early information -gathering window 309 

correlated with behavioral performance (dim 3: r = 0.428, p = 2.89e –02; dim 7: r = 0.430, p = 2.81e –310 

02; Fig. 4f, right), whereas later or L ΔI encodings did not. Together, these results suggest that 311 

behaviorally relevant neural responses are shaped by the early extraction of non -redundant 312 

predictive information. 313

# Discussion 333 

We show that neural activity during speech perception is best explained by a system optimized for 334 

the extraction of predictive information. Speech representations learned under this principle were 335 

consistently better encoded in neural activity than those derived from models based on optimal 336 

information compression. The central role of prediction is not surprising, given its ubiquity across 337 

brain functions and the well -established presence of predictive computations in speech processing 338 

at multiple levels , from phonemes and syllables to words 39 –41 , and even across longer temporal 339 

scales 27 . However, rather than focusing on prediction during speech inference, we approached the 340 

problem from a complementary perspective: how the brain learns the internal speech 341 

representations that are later used to support perception. In principle, different learning strategies 342 

could underlie speech perception. For example, the brain might first learn optimally compressed 343 

representations of speech, as proposed in classic efficient coding theories 4,5 , and subsequently build 344 

predictive mechanisms on top of these representations to guide perception. Under this hypothesis ,345 

models optimized for information compression should best explain neural activity, and their 346 

representations should be encoded in neural signals prior to stimulus onset (i.e., at negative lags), 347 

reflecting anticipatory predictions. However, our results do not support this scenario. Instead, models 348 

trained to extract predictive information produced representations that were more strongly encoded 349 

in neural activity, both at negative and positive temporal lags. This suggests that such 350 

representations are prioritized both by anticipatory processes (negative lags) and by stimulus -driven 351

speech information in neural activity closely tracked the model’s extraction of non -redundant 353 

predictive information within a critical ~200 ms speech window. Together, these findings support the 354 

idea that the brain relies on a predictive information principle during speech listening and learning. 355 

Our results, further show that speech perception is driven by the compression of such predictive 356 

information. Indeed, only low -dimensional output spaces produced speech representations whose 357 

neural encoding positively correlated with participants’ speech recognition performance. Using the 358 

InfoNCE objective, we identified the minimal latent dimensionality that maximized predictive 359 

information and observed that higher -dimensional representations increasingly incorporated 360 

reconstructive (and thus less task -relevant) features. These findings are consistent with the notion 361 

that the nervous system operates und er metabolic constraints 42 and that efficient information 362 

representation may confer evolutionary advantage 43 . However, while previous findings have 363 

highlighted the importance of compressing predictive information in the visual system 8,44 –46 and in 364 

artificial representation learning models 47,48 , it has not, to our knowledge, been directly examined in 365 

speech perception . I mportantly, although compressed predictive representations best explained 366 

behavioral performance, overall neural activity was better captured by higher -dimensional, 367 

redundant representations. The role of redundancy in neural systems has long been debated 49 , and 368 

complete redundancy reduction is now considered neither achievable nor necessarily desirable 50 –52 

369 

particularly given its benefits for robustness and error correction . Rather than eliminating 370 

redundancy, the nervous system may instead represent and segregate redundant and non -371 

redundant components in an explicit and functionally accessible manner 49 . In line with this view, our 372 

results suggest that while redundant, high -dimensional representations account for global neural 373 

activity, it is the explicit, capacity -limited representation of non -redundant predictive information that 374 

drives behavioral performance. 375 

Finally, we found that such representations of compressed predictive information emerge specifically 376 

through prediction in a learnable latent space. The convenience of learning in an abstract, symbolic 377 

space was first proposed by Kenneth Craik (1943) 1 who first suggested that the brain constructs 378 

internal models of the world by performing predictions in a latent representational space. This idea 379 

has since been formalized in hierarchical predictive coding frameworks .53 –56 While not strictly a 380 

predictive coding model, our best contrastive models share the fundamental idea with this framework 381 

that such good representations must be learnt via prediction in a flexible, learnable latent space. In 382 

this context, the same predictive principle should be generally applied throughout the whole speech 383 

cortex hierarchy, from the lowest to higher -levels speech representations. In this regard , the wav2vec 384 

2.0 features were high -dimensional speech signals, possibly noisy and redundant in the context of 385 

a temporal prediction task , that can be considered intermediate representations within the broader 386 

hierarchical processing stream 21 . Our findings show that, when treating these features as fixed, 387 

optimizing predictive information through prediction error minimization of future inputs does not yield 388 

behaviorally relevant representations. Instead, our results reinforce the idea that the brain extracts 389 

predictive information through prediction in a learnable latent space, where potentially irrelevant, 390 

high -variance details can be filtered out. This directly links to current problems in machine 391 

representation learning 57 which share a similar goal with our nervous system: building 392 

representations that contain information readily useful for further downstream tasks. Indeed, in this 393 

field, successful learning depends not only on model size and training data but, critically, on how 394 

relevant information is represented , an aspect determined by the learning objective itself. Two main 395 

classes of approaches have emerged in machine learning: generative architectures and joint -396 

embedding architectures (JEAs )58 . Generative models, such as our predictive autoencoder (deep -397 

pAE), learn by reconstructing observed inputs, whereas JEAs learn representations directly in latent 398 

space without requiring accurate reconstruction. Our contrastive learning (CL) model belongs to this 399 

type of learning algorithms, specifically trained with an objective function that optimizes the selection 400 

of predictive information (InfoNCE) 59 . In temporal prediction setting s, JEAs training involves three 401 

steps: (i) encoding observations into abstract latent representations, (ii) predicting future latent 402 

states, and (iii) evaluating predictions against actual future representations. Interestingly, this 403 

procedure is reminiscent of Craik’s three -steps algorithm for internal model fitting .1

General framework 649 

When processing speech stimuli, the human brain transforms the observable auditory input into 650 

multiple latent representations that facilitate speech comprehension. The exact transformations 651 

applied by the brain are largely unknown but are reflected in the recorded EEG signals during the 652 

conducted experiment. We, then, model speech processing to extract latent representations using a 653 

fixed artificial neural network (ANN) backbone optimized for speech recognition, namely the wav2vec 654 

2.0, and a learnable joint -embedding architecture that uses contrastive learning (CL), or deep 655 

autoencoders (deep -AE and deep -pAE) on top of it. We aimed to compare latent representations 656 

extracted by the models with those extracted by the participants’ brains listening to the same 657 

sentences. To do so, we compute mutual information (MI) between latents inferred by the models 658 

and the participants’ EEG signals. The higher the MI values, the more model -extracted features align 659 

to the brain’s latent representations, reflecting stronger correspondence between the modeled and 660 

the real neural processes underlying speech perception. 661

EEG preprocessing 679

Speech encoding model: Dimensionality reduction with PCA 710 

To reduce the dimensionality of wav2vec 2.0 features and approximate the process of extracting 711 

relevant information, we employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA), a widely used 712 

dimensionality reduction method in neuroscience. PCA operates on the principle that relevant 713 

information is proportional to the variance in the input data. Since large portions of variance in a 714 

dataset are often captured within a small subset of dimensions, PCA effectively reduces 715 

dimensionality while retaining most of the essential information. PCA was applied to the matrix  D of 716 

concatenated wav2vec 2.0 speech representations. PCA works by performing eigendecomposition 717 

of the covariance matrix  ΣD, identifying a new coordinate system represented by eigenvectors. These 718 

eigenvectors correspond to the principal components (PCs), with each component ordered by the 719 

amount of variance it captures. The eigenvalues of  ΣD quantify the variance explained by each 720 

principal component, making it possible to prioritize dimensions that capture the most variance. 721

MI measures the reduction in uncertainty about a random variable  X given knowledge of another 915 

variable  Y. It serves as a statistical test against the null hypothesis that  X and  Y are independent, 916

Statistical Analyses 945 

To determine whether the speech latents (wav2vec 2.0 CL, PCA, deep -AE) were significantly 946 

encoded in the EEG recordings, the mutual information (MI) outputs were evaluated against 947 

surrogate data via circular shift. The surrogate data disrupted the original relationship between the 948 

EEG signals and the speech latents while preserving the statistical properties of each signal, 949 

including its autocorrelation 65 . Specifically, the EEG activity at each electrode and trial (epoched and 950 

bandpass filtered as described previously) was circularly shifted by a random number of samples, 951 

selected between N/3 and N – N/4, where N represents the number of samples in the shortest trial 952 

(296 samples). As in the original analysis, MI was computed between the EEG and each group of 953 

latents. This procedure was repeated 100 times to generate a surrogate distribution for each 954 

participant and each information component. One -tailed cluster -based permutation statistics 32 were 955 

then applied to test at the group level whether the original MI values were significantly greater than 956 

the mean of the surrogate distribution. The null hypothesis assumed that the real and surrogate MI 957 

values belonged to the same probability distribution. A nonparametric statistical test was used to 958 

avoid assumptions about the distributions of the information values. All MI output samples (one per 959 

condition for each participant) were combined into a single set and randomly partitioned into two 960 

subsets. The test statistic was calculated by employing a univariate statistical test to compute a lag -961 

specific t -value (using the formula for dependent samples). Lags with absolute t -values exceeding a 962 

predefined threshold were grouped into clusters based on temporal adjacency. The threshold for 963 

clustering was set to the 95th quantile of the T -distribution, corresponding to a critical alpha level of 964 

0.05. For each cluster, the cluster -level statistic was calculated by summing the t -values of all lags 965 

within the cluster. This procedure was repeated 5000 times with random permutations to construct 966 

a distribution of the largest (absolute) cluster -level statistics. The test statistic was then computed 967 

for the non -permuted data (real and surrogate) to identify clusters of lags, and their p -values were 968 

determined as the proportion of random permutations with a larger (absolute) test statistic than the 969 

non -permuted data (Monte Carlo estimate). This method inherently controls the false alarm (FA) 970 

rate, setting the probability of Type I errors to the critical alpha level (0.05), and addresses the 971 

multiple comparison problem (MCP) by performing hypothesis testing at the cluster level. To 972 

compare the MI values of CL -LΔI to CL -HΔI, as well as comparing CL vs deep -AE vs deep -pAE 973 

encodings, the same nonparametric statistical test was applied. Surrogate data were not required 974 

for this comparison, as the distributions of the two classes were directly compared using a two -tailed 975 

cluster -based permutation test. In the case of CL -LΔI and CL -HΔI, we specifically considered 976 

constraints on the model’s δ -information encoding preceding stimulus presentation (anticipatory 977 

activity). To account for a biological lag in information processing, we cross -correlated the  δ-978 

information pattern in both classes with their EEG information processing. For the statistical analysis, 979 

only channel -averaged MI values corresponding to valid lags ( ≤ peak) were included. 980
