Abstract 31 

Oxytocin-mediated milk ejection (ME) is pivotal to effective breastfeeding and productive 32 

health, yet behaviorally decoding and revealing neural mechanisms of ME remains 33 

challenging. Here, we combined in vivo calcium imaging and intramammary pressure 34 

recording to uncover the temporal connections between episodic activity of oxytocin neurons 35 

and ME in conscious lactating rats. Leveraging the association and behavioral responses in 36 

dam and pup, we developed a supervised machine learning framework (ME Decoder) to 37 

enable automated analyses of ME. Inspired by its interpretable features, we defined the 38 

activity-coupled dam-pup interactions (ADPI), manifested by high kyphosis of the dam 39 

followed by pup treading and stretch, as the behavioral signatures of ME. By ME Decoder 40 

and ADPI analyses, we detected reduced ME but unaffected activity of oxytocinergic neurons 41 

after systemic blockade of oxytocin receptor. Our study uncovers the oxytocinergic and 42 

behavioral signatures of ME and provides a generalizable approach for further investigation. 43

Introduction 48 

Breastfeeding is a universal behavioral feature of mammals that ensures the survival of the 49 

infants, fosters mother-infant bonding and supports the propagation of the species [1]. Central 50 

to lactation is the suckling-induced milk ejection, a neuroendocrine reflex driven by the 51 

pulsatile release of oxytocin from hypothalamic neurons into the bloodstream [2-4]. 52 

Circulating oxytocin then acts on the myoepithelial cells of the mammary gland to induce 53 

contraction of the alveoli and drive milk ejection [5, 6]. 54 

Early studies revealed that hypothalamic oxytocin neurons intermittently increase their action 55 

potential firing rate by 20-40-fold during suckling [7, 8]. These bursts occur at the same time 56 

across the oxytocin neuron population [9, 10], which generates pulsatile oxytocin release to 57 

triggers milk ejection [11, 12]. Using genetic labeling and in vivo calcium imaging, recent 58 

studies have reported pulsatile activity of oxytocin neurons in the paraventricular nuclei 59 

(PVN) during lactation in conscious animals [13-17], reminiscent of the milk ejection bursts 60 

observed previously [7, 8]. However, lactation is a dynamic and reciprocal process between 61 

the mother and offspring that extends beyond nutrition to shape maternal-infant bonding and 62 

maternal psychological wellbeing [18, 19]. Predominant studies focus on changes in maternal 63 

oxytocin neuron activity but miss the essential behavioral features of milk ejections [13, 14, 64 

17]. A comprehensive understanding of lactation requires the integration of both maternal 65 

oxytocinergic signals and the behaviors of the dam and pups. 66 

Computational ethology quantifies animal behaviors by computer vision and machine 67 

learning, and provides an objective framework different from traditional manual scoring [20, 68

multi-animal interactions [24]. By contrast, image-based approaches (e.g. DeepEthogram 72 

[27]) capture rich spatiotemporal information, yet sensitive to background variability and 73 

computational resources [28]. Breastfeeding involves close physical interactions between the 74 

dam and pups, during which frequent occlusions and complex motions occur. Thus, reliably 75 

detecting milk ejections demands a framework with computational robustness and 76 

physiological interpretability. 77 

To address these issues, we combined in vivo recoding of oxytocinergic activity with 78 

intramammary pressure (IMP) measurements and ethological analyses to characterize the 79 

neural and behavioral features of milk ejection in lactating rats. Our study defines the 80 

oxytocinergic and behavioral signatures of milk ejection and provides a foundation for future 81 

investigation. 82

different animals and situations, the ΔF/F data were converted into Z-scores [34, 35]. The 159 

area under curve (AUC) was calculated by trapezoidal integration of ΔF/F signals relative to 160 

baseline mean during the 0-5 s response window, and the peak half-width was defined as the 161 

time interval between 50% rise and 50% fall points. 162

Manual behavioral analysis of lactation videos 176 

Behavioral analysis criteria 177 

Lactation videos were manually annotated frame-by-frame using Elan (version 6.6) by two 178 

independent experimenters, who were both blind to animal identity and neural responses. 179 

There was high consistency (>90%) between annotations performed by different individuals. 180 

During natural lactation, pups are largely concealed beneath the dam’s abdomen, making it 181 

challenging to observe behavioral changes across the entire litter, even when concurrently 182 

recorded from the top, side, and bottom. The top view was selected for subsequent analyses 183 

because it provided the best view of both dams and pups, maximizing the number of visible 184 

pups. Based on the behavioral sequences of unobstructed pups during suckling, we 185 

categorized their actions into four different types (representative examples shown in Fig. 5B): 186

Criterion for activity-coupled dam-pup interactions 211 

To establish a reliable reference to recognize milk ejections in the learning process, we 212 

defined ADPI into four phases: the silent, active, consummatory, and energized phases, 213 

according to the temporal responses of dam and pups. Typically, the dam crouched over pups 214 

in a low kyphosis with pups quietly suckling. This phase, characterized by minimal activity, 215 

was referred to as the silent phase. Later, the dam moved into high kyphosis with pups 216 

treading legs, this was considered as the start of ADPI, the active phase. In the consummatory 217 

phase, the pups engulfed a large amount of milk and exhibited an extended stretch posture, 218 

indicating the climax of ADPI. After the extension, pups showed variable activities, with 219 

some directly changing into the silent phase, some still treading actively, some rotating their 220 

bodies, and some detaching from nipples. This was considered as the end of ADPI, the 221 

energized phase. After the above four phases, the dam and all pups would revert back into the 222 

silent phase. 223 

Design of ME Decoder 224 

To detect milk ejections (ME) in videos, we developed ME Decoder, a two-stage framework 225 

inspired by temporal action localization methods [37]. ME Decoder has two modules: the ME 226 

Detector and the ME Refiner. The ME Detector module comprises a Temporal Shift Module 227

In detail, the TSM in ME Detector takes a query video as input and outputs behavioral 230

Results 247 

Maternal oxytocin neurons exhibit episodic calcium activity during continuous suckling 248 

To uncover the activity of magnocellular oxytocin neurons during lactation, we recorded the 249 

calcium responses of oxytocin neurons in the SON using the calcium sensor, GCaMP6m, in 250 

conscious rats in mid-lactation (postpartum day 11, PPD11) (Fig 1A-B and Fig S1A). While 251 

oxytocin has been shown to regulate maternal behavior [40-43], we observed no changes in 252 

calcium signal in oxytocin neurons when dams retrieved or groomed pups (Fig 1C and Fig 253 

S1B-C), likely due to the low sensitivity of calcium recording. Neither did the calcium signal 254 

change at the dam-pup reunion or at the onset of suckling (Fig 1C-1D and Fig S1D). After 255 

continuous suckling for several minutes (661.2 ± 78.6 s), maternal oxytocin neurons 256 

exhibited a dramatic calcium elevation (Fig 1C and 1E), which was much larger than the 257 

small transients previously observed during social touch [44]. Such robust calcium elevations 258 

occurred every few minutes subsequently during suckling (Fig 1F), with a mean interval of 259 

318.2 s (318.2 ± 48.1 s), a duration of 5.9 s (5.9 ± 0.2 s), a rapid rise to peak within 2 s, and a 260 

return to basal levels within 4 s (Fig 1E and Fig S1E-H). These episodic calcium waves are 261 

temporally consistent with the time course and duration of burst firing of oxytocin neurons 262 

during electrophysiological recordings in lactating rats [12], as well as with the calcium 263 

waves in PVN oxytocin neurons of lactating mice and rats [13-17]. 264 

Similar to previous observations in mice [13, 17], episodic calcium waves discontinued when 265 

suckling stopped (Fig 1F). Moreover, local application of lidocaine to inhibit sensory 266 

transmission from nipples [45], significantly delayed the onset of calcium waves and reduced 267

Attenuated suckling-evoked oxytocin neuron activity and milk ejection during 273 

pentobarbital anesthesia 274 

To examine the temporal correlation between oxytocin neuron activity and milk ejection, we 275 

simultaneously recorded calcium and IMP in anesthetized lactating dams. Under 276 

pentobarbital anesthesia, the first calcium wave of oxytocin neurons occurred ~40 min after 277 

suckling onset, in contrast to ~10 min in conscious dams (Fig 2A-2B). Overall, the calcium 278 

waves in anesthetized dams had a significantly smaller amplitude, shortened duration, 279 

decreased area under curve, and slightly longer inter-wave interval (Fig 2C-I). Using 280 

concurrent IMP recording, we observed that each calcium wave was followed by an IMP rise 281 

(Fig 2J), with a delay of 15.0 ± 2.9 s (Fig 2K), consistent with the latency between the burst 282 

firing of oxytocin neurons and IMP elevation reported previously [7, 12]. These data suggest 283 

that episodic calcium waves represent the synchronized bursts of oxytocin neuron population, 284 

which induce milk ejection. 285 

Notably, as animals gradually recovered from anesthesia, the calcium wave-to-IMP interval 286 

gradually shortened and stabilized at 13.6 ± 1.0 s by the time of emergence (Fig 2L), in line 287 

with the time course required for oxytocin to reach and affect the mammary gland following 288

A deep learning framework automates milk ejection detection 292 

The requirement for general anesthesia during IMP measurement precludes its use in 293 

assessing milk ejection in conscious animals. To investigate lactation-related behaviors and 294 

their health benefits [46, 47], it is important to develop an approach to enable noninvasively 295 

quantification of milk ejections in freely behaving dams. Previous studies have shown that 296 

pups may exhibit body extension (pup stretching) upon ingesting milk [31, 48, 49], 297 

suggesting that milk ejections can be decoded behaviorally. To test this possibility, we 298 

adopted a computational ethology approach, aiming to identify behavioral signatures of milk 299 

ejection under natural conditions. 300 

We first applied DeepLabCut to track keypoints on both dams and pups. While the tool 301 

effectively tracked the dams, reliable pup tracking was unsuccessful due to their frequent 302 

occlusion by the dam. Keypoint analyses of the dam revealed transient displacements and 303 

angular changes in the skeleton of the lactating dam, but none of them correlate with the 304 

episodic occurrence of calcium waves (Fig 3A-3B). These data indicate that skeleton-based 305 

postural changes of the dam alone are insufficient to represent milk ejections. 306 

To circumvent the limitations of the skeleton-based approaches, we designed an image-based 307 

supervised framework, ME Decoder, to fully capture behavioral changes related to milk 308

ME decoder was trained to recognize milk ejections according to the coupling time between 315 

episodic oxytocinergic calcium waves and milk ejections (Fig 2J-K). Video segments of 9 s 316 

duration, starting ~10 s after the onset of calcium waves, were used as positives for model 317 

training, whereas 9 s video segments from the rest videos as negatives (Fig 4A). Following 318 

architectural optimization, ME Decoder reliably identified milk ejection episodes, achieving 319 

an F1 score over 0.8 with IoA (intersection over area) around 0.6 in the validation dataset 320 

(Fig 4B). When applied to a new test dataset (dataset 1, comprising 87 milk ejections in 9.58 321 

h of videos), the model identified more than 75% of milk ejections, with ~90% accuracy (Fig 322 

4C). The majority of automatically identified milk ejection (Ai-ME) video segments showed 323 

durations of 8-10 s (Fig 4D), with 79.4% occurring 6-10 s after calcium waves of oxytocin 324 

neurons (Fig 4E). These data are highly overlapped with the ground truth milk ejections 325 

(GT-ME, defined as events occurring 7-16 s after calcium elevation) (Fig 4F-4G), suggesting 326 

that ME Decoder reliably detected milk ejections. 327

Interpretable behavioral signatures define milk ejection 337 

ME Decoder highlights dam-pup interacting areas as salient features of milk ejection. To gain 338 

further insights to the specific behavioral changes and movements associated with ME, we 339 

next performed fine-grained analyses of behavioral dynamics and divided behavioral changes 340 

occurring during lactation into three different categories for either dams (D1-D3) or pups 341 

(P1-P3) based on the head and back movement of the dam, as well as that of pups’ bodies and 342 

limbs (Fig 5A-5B, Video S1-S6, detailed in methods). To emphasize the dynamic nature of 343 

these events, the basal quiescent state of lactation, with no apparent movement of both dam 344 

and pups was referred to D0 or P0 (Video S7). 345

calcium signals. In a short time period after calcium waves when milk ejections were 347

Discussion 385 

Understanding lactation is important for reproductive health. In the present study, we 386 

developed a supervised deep-learning framework (ME Decoder) to allow automated and 387 

noninvasive detection of milk ejections, providing a novel approach for quantitative 388 

measurements of lactation. ME Decoder learned from the sequential behavioral responses of 389 

ADPI, manifested by high kyphosis of the dam, followed by pup treading and stretching, for 390 

discriminative localization of milk ejections. Using these new tools, we verified that 391 

systematically blocking oxytocin signaling inhibits milk ejections but do not disturb milk 392 

ejection-triggering pulsatile oxytocinergic firing. These findings provide important insights 393 

into mechanistic understanding the interplay between oxytocin neuron activity and behavioral 394 

output of natural lactation in freely behaving rats and presents a generalizable framework for 395 

investigating lactation-related physiological and emotional impacts. 396 

Automation of milk ejection analyses 397 

A comprehensive understanding of lactation requires a methodological paradigm that is both 398 

precise and applicable to natural conditions. Traditional approaches, IMP recording provides 399 

direct physiological readout of milk ejection but necessitates anesthesia [52], which precludes 400 

faithful representation of the full suite of physiological processes in conscious animals [54]. 401 

Machine learning has emerged as a powerful approach for automating behavioral analysis 402 

[20]. However, due to multi-animal interactions and frequent visual occlusion of pups, 403 

attempts using skeleton-based approaches (e.g. DeepLabcut) were unsuccessful [22, 55]. 404 

Therefore, we developed a new strategy to monitor and quantify behavior during suckling, 405

ME Decoder. 406 

ME Decoder is a computer vision-based deep learning model that combines architectures for 407 

feature detection and refinement, enabling dynamic and complex behavioral analyses 408 

involving multiple animals. This framework is user-friendly and cost-effective, requiring only 409 

overhead-view videos as input. The adaptability of this framework has been validated in 410 

different experimental conditions, including variations in environmental settings and animal 411 

models, establishing ME Decoder as a versatile resource to investigate lactation. Training the 412 

framework with different datasets will expand its application in additional ethological 413 

analyses other than lactation. 414 

Behavioral signatures of lactation 415 

Breastfeeding is a universal feature of all mammals and constitutes a critical component of 416 

maternal behavior. While other maternal actions such as pup retrieval have been extensively 417 

studied [40, 56, 57], much less attention has been paid to behavioral features of lactation. 418 

Because behavioral manifestations of milk ejection remain unclear, quantitative and 419 

systematic ethological analyses of lactation are challenging. While pup stretch has been 420 

suggested to indicate milk ejection [49], discriminating rigid stretch form other pup 421 

movements is challenging during natural lactation, as its occurrence, representation and 422 

duration varies highly. By contrast, the high kyphosis (D1) of the dam and switching of pups 423 

(P3) are readily recognized. We noticed that when using D1 and P3 as references, it becomes 424 

much easier to recognize pup treading (P1) and stretching (P2) often occurs in-between these 425 

two actions. 426

Inspired by the spatiotemporal features learned by the ME Decoder, we further defined these 427 

interpretable behavioral sequences (P1-D1-D2-D3), namely, ADPI, as representations of milk 428 

ejections. ADPI, which integrates characteristic actions from both the mother and offspring, 429 

transforms the ambiguous individual actions into a robust behavioral pattern. This enables 430 

precise analyses of milk ejections and reduces the needs for invasive IMP and neuronal 431 

recordings, and serves as an important complementation and verification for ME Decoder 432 

analyses. 433 

Neural modulation of milk ejection 434 

The unique burst firing of oxytocin neurons during suckling has been a subject of intense 435 

interest. Central oxytocin signaling has been proposed to facilitate burst firing of oxytocin 436 

neurons via either synaptic or extra synaptic transmission [58-61]. These studies were mainly 437 

conducted in lactating rats under anesthesia, a brain state with globally inhibited neuronal 438 

activity. With new approaches developed in the present study, we demonstrated that 439 

systematically blocking oxytocin signaling had little effects on the burst firing of oxytocin 440 

neurons. While these data need to be further investigated using genetic ablation of oxytocin 441 

receptors in neurons, our observation is consistent with previous reports that systematic 442 

injection of another oxytocin receptor antagonist (F-792) did not affect the burst firing of 443 

oxytocin neurons [62]. However, both intra-cerebroventricular and intra-SON infusion of 444 

oxytocin receptor antagonist appeared to reduce plasma oxytocin and milk transfer [60, 63]. 445 

These data indicate that central oxytocinergic activity during milk ejection may be regulated 446 

at multiple levels. It is likely that the burst firing of oxytocin neurons is regulated by 447

upstream inputs [13, 64, 65], which initiate and coordinate population-wide synchrony of 448 

oxytocin neurons during lactation. 449 

Limitations of the study 450 

Fiber photometry recording detects the calcium signals at population level, thus may 451 

compromise the detection sensitivity and specificity. In addition, fiber location and virus 452 

expression may also increase the variation of recorded signals. Miniscope-based single-cell in 453 

vivo imaging or direct multi-single unit recording of action potential firing are alternative 454 

strategies that detect neuronal activity at single cell level to enable precise investigation of 455 

neuron-to neuron synchronization within burst. Moreover, the datasets used for training ME 456 

Decoder were primarily from the middle and late stages of lactation (PPD7-PPD20). Further 457 

optimization may be required for its application to earlier lactation phases, e.g. from 458 

PPD1-PPD6. 459
