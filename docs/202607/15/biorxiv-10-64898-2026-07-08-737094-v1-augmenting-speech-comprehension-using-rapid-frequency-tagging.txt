Abstract 21 

22 

Speech comprehension under adverse listening conditions benefits from visual 23 

speech, but whether visual input can be modulated to improve listening remains 24 

unclear. Here we used rapid frequency tagging, a non -invasive sensory stimulation 25 

technique, in which the visual tag at 55 Hz over the mouth region of a talking face 26 

was amplitude -modulated by the envelope of either the task -relevant or task -27 

irrelevant speech stream. When the visual modulation followed the task -relevant 28 

speech envelope, comprehension improved relative to task -irrelevant modulation 29 

and to an unmodulated tagging control . MEG responses showed enhanced 55 Hz 30 

visual tagging, altered 40 Hz auditory tagging and a non -linear 15 Hz31 

intermodulation. Individual comprehension gains were associated with 32 

intermodulation responses in left inferior frontal cortex, linking behavioural benefit to 33 

audiovisual interaction. These findings show that task -relevant visual stimulation can 34 

improve comprehension by strengthening audiovisual interaction , establish ing 35 

amplitude -modulated frequency tagging as a tool for probing and supporting 36 

comprehension in challenging environments. 37

Introduction 38 

39 

Listeners must navigate speech -in -noise across everyday contexts, such as crowded 40 

restaurants, busy offices and social gatherings 1. Speech comprehension in these 41 

environments depends not only on analysing the acoustic signal, but also on 42 

integrating visual information from the speaker’s face and body. V isual cues , such as 43 

lip movements and co -speech gestures , can guide attentio n2 and support 44 

comprehension 3,4 by providing temporal and articulatory cues to the acoustic 45 

properties of speech, thereby aiding phoneme discrimination 5. Furthermore, listeners 46 

can use visual information during naturalistic communication to generate predictions 47 

about upcoming speech, facilitating efficient language processing 6. However, the 48 

ability to select and understand speech in noisy environments varies substantially 49 

across individuals . Listeners with neurodevelopmental conditions 7, age -related 50 

hearing loss 8 or cochlear implants 9 often fac e particular challenges in understanding 51 

speech -in -noise . Identifying how speech cues can be manipulated to support 52 

comprehension in challenging listening conditions is therefore important both 53 

theoretically and practically . Here, we investigate whether speech comprehension in 54 

difficult listening contexts can be enhanced by manipulating speech -relevant visual 55 

cues. 56 

57 

Most approaches to improving speech -in -noise comprehension have focused 58 

primarily on enhancing auditory processing . However, growing evidence suggests 59 

that enhancing visual speech information may offer a promising an alternative means 60 

of supporting speech perception . Increasing the visual salience of the speaker’s 61 

mouth has been shown to improve speech intelligibility under degraded listening 62 

conditions 10 , suggesting that targeted enhancement of visual speech cues can 63 

support audiovisual perception. Here we asked whether speech comprehension can 64 

be improved by using visual input to deliver behaviourally relevant temporal 65 

information from the auditory speech signal, rather than simply by making visual 66 

speech cues more salient. 67 

68 

The speech envelope provides a natural candidate for such manipulation. It reflects 69 

slow amplitude fluctuations over time that convey rhythmic and prosodic information 70 

in speech 11,12 and plays a crucial role in speech understanding and cortical speech 71 

tracking 13 –15 , particularly in noisy environments 16,17 . Previous research has shown 72 

that speech comprehension was improved by expanding or enhancing the acoustic 73 

speech envelope cues 18,19 . The speech envelope has also been used as a form of 74 

visual rhythmic stimulation . For example, comprehension improved when 75 

participants view ed a visual stimulus whose size fluctuated congruently , rather than 76 

incongruently , with the auditory speech envelope 20 . Such findings provide evidence 77 

that visual presentation reflecting speech envelope dynamics can support speech 78 

perception in noise . However, such approaches have typically relied on simplified 79 

visual displays. We here ask whether auditory envelope information can be 80 

embedded in naturalistic visual input, such as the speaker’s mouth region, to 81 

enhance comprehension during continuous, naturalistic audiovisual speech. 82 

83 

Freq uency tagging offers a powerful approach to address this question while also 84 

tracking neural responses to sensory information . In this approach , sensory stimuli 85 

are presented at specific temporal rates , eliciting steady -state neural response s at 86 

the corresponding frequencies 21 –23 . T his allows concurrently presented signals to be 87

however, has often used lower stimulation frequencies, making the flicker perceptible 90 

and placing the evoked response within frequency ranges that also show 91 

endogenous oscillatory and resonance activity 25 . The visual cortex can nevertheless 92 

follow periodic stimulation across a broad frequency range, with steady -state 93 

responses observed up to at least 90 Hz and frequency -dependent resonance 94 

effects reported around 10, 20, 40 and 80 Hz 26 .95 

96 

Rapid Invisible Frequency Tagging (RIFT) builds on this capacity by applying visual 97 

stimulation above the perceptual flicker -fusion threshold 27 . The high -frequency visual 98 

tag can therefore be rendered imperceptible while leaving lower -frequency neural 99 

activity available for analysis, making RIFT particularly suitable for naturalistic 100 

paradigms 28 . RIFT has been used to investigate attention 25 , statistical learning 29 ,101 

speech planning 30 , natural reading 31 and audiovisual speech processing 32 . The 102 

frequency tagging framework can also be used to measure interactions between 103 

auditory and visual signals. When the two modalities are tagged at different 104 

frequencies, modality -specific responses can be measured separately at their 105 

respective tagging frequencies. If the signals interact nonlinearly, additional 106 

responses can emerge at intermodulation frequencies corresponding to sums and 107 

differences of the original tags 33,34 . Because these frequencies are not present in 108 

either input alone, intermodulation responses provide a frequency -specific index of 109 

nonlinear audiovisual interaction. Drijvers et al. 32 combined auditory tagging at 61 Hz 110 

with visual RIFT at 68 Hz and identified a 7 Hz difference -frequency response that 111 

was strongest when clear speech was accompanied by a congruent gesture. This 112 

response was localised to left inferior frontal and temporal regions, which have 113 

independently been implicated in integrating semantic information conveyed by 114 

speech and gesture 35 . Thus, modality -specific tagging and intermodulation 115 

responses provide complementary measures of auditory processing, visual 116 

processing and their nonlinear interaction. 117 

118 

In the present study, w e move beyond this approach by using RIFT not only to 119 

measure audiovisual processing, but also to manipulate naturalistic visual speech. 120 

Specifically, we applied a 55 Hz visual tag to the speaker’s lip region and modulated 121 

its amplitude with the auditory speech envelope. This allowed the temporal structure 122 

of speech to be embedded directly into the visual speech signal. Crucially, the 123 

modulation was derived either from the speech stream that participants were 124 

instructed to attend or from the competing speech stream they were instructed to 125 

ignore. This design allowed us to test whether visual envelope modulation improves 126 

comprehension because it carries behaviourally relevant speech information , rather 127 

than because rhythmic visual stimulation is present per se. Specifically, we tagged 128 

auditory speech at 40 Hz and applied a 55 Hz visual tag to the speaker’s lip region 129 

and amplitude -modulated this signal with the auditory speech envelope, allowing 130 

behaviourally relevant speech to be embedded directly into visual input. This design 131 

tests whether speech comprehension can be improved when visual speech carries 132 

the temporal structure of the attended auditory stream, rather than merely providing 133 

a salient visual cue. 134

Results 146 

To investigate whether visual rapid invisible frequency tagging (RIFT) modulated by 147 

the task -relevant speech envelope influences speech comprehension , participants 148 

completed a speech comprehension task under different tagging conditions . Figure 1 149 

illustrates the experimental paradigm, including the dichotic speech task, the visual 150 

and auditory tagging frequencies, and the amplitude modulation conditions (Figure 151 

1a,b) . Representative coherence spectra showed clear peaks at the visual and 152 

auditory tagging frequencies, confirming robust steady -state responses at 55 Hz and 153 

40 Hz, respectively (Figure 1c,d). The corresponding sensor topographies showed 154

tagging coherence was strongest over temporal sensors, consistent with the 156 

expected sensory origins of the two responses. 157 

158

behaviourally relevant envelope -based visual modulation from the effect of visual 163 

frequency tagging alone. Behavioural performance across these conditions is shown 164

Task -Relevant Amplitude Modulation Improves Speech Comprehension 186 

We first asked whether behaviourally relevant amplitude modulation (AM) of the 55 187 

Hz flicker of the visual input, guided by the auditory speech envelope, improves 188 

speech comprehension performance. A one -way repeated -measures ANOVA 189 

conducted across all five experimental conditions revealed significant differences in 190 

comprehension accuracy (F(4,156) = 4.82, p < 0.001 ; Supplementary Figure S1a ). 191 

Given our a priori focus on the role of behaviourally relevant AM, subsequent 192 

Bonferroni -corrected paired -samples t -tests focused on the three focal conditions: 193 

Task -Relevant AM, Task -Irrelevant AM, and Pure Tagging (Figure 2a; see 194 

Supplementary Table S1 for all pairwise comparisons) . Comprehension accuracy 195 

was higher when AM tagging was task -relevant. Specifically, participants showed 196 

significantly greater accuracy in the Task -Relevant AM condition than in both the 197 

Task -Irrelevant AM condition (t( 39) = 3.15, p = 0.031, d z = 0.50 ) and the Pure 198 

Tagging condition (t(39) = 3.14, p = 0.032, d z = 0.50 ), with both comparisons 199 

reflecting medium effect sizes. No significant difference was observed between the 200 

Task -Irrelevant AM and Pure Tagging conditions (p > 0.05; Figure 2a). Here dz201

Amplitude Modulation Alters Visual and Auditory Tagging Relative to Pure Tagging 266 

To determine whether amplitude modulation (AM) altered sensory tagging 267 

responses, we compared the two amplitude -modulated conditions (Task -Relevant 268 

AM and Task -Irrelevant AM) with the Pure Tagging condition. Visual tagging 269 

coherence at 55 Hz was significantly lower in both AM conditions than in the Pure 270 

Tagging condition , with widespread decreases across visual regions ( cluster -based 271 

permutation statistics; all ps < 0.0 5; Figure 4a,b ; Supplementary Table S 3). These 272 

findings indicate that introducing speech envelope modulation weakened steady -273 

state visual tagging responses compared with the temporally regular flicker used in 274 

the Pure Tagging condition. 275 

276 

Amplitude modulation of visual tagging frequency had a more selective effect on 277 

auditory tagging . Relative to Pure Tagging, the Task -Relevant AM condition showed 278 

significantly reduced 40 Hz auditory coherence with a large cluster spanning auditory 279 

regions (cluster -based permutation statistics; all ps < 0.0 5; Figure 4 c; Supplementary 280 

Table S3 ). No significant auditory -coherence difference w as observed between the 281 

Task -Irrelevant AM and Pure Tagging conditions. This suggests that auditory tagging 282 

was reduced specifically when visual amplitude modulation carried behaviourally 283 

relevant speech envelope information , rather than in the absence of speech 284 

envelope modulation. 285 

286 

Together, these findings reveal two complementary effects of amplitude modulation. 287 

First, relative to Pure Tagging, speech envelope modulation generally reduced 55 Hz 288 

visual tagging coherence, likely reflecting the disruption of steady -state visual 289 

entrainment by slower luminance fluctuations. Second, within the amplitude -290 

modulated conditions, task relevance shifted the balance between visual and 291 

auditory tracking: behaviourally relevant visual modulation enhanced visual tagging 292 

while attenuating auditory tagging. This opposing pattern suggests that audiovisual 293 

speech processing was reweighted according to task relevance, with behaviourally 294 

relevant visual speech envelope information enhancing visual speech tracking while 295 

reducing auditory tagging. 296

Discussion 374 

Here we demonstrate that non -invasive visual stimulation can improve speech 375 

comprehension by embedding the attended speech envelope into naturalistic visual 376 

input. Using amplitude -modulated (AM) frequency tagging, we causally manipulated 377 

the visual speech by embedding the speech envelope of either the attended or 378 

unattended auditory stream into the speaker’s mouth region. This allowed us to test 379 

whether task -relevant visual envelope information improves speech comprehension 380 

while simultaneously measuring visual tagging, auditory tagging, and their 15 Hz 381 

intermodulation response. Behaviourally, speech comprehension improved only 382 

when the visual modulation followed the attended speech stream. At the neural level, 383 

task -relevant visual modulation enhanced visual tagging, reduced auditory tagging 384 

relative to task -irrelevant modulation, and increased 15 Hz intermodulation 385 

responses between the visual and auditory tags. Furthermore, left inferior frontal 386 

intermodulation responses covaried with individual gains in speech comprehension. 387 

Together, these findings suggest that auditory -envelope modulation of visual speech 388 

improves comprehension not simply by increasing visual salience, but by 389 

strengthening behaviourally relevant audiovisual interaction. 390 

391 

The behavioural results show that the benefit of visual modulation depended on its 392 

relevance to the attended speech stream. Speech c omprehension accuracy was 393 

higher in the Task -Relevant AM condition than in both the Task -Irrelevant AM and 394 

Pure Tagging conditions. This distinction is important because both amplitude -395 

modulated conditions introduced visible luminance fluctuations around the mouth, 396 

but only the modulation carrying the attended envelope improved comprehension. 397 

The effect therefore cannot be explained solely by the presence of visual flicker or by 398 

increased visual salience. Instead, the visual signal had to contain temporal 399 

information that was relevant to the speech stream participants were trying to 400 

understand. This finding extends previous work showing that visual speech cues 401 

improve intelligibility in challenging listening conditions 3,36 –38 and that enhancing the 402 

visibility of the mouth region can support speech perception under degraded listening 403 

conditions 39 . The present study goes beyond these demonstrations by showing that 404 

visual speech can be experimentally structured to carry the temporal dynamics of the 405 

attended auditory stream. 406 

407 

The visual tagging results support this interpretation. Visual coherence at 55 Hz was 408 

stronger when the visual flicker was modulated by the attended speech envelope 409 

than when it was modulated by the ignored speech envelope. Moreover, participants 410 

who showed larger task -relevance -related increases in visual tagging also showed 411

Auditory tagging showed a complementary pattern. Forty -hertz auditory coherence 426 

was reduced in the Task -Relevant AM condition relative to both the Task -Irrelevant 427 

AM and Pure Tagging conditions. This reduction should not be interpreted as a loss 428 

of auditory speech processing. Rather, it suggests that auditory tracking was altered 429 

when the visual signal carried information about the attended speech stream. This 430 

interpretation is consistent with evidence that attention and task demands shape 431 

multisensory integration 41,42 , and that visual speech can modulate auditory cortical 432 

processing 43,44 . Related work has further shown that neural tracking of continuous 433 

speech is systematically influenced by acoustic degradation and listening difficulty, 434 

suggesting that changes in speech tracking responses reflect adaptive processing 435 

demands under adverse listening conditions 45 . In the present study, task -relevant 436 

visual modulation enhanced visual tagging while attenuating auditory tagging, 437 

suggesting that neural tracking did not simply increase across modalities. Instead, 438 

the balance of audiovisual tracking appeared to shift toward the visual stream when 439 

it carried useful speech envelope information. This interpretation is further supported 440 

by the relationship between the brain and speech comprehension . Visual tagging 441 

differences were associated with comprehension gains, whereas auditory tagging 442 

differences were not. Thus, the behavioural improvement appears to be more closely 443 

linked to enhanced tracking of task -relevant visual speech information than to 444 

changes in auditory tagging alone. Taken together, the opposing visual and auditory 445 

tagging effects suggest a task -dependent shift in audiovisual speech processing, in 446 

which visual speech information gained functional relevance when it carried the 447 

temporal structure of the attended speech stream. 448

between the 55 Hz visual tag and the 40 Hz auditory tag. Because this frequency is 453 

not present as a primary tag in either modality, it provides a frequency -specific index 454 

of nonlinear interaction between the auditory and visual neuronal signals. Task -455

audiovisual speech perception 46 , and with evidence that frontal and motor signals 460 

can modulate auditory cortical tracking during continuous speech 14 . The right frontal 461

480 

The present findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, 481 

although the high -frequency RIFT carrier was designed to be imperceptible, the 482 

speech envelope modulation produced visible luminance fluctuations around the 483 

mouth. As a result, the behavioural improvement observed may partly reflect 484 

enhanced visual saliency rather than speech -specific temporal alignment driven by 485 

the RIFT per se . In addition, the current study did not include a control condition in 486 

which the speech envelope was applied directly to the lip region without RIFT. 487 

Including such a condition would allow future work to determine whether the 488 

behavioural effects observed here are specific to amplitude -modulated RIFT or 489 

extend more generally to visually presented speech envelope dynamics. Second , the 490 

auditory tag was perceptible as a low -frequency buzz. Behavioural performance 491 

indicates that speech comprehension was preserved, but future work could explore 492 

less perceptible auditory tagging approaches. Finally, the present sample consisted 493 

of young adults with normal hearing. Whether the same mechanism supports 494 

comprehension in older adults or listeners with hearing difficulties remains to be 495 

tested. Despite these limitations, the present findings suggest that non -invasive 496 

sensory stimulation, when structured by task -relevant speech information , can 497 

influence speech perception under challenging listening conditions. 498 

499 

Beyond its theoretical implications, amplitude -modulated RIFT may have potential 500 

relevance for populations that experience difficulties understanding speech in 501 

complex listening environments. Speech -in -noise perception is known to decline with 502 

age and is strongly associated with increased listening effort and reduced sensitivity 503 

to temporal speech cues 51,52 . Older adults and listeners with hearing impairment s504 

often rely more heavily on visual speech information, suggesting that cross -modal 505 

compensatory mechanisms play a critical role when auditory processing becomes 506 

unreliable 5,53 . By delivering behaviourally relevant temporal structure via the visual 507 

modality, amplitude -modulated RIFT may provide a route for supporting speech 508 

comprehension . This distinguishes the approach from conventional hearing aids, 509 

which primarily operate through amplification and noise -reduction strategies but do 510 

not directly address multisensory integration or temporal prediction mechanisms .511

Furthermore , this approach may also offer advantages relative to other intervention 521 

strategies. Perceptual training and speechreading programmes can improve 522 

audiovisual speech perception but typically require prolonged learning and show 523 

substantial inter -individual variability 55 . In contrast, amplitude -modulated RIFT 524

Conclusion 531 

This study demonstrates that RIFT, a non -invasive sensory stimulation technique, 532 

can improve speech comprehension when guided by task -relevant speech envelope 533 

information. By embedding the attended speech envelope into visual speech, 534 

amplitude -modulated RIFT enhanced visual tagging, altered auditory tagging, and 535 

strengthened 15 Hz intermodulation responses between auditory and visual signals. 536 

Critically, individual gains in comprehension were associated with intermodulation 537 

responses in left inferior frontal cortex, suggesting that nonlinear audiovisual 538 

interaction was linked to the effective use of task -relevant visual information. These 539 

findings establish amplitude -modulated RIFT as a non -invasive approach for both 540 

probing and shaping audiovisual speech processing in challenging listening 541 

conditions. 542

Frequency Tagging 568 

The stimuli were presented using the PROPixx DLP LED projector (VPixx 569 

Technologies Inc., Canada). This projector has a refresh rate of up to 1 ,440 Hz. To 570 

achieve this refresh rate, each frame from the stimulus PC graphics card (120 Hz) 571 

was split into quadrants, increasing the refresh rate fourfold (480 Hz). The stimuli 572 

were presented in the centre of a grey background at a resolution of 1289 x 720 573 

pixels. The rectangular area (120 x 90 pixels) around the speaker’s lips was tagged 574 

using 55 Hz sine waves (Figure 1). There was also a small, tagged box in the bottom 575 

right corner of the screen where the photodiode was secured to measure the 576 

luminance changes on the screen (30 x 30 pixels). 577 

578 

Two types of frequency tagging were included in this study - pure tagging and 579 

amplitude -modulated (AM) tagging. Pure tagging consisted of a continuous 580 

imperceptible 55 Hz sinusoidal luminance modulation of the lip region with constant 581 

amplitude. Amplitude -modulated tagging was achieved by multiplying the pure 582 

tagging signal by the auditory speech amplitude envelope (see the Stimuli , Task and 583 

Experimental Conditions for more details). In the Task -Relevant and Task -Irrelevant 584 

AM conditions, the amplitude modulation introduced perceptible slow luminance 585 

fluctuations. The auditory speech stream was tagged at 40 Hz by multiplying the 586 

naturalistic speech waveform by a 40 Hz sinusoidal signal. Although the resulting 40 587 

Hz auditory tag was audible as a low -frequency buzz, behavioural performance 588 

(Figure 2) indicated that it did not interfere with speech comprehension .589

Stimuli, Task, and Experimental Conditions 591 

The stimuli used in this study were continuous audiovisual speech videos, as used 592 

and validated by Park et al .37 . Each video was between 7 and 9 minutes in length. 593 

The content of the videos was adapted from TED talks ( www.ted.com/talks/ ) and was 594 

recorded by a professional male speaker. In all conditions, participants watched a 595 

video recording while simultaneously hearing two different auditory speech in each 596 

ear. Participants were instructed to which ear they should pay attention to (task -597 

relevant speech) and which they should ignore (task -irrelevant speech) at the start of 598 

each condition. Participants attended to the same ear for all conditions. To reduce 599 

potential bias associated with attending to a particular ear, participants were 600 

unaware that the attended ear remained constant. The attended ear (left or right) 601 

was counterbalanced across participants, such that the attended ear remained the 602 

same across conditions within each participant (20 paid attention to the left - and 20 603 

to the right -side speech). Because the attended ear was counterbalanced across 604 

participants, any potential lateralisation biases were controlled at the group level. 605
