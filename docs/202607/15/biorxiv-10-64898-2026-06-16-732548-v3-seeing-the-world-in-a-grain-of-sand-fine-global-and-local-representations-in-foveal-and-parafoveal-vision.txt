Summary 29 

Foveal vision enables primates to perceive small objects with remarkable precision by resolving both 30 

fine-global form and fine-local detail. Yet current coarse-to-fine and local-to-global frameworks, 31 

derived largely from parafoveal recordings and large-object representations, posit that V1 encodes 32

Introduction 47 

Visual perception has fascinated scientists and philosophers for centuries. What appears effortless to 48 

us is the perception of objects in terms of both their global forms and local parts, with remarkable 49 

precision in the fovea. The fovea is a small, capillary-free depression at the center of the macula, 50 

approximately 0.4–0.5 mm in diameter corresponding approximately to the central 2° of retinal 51 

eccentricity. It contains the highest concentration of cone photoreceptors, which exhibit relatively 52 

slow temporal responses and high-fidelity signaling specialized for high-acuity form vision 1-3 (Figure 53 

1A ). This is reflected in visual acuity testing, in which both small and large optotypes, such as the 54 

letter “E,” can be seen clearly when presented in the fovea. However, the cortical mechanisms 55 

underlying the joint representation of the global form and local parts of a coherent object—for 56 

example, the letter “E” and its constituent strokes—remain unclear, especially in foveal vision, where 57 

global form and local detail are perceived with comparable sharpness. This question, foreshadowed 58 

by William Blake’s poetic musings, gained scientific substance with the work of the Gestalt 59 

psychologists. They studied the psychophysical laws of visual perception and proposed that the global 60 

percept of the whole is processed prior to the local parts of a stimulus 4,5 , emphasizing that the whole 61 

has emergent properties that are not the simple sum of its parts. This perspective laid the conceptual 62 

foundation for subsequent theoretical and physiological work on local–global representations in the 63 

visual brain. 64

Although global and local features have distinct visual characteristics, both can be characterized 65 

by distance-dependent spatial-frequency (SF) composition, which can be quantified using classical 66 

Fourier analysis of their spatial power spectra 6 . For example, Fourier analysis of the Portland Vase 67 

image revealed that local features are biased toward higher-SF components, whereas global structure 68 

is dominated by lower SFs ( Figure 1B ). Notably, the power spectrum shifts toward lower SFs as the 69 

aperture size increases. Thus, low-SF visual information is classically assumed to convey the “gist” 70 

global structure 7-9 , and to precede high-SF local information in the primate ventral visual pathway 9-

. This is referred to as coarse-to-fine analysis based on SF information analysis 9-11,13,14 , and is 72 

supported by neural recordings of parafoveal regions in V1, V2, and V4 toward object-selective 73 

regions in inferotemporal (IT) cortex 6,15-19 , where preferred SFs generally decrease along the ventral 74 

visual hierarchy when tested with sinusoidal gratings. 75 

However, global shape is not necessarily defined only by low-SF information. For both small 76 

and large objects, their global shapes are defined mostly by sharp edges and lines, and natural objects 77 

and scenes often contain a broad range of low- and high-SF components 20,21 . Furthermore, neurons 78 

representing the foveal region have very small receptive fields (RFs) and support exceptionally high 79 

visual acuity 15-17 . This high acuity allows foveal vision to resolve not only local details but also 80 

emergent global forms defined by high-SF components ( Figure 1AB ). Accordingly, as visual acuity 81 

declines exponentially with increasing eccentricity, global form remains readily perceived but 82 

becomes blurred in the parafovea and peripheral vision (2–6° and beyond) 22-24 . Furthermore, cortical 83 

neuronal recordings from parafoveal regions have revealed that the sizes of neuronal RFs increase 84 

markedly along the primate ventral visual pathway 25-28 , allowing cortical neurons to integrate 85 

information over progressively larger regions of visual space and to represent large objects and scenes 86 

of increasing complexity 26-30 . These findings form the basis of a dominant hierarchical feature-87 

integration framework, which posits that V1 primarily encodes local details whereas IT represents 88 

objects and scenes through progressive pooling and integration of lower-level features. However, this 89 

framework, which emphasizes a transformation from local to global representations, does not specify 90 

how fine global form of small objects is jointly represented with fine local information within foveal 91 

representations. 92 

As neuronal RF size increases with retinal eccentricity and along the visual hierarchy 15-17,27,31 ,93 

while visual acuity decreases radically, the spatial contents available for cortical processing change 94 

substantially across both visual space and cortical stages. Thus, it remains unclear how global forms 95 

spanning a wide range of SFs, together with fine local features, are neuronally represented across 96

Results 119 

The complexity of natural images, which contain multiple orientations and other visual attributes, 120 

limits their utility for isolating the neural mechanisms of global and local processing without applying 121 

oriented spatial filters 10,13,14,32 . To overcome these limitations, we constructed two-dimensional tire-122 

track-like global–local stimuli composed of aligned, equally spaced local line elements ( Figure 1D ), 123 

which can be reliably identified in neuronal responses at both single-cell and population levels. The 124 

SFs of these global–local stimuli were set at 0.5 cycles/° and 3 cycles/° representing coarse global 125 

and fine local information, respectively. Conceptually similar to the hierarchical stimuli introduced 126 

by Navon 7 , these stimuli enabled psychophysical validation showing that global form and local 127 

features with orthogonal orientations are perceived with comparable precision within the central 2° 128 

of the fovea ( Figure 1E ). In contrast, global form becomes increasingly dominant in the parafoveal 129 

region and beyond. This eccentricity-dependent decline in acuity affects local feature perception more 130 

strongly than global structure, indicating that global and local processing vary across retinal 131 

eccentricity along cortical hierarchy. 132 

To address cortical neuronal mechanisms underlying fine global and local representations in 133 

foveal and parafoveal vision, we performed two-photon imaging with single-cell resolution in awake 134 

macaques 33,34 , across four successive cortical areas along the ventral stream—V1, V2, V4, and IT— 135 

at eccentricities of 1–2° within the foveal region ( Figure 1F ). In the parafoveal regions of these 136 

cortices (2–6° eccentricity), we combined large-window intrinsic-signal optical imaging of 137 

population responses with extracellular electrophysiological recordings 6,34,35 .138 

139 

Distinct spatial components of global form and local features 140 

In contrast to control stimuli of oriented sinusoidal gratings, whose spatial power is concentrated at a 141 

single SF, global–local stimuli contain multiple SF components ( Figure S1A ), consistent with the 142 

Fourier analysis results obtained from the image of the Portland Vase. Notably, in addition to spectral 143 

power being concentrated at the low SF that matches the designed global SF of 0.5 cycles/°, global 144 

forms also contain high-SF components, including those derived from the oriented line endpoints. 145 

Accordingly, the power spectrum of global form information spanned from coarse to fine (0.5 to 8 146 

cycles/°). In contrast, fine local information remains sharp, as it is dominated by spectral power of 147 

high SFs, at the preset 3 cycles/° and above. 148

Importantly, as stimulus size increases progressively to match neuronal RFs across retinal 149 

eccentricity and individual cortical area along the visual hierarchy 27,28 , the proportion of global SF 150 

power rises rapidly relative to local SF power ( Figure S1BC ), suggesting that neurons with larger 151 

RFs are exposed to proportionally much more global information containing both low- and high-SF 152 

components. These results indicate that global form and local parts are represented in parallel but not 153 

in fixed proportion within inherently overlapping spatial-frequency domains. Note that stimulus scale 154 

and RF size increase across retinal eccentricity and the visual hierarchy, low-SF components increase 155 

markedly and dominate global representations. Thus, global and local representations arise from 156 

scale-dependent mixtures of low- and high-SF components rather than from strictly separable spatial-157 

frequency bands. This spatial analysis challenges the common practice of using low-SF filtering as a 158 

proxy for global information 9,32 .159

Hierarchical population representations of global and local in the parafovea 161 

Based on the spatial analysis of global form and local features ( Figure S1 ), we first investigated how 162 

global and local information are represented at the population level in the parafoveal regions across 163 

the visual hierarchy. Cortical population responses in V1, V2, and V4 at eccentricities spanning from 164 

1° to 6° were simultaneously recorded with intrinsic-signal optical imaging in eight hemispheres from 165 

five macaques ( Figure 1F ). Differential orientation maps (dark–bright domains) in response to 166 

drifting global–local stimuli, with angular differences between global and local orientations of 90°, 167 

45°, and 0°, were obtained ( Figure 2A ). Oriented drifting sinusoidal gratings served as control stimuli. 168 

By analyzing orientation preference maps (colored contours) overlaid on the differential orientation 169 

maps for each area 6,35 (Figure S2 ), we found that the population response profiles to grating stimuli 170 

in all visual areas exhibited peaks and troughs at the expected orientations (0°, 90°, 45° and 135°). 171 

For global–local stimuli, population responses in V1 and V2 aligned consistently with the local 172 

orientations. In contrast, those in V4 tracked only the global orientation and were unaffected by the 173 

orientations of local elements. To further illustrate global and local representations across retinal 174 

eccentricity in V1, V2, and V4, we constructed global–local preference maps by extracting the peak 175 

orientation of the response profile at each pixel in response to the above global–local stimuli ( Figure 176 

2B ). In V1 and V2, peak orientations were consistently centered at around 90° or 45°, corresponding 177 

to the local orientations of the stimuli, whereas in V4 peak orientations only represented the global 178 

orientations. These hierarchical population responses—global-dominant in V4 and local-dominant in 179 

V1 and V2—were consistently observed across all hemispheres and animals ( Figures S3 and S4 ). 180

However, we previously demonstrated that there exist high-SF functional domains in parafoveal 201 

V4 encoding local orientations of the global–local stimuli 6,37 , suggesting that both local and global 202 

features are represented in parallel in V4. To further investigate how global and local representations 203 

vary with retinal eccentricity, we focused on V4, a key intermediate stage linking early visual cortex 204 

with higher cortical areas. Because V4 has larger receptive fields and a lower cortical magnification 205 

factor than V1 and V2, a smaller cortical surface area in V4 can represent a relatively larger region 206 

of visual space. Within the global–local preference maps of peak responses (global: 0°, local: 45°), 207 

we observed red-hot and dark-blue regions encoding local and global orientations, respectively 208 

(Figure 2C ). Importantly, local orientations dominated V4 in its foveal regions within 2° eccentricity, 209 

whereas at more peripheral regions peak orientations shifted toward global encoding. These results 210 

indicate that V4 retains fine local information from foveal V1 and V2 inputs, while increasingly 211 

representing global information across retinal eccentricity within its larger RFs. Due to the limited 212 

spatial resolution of intrinsic-signal optical imaging, however, it remains unclear how global form 213 

and local information are encoded neuronally at each cortex of the visual hierarchy. 214

Neuronal encoding of global and local information within the parafovea 216 

To address the above question at the neuronal level, we extracellularly recorded 155, 90, and 189 217 

orientation-selective neurons in V1, V2, and V4, respectively, at eccentricities between 2° and 6° in 218 

three awake macaques ( Figure 1F ). We characterized the response tuning of individual neurons to 219 

global–local stimuli, in which global and local orientations differed by 90°. Oriented sinusoidal 220 

gratings were used as control stimuli to identify each neuron’s preferred orientation. Based on feature 221 

selectivity quantified by a global–local index (GLI) 6,35 , neurons were then classified as local-222 

orientation-selective, dual-orientation-selective or global-orientation-selective (Figure 3A and 223 

Figure S5A ). Note that a subset of neurons displayed dual tuning, with two response peaks 224 

corresponding to local and global orientations of global–local stimuli, respectively. These three types 225 

of neurons were found across all cortical areas, indicating that both global and local information from 226 

the same stimuli emerge at the neuronal level as early as V1 and propagate in parallel along the ventral 227 

visual hierarchy. 228

decreased along the visual hierarchy (median SFs: 3.64 cycles/°, 2.65 cycles/°, 1.41 cycles/° across 248 

V1, V2 and V4; one-way ANOVA, F = 7.00, p = 1.02 × 10 ⁻3 ) ( Figure 3B ). Note that the median 249 

preferred SF in V4 was higher than the ~1 cycle/° reported in a previous study 17 . This is likely because 250 

many of our V4 recordings were obtained from high-SF domains as previously identified 6 .251

local-selective neurons across all areas were biased toward high SFs. By contrast, global-selective 253 

neurons spanned a broad range of preferred SFs, from 0.125 to 8 cycles/° ( Figure 3ABC and Figure 254

results indicate that both coarse and fine global information can be successfully captured by neurons 256 

preferring low and high SFs, respectively, at each cortical area. Notably, a subset of neurons with 257 

low-SF preferences defined by sinusoidal grating stimuli (< 1 cycle/°; e.g., 12/109 in V1), responded 258 

well to the local orientation with its SF preset at 3 cycles/° ( Figure 3C and Figure S5 ). This 259 

discrepancy is likely due to the fact that these low-SF preference neurons are broadband tuned 260 

(Figure S5B ), although they showed significantly stronger responses at 0.5 cycles/° than to 3 cycles/° 261 

to sinusoidal grating stimuli. They were actually activated by the high-SF local information at 3 262 

cycles/° which dominate the Fourier power spectrum of the global–local stimuli ( Figures S5C ). 263 

Overall, at the neuronal level, global form emerges alongside local features as early as V1, but 264 

global dominance increases progressively from V1 to V4 ( Figure 3D ), consistent with population 265 

results obtained using intrinsic-signal optical imaging ( Figure 2 and Figures S2 and S3 ). This 266 

gradual shift in the relative dominance of global and local encoding may be explained by increasing 267 

RF sizes across cortical areas (two-way ANOVA, F = 73.81, p < 0.001) ( Figure 3E ). By contrast, 268 

there was no significant difference in the RF sizes among the three cell types within each cortical area 269 

(two-way ANOVA, F = 0.40, p = 0.67), suggesting that RF size alone does not distinguish global 270 

from local representations within a given cortical area. To further distinguish global- and local-271 

selective neurons, we compared the linearity of their responses to sinusoidal grating stimuli. In 272 

contrast to the linearity of local-selective neurons, both dual-selective and global-selective neurons 273 

exhibited greater nonlinearity in V1 and V2 ( Figure 3F ; one-way ANOVA, F = 19.31, p < 0.001 in 274 

V1, F = 7.34, p = 1.14 × 10 ⁻3 in V2, and F = 2.20, p = 0.12 in V4). Note that in V4 all three types 275 

including local-selective neurons exhibited nonlinearity. This nonlinearity suggests that the responses 276 

of dual- and global-selective neurons cannot be explained solely by the Fourier power spectrum of 277 

global–local stimuli, and may involve nonlinear spatial integration mechanisms, possibly including 278 

end-stopping as early as V1 25,36 . Thus, this stronger nonlinearity associated with global processing in 279 

V1 and V2 may reflect the emergent properties of global forms, consistent with the Gestalt view that 280 

the whole is not simply the sum of its parts. 281

Temporal processing of global and local information in the parafovea 286 

The perceptual coarse (global) to fine (local) framework, in which global information is proposed to 287 

be processed before local detail, is supported by observations that neurons preferring low SFs exhibit 288 

shorter response latencies than those preferring higher SFs when tested with sinusoidal gratings 9,38 .289 

These slower high-SF responses likely originate in the primate fovea, which is characterized by a 290 

high density of cone photoreceptors and midget ganglion cells with relatively slower temporal 291 

responses than those in the perifovea 2,39 . We confirmed the latency differences between low and high 292 

SF by using sinusoidal gratings in V1, V2 and V4 ( Figure 4AB ) and V4 in previous study 6 . In addition, 293 

these response latencies of either onsets or peaks exhibited monotonical increases for all SFs across 294 

the visual hierarchy. However, whether neuronal responses to coarse and fine global forms defined 295 

by low and high SFs, respectively, together with fine local detail within global–local stimuli are 296 

themselves temporally distinguishable has not been examined directly. Given the band-pass nature of 297 

SF tunings of neuronal responses to sinusoidal gratings 15-17 and the broadband SF power spectrum of 298 

global information in the global–local stimuli ( Figure S1 ), neurons preferring intermediate SFs (1–3 299 

cycles/°) could not be assigned to either low- or high-SF global groups. Thus, we only compared 300 

response latencies of global neurons in response to global–local stimuli, but based on their preferred 301 

SFs were either below 1 cycle/° (low-SF global) or above 3 cycles/° (high-SF global) measured with 302 

sinusoidal gratings. As the SF components of local features in the global–local stimuli were 3 cycles/° 303 

and above ( Figure S1 ), neurons in responses to local orientation were classified as high-SF local 304 

orientation selective. Therefore, responses latencies were compared only among low-SF global, high-305 

SF global and high-SF local groups at each cortical area along the visual hierarchy ( Figure 4CD ). 306

Discussion 478 

In our daily life, we can effortlessly resolve both fine global shapes or forms and fine local details of 479 

small objects in central vision (including foveola, fovea and parafovea), while also grasping the gist 480 

of multiple items in the surrounding scene (perifovea and periphery). However, a century-old question, 481 

dating back to the Gestalt psychologists and their proposal of “global precedence” 4,5 , remains 482 

unresolved: how are global form and local features represented at the neuronal level to generate 483 

coherent visual perception across the visual field? Current hierarchical theories of visual feature 484 

integration, including local-to-global and coarse-to-fine frameworks 9-11,13,14,25-28 , have not specifically 485 

explained how fine global forms and local details are jointly represented by cortical neurons in 486 

primate foveal vision. Our findings extend previous work on parafoveal and peripheral vision and 487 

establish a new cortical framework for understanding small-object perception in central vision. 488 

489 

Multi-scale global and local processing across eccentricity and cortical hierarchy 490 

The Gestalt notion of “global precedence” 4,5 gave rise to classical models of visual processing in 491 

which global form perception is associated with low-SF information and local feature analysis with 492 

high-SF components 6,8,9,46 . This view has shaped dominant frameworks in which coarse global 493 

structure is extracted early and subsequently refined by local analysis, for example through “hierarchy 494 

and reverse hierarchy” loops in which IT represents the global whole before fine local details are 495 

recovered from V1 inputs 9,11 . However, both natural image statistics 20,21 (Figure 1B ) and the global– 496 

local stimuli used here ( Figure S1A ) indicate that global structure is not confined to low SFs, but 497 

instead spans a broad range of spatial scales, including high-SF components. Consistent with this, our 498 

neuronal recordings from foveal and parafoveal regions showed that high-SF-preferring neurons were 499 

not limited to their expected role in local processing; a substantial high-SF population also encoded 500 

fine global information across ventral cortical areas. In foveal vision, neurons with small RFs and 501 

high-SF sensitivity support not only fine local analysis but also the encoding of fine-scale global form, 502 

enabling global and local information to coexist in high-resolution representations. Thus, these 503

findings revise conventional coarse-to-fine accounts by showing that in addition to coarse spatial 504 

processing, global perception also engages fine-scale processing in the foveal vision. They provide a 505 

neuronal basis for the capacity of central vision to resolve both precise global form and fine local 506 

detail from as early as V1 through IT, supporting high-acuity visual recognition in central vision 507 

(Figure 1A ). 508 

Current feedforward processing theories proposed that visual information advances from simple 509 

to increasingly complex features and culminates in higher cortical areas associated with conscious 510 

perception. Our results refine these classical hierarchical frameworks, which have emphasized a 511 

progressive transition from local representations in early visual cortex to global and complex 512 

representations in higher visual areas 11,25,26,28,29 . Although these feature integration and assembly 513 

theories suggest that object recognition may culminate in IT and other higher cognitive areas, we 514 

found that basic two-dimensional global and local spatial information is already encoded in parallel 515 

as early as V1 and across all stages of the ventral visual pathway, with a gradual shift towards global 516 

dominance along the hierarchy ( Figure 7A ). This dynamic multi-scale encoding, together with 517 

hierarchical enlargement of RFs, may allow the visual system to sample over increasing regions of 518 

visual space while preserving stable high-resolution perception of global forms and local details in 519 

the fovea ( Figure 1A ). In other words, IT neurons, which exhibit stimulus-dependent effective RF 520 

sizes 45,47 , may encode fine global form together with fine local detail within the foveal portion of their 521 

RFs, whereas neurons with larger RFs may represent coarse global form across the entire visual field. 522 

Beyond the expected high-SF local-selective neurons, the presence of both fine- and coarse-scale 523 

global-selective neurons are already present in V1, consistent with previous observations that V1 524 

neurons can encode natural complex structures, synthetic images and internal sharp illusory 525 

shapes 33,36,48-50 . Previous studies have also shown that IT neurons encode fine-grained information 526 

within facial stimuli by changing SF, stimulus size, or facial expressions 32,40,51 , consistent with both 527 

our present findings in both foveal and parafoveal regions and our previous evidence that fine-scale 528 

information is preserved in the parafoveal regions through successive stages of the ventral visual 529 

pathway 6,37 . Recent high-resolution fMRI further indicates that multiple visual areas, including IT 530 

and other higher-order regions, can be robustly driven by stimuli presented within the foveola and 531 

fovea 52 . At eccentricities within 1°, IT cells exhibit significant selective responses to small geometry 532 

forms 53,54 , consistent with the observation that a subset of IT cells have small RFs 47,53 . However, these 533 

studies did not explicitly dissect global and local features or distinguish their associations with fine-534 

and coarse-scale representations. Our findings extend these important observations by demonstrating 535

Closely spaced temporal processing of global form and local features 543 

In primate retina, the fovea cones exhibited slower light responses up to 30 milliseconds than 544 

peripheral cones 2,43 , supporting high acuity but slow visual perception 42,44 . Regardless, our neuronal 545 

latency results from parafovea recordings accord with classical accounts of coarse-to-fine perception, 546 

which propose that rapid processing of low-SF global information provides a causal basis for the 547 

perceptual precedence of global form 9,11,14 . We refined this framework by distinguishing fine global 548 

processing from coarse global processing: high-SF global-selective neurons exhibited the longest 549 

response latencies across parafoveal representations (2°–6° eccentricity) in V1, V2 and V4. The 550 

latencies of low-SF global, high-SF local, and high-SF global responses all increased monotonically 551 

along the visual hierarchy, consistent with feedforward propagation and progressive integration 552 

across successive cortical stages 28-30 . However, these response latencies were all below ~100ms, the 553 

timescale required for object identification and recognition 9,55,56 . The response delays among these 554 

groups were tightly clustered within only a few milliseconds ( Figure 4 and Figure S6 ), and how 555 

such small temporal differences contribute to rapid categorization and identification of small objects 556 

remains unclear. Regardless, these distinct yet closely spaced responses to global form and local detail 557 

suggest that meaningful visual representations emerge early within the ventral visual hierarchy prior 558 

to extensive engagement of higher cognitive cortices 9,12 . Furthermore, our finding that the temporal 559 

processing of fine and coarse global forms by high- and low-SF-preferring neurons emerges as early 560 

as V1 and persists across successive cortical stages does not support reverse-hierarchy models in 561 

which coarse-to-fine processing is proposed to proceed from IT to V1 9,11 . Although the precise role 562 

of top-down feedback from IT and other higher cortical areas to earlier visual cortices remain unclear, 563 

it may contribute to higher-level functions such as attentional modulation and size- and position-564 

invariant object representation 54,57,58 . Importantly, SF tuning to drifting gratings is itself dynamically 565 

time-dependent 59,60 , raising the possibility that coarse-global, fine-local and fine-global 566 

representations may likewise evolve over time. Consistent with this possibility, recent evidence 567

The advantage of fine global representation for small objects in foveal V1 576 

V1 neurons are not merely simple orientation and spatial-frequency filters 33,48,64 , as suggested by 577 

responses to sinusoidal grating stimuli, but also have the capacity to integrate local information into 578 

precise global representations of small objects. The simplest example of visual feature construction 579 

is the de novo generation of orientation and direction selectivity through spatiotemporal integration 580 

of collinearly organized inputs from the lateral geniculate nucleus by V1 layer 4 neurons with simple 581 

RFs 65-69 . Many V1 neurons also exhibit complex RF properties that encode line ends, angles and other 582 

local configurations 25,33,48 . Furthermore, V1 neurons can signal figure–ground segregation 70,71 .583 

Recent findings further demonstrate that this integrative capability critically depends on cross-584 

laminar interactions across V1 sublayers. For example, cascaded normalization mechanisms across 585 

V1 sublayers substantially transform spatial integration from input to output layers 72 , whereas high-586 

SF information in primate V1 is not merely inherited from feedforward P-pathway inputs, but can be 587 

selectively amplified by local recurrent circuits in superficial output layers 73 . More recently, studies 588 

using natural images and complex visual stimuli have shown that V1 neurons respond robustly to 589 

richer visual structures than simple Gaussian–Gabor filter models would predict 33,36,48-50,74 . Together, 590 

these findings indicate that V1 provides a more flexible substrate for spatial integration than classical 591 

filter-based descriptions alone would suggest. 592 

This computational advantage and capacity for basic global information processing at early 593 

cortical stage of the visual hierarchy is especially important for small-object representation in foveal 594 

vision. Small objects fall within the high-acuity foveal map, where V1 neurons have small RFs and 595 

preserve precise retinotopic relationships. Thus, retinotopic information about fine global form and 596 

local detail can be represented in parallel before spatial information is degraded by pooling across 597 

eccentricity in higher cortical areas for progressively coarse global representations ( Figure 7 ). This 598 

view is consistent with our finding that neurons selective for global information over increasing visual 599

Several limitations of the study 610 

First, selective attention can bias perception toward either global structure or local detail 75,76 . Because 611 

our recordings were performed during passive fixation in awake monkeys, without task demands that 612 

selectively emphasized local or global information, we could not determine how attention modulates 613 

global–local processing. Thus, the present study addresses feedforward and intrinsic global–local 614 

representations rather than selective visual attention. Second, because we used two-dimensional 615 

global–local stimuli rather than natural three-dimensional (3-D) objects, our findings should be 616 

interpreted as evidence that V1 responses most likely represent two-dimensional global 617 

contours/outlines, including fine-scale high-SF global structure such as aligned line endpoints in the 618 

stimuli, rather than as evidence that 3-D global objects are represented in V1. In fact, 3-D objects are 619 

represented in IT through hierarchical cortical integration 28 . Third, the cortical mechanisms 620 

underlying global and local processing in the foveola, corresponding approximately to the central 0– 621 

1° of visual space, remain unresolved. This limitation reflects not only the technical challenges of 622 

neuronal recording or imaging in the primate visual system 52,77 , but also a more fundamental 623 

constraint: the spatial resolution of the foveola exceeds that of standard visual display systems. 624 

Consequently, stimuli with sufficiently well-defined global and local features cannot yet be presented 625 

at a scale matched to the extremely small RFs of foveolar neurons. Although our recordings did not 626 

directly sample the foveola, our near-foveolar dominance of high-SF representations of both global 627 

form and local features suggests that fine-scale global structure may extend toward the center of gaze, 628 

where the fine global form and local detail of very small objects will be resolved with very high visual 629 

acuity, albeit with longer processing times as early as the fovea of primate retina 2,43 .630

A neuronal framework for foveal vision of global and local information processing 632 

Together, these population- and single-neuron results reveal distinctive encodings of global 633 

information across multiple SF scales, alongside high-SF local information across retinal 634 

eccentricities from V1 to IT. Extending the Gestalt notions of global precedence and filling-in 635 

theory 4,5 , our findings show that global processing begins with rapid coarse representations, but also 636 

encompasses fine-scale global representations that emerge through coordinated multi-scale and 637 

temporally structured processing as early as V1. Although the relationship between neuronal activity 638 

and perceptual experience remains difficult to resolve, our findings provide a neural basis for the 639 

coherent encoding of fine global form and fine local detail, together with rapid processing of coarse 640 

global information in foveal and parafoveal regions within each cortical stage. These results support 641 

a dynamic neuronal framework of integrated vision, in which global and local information are jointly 642 

constructed across retinal eccentricities particularly in the fovea and along the ventral visual stream. 643 

Within this framework, IT neurons, with their large RFs and convergent foveal and parafoveal inputs, 644 

may dynamically integrate coarse and fine information to support the categorization and recognition 645 

of the same or different objects across a wide range of sizes. For example, coarse global structure 646 

may emerge first to support rapid categorization, followed by the progressive hierarchical filling-in 647 

of various local details and features that support object identity across spatial scales 40 . More broadly, 648 

this framework helps explain how the primate visual system integrates precise central vision with 649 

broader peripheral representations to construct coherent perception across large visual field. Together, 650 

these findings provide a neural framework for addressing and refining a century-old question in 651 

Gestalt theory: how coherent wholes and their constituent parts are jointly constructed in active foveal 652 

vision to support fine-scale perception during reading, object recognition and other visually 653 

demanding behaviors. 654
