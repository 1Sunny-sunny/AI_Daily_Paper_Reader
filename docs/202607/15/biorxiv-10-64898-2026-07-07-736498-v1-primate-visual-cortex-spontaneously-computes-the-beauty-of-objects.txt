# Abstract 23 

During everyday vision, b eauty i s a spontaneous experience. Perce ived beauty varies 24 

substantially across object categories : We find puppies more beautiful than moths and roses 25 

more beautiful than potatoes. Here, we provide evidence that early processing in human and 26 

non -human primate visual cortex already reflects perceived beauty, even without deliberate 27 

judgments of beauty. First, in a combined EEG -fMRI analysis, we show that beauty ratings for 28 

hundreds of object categories from the THINGS database are predicted from neural responses 29 

measured in separate group s of human participants viewing the same images without explicit 30 

beauty judgments . Beauty -related representations emerged in occipital visual cortex within 31 

100ms and peaked around 130ms. Second , multi -unit activity in macaque visual cortex also 32 

predicted human beauty judgments , despite the animals’ highly limited knowledge of the 33 

objects . These results indicate a perceptual basis for the beauty of visual categories, which may 34 

underlie spontaneous preference formation in everyday situations .35

# Introduction 42 

The experience of beauty is an integral part of our everyday lives (1, 2 ). For example, when 43 

walking down a street, we may admire a beautiful building or a stunning cloud formation (3).44 

Within coarse -scale visual domains , such as architecture, faces, or artworks, there are 45 

ex ampl es that are consistently perceived as more or less beautiful. However, everyday visual 46 

objects themselves also differ vastly in t heir aesthetic appeal: We find roses more beautiful 47 

than raisins and flamingos more beautiful than vultures . Here, we investigate how the beauty of 48 

objects is fundamentally determined by perceptual computations in the visual cortex. 49 

Some previous research suggests that aesthetic experience require s thought (4) and therefore 50 

depends on intentional higher -level cognition linked to neural responses in orbitofrontal areas 51 

(5) and the Default Mode Network (6). Contrasting this view , numerous EEG studies have 52 

reported rapid beauty -related responses, sometimes starting as early as 100ms after stimulus 53 

onset, indicating that representations of beauty first emerge in visual cortex (7-10 ). More recent 54 

computational approaches demonstrate that even artificial neural networks with orthogonal 55 

training objectives like categorization are capable of predicting human beauty judgments (11 -56 

15 ), suggesting that visual beauty is firmly grounded in the analysis of visual features. 57 

Yet, previous investigations only assessed the neural correlates of perceived beauty across 58 

exemplars of one or few categories, often failed to account for alternative explanations related 59 

to related concepts like emotional valence, and provided a characterization limited to the 60 

spatial or temporal domain . Furthermore, most studies employed explicit beauty -rating tasks 61 

while measuring beauty -related neural responses, leaving it unclear how beauty arises 62 

spontaneously from cortical activations that emerge incidentally during visual processing. Here, 63 

we provide a strong test of such a representation by (1) delineating uniquely beauty -related 64 

representations across a very broad range of visual categories , and (2) by assessing how the 65 

beauty of visual categories can be predicted from visual responses that occur incidentally, 66 

without a beauty -related task. If such incidental visual responses indeed predict beauty on the 67 

category level, we predict that they do so during early processing in the visual cortex . In 68 

addition, we hypothesize they are fundamental enough to not only occur in humans, but even in 69 

a non -human primate model. 70 

In our study , w e predict ed human beauty ratings for hundreds of object categories from brain 71 

responses in a set of participants that merely viewed these images in a task regime unrelated to 72 

beauty . We use d EEG , MEG and fMRI responses in humans (16, 17 ) and performed multimodal 73 

fusion analysis (18, 19 ) to resolve the neural representations of beauty in both space and time. 74 

To validate and extend our results across species, we further predict ed beauty ratings from 75 

visual cortex activity in non -human primates that viewed the same images (20 ).76 

Our results show that early responses within 100ms of processing , localized to the visual 77 

cortex , predict the beauty of visual categories , demonstrating a spontaneous, rapid 78 

representation of beauty that emerges in parallel to object recognition and in the absence of a 79 

beauty -related task . Moreover, we show that neural responses to the same objects in the 80 

macaque visual cortex also predict human beauty ratings , revealing that early visual responses 81 

predict beauty even for non -human primates. Together, these findings establish a perceptual 82 

neural basis for the perceived beauty of natural object categories , which may form the basis for 83 

the spontaneous appreciation of beauty in everyday situations .84

# Results 87 

To reveal the neural correlates of beauty across many visual categories , we employed an 88 

extensive sampling approach, based on the THINGS database (21 ), which contains photographs 89 

of 1,854 object categories. In an online experiment, observers (N=3,750) rated the beauty of 7 90 

different images for each of the 1,854 categories , with each image rated by 24 to 36 raters 91 

(M=30.51) . Collapsing across the individual images, we obtained beauty rating s for each 92 

category , which we re subsequently related to neural responses to the object categories, 93 

recorded in experiments unrelated to beauty .94 

Rapid responses in human visual cortex predict the beauty of visual categories 95 

We related beauty ratings to spatially and temporally resolved human neuroimaging data , using 96 

human fMRI (17 ) and EEG (16 ) recordings (s ee Fig. 1 A) , acquired for a subset of 634 out of the 97 

1,854 object categories. During the EEG and fMRI sessions, participants viewed 12 example 98 

images for each category while performing a simple oddball detection task that was unrelated 99 

to beauty (see Methods) . Notably, the EEG data was recorded under a rapid presentation 100 

regime, which likely prevented participants from making slow and deliberate judgments of 101 

beauty. This experimental regime allowed us to assess whether neural representations that 102 

arise spontaneously and are unrelated to beauty judgments can still predict the beauty of 103 

object categories as assessed by our online observers. 104 

To obtain a spatiotemporal mapping of beauty -related representations , we performed model -105 

based EEG -fMRI fusion analysis (19, 22, 23 ). Analogous to representational similarity analysis 106 

(RSA), we first computed representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) capturing pairwise 107 

dissimilarities of EEG responses to the stimuli at every time point and then averaged them 108 

across participants. These RDMs provided a temporally resolved view across the visual 109 

processing hierarchy. Second, we computed pairwise dissimilarit ies in fMRI responses in each 110 

of 50 homotopic bilateral brain parcels (24 ), which we again averaged across participants. The 111 

resulting RDMs provided a spatially resolved view across the whole brain. Bilateral parcels were 112 

chosen as we did not expect strong lateralization effects , given the stimulus diversity . Third , we 113 

computed the pairwise dissimilarities in beauty ratings averaged across all exemplars for each 114 

category . The resulting RDM provided a measure of how similar each category was to every 115 

other category in its perceived beauty. All RDMs had 200,661 unique entries, i.e., pairwise 116 

comparisons of 634 categories. We utilized commonality analysis (25 ) to calculate the tripartite 117 

shared variance between EEG, fMRI and beauty ratings , extracting the timepoints and parcels 118 

where a portion of the representational similarity between EEG and fMRI is driven by beauty .119 

Critically , to ensure that the resulting commonality coefficients are uniquely attributable to 120 

dissimilarities in beauty, rather than other judgments correlated with beauty, all models 121 

accounted for dissimilarities in a set of 12 other property ratings for all categories :122 

Manmadeness, natural ness , preciousness, pleasantness, cuteness, arousal, animacy, 123 

heaviness, as well as the abilities to move, be moved, grasped, and held. These ratings were 124 

taken from the THINGSplus dataset (26 ). For each of these properties, we generated an RDM 125 

based on pairwise rating differences, which we partialled out in all analyses. 126 

We report commonality score time courses , epoched between -100ms and 700ms relative to 127 

stimulus onset, for a set of 10 bilateral parcel pairs from the Yan atlas (24 ). These parcels were 128 

selected a priori, based on a n initial RSA solely on the fMRI data , in which they yielded 129 

significant correlations between regional fMRI RDMs and beauty RDMs . Parcels included 130 

regions in the occipital visual cortex, the dorsal attention network (27 ) as well as the default 131 

mode network (see Fig. 1 C & D , for a detailed report see Supplementary Table 1 ). Significance 132 

of the commonality coefficients was assessed using a permutation test at each time point and 133 

False Discovery Rate (FDR) corrections (28 ) across time. For this purpose, we developed a novel 134

We performed two supplementary analyses solidifying our results. First, we investigated 177 

whether our results truly reflect beauty on the category level, rather than the beauty assigned to 178 

individual images. To this end , we re -ran the EEG -fMRI fusion analysis using only the exemplar 179 

images shown in the EEG and fMRI study that were not rated for beauty in the online experiment. 180 

The results in occipital visual parcels remained significant (see Supplementary Figure 1 A), 181 

indicating that the representations are indeed driven by the beauty of object categories rather 182 

than individual exemplar image s. Second , we tested the reproducibility of our results across 183 

neuroimaging methods. To this end , we additionally performed an MEG -fMRI fusion analysis 184 

using MEG data recorded for the same object images (17 ). This analysis yielded a notably 185 

similar pattern , showing an early and reliable neural representation of beauty (see 186 

Supplementary Figure 1 B).187 

Together , these results reveal a rapid representation of the beauty of object categories in the 188 

visual cortex , spatially and temporally co -occurring with cortical responses related to object 189 

recognition (16, 17, 22, 29 ). Representations of beauty emerg ed within the first 100ms of 190 

processing and were sustained beyond 200ms. The significant and much larger commonality 191 

coefficients in occipital visual areas , in contrast to the parcels outside the visual system ,192 

suggest that visual object processing is immediately reflective of beauty , in particular early 193 

processing in the visual cortex .194 

Responses in monkey visual cortex predict human beauty judgments 195 

Our human neuroimaging results suggest that categor y-level beauty is fundamentally reflected 196 

in visual cortex responses that emerge spontaneously during object perception . Humans, 197 

however, learn to associate certain object categories with their beauty, and visual 198 

representations may be shaped by those associations. For instance, we may repeatedly see 199 

rainbows, have a pleasurable feeling when seeing them, and talk about their beauty. The 200 

association with beauty may shape their representation in visual cortex. Alternatively, there may 201 

be something fundamentally sensory about the beauty of rainbows, with their visual features 202 

acting as a true precursory of beauty, even in the absence of such associations. If there existed 203 

such a visual precursor for perceiving beauty , we should be able to predict human beauty 204 

judgments from cortical responses in non -human primates as well , given their highly similar 205 

visual object processing architecture (30, 31 ). A successful prediction from monkey visual 206 

cortex would further corroborate that the effects are large ly independent from human 207 

associations with an object's beauty : After all, monkeys will not have encountered , and 208 

appreciated, most of the object categories in the THINGS repertoire .209

# Discussion 244 

We show that the beauty of object categories is coded in early neural responses in the visual 245 

system. By modelling neural representations in temporally and spatially resolved brain 246 

responses , validated across species, our results convergently suggest a perceptual basis for the 247 

beauty of visual objects , even in the absence of a beauty -related task regime . This is consistent 248 

with the remarkable predictive power of computer vision models for beauty judgments, even in 249 

the absence of beauty -related training regimes (11 -14, 32 ). Such representations of beauty may 250 

function as a perceptual basis for aesthetic experience s with visual stimuli , enabl ing the 251 

spontaneous , sometimes surprising , experience s of beauty in everyday life. 252 

The neural representation of visual beauty during perceptual processing aligns well with 253 

theories in visual aesthetics (5, 32 -39 ), which posit that the analysis of perceptual attributes 254 

and its corresponding activity in sensory cortical areas is critical for aesthetic appreciation . It is 255 

further consistent with previous findings targeting the neural correlates of beauty within 256 

stimulus categories like faces an d scenes, with early EEG responses predicting within -category 257 

beauty (8-10, 40 -43 ) and visual features extracted by DNNs predicting human beauty ratings (11 -258 

14, 44 ). While many theories in visual aesthetics stress the importance of cognitive factors and 259 

meaningful engagement with a stimulus (4, 6, 32, 45, 46 ), our results complement these 260 

conceptions by show ing that early visual responses may serve as a precursor to the aesthetic 261 

evaluation of object categories , even in the absence of explicit beauty judgments. The se 262 

representations of categor y-level beauty in the visual cortex thus emerge somewhat 263 

independent ly of the task , in line with previous findings o f a task -independent evaluation of 264 

symmetry in abstract patterns (43, 47 ) and consistent with recent EEG work showing no 265 

substantial difference in the emergence of representations related to scene beauty between an 266 

explicit beauty judgment task and an unrelated judgment (48 ). Together, obtaining our results in 267 

absence of a beauty -related task and under a rapid presentation regime in the EEG experiment 268 

suggests that the neural correlates of beauty partly originate from incidental processing in 269 

visual cortex that occurs without deliberate evaluation. 270 

While occipital , occipito -temporal and occipito -parietal regions have been implied in the 271 

representation of visual beauty by several studies (49 -53 ), other s highlight the importance of 272 

regions outside the visual system, including the default mode network (DMN ) (6, 54, 55 ), frontal 273 

brain areas (6, 13, 56, 57 ). In our EEG -fMRI fusion, we did not find significant effects in such non -274 

visual regions. This is consistent with the view that regions like the DMN are linked to the 275 

deliberate evaluation of a stimulus, for instance to assess its relevance in relation to the self 276 

(55 ). In our study, with a beauty -un related task an d a rapid presentation regime, only the 277 

spontaneous perceptual foundations , and potential triggers, of such later and deliberate 278 

evaluations may emerge. 279 

The spontaneous representation of beauty during visual percept ion is reminiscent of correlates 280 

and representations of emotion in the visual cortex (58, 59 ). Indeed, emotions like the feeling of 281 

pleasantness feature prominent ly in theories of aesthetics (46, 60 -62 ), and pleasantness 282 

accounts for a substantial share of variance in beauty ratings (63, 64 ). Yet beauty and pleasure 283 

are not the same . Our results show that the neural correlates of beauty on the category level are 284 

not accounted for by the pleasantness of that category (which we controlled for in our analysis) ,285 

demonstrating a neural computation of beauty in the visual system that goes beyond the coding 286 

of emotional associations with an object category. 287 

Beyond affective factors , there is a wealth of semantic associations for objects of a certain 288 

category , which can modulate our aesthetic experiences (65 ) and which might also modulate 289 

visual brain responses to these categories. Our results suggest that semantic associations are 290 

not critical for the neural correlates of beauty to emerge . First, beauty -related neural responses 291

While our study demonstrates a spontaneous representation of beauty across object 297 

categor ies , it prompts further questions on the nature of the underlying representations. First, 298 

while our study suggests that visual feature coding is critical for distinguishing the beauty of 299 

categories, it remains unclear which features render a category beautiful. Future studies could 300 

distill the contributions of individual image features , such as contour and curvature (66 ), order 301 

and complexity (67 ), meaningful object dimensions (21 ), or behavioral factors like affordance s302 

(40 ). Alternatively, future work could focus on overarching computational principles that 303 

transcend individual feature preferences, such as the efficient allocation of metabolic 304 

processing demands (15 ), the efficient integration of visual features (14 ) or the spatiotemporal 305 

predictability of stimulus features (45 ). Second, we quantified the beauty of object categories 306 

by averaging ratings across observers, and we averaged neural responses across participants. 307 

This approach revealed robust neural representations of object beauty, measured as mean 308 

rated beauty across individuals, thereby indicating a level of universality. Yet, aesthetic appeal is 309 

known to differ between individuals (68 -70 ), with traits like beauty appreciation (71 ) or taste 310 

typicality (72 ) influencing individual preferences. Future studies could combine neural 311 

recordings and subsequent beauty ratings in the same participants to test whether even the 312 

earl iest neural computations of beauty already reflect individual preferences. 313 

Our results revealed that neural activity in the macaque ventral visual stream may predict 314 

human preferences in beauty. This raises the question whether the animals would show similar 315 

preferences to humans , for example when probed in a preferential looking or a self -paced free 316 

viewing paradigm. Such findings would suggest an underlying universality in the perception of 317 

bea uty, which may fundamentally stem from commonalities in visual processing across 318 

species. Such commonalities may arise from a preference for natural feature distributions, 319 

where patterns reverberating an intact nature , such as for example in biophilic design (73, 74 ),320 

may be ones to commonly entice the visual syste m.321

# Materials and Methods 323 

Visual stimuli: THINGS 324 

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the spatiotemporal neural dynamics of object 325 

category beauty . To broadly sample visual object categories, we used images from the THINGS 326 

database (21 ). The database consists of 1,854 object categories, each represented by ≥12 327 

image exemplars (in total 26,107 images). These were extended by another 1,854 THINGSplus 328 

exemplar images by Stoinski, Perkuhn and Hebart ( 26 ). All images are high -quality photographs ,329 

depicting concrete objects or other nameable categories in a naturalistic environment, from 330 

animals and plants over food and clothing items to a variety of manmade objects and nameable 331 

materials (e.g., gold, wood, sand) . The e xemplar images may display one or more individual 332 

objects pertaining to their category (e.g., multiple apples or flowers). 333

workers via Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) . Before ratin , informed consent was obtained from 336 

all participants. After non -adherent worker exclusion (at least three HITs of either a total 337 

duration below 30s on 10 subsequent trials, a variance in rating of less than 1 on 10 subsequent 338

Spatial preselection – fMRI RSA 453 

Before conducting the M/EEG -fMRI fusion analys es, we performed a hypothesis -free spatial 454 

preselection in the fMRI voxel space . The rationale for this was that brain regions which do not 455 

represent beauty in the first place do not need to be included in the subsequent fusion analysis, 456 

saving compute time and reducing false -positive rates. 457 

In our preselection routine , we first parcellated the subject -specific brain -wide fMRI data using 458 

the homotopic local -global parcellations by Yan et al. (24 ) in their 100 -parcel resolution, which 459 

were downloaded from GitHub 460 

(https://github.com/ThomasYeoLab/CBIG/tree/master/stable_projects/brain_parcellation/Yan2 461 

023_homotopic/parcellations/MNI/kong17 ). The parcellation, originally in MNI152 -space, was 462 

transformed into individual subject space for each fMRI subject, using inverse transformation 463 

files from Hebart, Contier, Teichmann et al. (17 ). Subsequently, for every subject and parcel 464 

separately, we performed a reliability -based voxel preselection: Every voxel was ranked by 465 

descending noise ceiling, subsequently selecting the best 100 voxels, while ensuring that the 466 

100th voxel did not fall below a 5% cutoff value. This resulted in a final set of 10,000 voxels per 467 

participant, across 100 parcels. After trimming down the fMRI category -level responses to the 468 

matching 634 object concepts, we constructed 50 RDMs using the 200 voxels of every 469 

homotopic bilateral parcel pair, again calculated via Pearson distance, and again converting 470 

them into RDVs. Lastly, RDVs were again averaged across subjects. 471 

To preselect the relevant bilateral parcels for the fusion commonality analysis, these RDVs were 472 

used in a simple R epresentational Similarity Analysis (RSA ) (75 ), correlating them with the 473 

beauty RDVs (Pearson correlation). After statistical testing, activity in 10 bilateral parcel s was 474 

significantly ( FDR -corrected p<.05) correlated with beauty (see Supplementary Table 1 ). We 475 

then performed fusion c ommonality analysis (described below) on these 10 bilateral parcel 476 

pairs , for all EEG (or MEG , see Supplementary Fig. 1 ) timepoints. 477

Statistical testing 507 

Statistical significance was determined via one -sided permutation tests . For the human fMRI 508 

RSA and the non -human primate MUA RSA , the neural RDM (i.e., the critical dependent variable )509 

was shuffled randomly for 2,5 00 iterations per timepoint . Repeating the analyses with shuffled 510 

RDMs yielded a null distribution of permuted (partial) correlation coefficients . Th is modest but 511 

adequate (80 ) number of permutations was chosen to keep the compute time feasible , given 512 

the large RDM size. The p-value was determined by the proportion of permuted coeff icients 513 

exceeding the true coefficient . All gathered p-values of an analysis were simultaneously 514 

corrected for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) approach, specifically 515 

the Benjamini & Hochberg (28 ) method, implemented in Matlab . For the M/EEG -fMRI fusion 516 

commonality analyses, we developed and deployed a novel permutation testing approach to 517 

reliably correct for the inflations of commonalities that arise from collinearities between 518 

predictors (e.g., the EEG and the human rating predictor of interest) . In simple permutation 519
