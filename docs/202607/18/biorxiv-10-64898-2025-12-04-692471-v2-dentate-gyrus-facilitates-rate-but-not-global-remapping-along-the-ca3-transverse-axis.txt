## Abstract 

The hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) is widely implicated in transforming overlapping experiences into discrete memory traces through granule-cell sparsity. Although proximal CA3 is functionally aligned with the DG through dense reciprocal connectivity, how DG output contributes to memory discrimination within proximal CA3 remains elusive. By combining selective DG lesions with high-resolution recordings across the entire CA3 proximo-distal axis, we examined neural activity as rats explored environments varying in contextual similarity. Global remapping in CA3 remained robust following DG lesion, whereas rate remapping was substantially attenuated. Discrimination impairments were most pronounced in proximal CA3 and attenuated along the transverse axis toward distal CA3, paralleling the topography of mossy-fiber projections. Our findings provide causal evidence that this enhanced rate remapping in proximal CA3, relative to more distal CA3, is related to its preferential DG inputs. Additionally, dentate granule cells and mossy cells coded for complementary aspects of local cue information and mediated CA3 population responses to object manipulation. These data support a mode-specific framework for contextual discrimination: global remapping operates independently of DG input, whereas rate remapping in CA3 is facilitated by DG-mediated rate modulation to enable finer discrimination. These mechanisms illuminate how the DG facilitates the disambiguation of overlapping experiences, potentially via pattern separation.

## Introduction 

The hippocampal formation plays a central role in spatial cognition 1 and episodic memory encoding 2 . In rodents, hippocampal place cells generate a structured internal representation 

of the local environment 3 , anchoring experiential events to specific spatial locations 4. This mapping function emerges from the interplay of two complementary processes: pattern separation, which transforms overlapping inputs into orthogonal neural representations to preserve memory specificity, and pattern completion, which reconstructs complete representations from partial or degraded inputs 5,6 . Computational and anatomical models attribute these distinct operations to separate hippocampal subregions. The dentate gyrus (DG), comprising a vast population of granule cells (GCs) 7, is proposed to execute pattern separation through sparse and selective GC activation in response to entorhinal input. In contrast, CA3, characterized by its extensive recurrent auto-associative connections 8, is thought to support pattern completion via attractor dynamics that stabilize and retrieve previously encoded activity patterns 9-12 .

Extensive experimental evidence implicates the DG as a critical substrate for memory and pattern separation. Targeted DG lesions impair both spatial reference and working memory 13-15 , compromise the formation or retrieval of associations guided by contextual or spatial cues 16-19 , and diminish the stepwise rate changes of CA3 neurons during discrimination of graded visual scenes—a signature of pattern separation 20 . Activity-dependent labeling reveals that GCs are recruited sparsely and selectively across distinct environments 21,22 . In vivo imaging and electrophysiological recordings in behaving rodents demonstrate that alterations in environmental cues elicit discrete shifts in GC spatial representations (remapping), aphysiological hallmark of contextual discrimination 23-25 , and in many cases this discrimination is enhanced for small differences, indicating pattern separation 26,27 . However, dentate function is not mediated by GCs alone. Mossy cells (MCs), the second major excitatory population within

the DG, integrate feedforward GC input with feedback from proximal CA3 (pCA3) pyramidal neurons and regulate GC excitability through extensive associational projections and engagement of inhibitory interneurons 28 . These two excitatory populations exhibit distinct yet complementary activity profiles: GCs activate infrequently and in a highly context-specific manner, whereas MCs sustain broader, condition-specific firing patterns across multiple environments 29-35 . Selective manipulation of either population impairs contextual discrimination 36,37 , object-place associative learning 38 , or novel-object recognition 39 , indicating that GCs and MCs function cooperatively within the DG microcircuit to support its mnemonic role in pattern separation. 

Classical frameworks conceptualize CA3 as a unitary, homogeneous attractor network dedicated to pattern completion, yet a growing body of evidence has revealed pronounced anatomical and functional heterogeneity along its transverse axis. Pyramidal neurons differ systematically from proximal to distal CA3 (dCA3) in dendritic architecture, axonal projections, and transcriptional signatures 40 . These structural distinctions are mirrored by the organization of afferent and efferent connectivity: direct entorhinal input and auto-associative connectivity increase along the proximodistal axis, whereas mossy-fiber (MF) projections from the DG decrease in parallel 8. Corresponding gradients in intrinsic excitability and synaptic strength have also been observed 41 . Functionally, pCA3 lesions produce larger discrimination deficits than dCA3 lesions 42 , and immediate-early-gene imaging reveals segregated processing of spatial versus non-spatial information along the proximodistal axis 43-45 . Moreover, pCA3 is especially responsive to subtle contextual variation, whereas dCA3 displays relative insensitivity to such changes 46 . Electrophysiological recordings across CA3 reveal a clear transverse gradient in place-cell dynamics, with strong remapping observed in proximal regions and markedly weaker remapping toward the distal pole 47,48 . Collectively, these observations suggest that pCA3 operates in concert with the DG as part of a dedicated pattern-separation module 27 , rather than functioning as a uniform component within the classical CA3 attractor network responsible for pattern completion.

The dense projection of MFs from the DG to pCA3, reinforced by pCA3 back-projections and the recurrent GC-MC network within the hilus 8,28 , establishes the structural substrate for coordinated population responses to contextual changes across these regions. Whether the enhanced sensitivity in pCA3 emerges primarily from DG output remains unresolved. To address this, we combined bilateral DG lesions with large-scale recording of CA3 place cells across the transverse axis as rats foraged freely in environments of varying contextual similarity. Eliminating DG input substantially attenuated rate remapping in pCA3 while leaving global remapping largely intact, supporting a mode-specific framework for contextual discrimination. Global remapping, engaged during discrimination of categorical changes to environmental identity, operates independently of DG input, whereas finer, parametric discrimination through rate remapping is facilitated by DG-mediated rate modulation, with the strongest contribution in pCA3. In addition, DG inputs are most critical for local object discrimination in CA3, with GCs and MCs contributing to different dimensions of object features. These findings delineate how the entorhinal-hippocampal circuit deploys distinct computational strategies to transform overlapping inputs into separable memory representations under varying contextual demands 49 .

## Results 

Recording sites and lesion profile in the hippocampus 

To determine how DG inputs influence contextual coding in CA3, movable tetrodes were implanted into the right hippocampus of 13 rats trained to forage in arenas where spatial, non-spatial, or local object cues were systematically manipulated (Fig. 1A). Seven animals received bilateral colchicine injections to selectively ablate DG segments innervating the CA3 recording sites (LES group) (Fig. 1B), while six rats received sham injections (CON group). In controls, tetrodes terminated in or immediately below the supra- and infrapyramidal blades of the DG (23 sites), across the entire proximodistal axis of CA3 (39 sites) (Figs. 1C; S1). In lesioned rats, all 46 tetrode tips localized to CA3 and were spatially indistinguishable from control placements (Fig. 1C, D). Colchicine treatment produced near-total GC depletion at injection sites while sparing CA3 pyramidal layers (Fig. 1F). GC sparing was quantified by measuring intact GC-layer length across sections spanning ±500 μm from the CA3 recording center along the dorsoventral axis 50,51 and expressed as a percentage of age-matched intact hippocampi. Lesioned rats retained no more than 14.3% of dentate tissue bilaterally in the target zone, with CA3 volume remaining intact (Fig. 1G). Beyond near-complete GC loss, MCs and interneurons associated with the GC circuit 28 (Fig. 1H) were immunostained in three additional, unrecorded rats following ipsilateral colchicine injection and were also substantially reduced (Fig. 1I–N). Consistent with this anatomical disruption, residual DG neurons in the recorded rats exhibited spatially diffuse firing with limited spatial information (Fig. 1O), confirming effective disruption of the local DG microcircuit and thereby depriving CA3 of DG input.

Because spatial geometry exerts a dominant influence on hippocampal remapping—which is believed to perform distinct episodic memory functions 52 —rats were first tested in two spatially distinct rooms using similarly shaped enclosures (two-room task). DG lesion modestly attenuates CA3 stability during global remapping without significantly affecting discrimination between rooms 

We next assessed the impact of DG ablation on CA3, beginning with global remapping using the two-room paradigm. As in controls, two of seven lesioned rats exhibited only rate remapping and were therefore excluded from subsequent global remapping analysis (Fig. S2A). The lesion did not alter exploratory behavior (Fig. S2J). In the remaining lesioned animals, active CA3 neurons displayed substantial reorganization of spatial firing patterns between rooms (Fig. 4A). 

Single-cell SCs between rooms remained low in both groups (Fig. 4B, C), with no difference in the fraction of globally remapped neurons (SC < 0.5, chi-square test, control vs. lesion animals, all p > 0.55), indicating that global remapping remained robust overall. In contrast, SCs across repeated trials within the same room were significantly reduced in lesioned animals (Fig. 4D), as previously reported 64 . The compromised stability and preserved discrimination were also evident at the tetrode level: regression lines of mean SC versus normalized tetrode position were well separated for within-room ( p = 0.001) but intersected for between-room ( p = 0.681) comparisons (Fig. 4E, F), with comparable trends at the animal level (Fig. S2K, L). CA3 temporal and spatial coding are partially degraded following DG lesion 

Given that the DG supports CA3 stability across repeated trials, we examined its contribution to CA3 temporal and spatial coding. In control rats, raw local field potential (LFP) power was strongest in pCA3 and declined distally. Following DG lesion, power decreased sharply in pCA3, moderately in mCA3, and minimally in dCA3, yielding comparable levels between pCA3 and dCA3 (Fig. S7A). Because raw LFP power reductions in pCA3 could partly reflect loss of volume-conducted potentials from DG, we decomposed LFPs into periodic (oscillatory) and aperiodic (non-oscillatory) components 65 . After removing the aperiodic component—which captures broadband signals including dentate spikes 66 and other non-oscillatory DG activity— theta oscillations were significantly enhanced in lesioned animals (Fig. 5A). This enhancement argues against a simple loss of volume-conducted DG input and instead suggests altered local CA3 theta generation. Consistent with this, theta phase-locking strength was lowest in pCA3 in controls, and this pCA3-to-dCA3 gradient was significantly attenuated by enhanced phase-locking in pCA3 following lesion (Fig. 5B). This attenuation may reflect disrupted theta phase precession 67,68 (Fig. S7B–D), a process normally driven by DG inputs 69 .

## Discussion 

Hippocampal contextual discrimination is thought to operate through two complementary modes: global remapping, in which coordinated shifts in firing location and rate signal categorical environmental changes, and rate remapping, in which firing rates adjust parametrically to subtler sensory changes while spatial configurations remain stable 49,52 . These modes are believed to engage distinct circuit mechanisms, yet their differential dependence on DG input has remained unclear. In particular, whether DG outputs enhance population

responses to altered context across the CA3 transverse axis—and whether this contribution differs for global versus rate remapping—has not been established. By integrating focal DG lesions with contextual manipulations and spatially resolved recordings of CA3 ensemble dynamics, three key insights emerged. First, DG input modulates temporal and spatial tuning and enhances the magnitude of rate remapping in CA3, while exerting limited influence on global remapping. Here we extended the concept of rate remapping to include object manipulations, as these evoked responses analogous to classical rate remapping: subpopulations of neurons showed rate modulation to altered stimuli, with field emergence and suppression representing extremes of a continuous spectrum of rate change. This extension is justified empirically by the spatial confinement and rate-parametric nature of the responses, though we note that the original definition of rate remapping did not involve objects. Second, DG input maintains the proximo-distal gradient in CA3: impairments in rate-modulated discrimination were most pronounced in pCA3 and attenuated along the transverse axis toward dCA3, paralleling the topography of MF projections. Our findings thus provide causal evidence that the enhanced contextual sensitivity in pCA3, relative to more distal CA3, is related to its preferential DG inputs. Third, GCs and MCs code for complementary aspects of local cue information, with GCs detecting object presence and novelty and MCs signaling object removal. Of these, the object manipulation effects—where DG lesions most dramatically attenuated pCA3 responses to object placement and relocation—provide the most robust and novel evidence for a selective DG contribution to rate-modulated contextual discrimination. Collectively, these findings establish that the DG enables contextual discrimination primarily through rate modulation rather than global reorganization—likely via MF projections to CA3— and highlight how GCs and MCs collaboratively distinguish overlapping inputs prior to their integration into the hippocampal memory network. 

GCs exhibit robust remapping during contextual manipulation 

Prior evaluations of pattern separation with the DG and CA3 have yielded inconsistent results, with some studies describing stronger discriminative signals in GCs 26,27,30,34 and others reporting weaker effects 24,31 . Such inconsistencies likely arise from variation in species,

In the present study, room discrimination reached its maximum when the entire GC population was analyzed (Fig. 2I), primarily attributable to the high room selectivity in active GCs (Fig. S2H), while the decorrelation of spatial firing between rooms remained constant across cell types or regions (Fig. S2I). Consequently, apparent global remapping strength in the DG depends on whether analyses isolate environment-specific subsets. Report of weak remapping 24 has primarily relied on the minority of GCs active across contexts, overlooking the selectively engaged majority 33 ,highlighting how sampling bias can distort estimates of remapping magnitude. 

During rate-based contextual manipulation, GCs exhibited stronger rate modulation than MCs and surpassed the response amplitude observed in pCA3 under most conditions, except for the object removal (Figs. 2, 3), indicating that the DG primarily influences downstream discrimination through rate coding. These findings appear to contradict a previous mouse study that reported modest remapping in GCs but robust modulation of MCs 31 , a result likely influenced by differences in cell-type classification. The present analysis used mean firing rate, burstiness, and sleep-wake ratios within a random-forest framework (Fig. 2B–F), aligning with multiple prior studies 27,30,32,33 . Potential roles of entorhinal inputs to CA3 remapping 

The hippocampus engages two mutually exclusive modes of contextual discrimination 49,52,77 :global remapping is elicited at the level of place-cell identity by substantial afferent divergence—such as changes in spatial configuration 52 or sensory modality 78 —whereas rate remapping is evoked by finer sensory changes (e.g., color 52 , odor 79 , or objects 60 ) without neuron identity shifts. This dual-mode discrimination is often accompanied by distinct activity changes within the entorhinal cortex 80-86 , one synapse upstream (and downstream) of the hippocampal formation 87 .

The medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) contains spatially modulated neurons—including grid, border, and head-direction cells 80,88-90 —that likely refine but do not determine place-cell position selectivity 91-94 . Although shifts in MEC activity can elicit hippocampal remapping 95-99 ,the causal contribution of MEC spatial cell realignment to hippocampal global remapping remains unresolved, given that the latter can occur without MEC or grid-cell input 100,101 .Whether MEC realignment actively drives global remapping or merely accompanies it 81 , MEC inputs alone may suffice to support robust hippocampal discrimination between spatial contexts independently of DG input, as selective DG lesion had negligible impact on CA3 discrimination accuracy during room-switch-induced global remapping (Fig. 4K).

In addition, dCA3 retained the strongest response to object placement and relocation, following selective DG ablation (Figs. 6J; 7H), which likely reflects direct entorhinal projections conveying robust object-selective signals 102-105 .

Limitations of the study 

This study has several limitations. First, the absence of parametrically varied sensory input similarity precludes direct conclusions regarding pattern separation. Consequently, our findings on the importance of DG inputs to CA3 rate coding under binary manipulations are restricted to remapping phenomena rather than pattern separation mechanisms. 

Second, the object-placement and object-relocation tasks provide the strongest support for a selective DG contribution to CA3 rate coding, with pronounced, spatially concentrated discrimination loss following DG lesion. The color-reversal task offers convergent but more limited evidence due to the modest absolute magnitude of rate remapping in controls. While an alternative interpretation—that DG lesions produce comparable, weak effects on both remapping modes primarily limited to pCA3—remains tenable, several observations argue against this view: (i) globally remapped neurons were not reduced in the two-room task, whereas rate-remapped neurons were substantially reduced; (ii) between-room discrimination remained largely intact, whereas rate modulation was attenuated across three tasks; (iii) the relationship between discrimination loss and stability loss was reversed between the global-

Future studies using geometrically morphed environments 34,85 , which evoke stronger, graded rate modulation, will be needed to confirm the role of DG input in rate remapping and to reliably examine its contributions to pattern separation. 

Third, we used colchicine for selective DG ablation 14 , a relatively simple method more feasible in rats compared to modern techniques such as optogenetic and chemogenetic silencing, which require tissue-specific promoters to achieve comparable selectivity 106-108 . However, this approach is irreversible, precluding timely manipulations of DG activity and within-subject designs; thus, our interpretation of DG influence on CA3 network dynamics may be confounded by inter-group variability, structural reorganization, and compensatory plasticity.

Given that GCs exhibited stronger contextual discrimination than MCs in our dataset, adult-born GCs were likely classified as putative GCs. Future investigations should exploit transgenic models that selectively label DG subpopulations to clarify how GCs, MCs, and other subtypes shape CA3 rate coding under diverse contextual demands, advancing mechanistic understanding of pattern separation across the hippocampal circuit.

Permutation test 

This procedure was used to examine differences in CA3 proximo-distal gradients in control and lesioned groups. Tetrode data and recording positions from both groups were pooled and randomly resampled without replacement into two groups. For each iteration, the difference in regression slopes between the two groups was calculated. This process was repeated 10,000 times to generate a null distribution of slope differences. The observed slope difference was then compared against this distribution; the p value was defined as the proportion of permuted values exceeding the observed difference.

Statistics and reproducibility 

Results are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on normality. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used to examine the fixed effects of group (lesion vs. control), CA3 band (pCA3, mCA3, dCA3), and their interaction on ΔSC, ΔRD, and spatial coding metrics, with random intercepts for neurons nested within rats. LMMs with random slopes were evaluated but not used, as they may be unreliable due to unbalanced cell sampling across animals and CA3 bands 130-132 . To account for repeated measurements of spatial bins across CA3 bands and groups, PVCs were analyzed with repeated-measures two-way analysis of variance (rmANOVA) with both bands and groups treated as within-subject factors. Variability of the PVC measure originates from spatial bins. Significant interactions were followed by Kruskal–Wallis (independent) or Friedman (related) tests. Pairwise comparisons used paired or unpaired t-tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum or signed-rank tests as appropriate. Frequency data were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests; linear relationships were assessed with Pearson correlation coefficients. Holm-Bonferroni post hoc tests were used to identify significant differences between pairs. Multiple comparisons between frequency bands were corrected using false discovery rate (FDR). In all cases, the cell (or the spatial bin, for PVC, or the tetrode, for LFP) was the unit of statistical analysis, not individual session-pair comparisons. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed in SPSS v25 (IBM, RRID:SCR_002865), and plots were created in Origin 2024 (OriginLab Inc., Northampton, MA, USA).
