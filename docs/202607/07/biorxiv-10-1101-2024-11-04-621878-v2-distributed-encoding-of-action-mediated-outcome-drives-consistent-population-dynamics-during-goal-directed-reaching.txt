Summary 20 Anticipating the outcomes of actions is central to goal-directed behaviour, but how such 21 expectations are encoded across the brain during ongoing movement remains unclear. To address 22 this, we recorded spiking activity from cortical and subcortical regions using multiple Neuropixels 23 probes simultaneously in head-fixed mice performing a water-reaching task. We found that 24 distributed neural population dynamics were strongly modulated by the availability of reward 25 beyond their encoding of forelimb kinematics. Principal component analysis revealed conserved 26 population dynamics across brain regions and sessions that depended on reach amplitude and 27 reward availability. Generalized linear models revealed outcome-related encoding within region-28 specific population dynamics, in addition to kinematic encoding, with the strongest outcome signals 29 expressed in frontal cortico-thalamic regions. Unsupervised cluster analysis further identified 30 outcome-encoding subpopulations that were enriched in frontal cortices and disproportionally 31 contributed to the shared global latent dynamics. Together, these findings demonstrate that action-32 mediated outcome expectations are encoded in movement-related population dynamics that are 33 shaped by functional clusters of neurons across the brain. 34

Introduction 35 The brain generates movement to achieve specific goals. While the neural correlates of movements 36 have been extensively studied in motor cortex, where neurons encode kinematic variables 1–5 , large-37 scale brain-wide recordings have shown that movement-related activity is distributed across 38 widespread cortical and subcortical areas 6–9 . Similar widespread distributions have been reported 39 for non-motor representations, such as sensory stimuli, choice and outcome 6,10–12 . These internal 40 representations are often studied in temporally isolated task epochs outside the movement period, as 41 it is challenging to dissect them from the strong and widespread activation during movement. Thus, 42 it is unclear to what extent internally generated signals are expressed during ongoing action, and 43 how they interact with motor dynamics at the single-neuron and population level. This question is 44 especially relevant for goal-directed actions, where animals not only process sensorimotor feedback 45 signals, but also must continuously adapt their internal representation of the goal based on the 46 predicted and actual outcome of the action 13 .47 Population level analysis can extract robust latent dynamics despite the diversity of single-neuron 48 responses 14–17 . Such latent dynamics have been observed across brain regions and shown to reflect 49 behaviourally relevant computations 18–21 , such as the representation of motor timing 22 . In motor 50 cortex, distinct latent subspaces separate movement preparation, initiation, and execution 23–26 , while 51 low-dimensional dynamics also correlate with spontaneous facial movements on the scale of 52 seconds 27,28 . However, movement accounted for only 30-50% of variance in lower dimensions and 53 higher dimensions appeared to be unrelated to motor output 27 , suggesting that non-motor signals 54 contribute significantly to population dynamics during movement. This raises the question of what 55 internal representations underlie these dynamics during ongoing movement at millisecond 56 timescale, and how such global population-level activity relates to the functional tuning of 57 individual neurons within different brain regions 29 .58 One potential internal variable shaping ongoing population dynamics during fast goal-directed 59 movements is the anticipation of reward or outcome, as seen in dopamine signals that track reward 60 proximity during locomotion 30 . While outcome-related activity has been well established in 61 classical reward-processing regions such as striatum, prefrontal cortex, and midbrain 31,32 , recent 62 studies have shown outcome-related signals distributed across the brain, including motor cortex and 63 subcortical regions 8,33–35 . However, most of these studies have focused on reward expectation prior 64 to movement 36,37 , or on outcome encoding at the time of reward delivery or during reward 65 consumption 8,38 . These later periods can be confounded by consummatory behaviours such as 66 licking. In addition, outcome expectation can act as an “actionable representation”, a dynamic 67 signal that predicts and guides the ongoing movement 39 , yet this has not been systematically 68 studied across multiple brain regions during the action itself. Thus, it remains unclear how outcome 69 is represented during the ongoing execution of goal-directed actions. 70 To address these questions, we recorded the simultaneous spiking activity of over a thousand 71 neurons across motor and non-motor brain regions during a reaching task in head-fixed mice. We 72 found task-related stereotypical population activity in a latent subspace that was preserved across 73 regions and animals. Using unsupervised clustering and generalized linear modelling, we further 74 identified functional subpopulations modulated by reward availability during movement, 75 independent of reach kinematics, with strong enrichment in frontal thalamocortical regions. Our 76 findings show that functional subpopulations contribute to population dynamics within and across 77 regions that encode outcome-related representations during movement. 78

The animal then immediately reached towards the 84 droplet, grabbed it, and brought the droplet to the mouth (Fig. 1a). We found positively and negatively modulated neurons across all recorded regions. To 125 simultaneously record neuron spiking activity, we inserted two to three Neuropixels probes into the 126 brain (probe-1: premotor cortex corresponding to rostral forelimb area, medial prefrontal cortex, 127 orbitofrontal cortex, olfactory areas; probe-2: primary motor cortex corresponding to caudal 128 forelimb area, dorsolateral striatum; probe-3 in mice 5-7: hippocampus, thalamic nuclei, 129 hypothalamus; Fig. 2b). After spike sorting, quality control and region assignment (Fig. S2, see 130 Methods), we obtained a pooled dataset of 22,036 neurons exhibiting a wide range of activity 131 patterns during cued reaches (Fig. 2c). We included a mean of 1,574 neurons per region (range: 326 132 to 4,129, Fig. 2f). To broadly characterize their task-related modulation, we first focused on cued 133 reaches and determined the relative change in trial-averaged firing rate 500 ms after the cue relative 134 to 250 ms baseline before the cue (modulation index, see Methods). While we observed highly 135 heterogenous and skewed distributions of modulation across all recorded brain regions (Fig. 2d-e, 136 Fig. S3), region-specific differences were evident when analysed at the session-level (Fig. 2f). 137 Sensory, interlaminar and mediodorsal thalamus showed the highest fraction of positively 138 modulated neurons (~ 50%), with lower fractions in non-thalamic cortical and subcortical regions. 139 This effect mostly arises from neurons with intermediate firing rates (1–10 Hz, Fig. S3). Negatively 140 modulated neurons were found across all recorded regions. While the fraction of negatively 141 modulated neurons was lower than that of positively modulated neurons in most regions, the 142 hippocampus exhibited the strongest negative modulation, a pattern that was specific to sparsely 143 firing neurons (<1 Hz, Fig. S3). 144

The latent dynamics are consistent across sessions and animals. To assess the consistency of the 195 population dynamics across sessions, we computed pairwise alignment of the top ten PCs from 196 different recording sessions using both canonical cross correlation analysis (CCA) and Procrustes 197 distance (see Methods) 46,47 . These are well-established approaches to quantify the similarity of 198 neural latent dynamics across different sessions 47–49 . As a baseline control, we applied the same 199 alignment analyses to shuffled data (n = 100 per session), where PCA was performed on the 200 population firing rate in randomly selected time windows of equal length (4.5 s). This control sets a 201 lower bound for expected similarity that arise from task-independent neuron-to-neuron 202 covariance 48 . Latent dynamics were highly consistent across sessions, both within and across 203 animals (Fig. 3a-b). CCA showed highly correlated dynamics within the top canonical components 204 (CC) across sessions (Fig. 3c) and the Procrustes distance in the data was significantly lower 205 compared to control (d within_animal = 0.12 ± 0.12 and d across_animal = 0.17 ± 0.11 vs. d control = 0.83 ± 206 0.03; p < 0.001). These results suggest that population activity across different sessions and animals 207

The latent dynamics are strongly dependent on outcome and movement kinematics. To assess 210 the generalizability of latent subspaces across different reach types, we projected trial-averaged 211 activity from each reach type into PCA subspaces trained on other reach types and quantified the 212 variance explained (Fig. 3d, Fig. S4g). PC1 trained on cued long reaches explained most of the 213 variance both in cued (94%, 20/80 cross-validated) and in spontaneous long reaches performed with 214 reward (72%), but less variance in spontaneous long reaches without reward (63%, Fig. 3d). 215 Consistent with this, when projecting activity into the latent subspace trained on cued long reaches, 216 the peak amplitude of PC1 was comparable between rewarded (hit) cued and spontaneous reaches. 217 In contrast, spontaneous reaches executed in the absence of reward (miss) exhibited a significantly 218 reduced PC1 peak (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, the PC1 peak of rewarded reaches, both cued and 219 spontaneous, was significantly higher than unrewarded reaches in the latent subspace trained on 220 rewarded spontaneous long reaches (Fig. 3e). These results indicate that the dominant latent 221 subspace generalizes across rewarded movements and is strongly dependent on the successful 222 outcome. 223 In addition to outcome-related modulation, we found that PC1 peak amplitude scaled with reach 224 distance, increasing from short to intermediate to long reaches across all reach types (Fig. 3f). 225 Importantly, outcome-related modulation was present across reach distances and explained 26% of 226 PC1 peak variability in a linear mixed effect model that took movement distance, cue and outcome 227 into account (see Methods). Thus, while movement-related activity in dominant latent dynamics has 228 been established across multiple brain regions 6,7,27 , our results indicate that population activity is 229 strongly modulated by outcome beyond pure movement kinematics which we disentangle in the 230 following analyses. 231 

Outcome and kinematics uniquely contribute to population dynamics. Our previous analyses 232 on the trial level showed that the dominant population activity was dependent on outcome during 233 ongoing movement, suggesting an outcome representation that is distinct from the activity related to 234 reward consumption. However, as the animal is continuously executing goal-directed reaching 235 movements during the period of reward presentation until the droplet is consumed, movement 236 kinematics and outcome expectation are strongly correlated. To disentangle these factors, we 237 leveraged the timing variability of reward consumption, the kinematic variability of reach 238 movements and the fact that they were performed in the presence and absence of reward to fit a 239 generalized linear model (GLM). The model predictors included continuous kinematic variables 240 (bar distance, speed), kernels for events (cue, movement onset) and kernels for time periods of 241 reward presence and consumption (Fig. 4a). For each predictor we computed its share of explained 242 variance (R² contribution) of the full model R² on the neural activity that is uniquely attributable to 243 that variable calculated using a leave-one-out approach 50 (see Methods). Predictors showed minimal 244 multicollinearity (VIF range across conditions: 1.00-1.19), meaning that kinematic and outcome-245 related predictors were sufficiently separable and that comparison of relative contributions from 246 partial models is possible. This allowed us to isolate the contribution of specific outcome variables 247 to neural activity after regressing out movement kinematic parameters. 248 We first fitted the GLM on the continuous expression of PCA components that were calculated 249 based on trial-averaged activity of all recorded neurons during cued long reaches. Across sessions, 250 the GLM showed that paw distance to bar was the dominant predictor of PC1 activity (R² = 0.21 251 [0.17 to 0,27], mean [95% confidence interval], Fig. 4b). Crucially, reward presence was the 252 second-strongest predictor with a unique contribution of R² = 0.14 [0.11 to 0.18]. Outcome representation persists in the pre-reward movement window even when movement 299 

trajectories are matched. To further establish that outcome-related activity is present during goal-300 directed movement before reward consumption, and that it is not driven by subtle kinematic 301 differences, we focused on the “approach period”, defined as the period of continuous decrease in 302 paw-to-droplet distance (Fig. 5a). As mentioned above, we found no significant differences between 303 rewarded versus unrewarded reaches with respect to speed and reach direction on a session level 304 (Fig. 1f, p > 0.05 in LME). Despite these similarities between reach types, individual reaches 305 exhibited substantial within-session variability in trajectory length (SD = 3 mm, mean standard 306 deviation across sessions), mean speed (SD = 2.8 cm/s), ML angle (SD = 26°), and DV angle (SD = 307 14°, Fig. 5b). This within-session variability enabled us to perform a trial-by-trial comparison of 308 outcome-related activity between reach trajectories with varying degrees of similarity. To this end, 309 we computed the difference in average global PC1 activity during the approach period between all 310 possible rewarded–unrewarded long-reach pairs within each session and grouped these trial pairs by 311 similarity in kinematic features. Across all kinematic features, PC1 activity was consistently higher 312 for rewarded than unrewarded reaches (Fig. 5c). Importantly, this outcome-dependent activity 313 difference persisted even for reach pairs with high kinematic similarity ( Δkinematic feature ≈ 0) and 314 was significantly greater than in shuffled controls (Fig. 5c). Taken together, these results indicate 315 that outcome encoding in latent population dynamics reflects an internal representation during 316 ongoing movement that cannot be explained by differences in movement kinematics. 317

A distinct subpopulation of neurons encodes outcome-related information during movement. 333 While PCA showed that outcome and kinematics are jointly encoded at the population-level, it does 334 not reveal whether specific subpopulations of neurons contribute to the different behavioural 335 variables. To identify functionally interpretable subpopulations, we applied an unsupervised 336 clustering method (Rastermap) to the continuous firing rate of all neurons recorded during a 337 session 51 . This allowed us to cluster neurons based on shared temporal firing patterns across the 338 entire session, independent of trial structure (Fig. 6a, see Methods). We found that the average 339 activity of one cluster of neurons was highly correlated to the onset of reward consumption (Fig. 340 6a). The average firing rate of this cluster sharply increased upon movement onset and peaked at the 341 time when the paw reached the mouth. In rewarded reaches, this time point corresponds to the start 342 of reward consumption. Notably, this peak firing rate was similar between cued and spontaneous 343 rewarded reaches and markedly reduced in unrewarded long reaches. This outcome-dependent 344 difference in trial-averaged activity was consistently observed across sessions, when selecting for 345 the most outcome-encoding cluster, which we describe below (Fig. 6b). To identify these outcome 346 encoding clusters in each session, we applied the previous GLM approach to each Rastermap 347 cluster to determine which behavioural variables best explained their activity (Fig. 6c, 6d). For each 348 session, we labelled the cluster with the highest R 2 contribution from the reward presence predictor 349 as the top reward presence cluster (RP1). Across sessions, RP1 clusters explained 0.15 [0.11 to 350 0.18] (mean [95% confidence interval]) of the variance related to reward presence (Fig. 6d). 351 Movement kinematics were also encoded, but at a lower level (paw distance: 0.1 [0.07 to 0.17] ,352 speed: 0.07 [0.05 to 0.09]). Contributions from other variables, including reward consumption, were 353 smaller (R² < 0.05). Thus, combining unsupervised clustering of neurons with GLM analysis 354 revealed a distinct neuronal subpopulation that exhibited outcome encoding during ongoing 355 movement that could not be explained by differences in kinematics alone. 356

Discussion 397 Using simultaneous large-scale recordings of neurons across multiple brain regions during a goal-398 directed reaching task (Fig. 1), we show that movement-related latent dynamics extend beyond 399 classical motor cortical and thalamic regions 23,24,52 . These global dynamics were consistent across 400 individual animals and recording sessions (Fig. 3). We found that both latent population dynamics 401 and a subpopulation of neurons encoded for outcome during reaching, before reward consumption, 402 and that this outcome encoding could not be explained by differences in movement kinematics (Fig. 403 4-6). Our findings extend prior reports of widespread movement- and outcome-related 404 activity 6,8,33,35,53 by dissecting a distributed signal that reflects internal outcome expectations during 405 ongoing movement at both single-neuron and population levels. 406 Low-dimensional latent dynamics during reaching were highly conserved across individuals and 407 shared across cortical and subcortical regions. Previous studies have observed movement-related 408 latent dynamics outside of motor cortex 4,8,27,28 , and that they can be preserved across tasks 54 , time 48 ,409 subjects and shared between motor cortex and striatum 49 . We extend these findings by showing that 410 preserved latent dynamics of goal-directed actions across individuals are not restricted to motor 411 cortex and striatum but can be generalized to other non-motor brain regions as well, such as 412 different thalamic nuclei and the hippocampus. The persistence of this low-dimensional manifold 413 across independently sampled neuronal populations suggests that it reflects a robust and global 414 computational mode that is well suited to integrate sensorimotor and internal signals across brain 415 regions 55 .416 A key internal signal during goal-directed movements, such as reaching for a reward, is the 417 continuous change in likelihood of the expected outcome, defined here as the presence or absence 418 of the water droplet. We found a strong modulation of the dominant latent dynamic and of 419 functionally clustered neurons by the presence of reward that existed after controlling for 420 differences in movement kinematics using a GLM (Fig. 4) and a trial-by-trial kinematic similarity 421 analysis (Fig. 5). These results rule out that the outcome encoding is merely a consequence of subtle 422 differences in movement trajectory. Thus, movement-related latent dynamics can be strongly 423 modulated by outcome expectation and are not limited to representing motor preparation or 424 execution 23–25 . This could explain why movement kinematics did not fully explain behavior-related 425 latent dynamics in previous studies 8,23,27,28 .426 Most previous work on outcome encoding has focused on reward prediction signals before 427 movement or outcome-related signals during or after reward consumption 8,24,35,37,38,56–58 . Only few 428 studies have analysed outcome encoding during the execution of a goal-directed movement in 429 individual brain regions 59,60 , and to our knowledge, none have done so using simultaneous large-430 scale recordings across multiple cortical and subcortical areas. We show that anticipated outcome is 431 encoded at the population-level during ongoing movement, and that this signal is widely distributed 432

The rapid fluctuations of this population 433 activity and its peaking at the onset of reward consumption align with previous findings that the 434 temporal evolution of population dynamics is well suited to represent prospective timing 22,61–63 .435 Furthermore, our result of action-dependent outcome expectation resembles goal and reward 436 representation during spatial navigation 64–68 and dopaminergic ramping with reward proximity 30 .437 These findings support theoretical predictions that internal representations are actionable 39 , meaning 438 that they depend on the execution of an action. For example, the outcome expectation will 439 momentarily change based on the ongoing course of goal-directed action, or in our case the 440 kinematics of a reach. Our study supports action-mediated outcome encoding during forelimb 441 reaching. We propose that this reflects a more general computational mechanism across behaviours, 442 in which the brain integrates sensory feedback and motor state to update internal goal 443 representations in real time. 444 A functionally defined subset of outcome-encoding neurons contributes to the shared latent 445 dynamics. Using unsupervised clustering and GLM, we identified a subgroup of RP1 neurons 446 showing mixed encoding for outcome and movement that were distributed across many regions. 447 Large-scale multi-region studies have found neurons encoding for various sensorimotor 8,38,69 or 448 internal variables, such as outcome 8 , decision 6 , vigor 70 , and thirst 12 , across the brain with many 449 exhibiting mixed selectivity 8,15,38 . However, how this heterogeneous tuning at the single neuron 450 level relates to “multiplexed” global latent subspaces 71 and the emerging framework of neural 451 manifolds 55 is not well established, also because simultaneous recording of many brain regions with 452 large neuron populations are still rare. We found that RP1 neurons exhibited high global PC1 453 coefficients suggesting that they may be key drivers of outcome-dependent latent dynamics across 454 the brain. We found significantly higher 459 fractions of RP1 neurons in primary motor, premotor and prefrontal cortex compared to other 460 regions, while RP1 neurons were underrepresented in hippocampus, striatum and olfactory regions 461 (Fig. 6). These results are consistent with the established role of motor and prefrontal cortex in 462 outcome encoding 33,72–74 , extending it into the execution phase of goal-directed movements. The 463 high prevalence of outcome-encoding neurons in frontal cortex suggests that these regions could act 464 as key hubs for distributing outcome-related information to functionally coupled neurons across the 465 brain. A similar principle was observed in a multi-region study 8 , where choice signals could be 466 decoded early from sufficiently large numbers of neurons in many areas, but regions with a higher 467 fraction of selective neurons exhibited the earliest and strongest choice information, implying that 468 they originate from those regions and propagate to connected regions. Analogously, we propose that 469 frontal cortical RP1 neurons could generate and disseminate outcome-related population dynamics 470 across the brain. Future experiments that selectively perturb these RP1 neurons will be essential to 471 determine their causal role in driving outcome-encoding latent dynamics in this multi-regional 472 circuit. 473

Modulation index 776 The modulation index was calculated by the CellExplorer processing module 86 . It represents the 777 relative increase in trial-averaged mean firing rate within 500 ms after the cue relative to the mean 778 baseline firing rate within 250 ms before the cue. The p-value was calculated using a two-sample 779 Kolmogorov-Smirnov test between the firing rate distribution and the post-cue and baseline period 780 across trials. Significantly tuned neurons were identified after Bonferroni correction for multiple 781 comparisons (alpha = 0.05). 782

PCA within-reach type with cross-validation : To assess the reliability of latent dynamics, trials were 794 randomly split into 80% training and 20% test sets. Trial-averaged activity was computed separately 795 for each split, and PCA was performed on the training average. Both training and test trial-averaged 796 population trajectories were projected into the PCA subspace defined by the training data. For each 797 principal component, the resulting time courses from training and test data were then compared by 798 computing their correlation, providing a measure of how reliably each component was reproduced 799 in held-out data. Similarity between trajectories was quantified for each principal component using 800 the squared Pearson correlation coefficient (R²). 801 

PCA projection across reach types: To compare latent dynamics across reach types, PCA was 802 computed on the trial-averaged activity of a reference reach type (“training subspace”), and the 803 activity from other reach types was projected into the same subspace (“projected latent dynamics”). 804 Similarity between the reference and projected trajectories was quantified per component using R². 805 This approach assessed the extent to which population dynamics generalize across reach types. 806 

Alignment and within-session scaling: Because the sign of principal components is arbitrary, 807 component signs were aligned across sessions based on the cued reach. To enable comparison of 808 latent amplitudes across sessions and conditions, projected latent dynamics were scaled within each 809 session by the peak absolute amplitude of the corresponding component in the training PCA 810 subspace, computed within 500 ms post-movement time window. This normalization preserved 811 temporal structure while allowing comparison of relative amplitudes across reach types. 812 

Across-session similarity using CCA and Procrustes distance 813 To compare the similarity of latent dynamics across sessions, we performed separate PCA for each 814 reach type within each session and retained the first 10 principal components. For each reach type, 815 pairwise comparisons were then computed between all session pairs using canonical correlation 816 analysis (CCA) and Procrustes analysis. CCA was used to quantify the correlation between aligned 817 latent trajectories across sessions, and the canonical correlation coefficients were extracted for the 818 first 10 components. Procrustes distance was used to quantify the geometric similarity of latent 819 trajectories across sessions independent of translation, rotation, and scaling. Session pairs were 820 classified as within-animal or across-animal based on the identity of the recorded mouse. As a 821 control, we repeated the same across-session CCA and Procrustes analyses using latent trajectories 822 derived from PCA on randomly sampled 4.5 s segments of neural activity from each session. For 823 each session, 100 random control trajectories were generated, pairwise similarity was computed 824 across sessions, and the resulting CCA coefficients and Procrustes distances were averaged across 825 shuffles. This control provided an estimate of the similarity expected from task-independent 826 population covariance. 827

Generalized linear models 844 We fitted Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) to explore how task variables were encoded in 845 different types of neural activity. The model structure was based on Engelhard et al. (2019) 50 . As 846 the dependent variable, we used the continuous expression of neural activity. To obtain the time-847 varying expression of latent PCA dynamics, we projected each neuron’s continuous and normalized 848 firing rate timeseries onto the previously computed PC/dPC weights by multiplication. We then 849 performed multiple linear regression against a set of behavioural predictors. GLMs were trained on 850 trial-wise data and evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation. A trial covered the period between the 851 reach onset and offset and included up to one second of baseline activity before and after a reach. 852 We used two classes of predictors: 1) continuous predictors: distance of the second digit to the bar, 853 and its speed; 2) event predictors: movement-onset, cue, reward-presence, and reward-consumption. 854 Following Engelhard et al. (2019), event predictors were convolved with spline basis sets generated 855 using the bs package in R (200 ms long splines with 7 degrees of freedom for movement-onset and 856 cue, 1 sec long for the reward-consumption predictor). Finally, reward 858 presence predictor was a step function set to 1 when  droplet likelihood exceeded 0.9. All other 859 model parameters followed Engelhard et al. (2019). Predicted activities were calculated using the 863 full model. 864
