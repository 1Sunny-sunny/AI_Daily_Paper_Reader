## SUMMARY 7

Object manipulation is a dexterous behavior requiring ongoing sensorimotor coordination to establish and maintain a grasp during consecutive movements. Although grasp initiation is known to modulate activity in primary motor cortex (M1), it remains unclear if sustained grasp maintenance is represented in M1, whether it corresponds to an intrinsic M1 state or one that emerges through learning, and how this state contributes to skilled behaviors involving object manipulation. We addressed these questions using two-photon calcium imaging in mice learning a self-initiated, self-paced, reach-grasp-carry task. This task allowed mice to control trial structure and timing, revealing two kinematically similar but functionally distinct carry behaviors: active grasps, in which mice maintained control of a sucrose pellet and received sensory feedback from the held object, and empty grasps, in which mice completed the same carry movement after failing to grasp the pellet. As behavior was refined, empty grasps became less frequent and the M1 representation of active grasp maintenance became more reliable and more distinct from empty grasp. This distinction was evident in single-neuron activity, population activity, and functional connectivity between neurons. These differences were sufficiently robust to support accurate decoding of active and empty grasp state from neural activity and improved encoding of single neuron activity when models incorporated condition-specific functional network coupling. Together, these findings show that learning refines M1 activity into a distinct and reliable representation of active grasp maintenance, revealing sensorimotor integration during object contact as a key neural substrate of learned dexterous behavior.

## INTRODUCTION 24 

Maintaining a grasp during subsequent movements is not a simple motor behavior; it requires ongoing coordination of digit 25 

posture, grip force, and sensorimotor feedback to ensure that the object remains secured. Although grasping has been extensively 26 

studied in primates and, to a lesser extent, in rodents 1–8 , less is known about active grasp maintenance and how it is learned 27 

during motor skills that require sustained object control. To fully comprehend how sensorimotor integration underlying object 28 

manipulation occurs, we must first understand whether the primary motor cortex (M1) represents the sensorimotor state of 29 

sustained grasp–in addition to the kinematics related to grasping–and characterize how these representations emerge. 30 

Although it remains unclear how M1 represents the maintenance of a grip during subsequent movements, M1 is strongly 31 

implicated in grasp control. Inactivation or disruption of M1 broadly impairs skilled forelimb movements 9,10 , and M1 32 

is specifically implicated in controlling hand shape and grip force during grasping 7,8 . More recent work in primates has 33 

characterized separable M1 representations of grasp by grasp type, hand shape, and grip force 4,5 , while also showing that grasp 34 

representations can be higher dimensional than representations of simpler actions such as reaching 6. The visual presence of an 35 

object, somatosensory stimulation of the hand, and tactile sensory expectation like that experienced during active grasping 36 

can also modulate M1 activity and alter population dynamics 11–16 . This body of work establishes that grasping depends on 37 

sensorimotor interactions distributed across cortical circuits. However, most studies of grasp-related activity have focused on 38 

grasp initiation, grip type, or force production during discrete grasp-and-release behaviors. Much less is known about how M1 39 

represents the maintenance of an object-related grip during a subsequent movement, such as carrying an object to the mouth. 40 

It is additionally unknown how this representation of maintained grasp might develop over learning. Learning alters M1 41 

activity patterns, dendritic structure, and functional interactions among neurons 17–22 , and inactivation of M1 can prevent the 42 

acquisition of skilled movements 23 . Examining active grasp maintenance across learning therefore provides an opportunity to 43 

determine how a sensorimotor representation emerges as behavior is refined. This question is especially important for dexterous 44 

skills: whereas some learned non-dexterous behaviors can be executed with reduced M1 engagement, reach-grasp-carry 45

Thus, characterizing how the representation of active 46

Here, we investigated how M1 represents active grasp maintenance as mice learned a self-initiated, self-paced, reach-grasp-49 

carry task. Using two-photon calcium imaging, we tracked M1 activity as animals learned to reach for, grasp, and carry a 50 

sucrose pellet to the mouth. Because mice controlled trial structure and timing, they were free to continue attempts after failing 51 

to secure the pellet. Thus, this task produced two closely related carry behaviors: active grasp, in which mice established and 52 

maintained a grip on the pellet during carry, and empty grasp, in which mice performed a similar carry movement without a 53 

grasped pellet. This naturalistic structure allowed us to separate the sensorimotor state of active grasps from the kinematics of a 54 

more general grasp-carry motion, isolate neural activity associated only with active grasp maintenance, and examine how that 55 

representation changed as behavior was refined. We find that, over learning, active grasp maintenance becomes represented as a 56 

distinct M1 state expressed in single-neuron activity, population activity, and functional network structure, identifying M1 as a 57 

site where object-related sensory feedback is integrated into the control of skilled dexterous behavior. 58 

## RESULTS 59 

Mice perform self-paced reach-grasp-carry task 60 

Head-fixed mice were trained to reach for a sucrose pellet placed on a narrow pedestal. The task was not externally cued; 61 

instead, each trial began at the onset of a self-initiated reach. After a pellet was presented, mice typically made a bout of several 62 

reaches. Each bout ended in either success–defined as grasping the pellet, carrying it to their mouth, and consuming it–or 63 

failure–in which the pellet was either knocked off of the pedestal or dropped during the carry movement. 64 

The pedestal diameter was similar to that of the sucrose pellet, making the pellet easy to dislodge, and consequently, 65 

rendering the task challenging. Because the narrow pedestal limited paw repositioning after contact with the pellet, mice 66 

had to execute an accurate reach and grasp from the outset, requiring precise paw placement and coordinated digit control. 67 

Successful retrieval then required that they maintain their grasp while transporting the pellet to their mouth, further increasing 68 

task complexity. Performance, measured by success rate, plateaued after 2-5 days of training in the majority of mice. 69 

We recorded mouse behavior using two high-speed (200 fps) cameras and employed markerless keypoint tracking to 70 

reconstruct the kinematics of each reach 24,25 . Using keypoint tracking from both cameras, we triangulated keypoint locations 71 

to create a three-dimensional rendering of the mouse forelimb, allowing the tracking of detailed kinematic information (Figure 72 

1A). Tracking of the elbow and wrist reflected activation of larger forelimb muscles involved in larger reaching and carrying 73 

movements, while tracking of the digits allowed for reconstruction of digit kinematics and paw posture during grasping. 74 

We segmented the behavior into reach, grasp, and carry phases. The reach phase primarily involved coordinated activation 75 

of proximal muscle groups to move the forelimb toward the pedestal (Figure 1B), whereas the grasp phase required distal muscle 76 

coordination and the resultant fine digit control to close around the pellet (Figure 1C). The carry phase required coordinated 77 

gross and dexterous control to maintain the grasp while transporting the pellet to the mouth (Figure 1D). Because successful 78 

task completion depended on maintaining the grasp during carry so that the pellet can be consumed, we focused our analyses 79 

on the carry phase of the behavior. 80 

During the carry phase, trials fell into two categories: active grasp and empty grasp. Active grasps were carry instances in 81 

which mice established a grip on the pellet and maintained it during paw transport to the mouth (Figure 1E, Video S1). Empty 82 

grasps occurred when mice proceeded into the carry phase of the behavior without first establishing a successful grasp of the 83 

pellet (Figure 1E, Video S2). 84 

Learning shifts carry behavior from empty to active grasp 85 

Active and empty grasp trials had nearly indistinguishable large-scale kinematics, with negligible differences in the correlation 86 

between keypoint trajectories within or across carry categories (Figure 1F, mean correlation difference = 0.006). Consistent 87 

with this similarity, decoding of active or empty trial category from gross kinematics performed only slightly above chance 88 

(mean accuracy = 0.56 ± 0.02 ; n=7 mice, Figure 1G). Despite the similarity in gross kinematics, the two trial types differed 89 

fundamentally in potential behavioral outcome. While active grasps did not always lead to successful pellet retrieval and 90 

consumption, with drops occurring in the transition between single paw carry and dual paw eating behaviors, they retain 91 

the possibility of sucrose reward throughout the trial. In contrast, empty grasps could never result in reward. By comparing 92 

behaviors that are kinematically similar but functionally distinct–carry movements with the presence or absence of an active 93 

grasp–we evaluated whether M1 represents the complete sensorimotor state of active grasp maintenance or the movement 94 

trajectory alone. 95 

Empty grasps decrease in prevalence over learning, with the exact timing of the decrease varying across animals. On 96 

average, the number of empty grasp trials peaked at training day 3 (”empty grasp peak”, Figure 2A) and ranged between 97 

training days 1-4 across individual mice (Figure 2C). The number of empty grasp trials then fell, and the proportion of active 98

Behaviorally, the empty grasp peak and active-empty crossover defined the bounds of a ”grasp refinement window” during 103

empty grasp peak, animals were performing many empty grasp trials and any given carry instance was considerably more likely 105 

to be an empty grasp than an active grasp (Figure 2A, C, E). During the grasp refinement window, the number of empty carries 106 

decreased because mice were less likely to complete the carry movement after failing to grasp the pellet, instead retracting their 107 

paw to the handlebar, which allowed for the delivery of a new pellet. The crossover point marked the end of the window, when 108 

animals became more likely to perform active grasps than empty grasps (Figure 2B-C, E). 109 

This refinement affected the number of successful trials without producing a comparably large change in success rate. On 110 

average across mice, success rate plateaued by training day 3, whereas the number of successful trials continued to rise over 111 

the grasp refinement window and did not plateau until training day 6 (Figure 2D). This distinction arises because success rate 112 

measures the fraction of pellet presentations that result in success and therefore does not capture repeated empty grasps that 113 

occur after a pellet has already been dislodged from the pedestal. Increasing the total number of successful trials required mice 114 

to refine their behavior so that they aborted trials after the reward was no longer available, thereby allowing more attempts with 115 

the possibility of success. Improved reward consumption in this reach–grasp–carry task therefore required multiple behavioral 116 

refinements, including recognition of pellet presence and improvement of grasp posture and grip force. Correspondingly, 117 

decoding trial category from fine-scale paw kinematics, including aperture, splay, and orientation, performs modestly but 118 

significantly better than chance (mean accuracy = 0.64 ± 0.03 ; one sided t-test, p=.0029, Figure 1G). The fine kinematic 119 

decoder indicates that active grasp maintenance in part corresponds to subtle paw postural changes as compared to empty grasp. 120 

Together, these results suggest that learning may depend in part on increasing differentiation between empty or improper grasp 121 

postures and active grasp maintenance, potentially through improved use of sensory feedback during grasp and carry. 122 

Active grasp maintenance modulates population and single cell activity 123 

Behavioral refinements over learning could be supported by differences in neural activity encoding the sensorimotor changes 124 

between active and empty grasp. We next sought to characterize these neural representations underlying the distinction between 125 

active and empty grasp. To obtain sufficient trial counts for robust decoding in all mice, we pooled trials across the grasp 126 

refinement window and restricted analyses to simultaneously recorded cells that were present on all days (Figure 2D; training 127 

days 3-6 for mice 2-7, training days 2-6 for mouse 1). 128 

Despite their kinematic and behavioral similarity, active and empty grasp trials were clearly differentiated by population 129 

and single cell activity. We first tested whether active and empty grasps could be decoded from single-neuron activity. For 130 

each neuron, we trained two support vector machine (SVM) models: one using a single feature, the time-averaged firing rate 131 

across the trial, and a second using eight features, each corresponding to the instantaneous firing rate in a single bin. A neuron 132 

was classified as ’decodable’ if at least one model achieved cross-validated accuracy with 95% confidence interval above 133 

chance. Just under half of all neurons (48.6%, n=1209/2486) were decodable (Figure 3D). Decodable neurons exhibited diverse 134 

response profiles. Some showed higher firing rates during active grasp than empty grasp (n=522, example in Figure 3A) and 135 

some showed lower firing rates during active grasp (n=433, example in Figure 3B). A smaller subset was decodable only when 136 

time varying dynamics were included in the instantaneous firing-rate model (n=204, example in Figure 3C). This diversity, 137 

including neurons positively modulated by one condition or the other and neurons with temporally structured differential 138 

activity, indicates robust single cell encoding of active grasp maintenance. 139 

Trial type could also be decoded from the full population activity with high accuracy (mean accuracy = 0.72 ±0.04; n=7 140 

mice) (Figure 3E). This performance was significantly higher than decoding from gross, fine, or combined kinematics (Figures 141 

3E, S1A), indicating that neural activity can access information that distinguishes between active and empty grasp beyond 142 

the paw position, shape, and orientation included in kinematic decoders. We then asked how different neural subpopulations 143 

contributed to population decoding. While non-decodable neurons could not individually distinguish between active and empty 144 

grasp with linear decoding, it is possible that they represent active-empty grasp differences at a population level. Although 145 

decodable neurons account for most of the population decoding (decoding accuracy similar to that of the full population; 146 

paired t-test, p = .773 ), non-decodable subpopulations supported limited but above-chance decoding of trial type in some mice 147 

(Figure 3E). Looking at the mutual information between single neural activity and trial category, we again see that, although 148 

significantly lower than in decodable neurons (Wilcoxon rank sum, p < 10 −16 ), non-decodable neurons contain a non-zero 149 

amount of information about active-empty grasp distinctions. The presence of this information, along with high fidelity 150 

decoding from complete population activity in M1, indicates robust encoding of active grasp maintenance at the population 151 

level, concentrated in decodable sub-populations but also spread across non-decodable cells in M1. 152

Functional networks are distinct during active and empty grasp 170 

Because reliable shifts in activation timing could alter the correlation structures among simultaneously active neurons, we next 171 

examined whether active grasp maintenance was associated with changes in functional network structure. This analysis allowed 172 

us to test whether active and empty grasps differed not only in the timing of individual neurons, but also in the broader pattern 173 

of pairwise correlations across the recorded population. To quantify these relationships, we constructed condition-specific 174 

functional connectivity matrices for each mouse. For each condition, active grasp or empty grasp, we generated an NxN matrix, 175 

in which entry ( i, j ) in the matrix k was the zero-lag Pearson correlation between simultaneously recorded neurons i and j176 

over all trials and time points of condition k. These matrices, or functional networks (FNs), are signed, weighted, undirected 177 

graphs describing pairwise relationships, or functional connections (FCs), among simultaneously recorded neurons during a 178 

given condition. The sign of each functional connection indicates whether the correlation was positive or negative, whereas the 179 

magnitude indicates the statistical reliability of the correlation. To identify significant functional connections, we compared 180 

each observed Pearson correlation to a null distribution generated for the same neuron pair during the same condition using 181 

rate- and temporal-dynamics-matched null models for each condition. A functional connection between neurons (i, j) during 182 

condition k was retained if the magnitude of its correlation exceeded the corresponding null distribution with a p-value falling 183 

below a 0.05 significance threshold after Bonferroni correction for the number of pairwise interactions per mouse. Connections 184 

that did not pass this significance threshold for condition k were considered null and set to zero in FN k. These null models 185 

preserve both the average firing rate and carry PETHs for each neuron, so significant functional connections are those whose 186 

magnitudes are greater than expected by both rate and carry-related timing dynamics. 187 

Using this metric, we found that decodable neurons were more highly connected in both functional networks, having 188 

a higher average number of non-zero functional connections per neuron across either network (121.00 ±0.96) compared to 189 

non-decodable cells (115.15 ±0.95). We also found that the active grasp FN was sparser than the empty grasp FN. Active 190 

grasp networks contained non-zero connections in 28.17 ±0.45% of all possible interactions, while empty grasp networks 191 

contained non-zero connections in 30.27 ±0.72% of all possible interactions (example functional networks in Figure S2A). 192 

Although active grasp networks were sparser, the pairwise relationships that remained were stronger than those in empty grasp 193 

networks. Active grasp networks had higher magnitude FCs than empty grasp networks for both positively and negatively signed 194 

connections (median active grasp FC magnitude = 0.128; median empty grasp FC magnitude = 0.101; Wilcoxon rank-sum, 195 

p < 10 −16 ; Figures 4A, S2B-C). Comparison with rate- and spatiotemporal activity-matched null models indicated that FC 196 

magnitude differences between conditions could not be explained by firing rate differences alone (Figure S2D; p < 10 −16 ). 197 

This shift in FC magnitude, unexplained by changes in single cell properties like rate and temporal patterning, indicates that in 198 

addition to single cells and populations, functional networks distinguish between active and empty grasp. 199

coupling terms within a single model. Thus, although shared functional connections contribute to encoding performance, the 252 

overall network structure changes sufficiently between trial types that condition-specific coupling provides a better account of 253

To verify that matched, condition-specific encoding performance relied on condition-specific connectivity, rather than 255

perturbations. We first compared the matched FN-coupling model with an aggregate population model, in which the coupling 257 

term was replaced by the summed activity of all simultaneously recorded neurons, excluding the neuron being predicted. This 258 

summed-population model performed poorly, with prediction accuracy near chance and significantly lower than the matched 259 

FN-coupling model (Figure 5C, paired t-test, p < 10 −16 ). We then tested whether the specific structure of the functional 260 

network was necessary by permuting the FN weights and connectivity. In one control, FC weights were randomly re-assigned 261 

across all possible neuron pairs, disrupting both functional network topology and the magnitude of connections. This model 262 

performed at chance and significantly worse than the matched FN-coupling model (Figure 5C, paired t-test, p < 10 −16 ). 263 

In a second control, weights were permuted only among existing FCs, preserving network topology while disrupting the 264 

magnitude and sign assigned to each coupling. This model performed slightly above chance, but still significantly lower than 265 

the unpermuted matched FN-coupling model (Figure 5C, paired t-test p < 10 −16 ). Each control model also performed worse 266 

than the mismatched coupling model (paired t-tests, all p < 10 −16 ), indicating that the limited shared structure between active 267 

and empty grasp networks supports more predictive power than would be expected if the network were entirely unrelated across 268 

conditions. Together, these controls show that encoding performance depends on the specific FN-derived coupling structure, 269 

rather than simply on summed population activity or arbitrary coupling among recorded neurons. Because each model included 270 

the same condition-matched kinematic features, the selective improvement produced by condition-specific FC weights and 271 

connectivity indicates that active and empty grasp functional network structure contributes to single-neuron encoding beyond 272 

what can be explained by differences in kinematics alone. 273

## DISCUSSION 296 

Here, we show that M1 activity distinguishes active grasp maintenance from kinematically similar empty grasp carries, 297 

implicating M1 in encoding sensory information in addition to motor information. Active and empty grasp representations 298 

were distinct at the level of single-neurons, populations, and functional network structure. These distinctions became more 299 

stable and increasingly separable as animals learned to prefer active grasps over empty grasps in a self-paced reach-grasp-carry 300 

task. These results suggest that M1 may be involved in sensorimotor integration during behavior that requires it. Additionally, 301 

learning-related changes in M1 are not restricted to those underlying refined movement kinematics. Rather, our findings suggest 302 

that M1 activity is reorganized during learning to support the tactile-motor coordination required during an actively maintained 303 

grasp. 304 

These insights were enabled by a new naturalistic behavioral framework in which mice determined the timing and structure 305 

of their own reach-grasp-carry attempts. Because animals could continue into the carry phase after failing to secure the pellet, 306 

the task generated empty grasps as naturally occurring ’catch trials’ in the process of learning. These movements had strong 307

kinematic similarities to active grasp trials. Three dimensional paw and forelimb trajectories were similarly correlated across 308 

and within trial categories, and kinematic features supported only modest decoding of active versus empty grasp. Thus, the 309 

existence of these trials allowed us to distinguish between neural representations of kinematics, which were largely shared 310 

between active and empty grasps, and sensorimotor state, which differed between active grasp–a condition with a pellet and 311 

requiring continued incorporation of tactile information to maintain the grasp–and empty grasp–a condition lacking the presence 312 

of the pellet entirely. 313 

Distinctions between representations of active and empty grasp also cannot be attributed purely to reward anticipation, 314 

successful trial completion, or pellet consumption, which are all known to modulate M1 activity 22,29–31 . To explicitly exclude 315 

any instances of sucrose consumption, we considered a limited set of trial time points that did not include the transition between 316 

carry movements and eating. Additionally, the separation between active and empty grasp is not perfectly predictive of reward. 317 

While empty grasps could not lead to reward because the pellet had not been secured, active grasps also did not always result in 318 

pellet consumption because mice could still drop the pellet as they began attempting to eat it. Furthermore, mice frequently 319 

made mouth-directed movements and licked at their paws following empty grasps–behavior consistent with reward expectation 320 

despite the lack of pellet presence. These observations argue against a simple account in which active-empty representational 321 

differences reflect only task success or expected reward. The most parsimonious interpretation is that the distinction in M1 322 

activity reflects whether the paw is actively engaged with and maintaining control of the pellet during carry, potentially through 323 

incorporation of tactile, proprioceptive, grip force, and other signals associated with object contact and grasp maintenance. 324 

A growing body of work supports the view that motor and somatosensory cortices operate as an integrated sensorimotor 325 

system rather than as discrete functional units. Sensory and motor cortical areas are highly interconnected 32–34 , providing an 326 

anatomical substrate for rapid communication between regions. Consistent with this organization, sensorimotor information is 327 

distributed across both primary somatosensory cortex (S1) and M1 35,36 . In mice, detailed kinematic representations can be 328 

found in S1 in addition to M1 37 , paw photostimulation evokes M1 responses 12 , and proprioceptive signals have been located 329 

in M1 38,39 . In primates, both direct tactile stimulation of the hand and the expectation of tactile signals evoke M1 responses 330 

and can alter M1 population dynamics 13–16 . Our findings extend this view by showing that, during naturalistic, dexterous 331 

behavior, mouse M1 activity distinguishes between kinematically similar but functionally distinct movements with or without 332 

a grasped object. These distinctions, unexplained by subtle kinematic differences between trial categories, implicate M1 in 333 

encoding the tactile or proprioceptive signals supporting a successfully maintained grasp. Although we do not directly identify 334 

the sensory signals that underlie this differentiation, the existence of a robust distinction between active and empty grasp 335 

representations supports a view of M1 as part of a sensorimotor circuit that represents behaviorally relevant variables associated 336 

with maintaining an object-related grip. 337 

Our task design also allowed us to examine changes in neural activity linked to grasp-specific learning. Because training 338 

sessions lasted a fixed amount of time, mice faced a natural trade-off between time spent completing unrewardable empty 339 

grasps and the number of pellet presentations they could attempt. Thus, animals could increase the number of rewards without 340 

requiring a corresponding increase in success rate by learning to perform fewer empty grasps. The grasp refinement window–a 341 

period where animals learned to preferentially abort trials if the specific sensorimotor state associated with an active grasp was 342 

not achieved–is unique to a self-paced task design. When aligning to each animal’s specific refinement window, we found 343 

that representations of active grasp became more stable and distinct from representations of empty grasp over time, mirroring 344 

behavioral changes. Although these data do not establish whether this neural refinement is required for behavioral improvement, 345 

the alignment between the active-empty crossover, increased active grasp representational stability, and greater active-empty 346 

discriminability suggests that learning refines M1 activity around the sensorimotor distinctions between empty grasp and active 347 

grasp maintenance. 348 

Maintaining an object during carry is not reducible to controlling gross forelimb trajectory alone. It requires fine digit 349 

control, appropriate exertion of grip force, and sensorimotor monitoring of the paw-object interaction. The active-empty grasp 350 

contrast isolates a behaviorally meaningful M1 state associated with grasp maintenance, characterized by single cell modulation, 351 

population-level decodability, and functional network changes, that is not well explained by measured kinematics alone. The 352 

stabilization and increased discriminability of this state paralleled the reduction in empty grasps during training, suggesting 353 

that learning increasingly structures M1 population activity around the demands of maintaining an object-related grip. These 354 

findings show that, during naturalistic skilled dexterous behavior, M1 activity differs according to whether a movement is 355 

coupled to an actively maintained grasp. This implicates M1 in sensorimotor, rather than purely motor, computations. More 356 

broadly, the results emphasize that successful object manipulation depends not only on generating appropriate movements, but 357 

also on integrating motor output with information about whether control of the object is maintained. 358

Behavioral Training 400 

Food restriction, habituation, and shaping 401 

At least one week after headplate and cranial window placement, mice began food restriction, in which they received a limited 402 

amount of pellet food, titrated to maintain their weight between 85% and 90% of their baseline weight. Food restriction and 403 

habituation began at the same time. Mice were single housed throughout food restriction, habituation, and training. Mice were 404 

habituated to the researcher and to head fixation over at least five days, with head-fixation duration gradually increased before 405 

shaping began. During habituation, mice were briefly head-fixed in the two-photon microscope to assess GCaMP6f expression. 406 

Only mice with sufficient GCaMP6f expression proceeded to behavioral training. 407 

During shaping, mice were head-fixed inside the 2-photon microscopy rig. On the first day, sucrose pellets were presented 408 

on rotating pedestals positioned at licking height. On the second day, pellets were placed in a trough first positioned at licking 409 

height, then progressively lowered to a height at which the pellet could be reached with the contralateral forelimb (relative to 410 

recording location), but not licked. If the animal began reaching with its ipsilateral forelimb, the handlebar for the ipsilateral 411 

side was progressively raised to block ipsilateral and encourage contralateral reaching. To advance from from shaping to 412 

training, mice had to successfully reach for and consume at least 15 pellets during a thirty minute recording session. Shaping 413 

was performed daily until this criterion was met. The following day would become the first training day. Mice underwent no 414 

more than four days of shaping, although some advanced to training after only two days. Imaging took place every day of 415 

shaping. 416 

Training 417 

During training, sucrose pellets were presented one at a time on rotating delivery pedestals positioned directly in front of the 418 

mouse (head-fixed adaptation of pedestal-based reach-to-grasp tasks in de Laittre and MacLean 40 and Becker, Calame et al 41 ). 419 

A new pellet was delivered after the previous pellet was removed, either through successful retrieval or by being knocked off 420 

the pedestal, and the mouse reset to placement of their paws on the handlebar. If the mouse did not initiate a reach for more 421 

than 30 seconds, the pedestal was refreshed with a new pellet. Each session consisted of 12 behavioral segments, each with 2.5 422 

minutes of pellet availability followed by a 1–2 minute break during which no new pellets were delivered. This yielded 30 423 

minutes of recorded behavior and neural activity per day. Imaging was performed on every training day. 424 

Behavioral video recording and kinematic reconstruction 425 

Videography and keypoint extraction 426 

High-speed infrared video of reaching behavior was acquired at 200 Hz using two FLIR Blackfly S 16S2C cameras. One 427 

camera was positioned approximately 30° from head-on, offset to the mouse’s right and angled 20° from above, to capture both 428 

forelimbs and paws. The second camera was positioned approximately 80° from head-on, also offset to the mouse’s right, to 429 

provide a detailed view of the right forelimb. Kinematic features were extracted from the video data using DeepLabCut 24,25 .430 

3D keypoint triangulation 431 

A 0-lag, 10th order, 15Hz low pass butterworth filter was applied on all keypoints using the scipy function filtfilt . Following 432 

filtering, time points with high uncertainty (likelihood <0.4) were removed. Linear interpolation using the numpy interp 433 

function was completed over a maximum of 20 frames of high uncertainty data, whose likelihood was then manually set to 434 

> 0.4. 3D DeepLabCut was used to triangulate the two sets of keypoint data with a likelihood threshold of 0.4 25 .435 

Calculating kinematic variables of interest 436 

The paw centroid was calculated as the mean of the four keypoints located at the digit 2 metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, digit 437 

3 MCP joint, and the radial and ulnar protrusions of the wrist, with their mean position representing the approximate center of 438 

the palm. Splay was the distance between the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints of digits 2 and 3. Aperture was calculated 439 

as the distance between the average of keypoints at digits 2 and 3 PIP joints and the average of keypoints at the radius and ulnar 440 

protrusions of the wrist. Orientation was the y component of the normal vector to the plane of the palm (defined by keypoints at 441 

the digit 2 MCP joint, digit 3 MCP joint, and average of the two wrist keypoints), with the y position of the wrist subtracted 442 

such that all negative orientation values represented palm-down orientations while positive values represented palm-up paw 443 

orientations. 444 

Segmenting behaviors 445

first segmented into candidate behavioral events based on peaks in the wrist keypoint in the y direction of the most lateral 447 

camera using the scipy function find peaks . A random forest classifier using the scikit-learn class RandomForestClassifier 448 

was then trained on a subset of human curated data, which included all 2D keypoints from both camera angles, to classify 449 

these candidate events into reach, grasp, carry, fidget (when the animal moved its paw without reaching towards the pellet), 450 

non-movement (spurious events, related to keypoint jitter or obscured keypoints), and food manipulation. After the classifier 451

Drop timing 466

Success classification and success rate calculation 468 

For each of the mice in each of the days, all instances of grasp and carry were manually classified as a success (defined as 469 

resulting in consumption of the pellet by the end of the behavior) or fail (no pellet consumption). Failures included instances 470 

where the pellet was no longer present. 471 

Success rate was calculated for each mouse on each training day as the total number of successes divided by the total 472 

number of pellet presentations. To determine the number of pellet presentations, pedestal rotation times were tracked during all 473 

recording sessions. For each pedestal rotation time, DLC pellet keypoint likelihoods from both camera angles were used to 474 

determine if the pellet was present. As the pedestals were narrow, pellets sometimes fell off the pedestal before the pedestal 475 

rotated into place in front of the mouse. A pellet was determined to be present if the pellet likelihood was > 0.9 in keypoints 476 

from both camera angles for at least 125ms in the time from 50ms preceding to 200ms following the pedestal stopping in front 477 

of the mouse. This amount of time was chosen after observing videos to account for spontaneous pellet dislodgement but not 478 

animal-related task behavior. 479

Quantification and statistical analysis 502 

Peri-event time histograms (PETHs) 503 

PETHs were calculated from neural activity aligned to paw rotation during the carry. For visualization, PETHs are shown from 504 

150 ms before to 400 ms after carry onset. Quantitative analyses were restricted to 0–250 ms after carry onset to avoid activity 505 

preceding grasp establishment or following the onset of reward consumption. For population visualizations, each cell’s PETH 506 

was normalized within the -150-400ms window. 507 

To compare PETHs within pairs of days and between active and empty grasps, trials were randomly split in half within 508 

each carry category. From each trial split, a PETH was calculated for each cell (for a total of 4 PETHs per cell, two per carry 509 

category). For within-category comparisons, a Pearson correlation was computed between two PETHs of that condition. For 510 

across-category comparisons, a Pearson correlation was computed between one PETH from either condition. Splits were 511 

repeated 1000 times, then correlations were averaged per cell and comparison type across repeats. 512 

To compare PETHs across days, each cell’s PETH was calculated separately from all trials of the same type for each day. 513 

Pearson correlations were computed between the same cell’s PETHs on all pairs of consecutive days (e.g. training days 1 and 2, 514 

then 2 and 3). The mean correlation across cells for each pair of days was used as the measure of cross-day representational 515 

similarity. 516 

Determination of decodable cells 517 

A linear kernel support vector machine (SVM) was used to predict trial category from single-neuron activity. Two models were 518 

trained for each neuron: a single feature model using mean activity across the full trial duration, and an eight feature model, 519 

using instantaneous inferred spike count of a given neuron at 8 time points in a trial, which corresponds to approximately 240 520 

ms at our 30 Hz imaging rate. Models were trained using a 90/10 train/test split and downsampling for active/empty class 521 

balancing. Neurons were classified as decodable if the 10-fold cross-validated accuracy, including the 95% confidence interval, 522 

exceeded chance for either model. Decodable neurons were further classified as time-dependent if only the eight-feature model 523 

exceeded chance. They were classified as increased or decreased if they were decodable and showed higher or lower mean 524 

activity, respectively, during active grasp trials compared with empty grasp trials. 525 

Kinematics decoder 526 

Three linear kernel SVMs were trained to predict trial category from the 3D kinematics in each trial. For the gross kinematic 527 

decoder, the predictors were the instantaneous x, y, and z position of the paw (3 kinematic parameters at 8 time points each for 528 

a total of 24 features). For the fine kinematic decoder, the predictors were the splay, aperture, and orientation at each timepoint 529 

in the trial (3 kinematic parameters at 8 time points each for a total of 24 features). For the full model, all six fine and gross 530 

kinematic parameters were used (total of 48 features). Any trials where any of the six kinematic parameters contained missing 531 

data (from the paw being obscured relative to the cameras) were excluded. Models were trained using a 90/10 train/test split 532 

and downsampling for class balancing. Scikit-learn’s GridSearchCV function was used on training data to select between 533 

L1 and L2 regularization and fit the regularization parameter for each fold. Accuracies shown are 10-Fold cross-validated 534 

accuracies on balanced data. 535 

Neural population decoder 536 

A linear kernel SVM with L1 regularization was trained to predict trial category from population activity of each mouse. The 537 

predictors were the average firing rate across all time points in the given trial for each simultaneously recorded neuron. Models 538 

were trained using a 90/10 train/test split and downsampling for class balancing. Scikit-learn’s GridSearchCV function was 539 

used on training data to fit the regularization parameter for each fold. Accuracies shown are 10-Fold cross-validated accuracies 540 

on balanced data. 541 

For decoder training over days, pairs of days are used to provide a sufficiently high trial count for each model and accuracies 542 

shown are the 10-Fold cross-validated accuracies. 543 

Mutual Information between Neurons and Carry Type 544 

Using the Java Information Dynamics Toolkit 45 , we estimated mutual information between single cell neuronal activity and 545 

trial type with a continuous-discrete Kraskov estimator 46 . Estimations were repeated 100 times, then the average of the repeated 546 

estimates was taken as the mutual information value. 547 

Functional networks calculation and determination of significant correlations 548 

Functional connections were defined as the Pearson correlation between each pair of neurons. To identify correlations that 549 

could not be explained trivially by co-varying firing rates, we constructed a firing-rate and trial-timing null model of functional 550 

connectivity using weighted random resampling of neural activity. This resampling strictly preserved each neuron’s overall 551 

firing rate across trials, and weights were based off of carry PETHs such that resampled data closely approximated trial-averaged 552 

time-varying activity dynamics. 553

Condition-Specific Encoding Models 582 

Ridge regression was used to model inferred spike rates. All linear models were fit using scikit-learn’s Ridge class. For the full 583 

model, the ridge parameter was selected with sckit-learn’s GridSearchCV function over a range of ridge parameters between 0 584 

and the total number of time points. All models used 2-Fold cross validation with 10 repeats for a total of 20 cross validation 585 

folds with matched train/test splits across models. Time points where any kinematic parameters contained missing data (from 586 

the paw being obscured relative to the cameras) were excluded from training and testing datasets. Cross-validated r2 values are 587 

reported as the median across folds. Splits were performed by trial to prevent any train-test data leakage from highly correlated 588 

time points within the same trial. Cross validation folds were stratified by the predicted neuron’s firing rate and day labels, 589 

ensuring that training and test sets had similar average firing rates and balanced trial distributions across pooled days. 590

Combined-Condition Encoding Models 615 

To determine how condition-specific functional networks contribute to encoding, we compared the condition-specific full model 616 

with a condition-combined model. In the condition-combined model, the coupling term was decomposed into three terms: 617 

one for functional connections shared across active and empty grasp trials, and one each for functional connections unique 618 

to a condition. Sign-flipped functional connections were not included. The shared-correlation coupling term used weights 619 

calculated from both active and empty grasp trials in the training data. In contrast, the condition-unique coupling terms used 620 

weights calculated only from the corresponding trial category within the training set. Because this model included more features 621 

than the condition-split model, its ridge parameter was selected using GridSearchCV , with internal train-test splits within the 622 

training data to find an optimal value for the ridge parameter. 623 

For comparisons to the condition-specific encoding models, the condition-specific encoding models’ cross-validated r2 is 624 

averaged between the two conditions. This averaged cross-validated r2 is then compared to the median r2 across folds in the 625 

combined-condition encoding model for each neuron. 626 

Unweighted Input Encoding Models 627 

To determine the extent of the contribution of functional network weights on encoding (rather than just information from other 628 

neurons), we compared the matched-condition full model to an unweighted model, which uses the unweighted sum of the 629 

activity of all other neurons as the coupling term. 630 

Permuted Functional Network Encoding Models 631 

To assess whether encoding depends on the identities of functional connections, rather than only on their weights, we compared 632 

the matched-condition full model with permuted functional network models. We used two permutation controls: weight-and-633 

connectivity permutations and weight-only permutations. In the weight-and-connectivity permutation, Pearson correlations 634 

calculated from the training data were randomly reassigned to non-diagonal entries of the connectivity matrix, regardless of 635 

whether that neuron pair had a significant correlation in the observed network. This permutation disrupted both connection 636 

identity and network topology. In the weight-only permutation, the functional network topology was preserved, but the 637 

correlation weights, including magnitude and sign, were permuted across existing functional connections. For each model, 638 

permutations were repeated 100 times per cross-validation fold. Model performance was averaged across permutations within 639 

each fold and then compared with the matched-condition full model. 640

Determination of Drop-Modulated Cells 667 

Drop modulated cells were determined by performing a one way ANOVA between the pre- and post-drop time points (125ms 668

after Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction. 673
