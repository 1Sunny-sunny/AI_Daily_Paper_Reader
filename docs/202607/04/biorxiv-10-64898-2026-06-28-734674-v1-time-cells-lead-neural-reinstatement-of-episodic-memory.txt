# Abstract 6

The episodic memory system provides humans with a unique ability to form and retrieve rich and detailed 7

memories. This capacity requires representing temporal context at encoding and recovering it at retrieval 8

to drive successful recall. Time cells and ramping cells in human medial temporal cortex have been 9

proposed as substrates of temporal context, but whether they participate in context recovery during retrieval 10 

remains unknown. Using microelectrode recordings from neurosurgical patients across two classical episodic 11 

paradigms, free recall and serial reconstruction, we identify time sensitive neurons and demonstrate that 12 

these neuron populations participate in neuronal assemblies and organized sequences on gamma and 13 

theta time scales respectively. Consistent with recovery of temporal context predicted by behavioral models 14 

of episodic processing, time cell firing precedes the broader assembly population and initiates sequential 15 

activation retrieval. These properties predict contextually-mediated recall behavior. We also identify phase 16 

coding by complementary populations as convergent mechanisms whose recruitment depends on task 17 

demands. Finally, we demonstrate the emergence of context–sensitive neurons in a naturalistic viewing 18 

dataset lacking the predictable temporal scaffold of canonical assays. These findings identify cellular 19 

mechanisms by which the human medial temporal cortex represents temporal context during episodic 20 

encoding and recovers it during retrieval. 21 

# Introduction 22 

Episodic memory requires the brain to represent temporal context as events unfold and to recover that context at retrieval 23 

to drive successful recall. According to influential theoretical accounts, a shifting representation of temporal context 24 

binds to item information at encoding, and reinstatement of that context at retrieval cues the items associated with 25 

each contextual state 1–3 . Subjects cluster their successive recalls by temporal proximity at encoding 4,5 , and successful 26 

retrieval tracks reinstatement of the context present at encoding 6,7 . It remains to be established how recovery of this 27 

context is implemented at the cellular level, and whether temporal context representation and recovery recruit different 28 

cellular mechanisms depending on task structure. To address these questions, we compared two episodic memory 29 

tasks, free recall and serial reconstruction, whose encoding periods are well-matched but differ in the functional role 30 

of temporal context. Free recall requires subjects to internally generate temporal context to cue item retrieval. Serial 31 

reconstruction provides item information and an external positional scaffold at retrieval, and the response order can in 32 

principle be supported by associative chaining between items without an internally generated temporal context 8,9 .33

Time cells and ramping cells have been proposed as cellular substrates of temporal context representation. Time cells 34 

fire at consistent moments within a structured time interval 10 and have been documented in rodent hippocampus and 35 

entorhinal cortex 11–14 and in human medial temporal cortex during episodic memory tasks 15–17 . Ramping cells, also 36 

referred to as ’temporal context cells’ in some published work, fire with monotonically changing rates over longer windows 37 

and have been identified across rodent, monkey, and human medial temporal and prefrontal cortex 18–20 . Theoretical 38 

accounts cast these populations as components of a common temporal code, in which ramping cells implement a 39 

Laplace-domain representation of time and time cells emerge as the inverse-Laplace readout when the relevant interval 40 

is bounded and predictable 18,19,21 .41 

A critical outstanding question is whether time cells and ramping cells participate in episodic retrieval by driving the 42 

recovery of temporal context. The temporal context model proposes that recovery of context at retrieval re-cues the 43 

items associated with that contextual state 1–3 . At the cellular level, if reinstated context cues content within an integrative 44 

event, then context-carrying neurons should fire before content-carrying neurons within the integration window of a 45 

reactivating cell assembly. Cell assemblies are coordinated co-firing populations that have been proposed as integrative 46 

units of memory representation 22–24 , with theta oscillations proposed as the temporal frame within which assembly 47 

integration occurs 25–27 . Multi-neuron sequences extend the same integrative principle across longer timescales, with 48 

established roles in rodent memory 12,28,29 and emerging characterization in human medial temporal cortex 30 . Prior 49 

work has shown that memory-sensitive neurons in human medial temporal cortex reinstate their encoding firing patterns 50 

at retrieval 31 , consistent with the population-level reinstatement predicted by the temporal context model. However, it 51 

remains unknown whether this reinstatement involves an ordered within-cycle cellular signature– specifically whether 52 

context-carrying neurons firing before content-carrying neurons during individual reactivation events– and whether such 53 

ordering depends on task demands, has not been established. In free recall, where context must cue retrieval, this 54 

ordering should be essential. In serial reconstruction, where items are externally provided, context-driven ordering may 55 

be less critical. 56 

While time cells and ramping cells represent temporal information through rate coding during encoding 11,15 , an 57 

alternative account proposes that neurons may also carry temporal information through the phase at which they fire 58 

relative to ongoing theta oscillations 25,32 . Initial intracranial reports identified phase relationships consistent with serial-59 

position coding during working memory 33 , but recent work has reframed these patterns as arising from interactions 60 

between stimulus timing and ongoing oscillations rather than reflecting an active ordering mechanism 34 . Whether 61 

the medial temporal lobe recruits multiple temporal representations during encoding, and whether engagement of 62 

phase-based codes depends on task structure, remains unclear. 63 

We tested these predictions by recording single units from human medial temporal cortex during two episodic memory 64 

tasks and a naturalistic viewing dataset. Specifically, we asked: (1) Do time cells and ramping cells carry rate-decodable 65 

temporal information, and do their properties differ between free recall and serial reconstruction? (2) Within reactivating 66 

cell assemblies, do time cells fire at earlier theta phases than other assembly members, and does this phase ordering 67 

depend on whether temporal context must cue retrieval? (3) Do multi-neuron sequences organize around time cells, and 68 

does their frequency track behavioral markers of contextually organized recall? (4) During encoding, do non-temporal 69 

neurons carry temporal information through theta-phase coding, and does the prevalence of phase-based representation 70 

depend on task structure?(5) Does classical temporal coding require bounded, predictable intervals, as theory predicts, 71 

or do they emerge across naturalistic temporal contexts? 72

# Results 73 

Time cells and ramping cells in human medial temporal cortex carry rate-decodable temporal information across 74 

both paradigms 75 

We recorded single units from the medial temporal cortex of 62 human epilepsy patients performing one of two verbal 76 

memory paradigms (Figure 1A,B). In free recall (FR), participants studied lists of 12 or 15 words and verbally recalled 77 

them in any order during a 30 – 45 s retrieval period. In serial reconstruction (SR), participants studied lists of 10 words 78 

and reconstructed the encoding order from a randomized choice set. Our final samples comprised 768 units in free 79 

recall and 247 units in serial reconstruction. Behavioral performance followed canonical serial-position curves in both 80 

paradigms (Figure 1C), with better recall of early and late items in both paradigms, consistent with primacy and recency 81 

effects. 82 

We identified two classes of temporally tuned neurons: time cells, which fire at consistent latencies within encoding 83 

lists, and ramping cells, which monotonically modulate their firing rate across longer windows bounded by either epoch-84 

time (list-time) or session-time. Both cell classes were identified using established criteria detailed in Methods. In free 85 

recall, 186 units met time-cell criteria (24.2% of 768), and in serial reconstruction 69 units did (27.9% of 247), with 86 

no significant difference in prevalence between paradigms ( χ2 (1) = 1.18, p = 0.277) . Time cells tiled the encoding 87 

interval in both paradigms (Figure 1D, E), with tuning peaks distributed across early, middle, and late portions of the 88 

interval. A significant fraction in each paradigm were active during both encoding and retrieval (20% in free recall, 18% 89 

in serial reconstruction, p< 0.01, binomial test), consistent with prior reports of context-dependent recruitment of partially 90 

overlapping time-cell subsets 1,4,35 . Ramping cells in both paradigms exhibited monotonic firing-rate trajectories across 91 

the session and within encoding epochs (Figure 1F). Thus, both temporal-coding cell classes were recruited in both 92 

paradigms. 93 

The temporal tuning properties of time cells and ramping cells differed systematically between paradigms. Time-field 94 

width as a fraction of the encoding interval was substantially smaller in free recall (median 10 .6% , IQR 7.5–16 .2% ) than 95 

in serial reconstruction (median 47 .0% , IQR 27 .9–95 .6% ; Mann–Whitney U = 171 , p < 1 × 10 −18 , Figure 2B), indicating 96 

that time-field width differs systematically with task. This task-dependent difference persisted when comparing absolute 97 

time-field widths (Supp. Figure 2A). Ramping-cell subtypes showed a complementary asymmetry between paradigms. 98 

Session-down ramps (cells whose firing rate declined across the session) were overrepresented in serial reconstruction 99 

(Figure 2C; p < 0.05 ), and epoch-down ramps (cells whose firing rate declined within each encoding epoch) were 100 

overrepresented in free recall ( p < 0.01 ). Anterior-posterior differences in ramping-cell composition emerged, with 101 

posterior hippocampus showing higher overall ramping-cell prevalence and enrichment of epoch-down ramps, while 102 

anterior hippocampus showed relatively more session-down ramps; however, there was no difference in the ramping 103 

time constants (Supp. Figure 2B,C,E,F). Time-cell properties did not vary between anterior and posterior locations(Supp. 104 

Figure 2A,B,D). These results demonstrate that the two paradigms recruited differently tuned temporal-coding populations 105 

even where the cell-class definitions were identical, an asymmetry potentially attributable to the greater demand on 106 

internally generated within-list temporal context in free recall or the need to differentiate across item lists in multiple time 107 

scales. 108 

We trained a logistic-regression decoder to predict the within-list time bin (20 bins) from population firing rates and 109 

applied it separately to four cell-type pools (Figure 2D). In both paradigms, the time-cell pool decoded the encoding 110 

interval more accurately than ramping cells alone, the union of the two, or a control pool of cells classified as neither 111 

(Figure 2E,F). The accuracy hierarchy held for both decoding accuracy and mean absolute error, indicating that the rate-112 

decodable temporal signal is concentrated in time cells rather than distributed across all temporally tuned populations. 113 

Decoding accuracy was higher for early than for late list positions (Figure 2G; ∗∗∗ p < 0.001 ), echoing the behavioral 114 

primacy effect 4,5 and indicating that decodable temporal information is non-uniformly distributed across the encoding 115 

interval. The narrower time fields in free recall (Figure 2B) predicted higher per-cell decoding accuracy in this paradigm 116

Time cells phase-precede assembly reactivation during episodic retrieval 121 

The temporal context model proposes that successful retrieval depends on the initial recovery of the contextual state 122 

present at encoding, which in turn re-cues the items 1–3,6,7 . Cell assemblies—transient co-firing populations that 123 

recur across encoding and retrieval—are integrative units through which both item and context information are jointly 124 

expressed 22–24 . Assembly activation is organized by theta oscillations, which govern when assemblies activate 36 . If 125 

temporal context recovery initiates item reinstatement at the cellular level, then context-carrying neurons should fire at 126 

an earlier theta phase than the broader assembly population. To test this prediction, we identified assemblies (3A) and 127 

examined whether time cells fire at a systematically earlier theta phase than other assembly members during retrieval 128 

reactivation events. Following established methods, we identified cell assemblies as groups of neurons co-firing within 129 

25 ms bins during encoding more than expected for independently firing cells 36,37 . We defined reactivation events as 130 

moments during retrieval when assembly-weighted population activity exceeded a per-assembly threshold 38 . Across 131 

sessions, these assemblies each comprised a stable fraction of the recorded population, and assembly members, but 132 

not nonmembers, tracked the strength of reactivation (Supp. ˜ Figure ˜ S3A,B). 133 

For each cell assembly, we identified the preferred theta frequency as the frequency within the theta band at which 134 

its member neurons were most strongly phase-locked to the local field potential. For each reactivation event of that 135 

assembly, we then computed the phase difference between time-cell spikes within a 150 ms window of the onset of 136 

reactivation and the population mean phase of the assembly’s contributing neurons, with negative values indicating 137 

time-cell spikes occur at an earlier phase. Time cells fired at a systematically earlier theta phase than the surrounding 138 

assembly population in both paradigms (Figure 3B). In free recall, the mean shift was ∆θ = −63 ◦, approximately −29 ms 139 

at 6 Hz (MRL = 0 .18 , cluster-bootstrap p = 0 .001 , 80 assemblies across 19 subjects). In serial reconstruction, the mean 140 

shift was ∆θ = −23 ◦, approximately −11 ms (MRL = 0 .48 , cluster p = 0 .002 , 42 assemblies across 12 subjects). The 141 

magnitude of the phase lead was larger in free recall than in serial reconstruction ( ∆θ = −63 ◦ vs. ∆θ = −23 ◦) but 142 

was present in both, indicating that time-cell phase precedence is a feature of assembly reactivation independent of 143 

whether temporal context is internally generated. This lead did not depend on assuming an oscillation or on specific 144 

analysis parameters: it was recovered when measured directly in spike time ( ≈ − 41 ms in free recall, ≈ − 9 ms in 145 

serial reconstruction), held across fixed theta frequencies from 3 to 9 Hz and across activation-window widths, and was 146 

present on the majority of individual reactivation events of a given assembly (Supp. ˜Figure ˜S3C–F,H). In contrast, ramping 147 

cells revealed a smaller phase lead in serial reconstruction ( ∆θ = −10 ◦, approximately −5 ms, cluster p = 0 .005 ) but 148 

no consistent phase relationship in free recall. The temporal organization of time cells, but not ramping cells, around 149 

assembly reactivation is consistent with the cellular prediction of the temporal context model in both paradigms, raising 150 

the mechanistic question of whether this organization shapes how strongly the assembly itself reactivates. 151 

Previous work has shown that the strength of assembly reactivation at retrieval tracks behavioral memory expres-152 

sion 35,38 . If time cells provide the contextual cue for assembly reactivation, assemblies containing time cells (TC-Asm) 153 

should reactivate more strongly than those without (non-TC assemblies), with this advantage scaling with successful 154 

recall. We compared reactivation strength (projection of instantaneous population activity onto assembly weight vector) 155 

between TC-Asm and non-TC assemblies, dividing lists by median recall performance. TC-Asm reactivated more strongly 156 

than non-TC assemblies in both paradigms (Mann–Whitney U , p < 0.001 in both; Figure 3C,D). This advantage was not 157 

attributable to assembly size, which was comparable for time-cell-containing and other assemblies (Supp. ˜Figure ˜S3G). 158 

A linear mixed-effects model with subjects as a random effect tested whether this TC-versus-non-TC advantage was 159 

modulated by recall success. In free recall, the interaction was significant (LMM p < 0.001 ). The TC-Asm advantage 160

Time cells lead multi-neuron sequences whose abundance tracks behavioral measures of contextual recall 177 

We examined whether multi-neuron sequences initiated by time cells predict contextually-organized recall behavior 30 .178 

This tests whether the same time-cell-leading principle that organizes individual assemblies extends to longer timescale 179 

retrieval chains that structure behavioral memory. We applied the Spike Pattern Detection and Evaluation (SPADE) 180 

algorithm to detect repeating temporal spike patterns against a dithered surrogate null (Methods) 40,41 . Significant 181 

sequences were detected in both paradigms, with the same patterns recurring across both encoding and retrieval epochs 182 

(Figure 4A). Pattern size, duration, and per-pattern occurrence count did not differ between paradigms (Figure 4B; 183 

free recall, 3.4 ± 0.7 neurons, 125 ± 82 ms, 17 ± 10 occurrences per pattern; serial reconstruction, 3.1 ± 0.2 neurons, 184 

185 ± 42 ms, 23 ± 16 occurrences; all p > 0.05 , Mann–Whitney U ). 185 

We next asked whether the neurons participating in sequences were drawn preferentially from the assemblies identified 186 

above. These neurons were significantly more likely to be assembly members than the recorded population at large 187 

(Supp. Figure S4A; ∗∗∗ p < 0.001 in both paradigms), and within sequences they were significantly more likely to be 188 

drawn from the same assembly than from different assemblies (Supp. Figure S4B; observed > shuffled, ∗∗∗ p < 0.001 in 189 

both). Time cells participated in sequences preferentially within their time fields, consistent with their tuning to specific 190 

moments of the encoding interval (Figure 4C; Wilcoxon signed-rank, ∗∗ p < 0.01 in both paradigms). A sequence that 191

Theta-phase coding represents temporal context across serial positions and within discovered task structure 209 

Theta oscillations provide a complementary mechanism through which temporal information may be organized during 210 

encoding. Theoretical accounts propose that the theta phase at which a neuron spikes may encode sequential position, 211 

with successive items represented at progressively shifted phases within each theta cycle 25,42 , and MTL neurons reliably 212 

phase-lock to theta during memory encoding 31,43 . Whether phase organizes temporal or sequential information in 213 

humans, however, remains unclear. Because time-sensitive neurons exhibit phase precession. a distinct phase-related 214 

phenomenon, we restricted this analysis to non-temporal neurons (i.e., neurons classified as neither time cells nor 215 

ramping cells). We tested for: (1) consistent phase differences across serial positions using phase locking analysis, and 216 

(2) graded relationships between spike phase and serial position using circular-linear correlation (Figure 5A, B). 217 

In serial reconstruction, the number of cells showing significant phase non-uniformity exceeded chance at all serial 218 

positions ( pperm < 0.05 ; Figure 5B), whereas in free recall, this occurred only at positions 5–9 and 11–15. The proportion 219 

of neurons showing significant theta phase-locking was significantly higher in serial reconstruction (21/141, 14.9%) than 220 

in free recall (28/413, 6.8%; χ2(1) = 8.58, p = 0.003). However, the mean preferred phase did not vary systematically 221 

across serial positions in either paradigm, suggesting neurons do not encode position through phase shifts (serial 222 

reconstruction: grand MRL of state mean vectors = 0.859, pperm = 0 .287 ; free recall: grand MRL of state mean vectors 223 

= 0.061, pperm = 0 .262 ; Figure 5B). Pairwise comparisons of phase-locked populations across serial positions (Jaccard 224 

similarity) revealed no significant overlap in free recall after FDR correction (all FDR corrected p > 0.05). In serial 225 

reconstruction, six position pairs showed significantly above-chance overlap (Figure 5C; FDR-corrected Jaccard similarity, 226 

p < 0.05), concentrated around positions 2, 5, 7, and 10. Together, these results indicate that phase-locked populations 227 

are largely position-specific in free recall, but that serial reconstruction additionally recruits a subset of shared neuronal 228 

populations across select serial positions. 229 

We tested whether phase carries graded positional information within individual lists using circular-linear correlation 230 

between theta phase and serial position. A small but significant proportion of neurons showed this relationship in both 231 

paradigms (serial reconstruction: 10.6% sig (15/141), pperm = 0 .005 ; free recall: 10.2% sig (42/413), pperm < 0.001 ;232 

Figure 5D), with no significant difference between conditions (10.6% vs. 10.2%; χ2(1) = 0.025, p = 0.874). This suggests 233 

both paradigms recruit phase-based positional coding at the single-neuron level. 234 

Serial position is an experimenter-defined variable that may not match how subjects naturally segment the encoding 235 

period. A phase code organized around internally defined population states would be obscured when parsed by serial 236 

position. We therefore tested whether theta phase coding is organized by latent population states rather than serial 237 

position. To identify these states, we fit an unsupervised hidden Markov model (HMM) to the full recorded population 238 

activity, including time cells and ramping cells. Model order was determined by cross-validated log-likelihood, which 239 

favored three states in free recall and four in serial reconstruction (Figure 5E). State-probability maps revealed that 240 

each state dominated a distinct contiguous portion of the encoding interval, tiling the period sequentially, and transition 241 

matrices confirmed the feed-forward structure of the model (Figure 5E). 242

Naturalistic viewing recruits ramping cells 258 

The cellular signatures of temporal context representation and recovery characterized above were obtained in paradigms 259 

with bounded, repeatable encoding intervals, the kind of temporal scaffold against which classical time-cell tuning is 260 

conventionally defined 11,12 . We next asked what happens to the temporal-coding architecture when this scaffold is 261 

absent, by examining a separate dataset in which patients viewed a 28-minute episode of Curb Your Enthusiasm (CURB; 262 

90 units across 12 patients; Methods). The episode comprised 17 scenes of unequal duration without repeated structure 263 

across the recording (Figure 6A), and patients made temporal judgments about the episode rather than encoding a 264 

structured list. This paradigm removed the bounded, repeatable interval that conventionally supports time-cell tuning 265 

while preserving a temporal demand on the participant. 266 

We focused on ramping cells, which may provide a more suitable framework for examining temporal representation 267 

across naturalistic timescales. Notably, scene boundaries in the episode provided natural structural breaks analogous 268 

to list boundaries in the episodic tasks. We therefore modeled ramping-cell firing using the same stepwise Poisson 269 

GLM extended to include scene-onset and within-scene-time predictors. Ramping cells were identified in 32 of 90 270 

units ( 35 .6% ) using the same stepwise Poisson GLM extended to include scene-onset and within-scene-time predictors 271 

(Methods; Figure 6B). The proportion of ramping cells identified in this paradigm was similar to that of free recall and 272 

serial reconstruction (Supp. Figure 5A). 273 

The detected ramping population was heterogeneous across timescales. Thirteen cells ( 14 .4% ) showed session-scale 274 

ramps spanning the full 28-minute episode, and 22 cells ( 24 .4% ) showed within-scene ramps reset at scene onsets. 275 

Both upward and downward ramps were detected within each timescale, with within-scene downward ramps the most 276 

prevalent subtype (18 cells, 20 .0% ; Figure 6B). The functional distinction between upward and downward ramping cells in 277 

human medial temporal cortex remains poorly characterized, as down ramping neurons are mostly considered in models 278 

of temporal context representation built from these populations. Single-cell examples illustrated both organizational 279 

scales, including a session-scale upward ramp (Figure 6D, top; p = 1 .3 × 10 −6) and a within-scene downward ramp 280 

(Figure 6D, bottom; p = 7 .6 × 10 −17 ). These observations suggest that ramping-cell activity provides a continuous 281 

temporal representation that operates flexibly across multiple timescales, persisting even when explicit task structure 282 

and bounded intervals are absent. 283 

# Discussion 284 

How the human brain represents the temporal structure of experience at the cellular level, and how it recovers that 285 

structure to guide retrieval, has remained a fundamental open question in episodic memory research. We addressed 286 

this by recording single neurons across two episodic paradigms that vary in task demands. Time cells and ramping cells 287 

were recruited in both paradigms, but with tuning properties that differed systematically: time fields were narrower in free 288 

recall, where temporal context must be internally generated, and ramping subtypes were asymmetrically distributed 289 

across paradigms in a pattern consistent with different demands on within-list (SR) versus within+across list (FR) 290

A key novel aspect of our results pertains to neuronal sequences. Multi-neuron sequences that recurred across 299 

encoding and retrieval, were structured by assembly membership, and correlated with behavioral measures of contextually 300 

organized recall are the central novel finding of this work. Sequences have been well characterized in rodent hippocampus 301 

during navigation and sleep consolidation 28,29,44 and have recently been identified in human medial temporal cortex 30 ,302 

but a direct link to the behavioral expression of episodic retrieval had not been established. Sequence frequency 303 

tracked temporal clustering in free recall and order accuracy in serial reconstruction, indicating that the same cellular 304 

mechanism serves contextually organized recall across paradigms. Within this framework, sequences initiated by a 305 

time cell were stronger predictors of recall organization than sequences broadly, both across and within subjects — 306 

the pattern the temporal context model predicts if temporal context must be recovered before item content can be 307 

retrieved 1,2 , making sequences that begin with a context-carrying neuron the cellular instantiation of that recovery event. 308 

That these sequences are structured by assembly membership and engage time cells preferentially within their tuned 309 

time fields indicates that replay is organized by the population-level co-firing structure established at encoding 22–24 .310 

The temporal context model has long predicted that retrieval begins with reinstatement of the contextual state present 311 

at encoding, which then re-cues the items bound to that context 1,2 . Our data offer what is, to our knowledge, the first 312 

direct cellular evidence of this prediction: time cells fired at a systematically earlier theta phase than the surrounding 313 

assembly population during individual reactivation events, across both paradigms. The within-cycle architecture has 314 

a clear comparative precedent in rodent theta sequences, where place cells encoding the earliest-traversed locations 315 

occupy the leading phase of each cycle 26,45–47 . In our paradigm, however, the leading-phase role is fixed to a functionally 316 

defined cell class (i.e. time cells) rather than rotating with the direction of spatial traversal 48 , suggesting that the 317 

theta-sequence principle generalizes from spatial navigation to episodic retrieval in a form tied to the unidirectional 318 

temporal structure of episodic experience, with behavioral association to support its relevance to memory 35,38 . That the 319 

same modulation was absent in serial reconstruction, where item identity is externally provided, is consistent with the 320 

interpretation that it is internally reinstated temporal context , rather than retrieval effort per se, that determines whether 321 

the time-cell-led assembly architecture is expressed. 322 

Rate coding via context–sensitive neuron populations (time and ramping cells) and phase coding relative to theta 323 

oscillations have been treated as largely parallel accounts of temporal information in human MTL 25,32,33 . Our data 324 

suggest they are complementary codes the recruitment of which depends on task demands. Phase locking was more 325 

prominently engaged in serial reconstruction than free recall, and exhibited item–specific phase coding at the population 326 

level. But neither paradigm produced the monotonic phase-position shift that classical theta-gamma accounts predict 25,32 ,327 

a null consistent with Liebe et al. 34 , who argued that apparent phase-serial position relationships reflect stimulus-timing 328 

interactions with oscillatory frequency rather than an active encoding mechanism. The use of HMM–defined states 329 

suggested that, when subjects must fully construct temporal context internally (i.e. in free recall), phase coding aligns to 330 

the brain’s discovered temporal groupings– a pattern consistent with chunking theories and indicating that phase-based 331 

coding depends on an internally structured temporal representation. 332 

In the Curb (naturalistic) paradigm, ramping cells were identified at multiple timescales — spanning the full episode 333 

and resetting at scene boundaries. This heterogeneity is consistent with the Laplace-domain proposal that populations 334 

with different time constants collectively implement a continuous, scale-invariant representation of elapsed time 18,49,50 .335

Spike detection and sorting 386

analysis. To prevent duplicate counting of neurons detected across multiple microwires, unit pairs whose spike trains 395

Behavioral tasks 404 

In the free-recall task, patients studied lists of monosyllabic nouns presented one at a time on a bedside laptop and 405 

then recalled them aloud, a paradigm we have described previously 15,36 . Each word remained on screen for 1.6 s, 406 

and a jittered interval of 0.8 to 1.2 s separated neighboring words. Lists contained 15 words at TJUH and 12 words at 407

problems followed the format A + B + C =? . Patients then recalled as many words as possible, in any order, during a 409 

30-s (UTSW) or 45-s (TJUH) retrieval period, and completed 4 to 25 lists per session. 410 

In the serial-reconstruction task, patients studied lists of 10 monosyllabic nouns, with each word presented for 1.6 s 411 

and followed by a 1-s blank screen, and then performed a 30-s arithmetic distractor task. At test, all of the studied items 412 

reappeared together in a randomized array, and patients reconstructed the studied order by dragging into one of ten 413 

sequential boxes.. We scored an item as correctly recalled only when it occupied its correct absolute serial position 414 

within a run of at least two consecutive, correctly placed items. Patients completed 20 to 25 lists per session. 415

program Curb Your Enthusiasm lasting approximately 28 minutes. After viewing the episode, participants were presented 417

Time cell identification 427 

Time cells were identified using a previously published nonparametric procedure optimized for detecting temporally tuned 428 

neurons in human medial temporal lobe recordings 15 , following established approaches in the time-cell literature 11,12 .429 

The procedure tested whether each neuron’s firing rate varied significantly with elapsed time within the encoding interval 430 

without assuming a particular form for the temporal tuning relationship. For each neuron, the session-wide spike train 431 

was convolved with a Gaussian kernel (standard deviation: 0.5 s) to obtain a continuous estimate of instantaneous 432 

firing rate, referred to as the tuning curve. For each encoding list, the corresponding segment of the tuning curve was 433 

divided into contiguous, non-overlapping time bins (bin width: approximately 1 s), and the mean firing rate was computed 434 

within each bin, yielding a list-by-bin matrix. Temporal modulation across bins was tested using a Kruskal–Wallis test, a 435 

rank-based nonparametric test robust to the non-normal, Poisson-distributed nature of neural spike counts. Neurons 436 

meeting the threshold of p < 0.05 were advanced to a permutation test to determine whether their temporal tuning 437 

exceeded chance levels. For each neuron, a null distribution of test statistics was generated by circularly shifting the 438 

session-wide tuning curve by a randomly selected amount (1,000 shuffles), then re-binning and re-testing. Circular 439 

shifting preserves the temporal structure and autocorrelation of the neuron’s firing while disrupting the alignment of 440 

spikes to the behavioral epoch, isolating genuine time-dependent firing. A neuron was classified as a time cell when 441 

fewer than 5% of shuffles yielded a smaller p-value than the unshuffled data. This identification procedure was applied 442 

identically to both memory-task datasets and, for retrieval analyses, to tuning-curve segments spanning each retrieval 443 

period. Each time cell was further classified by the behavioral context in which it achieved significance: encoding only, 444 

retrieval only, or both periods. 445 

Time fields were localized within each identified time cell’s trial-averaged tuning curve for analyses requiring specifica-446 

tion of a temporal receptive field, including cross-task comparisons of field widths. The tuning curve was re-binned at 447 

finer temporal resolution (bin width: approximately 0.2 s), and contiguous runs of consecutive bins were identified whose 448 

mean firing rate exceeded the neuron’s session-wide mean firing rate. A run was designated as a time field when two 449 

conditions were met: (1) the run spanned a minimum contiguous duration of 1 s, and (2) the neuron exhibited firing within 450 

that time window on at least half of the lists within that task. Because time fields were defined exclusively for neurons 451 

already classified as time cells, cross-task comparisons of field widths were made between the independently identified 452 

free-recall and serial-reconstruction populations, ensuring that any task-dependent differences in field properties reflected 453 

genuine variations in temporal tuning rather than differences in the composition of identified time cells. 454

Population decoding of elapsed time 481

logistic-regression decoder was trained to predict the time bin from population activity vectors. Each encoding list was 483

Cell assembly identification 511 

Cell assemblies were identified following previously established procedures for human medial temporal lobe recordings 36 ,512 

adapted from a principal- and independent-component analysis framework developed in rodent studies 37 . Analysis 513 

was restricted to neurons with baseline firing rates ≥ 0.5Hz . For each recording session, spike trains were binned at 514 

25-millisecond resolution during encoding (a timescale chosen to match the gamma band), and z-scored to prevent 515 

high-firing rate neurons from dominating the analysis. The resulting neuron-by-bin matrix was subjected to principal 516 

component analysis with components retained if their eigenvalues exceeded the upper bound predicted for independent 517 

firing by the Marchenko–Pastur distribution, thereby isolating genuine co-firing structure. The firing-rate matrix was then 518 

projected onto retained components and independent component analysis (fastICA) was applied, so that the number of 519 

recovered assemblies equaled the number of significant components. Each assembly was described by a weight vector, 520 

normalized to unit length and signed such that its maximum weight was positive. Neurons were classified as assembly 521 

members if their absolute weights exceeded the ensemble mean plus one standard deviation. Assemblies were required 522 

to contain at least two member neurons to exclude single-neuron contributions. To validate that the detected assemblies 523 

reflected genuine neural organization rather than thresholding artifacts, the observed number was compared against the 524 

null distribution obtained from circularly shifted spike trains. 525 

The moment-to-moment expression of each assembly was quantified as as 526

Hidden Markov model 692 

Neural population dynamics during encoding were modeled using a left-to-right event-segmentation hidden Markov 693 

model (HMM), following the framework of Baldassano et al. 61 . The model was applied separately to the free-recall 694 

(FR) and serial-recall (SR) datasets, with single units pooled across subjects to form a pseudo-population for each task. 695 

Each encoding list constituted one trial. Spike trains were convolved with a normalized Gaussian kernel ( σ = 250 ms) to 696 

estimate instantaneous firing rate. The smoothed firing rate trace for each neuron on each list was resampled to 530 697 

uniformly spaced time points, after which 15 bins were trimmed from each edge to mitigate boundary artifacts, yielding 698 

500 time points per trial. The firing rate of each neuron was then z-scored within each trial independently; neurons with 699 

zero variance on a given trial were excluded from the emission computation for that trial. Neurons missing data on 10 or 700 

more lists, and lists in which no neuron had data, were excluded prior to model fitting. 701 

The HMM assumed a left-to-right state topology, whereby transitions were permitted only to the current state or the 702 

next state, enforcing a monotonically progressing sequence of K discrete neural states. Emission distributions were 703 

modeled as independent Gaussians over z-scored firing rates. Parameters were estimated via Expectation-Maximization 704

Naturalistic viewing analyses 712 

To determine whether temporally tuned neurons emerge outside the bounded, repeating structure of the memory tasks, 713 

single units were analyzed during a naturalistic viewing session in which patients watched a single episode of Curb Your 714 

Enthusiasm . To relate the episode’s temporal structure to behavioral timing, patients’ periodic judgments of elapsed time 715 

were examined. Each judgment was correlated with the true elapsed time using Pearson correlation, and the strength of 716 

this relationship was summarized across scenes with bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 iterations), quantifying the 717 

fidelity of temporal perception during naturalistic viewing. 718 

For ramping cells, the generalized linear mixed-effects model was adapted to the continuous temporal structure of the 719 

naturalistic episode. For each neuron, spikes were counted in one-second windows across the full episode. A Poisson 720 

generalized linear mixed-effects model was fit with predictors including elapsed time from the episode start, elapsed time 721 

within the current scene, and scene onset; a random intercept for scene captured baseline firing-rate differences between 722 

scenes. A neuron was classified as a ramping cell when the full model significantly improved over a reduced model 723 

containing only the scene random intercept (likelihood-ratio test, α = 0.05) and when at least one temporal predictor 724 

(episode time or scene time) contributed significantly (p < 0.05). 725
