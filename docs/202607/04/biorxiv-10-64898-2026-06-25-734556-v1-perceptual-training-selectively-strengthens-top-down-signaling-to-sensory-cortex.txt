SUMMARY 1

Sensory cortical neurons adjust their responses to enhance the detection and discrimination of 2

behaviorally relevant stimuli. Top-down signals from frontal cortical regions are thought to mediate 3

these changes, and prior work has proposed that these signals may strengthen during perceptual 4

learning. However, this hypothesis has not been tested directly. Here, we asked whether neurons 5

in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), a higher-order brain region implicated in top-down control of 6

sensory cortex, alter their activity during auditory perceptual learning and whether learning-related 7

changes are transmitted to sensory cortex. We found that OFC neurons encode both trial outcome 8

and stimulus-related information, and that the strength and sensitivity of these signals increase as 9

animals learn to detect progressively weaker amplitude modulations. Notably, learning-related 10 

changes were conveyed to auditory cortex, but not visual cortex, indicating that perceptual learning 11 

selectively strengthens signaling within a task-relevant frontal-to-sensory cortical circuit. Together, 12 

these findings provide direct evidence that top-down cortical networks are modified during 13 

perceptual learning. 14 

Keywords : prefrontal cortex, perceptual training, auditory learning, cortical plasticity, sensory 15 

processing, neural circuits, sound perception, electrophysiology, fiber photometry, Mongolian gerbil 16

INTRODUCTION 18 

Practice can refine the ability to perceive sensory stimuli, leading to more sensitive detection thresholds and 19 

heightened acuity. This process—termed perceptual learning 1 —occurs across sensory modalities and 20 

contributes to complex skills, such as speech comprehension 2–9 and musical ability 10–16 . However, the neural 21 

circuits that enable perceptual learning remain incompletely understood. 22 

Neural recordings consistently demonstrate learning-related changes in the sensitivity or tuning of sensory 23 

cortical neurons to trained stimulus features 17–29 . Yet perceptual learning is also strongly influenced by non-24 

sensory factors, including attention, reward, and task demands 30–32 . These factors robustly modulate sensory 25 

cortical activity 33–36 , raising the possibility that learning-related plasticity may occur not only within sensory 26 

cortex, but also in the top-down brain networks that shape sensory processing. 27 

Indirect support for this idea comes from neurophysiological studies showing that sensory cortical stimulus 28 

representations are enhanced when stimuli acquire behavioral relevance, and that this enhancement grows with 29 

perceptual learning 19,28,37–39 . These observations are consistent with findings from human neuroimaging 30 

workbyer 40,41 and computational models of perceptual learning 42–44 , and collectively have led to the hypothesis 31 

that perceptual learning strengthens top-down signaling to sensory cortex 27 . However, direct evidence for this 32 

hypothesis is lacking. 33 

Among the candidate sources of top-down signals to sensory cortex, the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) is especially 34 

compelling because it integrates sensory, value, and task-related information 45–53 , and sends direct projections 35 

to multiple sensory cortical areas 54–60 . In the auditory cortex, OFC input modulates sound-evoked responses, 36 

conveys task and stimulus-related information, and can drive rapid auditory cortical plasticity 58,59,61 . Consistent 37 

with these observations, we recently found that the OFC is required for behavioral sound detection and for task-38 

dependent engagement of auditory cortical neurons 62 . These findings led us to hypothesize auditory perceptual 39 

learning enhances OFC-to-auditory cortical communication to enhance the representation of behaviorally 40 

relevant sounds. 41 

To test this hypothesis, we monitored OFC neurons and OFC projections to auditory cortex as Mongolian gerbils 42 

trained and improved on an auditory detection task. We found that OFC neurons transmit both trial outcome and 43 

stimulus-related information to the auditory cortex, and that the strength and sensitivity of these signals increase 44 

with learning. Notably, learning-related changes were not observed in OFC projections to visual cortex, indicating 45 

that plasticity was restricted to the OFC-to-auditory cortex pathway. Together, these findings provide direct 46 

evidence that perceptual learning is accompanied by strengthened signaling within a frontal-to-sensory cortical 47 

circuit. 48 

RESULTS 49 

Animals learn to detect weak amplitude modulations 50 

To evaluate how orbitofrontal-auditory signaling evolves over the course of perceptual learning, we trained 51 

freely-moving Mongolian gerbils to detect amplitude modulations (AM) embedded in broadband noise 63–66 . In 52 

this task, water-restricted animals learn to drink steadily from a spout while in the presence of the no-go stimulus 53 

(non-AM noise) and to withdraw from the spout when the no-go stimulus smoothly transitions to the go stimulus 54 

(5 Hz sinusoidal AM noise). Spout withdrawal is reinforced by pairing the go stimulus with a mild shock (Figure 55 

1A). Animals were trained once per day, every 1-2 days. 56

Animals were initially trained with a single easy go stimulus (0 dB relative to 100% depth AM noise; procedural 57 

training) until they reached a predetermined performance criterion ( d’ ≥ 2 on two consecutive training sessions, 58 

see STAR Methods). Gerbils achieved this criterion within 49-718 trials (median = 267 trials) across 4-7 sessions 59 

(median 4 days) (n = 26 animals across all experiments; 15 females). After reaching criterion, animals 60 

transitioned to perceptual training, during which we presented a range of progressively weaker AM depths over 61 

the course of ten days (Figure 1B). Figure 1C shows a representative subject's performance expressed as d' as 62 

a function of AM depth on three selected days spanning the training period. Threshold was defined as the AM 63 

depth at which the psychometric fit crossed d' = 1. Figure 1D shows this subject's threshold across the 10 days 64 

of perceptual training, reflecting an improvement rate of 4.92 dB/log 10 (day). 65 

At the group level, thresholds gradually improved across sessions (p < 0.001), at an average rate of 4.90 66 

dB/log 10 (day), and significantly differed between early and late training epochs (Figure 1E-F, p < 0.001). Subjects 67 

(n = 4, 3 females) who failed to learn were excluded from analyses involving learning-related changes (see 68 

Figure S1A and STAR Methods). 69 

We also measured the slopes and lapse rates of the psychometric functions across training. Psychometric 70 

slopes are a proxy for perceptual discrimination precision—i.e., steeper slopes indicate that detection 71 

performance transitions sharply from near-zero to near-maximum over a narrow range of AM depths, implying 72 

finer sensitivity around threshold. Lapse rates capture the probability of a random failure to respond even to 73 

clearly suprathreshold stimuli, independent of perceptual sensitivity. In practice, well-trained animals should 74 

show lapse rates near zero, indicating reliable responding at suprathreshold AM depths. In our sample, 75 

psychometric slopes significantly increased across training (p = 0.004, Figure S1B), while lapse rates remained 76 

low (1.9 ± 0.3%, mean ± SEM) and unchanged (p = 0.725, Figure S1C). 77 

Together, these data confirm that our task induces perceptual learning of AM depth detection. 78 

OFC neurons encode trial outcome and are sensitive to amplitude modulation depth 79 

We first sought to identify the dominant variables encoded by OFC neurons during performance of our AM 80 

detection task. To do so, six animals (four females) underwent procedural training until they reached our 81 

performance criterion of d’ ≥ 2 on two consecutive days. We then implanted these animals with chronic 64-82 

channel silicon probe arrays targeting the left OFC (Figure S2A) and recorded in vivo extracellular single-unit 83 

activity over the course of perceptual training (Figure 2A). We recorded from a total of 771 single-units and 959 84 

multi-units. Here, we focus on the responses of the 656 single-units classified via k-means clustering as regular-85 

spiking (putative excitatory) neurons (Figure S2B; see STAR Methods), initially pooling data across training days 86 

to establish basic response properties. 87 

OFC has been implicated in value encoding, outcome signaling, and reinforcement learning 45,67–70 . We therefore 88 

first asked whether log-transformed, baseline subtracted firing (hereafter referred to as “normalized firing”; see 89 

STAR methods) differed across trial outcomes in our task (Figure 2B-E). Visualization of firing from an individual 90 

unit (Figure 2C) and across the population (Figure 2D) revealed a pronounced increase in activity following spout 91 

offset, with elevated activity persisting for up to 3 sec after the event. Spout offsets associated with shock delivery 92 

following misses evoked the strongest responses, exceeding those observed on hit and false alarm trials (all p 93 

< 0.001; Figure 2E). Responses on hit trials were also greater than those on false alarm trials (p < 0.001). 94 

To determine how widespread outcome-related signaling was across the recorded population, we quantified 95 

spout offset responsiveness at the single unit level. Using a permutation test, we compared each unit’s firing 96 

during the outcome period to the firing during the immediately preceding non-AM noise epoch. Most units 97

Although prior work has shown that OFC neurons can respond to sounds 46,59,71,72 , including AM cues 58 , most of 100 

these studies used high intensity sounds that could drive strong onset responses. We therefore asked whether 101 

OFC units are sensitive to the behaviorally-relevant acoustic feature in our task: changes in AM depth embedded 102

OFC neurons can multiplex sensory and behavioral inputs to output value-rich sensory information 46,73,74 .108 

Therefore, we asked whether AM-sensitive units also exhibited spout offset responses. Similar to the overall 109

Because the analyses above excluded hit trials—which comprise the majority of strong AM depth 113 

presentations—we likely underestimated the prevalence of AM-sensitive OFC neurons. We therefore extended 114 

our analysis to ask whether AM depth information was also present during the post-spout offset period on hit 115 

trials. 116 

To quantify AM sensitivity during this epoch, and enable comparisons across neurons, we calculated neural d′ 117 

values using responses to the lowest AM depth presented to each unit as the reference condition (see STAR 118 

Methods). Conceptually, this metric assesses whether AM depths are distinguishable during the post-offset 119 

epoch, with larger d′ values reflecting greater separation from the weakest AM depth. To identify AM sensitive 120 

neurons, we plotted each unit’s d’ as a function of AM depth and fit the resulting functions with a sigmoid. Units 121 

whose fits differed significantly from a flat line and crossed an absolute d’ of 0.5 were classified as AM depth 122 

sensitive (see STAR Methods). 123 

This analysis revealed that approximately 10% of neurons (66/656) exhibited AM depth sensitivity during the 124 

post-spout offset period. As illustrated in Figure 2F-H, the relationship between neural d’ and AM depth could 125 

be either positive (31/66) or negative (35/66). AM depth threshold (defined as the depth at which the neurometric 126 

fit crossed an absolute d’ value of 0.5) did not differ between units with positive and negative fits (p = 0.568). 127 

Collectively, these findings suggest that a subpopulation of OFC units multiplex AM depth and trial outcome 128 

information. 129 

Perceptual training selectively strengthens outcome signaling in OFC neurons 130 

Our initial analyses revealed that OFC neurons are strongly activated following spout offsets. To determine 131 

whether these signals evolve over the course of perceptual training, we used generalized linear models (GLMs) 132 

to quantify changes in post-spout offset firing across two training stages: early (day 1) and late (days 8-10). 133 

Because a sizeable population of OFC neurons is AM-sensitive, we controlled for AM depth on hit and miss 134 

trials by restricting analyses to depths presented in both early and late training for each subject, and by including 135 

AM depth as a covariate in the GLM. 136

Perceptual training improves AM depth sensitivity in OFC neurons 145 

Having established that perceptual training selectively enhances outcome-related signaling in OFC neurons, we 146 

next asked whether training also alters AM depth representations during the post-spout offset period. To address 147 

this question, we focused on the 10% (66/656) of OFC neurons that exhibited AM sensitivity during this epoch 148 

on hit trials. 149 

Figure 4A depicts spout-offset related activity separated by AM depth for two representative neurons recorded 150 

during early (top) and late training (bottom). The neuron recorded during early training exhibited graded but 151 

modest responses to strong AM depths, resulting in a relatively poor neural threshold (-5 dB, top right). In 152 

contrast, the neuron recorded during late training shows stronger responses at lower depths, resulting in a 153 

substantially lower threshold (-13 dB, bottom right). This pattern was recapitulated at the population level (Figure 154 

4B-C), such that on average, neural thresholds were significantly lower (better) late in training (GLM/ANOVA; p 155 

< 0.001; Figure 4D). 156 

To determine whether neural and behavioral sensitivity improve in parallel over training, we plotted thresholds 157 

as a function of training day (Figure 4E, left). Both neural and behavioral thresholds significantly decreased 158 

across training (p < 0.001, GLM), indicating improved sensitivity. Improvements occurred in tandem, resulting in 159 

a strong positive relationship between neural and behavioral thresholds (p < 0.001, GLM). Figure 4E (right) 160 

illustrates the overall relationship along with individual subject fits (blue lines). 161 

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that OFC neurons multiplex AM depth and trial outcome-related 162 

information, and that these signals grow stronger over perceptual learning. 163 

Perceptual training selectively strengthens outcome signaling in the OFC-to-AC pathway 164

animals (four females; Figure 5A; Figure S4A). We then used fiber photometry to record calcium signals from 168 

OFC axons in left AC across 10 days of perceptual training (Figure 5B). 169

Consistent with our electrophysiological results, spout offsets associated with shock delivery following misses 173 

evoked the largest AUC values, followed by hits, then false alarms (Figure 5E; all p < 0.001). 174

Perceptual training improves AM depth sensitivity in the OFC-to-AC pathway 188 

Because OFC neurons multiplex AM depth and outcome information during the post-spout offset period, we next 189 

asked whether AM depth information was similarly present in the OFC projections to AC. To address this 190 

question, we aligned calcium signals to spout offset on hit trials and separated trials by AM depth. On average, 191 

signals scaled with modulation strength (Figure 5I-J). To quantify this effect, we used a GLM to test whether AM 192 

depth predicted the area under the curve (AUC; 0-3 sec post-spout offset), while controlling for training day. AM 193 

depth strongly predicted AUC magnitude (p < 0.001; Figure S4C). Similar AM-dependent scaling was observed 194 

at the level of individual training sessions, with 51% of sessions (43/84) exhibiting significant positive correlations 195 

between AM depth and AUC (Figure S4D). 196 

Perceptual training enhances AM depth sensitivity during the trial outcome period in a subset of OFC neurons 197 

(Figure 4). To determine whether similar changes are reflected in OFC projections to the auditory cortex, we 198 

compared spout offset-aligned calcium signals across training stages. On the first day of training, calcium 199 

signals largely overlapped across AM depth, indicating weak sensitivity (Figure 5K; left). By late training, signals 200 

exhibited a clearer separation across AM depths (Figure 5K; right). 201 

To quantify this effect, we modeled AUC values (0-3 sec post-spout offset) as a function of AM depth and training 202 

stage. AUC significantly increased with AM depth during both early (p = 0.001) and late (p < 0.001) training, but 203 

the relationship was significantly steeper late in training (interaction p = 0.008; Figure 5L). These results suggest 204 

that perceptual training strengthens AM depth sensitivity in the OFC-to-AC pathway. 205 

Auditory perceptual training does not alter signaling in the OFC-to-visual cortex pathway 206 

To determine whether training-related changes are specific to OFC-auditory signaling or reflect a global change 207 

in OFC output, we examined the OFC projection to primary visual cortex (VC), a region thought to be minimally 208 

involved in auditory detection 76–78 . We virally expressed the genetically encoded calcium indicator jGCaMP8s in 209 

OFC axons of 11 animals (seven females; Figure 6A, Figure S5A) and used fiber photometry to record calcium 210 

signals from OFC axons in left VC across 10 days of auditory perceptual training (Figure 6B). 211 

To characterize the signals carried by this projection, we initially pooled calcium signals across animals and 212 

training days. Spout offset elicited robust responses (Figure 6C-D), with dynamics similar to those observed in 213 

the OFC-to-AC projection: activity peaked after ~1 sec after spout offset and remained elevated for several 214 

seconds. Trial outcome discrimination was also evident, such that spout offsets associated with shock delivery 215 

following misses evoked the strongest responses, followed by hits, then false alarms (Figure 6E; all p < 0.001). 216

We next asked whether AM depth information is present in the OFC-to-VC projection during the trial outcome 227 

period on hit trials. Similar to OFC-to-AC axons, visual cortical projection activity scaled with AM depth, 228 

increasing with modulation strength (Figure 6I-J). To quantify this effect, we used a GLM to test whether AM 229 

depth predicted the AUC 0-3 sec post-spout offset, while controlling for training day. AM depth predicted AUC 230 

magnitude (p < 0.001; Figure S5C), but AM-dependent scaling was observed in only 23% (25/108; 24/25 positive 231 

slopes) of individual training sessions (Figure S5D), a substantially lower fraction than that observed in OFC-to-232 

AC recordings (51%; Figure S4D). A GLM comparing the AM depth × AUC relationship between the OFC-to-AC 233 

and OFC-to-VC datasets revealed a significantly stronger relationship (i.e. a steeper AM depth x AUC slope) in 234 

OFC-to-AC axons (p < 0.001; Figure S5E). These results suggest that while AM depth information is present in 235 

OFC axons within visual cortex, AM depth representations are weaker and less consistently expressed than 236 

those observed in auditory cortex. 237 

Finally, to test whether AM sensitivity in the OFC-to-VC pathway is affected by training, we compared OFC 238 

axonal responses in the visual cortex across training stages. Calcium signals moderately scaled with AM depth 239 

during both early and late training (Figure 6K), and the relationship between AUC magnitude and AM depth did 240 

not change from early to late training stages (Figure 6L; p = 0.484). 241 

Together, these findings demonstrate that the OFC-to-VC projection encodes both trial outcome and AM depth, 242 

but these signals are not modified by auditory perceptual training. 243 

DISCUSSION 244 

Decades of work have established that perceptual learning is accompanied by changes in sensory cortical 245 

representations of trained stimulus features 19,22–29,37–41,79 . Top-down signals from frontal cortical regions are 246 

known modulators of sensory cortical activity 54,56,57,59,61,62,80,81 , and theoretical 42–44 and experimental 247 

work 19,27,28,37–41,79,82–84 have led to the hypothesis that these signals strengthen with learning. Here we provide 248 

direct evidence for this hypothesis by demonstrating that perceptual learning enhances outcome- and stimulus-249 

related signaling in the OFC, and that these learning-related changes are conveyed to OFC axons in auditory 250 

cortex, but not visual cortex. Together, our findings suggest that auditory perceptual learning selectively 251 

strengthens OFC-to-auditory cortical communication to enhance the representation of behaviorally relevant 252 

sounds. 253 

Functional implications of enhanced OFC-to-AC signaling 254 

We found that most OFC neurons were strongly activated following spout offset. Response magnitudes varied 255 

systematically across trial outcomes, indicating that OFC neurons encode task-related information beyond spout 256 

withdrawal alone. The strongest responses were observed during miss trials, consistent with OFC’s prominent 257 

role in supporting reinforcement-related 50,85 and prediction error signaling 70 . Notably, responses were also larger 258

on hit trials than on false alarm trials. This difference may reflect variation in the strength of the underlying 259 

sensory evidence and/or perceptual confidence 86–88 . Together, these findings are in agreement with decades of 260 

work establishing the OFC as a central hub for value and outcome processing 45–53 , and add to a growing body 261 

of literature implicating the OFC in outcome computation during sound-guided behavior 58,62,86,89,90 .262 

Current models of OFC function propose that outcome signals can serve as top-down ‘teaching signals’ that 263 

enhance sensory cortical representations of behaviorally-relevant stimuli 73 . Consistent with this framework, we 264 

found that outcome-related signals are transmitted from OFC to AC, a region known to exhibit perceptual 265 

learning-related improvements in AM sensitivity 19 , and that perceptual learning selectively strengthened hit-266 

related signaling within this pathway. These results suggest that perceptual learning progressively strengthens 267 

OFC representations associated with stimulus detection, and that the transmission of these signals to the AC 268 

may reinforce or stabilize behaviorally relevant sensory representations, ultimately improving the detection of 269 

weak AM cues. Future loss-of-function experiments targeting the OFC-to-AC pathway will be required to directly 270 

test this interpretation. 271 

In addition to exhibiting robust spout offset responses, we found that a subpopulation of OFC neurons multiplex 272 

information related to AM depth. Notably, these signals exhibit weak AM depth sensitivity early on that 273 

strengthens as training progresses, in parallel with behavior. While these changes may simply reflect training-274 

related refinements in ascending acoustic input, we cannot rule out the possibility that they reflect changes in 275 

perceived stimulus salience 91 or perceptual confidence 86–88 . Regardless, our fiber photometry experiments 276 

revealed that these signals are fed back to the AC, where they may serve to further boost or refine responses 277 

to the behaviorally-relevant AM stimulus 59,61,73 .278 

At first glance, our findings may appear inconsistent with recent work showing that OFC activity increases during 279 

passive auditory habituation and that OFC silencing reverses habituation-related changes in AC 80 . However, our 280 

study fundamentally differs in behavioral context. Whereas habituation reduces the behavioral relevance of 281 

repeated sounds, perceptual learning increases the detectability of behaviorally relevant sensory features. 282 

Taken together, both studies suggest that OFC adaptively reshapes auditory cortical representations according 283 

to behavioral demands, suppressing responses to irrelevant stimuli while enhancing responses to relevant ones. 284 

Thus, OFC may act to dynamically sculpt sensory representations based on their relevance for behavior. 285 

Target specificity of training-related changes in OFC signaling 286 

Although OFC conveys both outcome-related and AM-depth signals to the visual cortex during auditory task 287 

performance, neither signal changed with perceptual learning. This result strengthens the argument that OFC 288 

may broadcast task state information across the brain to guide behavior 74 , while selectively strengthening 289 

information transfer to the sensory cortical area directly involved in solving the task at hand 54,58,62 .290 

It remains unknown whether improvements in AM depth detection generalize to analogous perceptual 291 

judgements in other sensory modalities (such as detecting weak modulations of light intensity). However, some 292 

forms of perceptual learning, particularly temporal interval discrimination, can transfer across sensory 293 

modalities 92–96 . An intriguing open question is therefore whether auditory temporal interval discrimination 294 

learning induces similar plasticity in OFC projections to both auditory and visual cortex. If so, such a finding 295 

would suggest that differential recruitment of OFC-to-sensory cortical pathways contributes to the variability in 296 

perceptual learning transfer observed across tasks, and may ultimately inform strategies for promoting learning 297 

generalization. 298

STAR METHODS 423 

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS 424 

Subjects 425 

Adult Mongolian gerbils ( Meriones unguiculatus ; n = 26, 16 females) used in this study were raised from 426 

commercially obtained breeder pairs (Charles River). Animals were housed with one or more same-sex 427 

littermates under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle and provided with ad libitum food and enrichment. Water access was 428 

ad libitum until animals were placed on controlled water access for behavioral experiments. All procedures were 429 

approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Maryland College Park. 430 

METHOD DETAILS 431 

Behavioral task and set up 432 

Animals were trained on an aversive go/no -go AM detection task as previously described 19,20,63,97 . A custom-433 

designed test arena (CCMI Plastics) containing a stainless-steel water spout and stainless-steel floor plate was 434 

placed in the center of a sound attenuating booth (GretchKen). Infrared beam-break detection of the animal 435 

snout near the spout triggered water delivery via a syringe pump (NE-1000, New Era Pump Systems). Stimuli 436 

were delivered via a calibrated free-field speaker (DX25TG59-04 1" Fabric Dome Tweeter, Peerless by 437 

Tymphany) positioned directly above the test cage. Electrical shocks were delivered via an H13-15 Precision 438 

Animal Shocker (Colbourn) gated by an H13-XX-SP01 electrical grounding relay (Colbourn) to minimize 439 

electrical noise during electrophysiological recordings. Behavior was monitored remotely via a Logitech C270 440 

USB webcam. Data acquisition, sound delivery, and digital outputs were controlled via an RZ6 multifunction 441 

processor (Tucker Davis Technologies, TDT) and the ePsych MATLAB toolbox 442 

(https://github.com/dstolz/epsych). 443 

Behavioral training and analysis 444 

Animals first learned to drink steadily from a spout while in the presence of a continuous unmodulated broadband 445 

noise (0.1-20 kHz, 45 dB SPL). Animals then underwent associative training, during which they learned to 446 

withdraw from the spout when the sound changed to AM noise (5 Hz rate, 100% modulation depth, 1 second 447 

duration) to avoid a mildly aversive shock (0.5-1 mA, 300 msec). 448 

No-go trials were defined as discrete 1-sec windows during the continuous non-AM noise stream. Go trials 449 

consisted of 1-sec presentations of AM noise. Go trials were pseudorandomly interspersed with 3-5 no-go trials. 450 

Trials were only triggered when animals made contact with the water spout. 451 

The animal’s spout contact was evaluated during the last 100 msec of each trial. If the animal broke spout 452 

contact for >50 msec during this monitoring window, the response was scored as a spout withdrawal. Spout 453 

withdrawal was considered a ‘hit’ on go trials and a ‘false alarm’ on no-go trials. Maintaining spout contact was 454 

considered a ‘miss’ on go trials and a ‘correct reject’ on no-go trials (Figure 1A). Miss trials were punished with 455 

a shock. Shock intensity was initially set low and adjusted individually due to variability in pain sensitivity across 456 

subjects 98 .457

Hit and false alarm rates were set to a floor of 5% and a ceiling of 95% to avoid infinite d’ values. 460 

Animals initially learned the association by training with a single, fully modulated (100% depth) AM stimulus. 461 

Following task acquisition ( d' ≥ 2 on two consecutive days), animals transitioned to perceptual training 19 . During 462 

perceptual training, the task was made progressively more challenging by reducing the AM depth across 463 

sessions. Because the decision axis for AM detection is logarithmic 100 , depths are typically referred to on a dB 464 

scale relative to 100% depth. We adopt that convention here. Thus, 0 dB refers to fully modulated (100% depth) 465 

noise, and negative numbers refer to shallower depths. This dB scale should not be confused with dB SPL, 466 

which indicates the root-mean-squared (RMS) sound level of the stimulus. To avoid confounding loudness 467 

perception with depth perception, the gain of the AM signal was adjusted to control for changes in average power 468 

across AM depths 101 .469 

Each perceptual training session began with the presentation of 0 dB ‘reminder’ trials to ensure the animal was 470 

willing to perform the task. Once the animal achieved three consecutive hits or consumed 0.5 mL of water 471 

(whichever came first), we began delivering no-go and go trials as described above. 472 

During the first perceptual training session, animals were initially presented with five AM depths selected to 473 

bracket the expected detection threshold based on prior literature (0 to -12 dB re: 100% AM, in -3 dB 474 

steps) 19,20,63,97,102 . The starting depths in subsequent sessions depended on the animal's threshold estimate from 475 

the previous session. If needed, stimulus values were increased or decreased once per session using 476 

predetermined step sizes to maintain threshold bracketing. Perceptual training sessions lasted until animals 477 

were satiated or until ~100 AM trials were completed, whichever happened first. Across all sessions, animals 478 

completed a median of 100 AM trials/session (range: 33-109). 479 

To maintain threshold bracketing throughout perceptual training, some AM depths below the animal’s estimated 480 

detection threshold were presented. Punishing animals for failing to detect these sub-threshold stimuli would 481 

likely increase false alarm rates and/or reduce task engagement. Therefore, sub-threshold AM depths were 482 

never paired with a shock. Previous studies have shown that this approach does not prevent perceptual 483 

learning 19,20,63,97 and does not result in secondary conditioning to the presence or absence of shock 103 . For 484 

response-aligned neural analyses, only hits, false alarms and suprathreshold (i.e., shocked) misses were 485 

analyzed. 486 

For each perceptual training session, percent correct responses were plotted as a function of AM depth and fit 487 

with a cumulative Gaussian function using the open-source toolbox psignifit 4 104 . We clamped the fit to the lowest 488 

AM depth presented (options.stimulusRange = [lowest AM, -0.01]); otherwise, default parameters were used. 489 

After fitting, data were converted back to d’ space. Thresholds were defined as the AM depth at which the fitted 490 

curve crossed d’ = 1. 491 

To identify learners and non-learners, we quantified performance using two metrics. First, improvement was 492 

calculated as the difference between the threshold on the first day of perceptual training and the average 493 

threshold across the final three training days. Second, each subject’s threshold was plotted as a function of 494 

log 10 (day), and the slope of a linear regression fit to these data was used as a measure of the rate and direction 495 

of threshold change. Animals exhibiting either negative improvement or a positive regression slope, both 496 

indicative of worsening perceptual performance, were classified as non-learners and excluded from analyses of 497 

learning-related changes (Figure S1A). 498 

Neural measures (described below) were compared between early (day 1) and late (days 8-10) stages of 499 

perceptual training. Late training was defined as the period after perceptual performance had reached 500 

asymptote. To estimate the timing of asymptote, mean AM detection thresholds were plotted as a function of 501

warming pad, and the head was secured in a stereotaxic frame (Kopf). Isoflurane (1-3%) and oxygen (>93%) 514 

were steadily delivered at a flow rate of 2 L/min throughout the surgery. Lubricant ophthalmic ointment was 515

9.05-9.50 mm rostral, 2.50-3.20 mm ventral, angle=0° from vertical. Following electrode implantation, the 521

implant was secured and protected with dental cement (Palacos). Animals recovered on heat under observation 524 

and were provided with minocycline-treated drinking water for the following seven days. Controlled water access 525 

and behavioral experiments began at least seven days after surgery. 526 

Electrophysiology acquisition and processing 527 

Electrophysiological activity was recorded extracellularly from the OFC of freely-moving animals during 528 

behavioral training sessions that took place every 24-48 hours. Signals were collected using one of two 529 

acquisition systems: (I) Analog signals were amplified and digitized at 24.4 kHz (PZ5; Tucker Davis 530 

Technologies, TDT) via a 64-channel wireless headstage and receiver (W64, Triangle BioSystems), and 531 

synchronized with behavioral timestamps through RZ2 and RZ6 processors running the Synapse software suite 532 

(TDT) and the Synapse API for MATLAB (MathWorks). Raw signals were saved using an RS4 data streamer 533 

(TDT). (II) Signals were amplified and digitized at 30 kHz by a 64-channel RHD headstage (Intan Technologies) 534 

connected to a slip-ring commutator (Taidacent; purchased from Amazon), and synchronized with behavioral 535 

timestamps via an RHD recording controller (Intan) and OpenEphys software 109 .536 

Offline signals were high-pass filtered at 150 Hz and common-median referenced across channels. Spike sorting 537 

was performed using Kilosort4 110 . Sorting results were manually curated using Phy ( https://github.com/cortex-538 

lab/phy ). During curation, clusters were classified as putative single units based on a high signal-to-noise ratio, 539 

a clear refractory period gap in the autocorrelogram, and cross-correlogram isolation from neighboring clusters. 540 

Following curation, unit quality was assessed using Allen Brain Institute metrics 541 

(https://allensdk.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_static/examples/nb/ecephys_quality_metrics.html ): 'presence ratio', 542 

'amplitude cutoff fraction missing', and 'ISI violation false-positive rate' (isi_threshold = 1.5 ms; min_ISI = 0.15 543

Following unit isolation, firing rates were computed across three temporal windows: (1) Non-AM: a 0.9-sec 548 

window during correct rejection trials, when the animal was drinking from the spout prior to AM onset; (2) AM: a 549 

0.9-sec window following AM stimulus onset; (3) Outcome: a 0.9-sec window following spout offset. Because 550 

miss trials were accompanied by a shock artifact spanning approximately ±0.3 sec around spout offset, the 551 

response window was shifted by 0.3 sec on miss trials to exclude the artifact. 552 

To classify units by spike-waveform shape, we analyzed mean extracellular waveforms from well-isolated single 553 

units at the recording channel with largest amplitude. From each waveform we extracted three features (Figure 554 

S2B). Peak-to-peak duration was the time between the principal peak and the subsequent smaller peak. The 555 

peak-to-peak ratio was the absolute ratio of the first and second peak amplitudes. Repolarization duration 112 556 

was the time from the second peak to the subsequent inflection point, defined as the first zero-crossing of the 557 

second derivative of the waveform occurring after the second peak. Units with atypical waveforms—those whose 558 

principal trough amplitude was smaller than the subsequent peak—were excluded. These three features were 559 

z-scored and submitted to k-means clustering (k = 2, 25 random initializations). Only regular-spiking units (cluster 560 

with higher peak-to-peak duration; putative excitatory neurons) were analyzed in this study. 561 

Tonic spike counts (i.e., during the non-AM period while animals quietly drank from the spout) showed 562 

substantial overdispersion relative to a Poisson process (Goodness of Poisson fit Akaike Information Criterion 563 

(AIC)= 4 x 10 3) and were better described by a negative binomial distribution (AIC = 2.9 x 10 3 ; Likelihood ratio 564 

test of Poisson versus binomial fits: χ²(1) = 1.1 x 10 3, p < 0.001), characteristic of cortical neurons 113–115 .565 

Therefore, firing rates were natural log-transformed [ln(FR + 1); the +1 offset avoids ln(0)] to stabilize variance 566 

across the population prior to baseline subtraction 116 , yielding a log-ratio of evoked to baseline (non-AM) firing. 567 

This baseline-normalized log ratio is henceforth referred to as 'normalized firing'. 568

Previous studies defined neurometric threshold as the AM depth at which the neurometric fit crossed a d’ of 596 

119,97,117 . These studies focused on auditory cortical recordings and pooled hit and miss trials when calculating 597 

neural d’ . In contrast, our analyses were restricted to either hit or miss trials, substantially reducing the number 598 

of trials contributing to each neural d’ estimate. Because the firing rate variance inversely scales with the number 599 

of observations, and because we were recording in a higher-order brain region rather than a classical auditory 600 

structure, we expected to observe lower d’ values than those reported previously. To account for this difference, 601 

we chose a more liberal criterion of d’ = 0.5 when defining neurometric threshold. Units exhibiting monotonic fits 602 

that significantly differed from a flat line and reached an absolute d’ value greater than 0.5 were therefore 603 

classified as AM depth sensitive. 604 

Permutation test for task-evoked firing rate modulation 605 

To identify units responsive to trial outcome, we employed a permutation test comparing log-transformed firing 606 

rates during the outcome period to those during the preceding non-AM period on a trial-by-trial basis. For each 607 

unit, the observed test statistic was the difference in mean firing rates between the outcome and non-AM periods. 608 

A null distribution was generated by randomly permuting the period labels (outcome vs. non-AM) across all trials 609 

999 times and recomputing the mean difference on each iteration, yielding a distribution of differences expected 610 

under the null hypothesis of no outcome-dependent modulation. A two-tailed p-value was computed as the 611 

proportion of permuted differences whose absolute value equaled or exceeded the absolute observed 612 

difference. This test was performed separately for each unit × trial type combination. Units with insufficient data 613 

(mean log firing rate < 0.1, fewer than 2 trials, or zero variance in paired differences) were excluded and classified 614 

as unresponsive. 615
