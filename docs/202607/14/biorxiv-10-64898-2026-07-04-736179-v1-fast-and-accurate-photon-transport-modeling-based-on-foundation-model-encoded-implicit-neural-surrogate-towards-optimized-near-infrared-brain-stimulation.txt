Abstract. Clinical treatment planning of near-infrared (NIR) brain stimulation requires patient-specic light dosimetry to optimize uence delivery to cortical targets. The gold-standard Monte Carlo (MC) pho-ton transport forward solver is accurate but computationally expensive and non-dierentiable for personalized inverse design across subjects. Here, we present a foundation-model (FM)-encoded, dierentiable implicit-neural surrogate for the MC solver. A pretrained 3D MRI/CT foundation model ,VISTA3D, is domain-adapted to head phantoms with known optical properties to encode the subject anatomy. Next, an implicit neural representation is used to predict light uence at arbitrary continuous coordinates. This formulation enables o-grid queries and gradients with respect to illumination parameters. Trained with a physics-informed, decade-stratied loss, the surrogate attains 

R2 ≈ 0.90 on held-out subjects. Ablation results show that the FM ben-et is contingent on domain adaptation. Benchmarked against standard learned surrogates, our model is the most accurate in the high-dose re-gion and best on dose-delity metrics ( γ-index, treated-volume DICE). Finally, gradient-based optimization through the surrogate recovers MC-consistent illumination congurations 50 240 × faster.

# 1 Introduction 

Near-infrared (NIR) brain stimulation, also known as transcranial photobiomod-ulation (tPBM), is a promising non-invasive treatment for neurodegenerative disorders [7, 14, 21]. Despite advantages such as minimal side eects and home-care suitability, its therapeutic ecacy relies on precise, patient-specic light dosimetry and dynamic closed-loop optimization. Currently, its clinical transla-tion remains hindered by static, non-personalized intervention strategies. Light propagation is strongly dictated by subject-specic anatomical variations across the scalp, skull, cerebrospinal uid (CSF), and brain tissues, leaving only 12% 

of the incident optical energy reaches the brain parenchyma [4]. Given the high optical heterogeneity of these structural layers, illumination parameters ,includ-ing source positioning, direction, and beam prole, profoundly impact energy delivery eciency [17, 2]. Consequently, there is a critical need to enable person-alized and self-adaptive illumination optimization that maximizes target light uence while adhering to safety constraints. Currently, Monte Carlo (MC) photon-transport simulations are widely used as reference models for heterogeneous head tissue [23]. Although GPU-accelerated MC solvers [5, 20] have improved forward evaluation speeds, they remain poorly suited for patient-specic inverse design. Gradient-based op-timization is hindered because conventional MC solvers are stochastic and non-dierentiable with respect to illumination parameters, requiring computa-tionally prohibitive dense searches over source congurations. While existing learning-based surrogates [10, 6] and denoisers [13] accelerate predictions, their reliance on xed grids often blurs sharp near-source peaks and limits cross-subject generalization. To overcome these limitations, we propose a foundation-model (FM)-encoded dierentiable implicit neural surrogate for photon-transport modeling. The framework factorizes the MC forward model into an anatomy-aware en-coder and a source-conditioned implicit neural representation (INR) [19, 15, 22]. Initialized from a 3-D medical-imaging foundation model (VISTA3D [9]) and domain-adapted via parameter-ecient ne-tuning [11, 8], the encoder extracts multi-scale anatomical priors from optical-property phantoms. The INR combines these anatomical features with source parameters and spatial coordinates to estimate light uence at arbitrary locations, enabling o-grid queries and dierentiable optimization of illumination parameters. In summary, our contributions include: (i) a dierentiable FM-encoded implicit neural photon-transport surrogate that supports ecient source-conditioned uence evaluation and gradient computation; (ii) a controlled benchmark against grid, operator, and coordinate surrogates, showing superior accuracy in high-uence regions and improved dose-delity metrics, including the γ-index and treated-volume DICE, with ablations conrming the importance of domain adaptation; and (iii) a gradient-based inverse-design framework that recovers MC-consistent illumination optima 50 240 × faster.

# 2 Method 

2.1 FM-encoded INR Surrogate and Inverse Design 

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the surrogate couples an anatomy-aware FM encoder with a source-conditioned INR to predict light uence Φ (W cm −2) from anatomy, illumination parameters, and spatial coordinates. The encoder is ini-tialized from a VISTA3D FM variant (NV-Segment-CTMR) pretrained on more than 30 , 000 CT and MRI scans covering 345+ anatomical classes [9]. We adapt it to the phantoms using LoRA with rank 32 and α = 64 [11], together with a masked-autoencoder (MAE) objective [8]. During adaptation, 64 3 patches are randomly cropped from the phantoms with a mask ratio of 0.75 and a patch size of 83. Each phantom is represented as a four-channel volume (μa, μ s, g, n ) at 

810 nm and normalized over xed physical ranges. After adaptation, the encoder is frozen and produces a subject-level ve-scale anatomical feature pyramid with channel dimensions 48 /96 /192 /384 /768 .For a continuous coordinate x, the multi-scale feature is tri-linearly sam-pled and combined with physics-informed conditioning, including a Fourier po-sitional encoding [16] and source-relative geometric features, i.e., source position, direction, normalized distance, and source-to-point angle. A compact four-layer GELU MLP with hidden width 512 then predicts y(x) = log 10 Φ(x). The source-relative terms provide a geometric prior for distance- and direction-dependent attenuation, while the FM-derived anatomical features encode subject-specic optical structure. This enables o-grid uence estimation and dierentiable in-verse design of illuminationgiven a target voxel, we optimize the source pose

# 3 Experiments and Results 

Dataset and Experimental Setup: We evaluate our method on two open-source datasets comprising 36 adult head phantoms: ScatterBrains [18] ( 16 heads with 5 segmented tissue classes) and BrainWeb [3, 1] ( 20 phantoms with 9 tissue classes, additionally modeling vessel, fat, muscle, dura and marrow). Each voxel is mapped to optical properties (μa, μ s, g, n ) at 810 nm on a 1 mm isotropic grid, and a disk source (radius 10 mm) is parameterized by elevation A ∈ [45 ◦, 90 ◦]

and azimuth B ∈ [−90 ◦, 90 ◦]. Per-voxel ground-truth log 10 Φ is generated with MCX ( 5×10 7 photons) for 96 source poses per head on a 10 ◦ grid. Phantoms are partitioned by subject into disjoint train/validation/test sets ( 26 /5/5, with both datasets represented in each). Applied to the training heads only, an equivariant 

±5◦ rotation and a ±5◦ o-grid angle perturbation with exact MCX targets expand the corpus to 7,296 (head, source-pose) scenes. 

Test-Set Performance: On the held-out subjects, the surrogate reached 

R2 = 0 .90 on log 10 Φ, SSIM 0.98 , PSNR 31 .1 dB, a γ (3% /2 px) of 0.44 ± 0.04 

(per-subject mean ± s.d. over 3 training seeds), and uence DICE 0.84 , with median deep-tissue relative error ≈19% and CSF layer error ≈24% . The across-seed s.d. was small ( R2 ± 0.008 , shallow ±0.003 ). A single forward solve cost 

≈0.014 s on one GPU versus 713 s per MCX solve (Fig. 4g). The surrogate reproduces the MC uence 3D distributions (Fig. 2), and tracks the source-to-brain proles with smooth o-grid ( 0.2 mm) and low per-tissue error (Fig. 3).

Ablation Studies: We evaluate input features, loss design, and encoder provenance by individually removing them from the validation-tuned model and testing on the test set (Table 2). Headline performance and critical loss ablations (no-PINN, no-linear) are reported as three-seed means. Other components use a single seed to assess relative eects. Every input feature contributes signicantly: removing the source-angle light code or positional encoding drops global R2 from 

0.91 to 0.58 and 0.67 , respectively. Source-relative features (r, cos θ) are critical for the near-source shell, where their removal drastically increases shallow error from 0.30 to 0.76 . In loss design, decade weighting is essential (geometric alter-natives drive R2 negative), and diusion-PINN improves the mean R2 by ≈0.19 ,albeit with high seed variance ( R2 ± 0.13 over 3 seeds). Crucially, removing the near-source linear term nearly doubles shallow error and degrades dose metrics, despite leaving deep error and global R2 unchanged. For encoder provenance, an unadapted FM performs worst, conrming that MAE-based LoRA ne-tuning is mandatory to realign the FM to the optical property domain. 

# 4 Conclusion and Discussion 

We present a fast ( ∼10 3× over MCX), dierentiable photon-transport surro-gate, coupling a domain-adapted 3-D medical-FM encoder for illumination op-timization. Three ndings are crucial: (1) domain adaptation is mandatory, as unadapted encoders fail on optical volumes whereas LoRA +MAE realignment achieves state-of-the-art; (2) a near-source linear loss ensures critical dose delity,

Limitations and future work. Generalization remains bounded by dataset complexity. While INR is spatially continuous, discrete tissue rounding and source-target landscapes limit the practical benet of sub-degree pose optimiza-tion. Finally, to translate these simulated, single-wavelength results to multi-wavelength, in vivo diuse-optics validation is necessary for clinical transla-tion. Despite these bounds, our FM-encoded INR delivers the continuous, peak-accurate eld required for practical cross-subject NIR dosimetry.
