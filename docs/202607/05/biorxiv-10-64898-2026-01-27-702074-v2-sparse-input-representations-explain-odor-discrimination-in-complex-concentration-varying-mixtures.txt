# SUMMARY 22 

In natural environments, animals must detect behaviorally relevant odors despite variability in 23 

both odor mixture composition and stimulus intensity. Although mice can identify salient odors 24 

embedded in complex mixtures, how target concentration and background complexity jointly con- 25 

strain discrimination remains unclear. We trained mice in a two-alternative forced choice task to 26 

identify target odors embedded in mixtures containing up to 16 background components. Af- 27 

ter performance stabilized, we systematically varied target odor concentration. Discrimination 28 

accuracy declined with decreasing target concentration but showed little additional dependence 29 

on background complexity. Using a biophysically grounded model of olfactory bulb glomerular 30 

responses, we show that linear decoding reproduces behavioral performance when intrinsic neu- 31 

ral noise dominates over background-driven variability. Manifold capacity analysis revealed that 32 

neural representations remain efficiently structured for odor discrimination despite variation in 33 

background complexity. These results define a noise-limited regime of olfactory discrimination 34 

in which target detectability is primarily constrained by neural sensitivity rather than background 35 

interference. 36

# INTRODUCTION 39 

Animals rely on sensory inputs to identify food 1,2 , locate mates 3, avoid predators 4,5 , and detect 40 

environmental hazards 6,7 . In natural environments, these signals are embedded within complex 41 

and dynamic sensory scenes, where relevant cues must be extracted from competing back- 42 

ground inputs. The process of scene analysis enables the brain to filter and organize sensory 43 

information, enhancing salient signals while suppressing irrelevant or interfering background ac- 44 

tivity 8,9 . This challenge is conserved across modalities. In audition, it is exemplified by the 45 

“cocktail party effect,” where a single voice can be isolated amid background noise 10,11 , and in 46 

vision, by figure-ground segmentation in cluttered environments 12,13 . Across these systems, per- 47 

ceptual limits often reflect a balance between the strength of relevant signals and interference 48 

from background inputs. 49 

Whether the olfactory system supports analogous forms of scene analysis, and what factors 50 

limit this ability, remains unclear 14–18 . In recent work, we demonstrated that mice can detect be- 51 

haviorally relevant target odors embedded in complex, variable backgrounds 19 . However, perfor- 52 

mance deteriorates when background odors activate overlapping glomeruli in the olfactory bulb, 53 

suggesting that interference at the level of population representations can obscure target signals. 54 

The degree of interference can also depend on the relationship between target and background 55 

odors. In both humans and rodents, backgrounds that are perceptually or neurally similar to the 56 

target can impair detection more strongly than dissimilar backgrounds, suggesting that target- 57 

background congruence is an important determinant of figure-ground segregation 19–21 . Com- 58 

putational analyses have shown that odor representations can remain linearly separable at the 59 

population level under certain conditions 22 , and humans are similarly capable of identifying indi- 60 

vidual mixture components 23 . These findings raise the question of what ultimately limits mixture 61 

discrimination: interference from overlapping background activity, insufficient signal strength, or 62 

an interaction between the two. 63 

A key variable that may govern this balance is odor concentration, as it relates to the sensi- 64 

tivity of the receptor repertoire. In natural environments, odor signals exhibit large fluctuations 65 

in concentration due to turbulent transport in airborne plumes 24–26 , as well as factors such as 66 

volatility and substrate disruption in surface-deposited cues. In addition, respiratory dynam- 67 

ics, including sniff frequency and inhalation volume, modulate the number of odorant molecules 68 

reaching the epithelium 27–29 . Together, these factors can strongly influence the strength and 69 

reliability of sensory input, effectively placing the system in different noise regimes. Despite 70 

this, how variations in signal strength interact with background interference to shape perception 71 

remains poorly understood. 72 

Here, we ask how target odor concentration and background complexity jointly determine 73 

performance in an odor-guided discrimination task. Specifically, we test whether limits on mixture 74 

discrimination arise primarily from weak sensory signals, from interference due to overlapping 75 

background activity, or from their interaction. We trained mice to identify target odors embedded 76 

in mixtures containing variable numbers of background components while systematically varying 77 

target concentration over several orders of magnitude. To interpret these behavioral results, 78 

we analyzed a biophysically grounded model of olfactory bulb glomerular activity, allowing us 79 

to examine how changes in signal strength and background structure shape the geometry and 80 

linear separability of population-level odor representations. Together, this approach provides a 81 

unified framework for understanding how neural noise and background interference constrain 82 

olfactory scene analysis. 83

# RESULTS 84 

Learning stabilizes performance across odor background complexity 85 

We trained mice to report the identity of one of two target odors by licking the corresponding 86 

water spout (Figure 1A,S1). Trials were self-initiated upon entry into the odor port, triggering a 87 

1-s odor presentation. Correct responses were rewarded with water, while incorrect choices led 88 

to reward omission. Trials ended at the first lick or after 20 s. 89 

After initial training on the two targets, mice were presented with the same targets embedded 90 

in mixtures of randomly selected background odors. During all trials, the concentration of the 91 

individual background odors remained constant (5 % (v/v)). The number of background compo- 92 

nents per trial was gradually increased until it approximated a uniform distribution across 0–16 93 

components once animals reached criterion performance (75%). 94 

Under this uniform mixture distribution, mice reached criterion performance rapidly and con- 95 

tinued to improve over approximately 1,000 trials (Figure 1B). Early in training, performance 96 

declined with increasing background complexity. This dependence diminished with continued 97 

training, and by Day 6, performance became largely independent of background complexity (Fig- 98 

ure 1C). A two-way ANOVA confirmed significant effects of background complexity and training 99 

day, as well as an interaction between them, indicating that the impact of background complexity 100 

decreased with learning. 101 

To determine whether these improvements reflected changes in behavioral strategy, we ex- 102 

amined sampling and action times. Neither sample time nor action time varied systematically 103 

with background complexity or across training (all p > 0.05 ; Figures 1D,E,S2A-C). Thus, improve- 104 

ments in performance cannot be explained by changes in sampling duration or motor execution. 105 

Together, these results show that with experience, mice reach a stable behavioral regime in 106 

which performance is largely independent of background complexity and not accounted for by 107 

changes in sampling or action timing. With a consistent behavioral baseline, we could next ask 108 

how signal strength and background interference limit mixture discrimination. 109 

Performance degrades systematically with decreasing target odor concen- 110 

tration 111 

Once mice achieved criterion performance under the uniform background distribution, we sys- 112 

tematically reduced the target odor concentration. Mice were initially trained at either 5 % or 2.5 113 

% (v/v), and concentrations were serially diluted (5.0, 2.5, 0.5, 0.25, 0.05, 0.005 %). Animals 114 

spent on average 4.19 ± 0.57 days ( 3949 ± 372 trials) per condition. As target odor concentration 115 

decreased, performance declined systematically, following a sigmoidal relationship (Figure 2A). 116 

A logistic function provided a good descriptive fit to the aggregated performance data ( R2 = 0 .58 ). 117 

To assess the effect of concentration while accounting for repeated measurements within 118 

animals, we fit a trial-level mixed-effects logistic regression with animal as a random effect. In- 119 

creasing concentration significantly increased performance ( βlog 10 conc = 0 .538 ± 0.013 ), corre- 120 

sponding to a 1.71-fold increase in the odds of a correct response for each 10-fold increase in 121 

concentration (95% CI: 1.67–1.76; p < 10 −300 ; n = 6 animals, 22,173 trials). 122 

Further analysis confirmed that the variation of performance across target odor concentra- 123 

tions was consistent across animals and training stage (Figure S3A–D). When low-concentration 124 

targets were presented unpredictably as interleaved “probe” trials rather than through continual 125 

training, performance declined more sharply with lower target concentration, indicating reduced 126 

sensitivity without repeated experience at those weak concentrations (Figure S3C). Together, 127

To determine whether these performance changes reflected altered decision strategy or dif- 130

faster, less accurate choices when sensory evidence was weak, consistent with previous ob- 133 

servations in olfactory decision-making 30 . Action times showed a similar, non-significant trend 134 

(log-linear regression, p = 0 .086 ; Figure S2C). 135 

Critically, the benefit of extended sampling depended on odor strength. At high concentra- 136 

tions, performance increased with longer sampling durations (Figure 2C), consistent with a clas- 137 

sical speed-accuracy trade-off 31,32 , whereas this relationship was absent at low concentrations. 138 

To further characterize how the benefit of extended sampling depended on odor strength, we 139 

estimated the speed-accuracy slope (the rate at which performance improved with sampling du- 140 

ration) separately at each target concentration (Figure 2D). At high concentrations (0.25%–5%), 141 

the slope was significantly positive (binomial generalized linear model, GLM, p < 10 −8 at all four 142 

concentrations), indicating that longer sampling improved discrimination. In contrast, the slope 143 

was near zero and non-significant at the two lowest concentrations (0.05%: slope = 5.4%, p 144 

= 0.21; 0.005%: slope = 0.5%, p = 0 .91 ), indicating that additional sampling provided no ben- 145 

efit when the target signal was weakest. The transition between these two regimes occurred 146 

between 0.05% and 0.25%, closely matching the inflection point of the psychometric curve (Fig- 147 

ure 2A). These results indicate that the speed-accuracy tradeoff is concentration-dependent: at 148 

high concentrations performance is limited by evidence accumulation and can be improved by 149 

longer sampling, whereas at low concentrations performance is constrained by signal strength 150 

and additional sampling is uninformative. 151 

We next asked whether the decline in performance at low concentrations reflected increased 152 

interference from background odors or a reduction in target signal strength. In a background- 153 

dominated regime, weak targets would be increasingly masked by distracting odors, leading to a 154 

strong dependence of performance on background complexity. In contrast, in a target-dominated 155 

regime, performance would decline uniformly across background conditions as target concentra- 156 

tion decreased (Figure 2E). The data support a target-dominated regime: performance declined 157 

systematically with decreasing target odor concentration and showed only a weak additional 158 

dependence on background complexity (Figure 2F). A one-way ANOVA across concentrations 159 

revealed a highly significant main effect of concentration ( F5, obs = 12 .56 , p = 1 .3 × 10 −6) but no 160 

additional effect of background complexity. Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons showed that dis- 161 

crimination accuracy at the weakest target odor concentration (0.005%) was significantly lower 162 

than at higher concentrations (0.25–5.0%; p < 0.01 ), while differences among higher concentra- 163 

tions were not significant. All behavioral effects were replicated using a distinct target-odor pair, 164 

demonstrating that these phenomena generalize across odor identities (Figure S4). 165 

Together, these results show that target odor concentration is the primary factor limiting per- 166 

formance, with background complexity playing a comparatively minor role. As target signals 167 

weaken, discrimination accuracy declines in a manner largely independent of background in- 168 

terference, consistent with a noise-limited regime. Behavioral adjustments in sampling track 169 

stimulus strength but do not account for the observed performance deficits, indicating that limits 170 

on mixture discrimination arise from the strength of the sensory signal itself. 171

substantially ( > 1 order of magnitude) stronger backgrounds or much more complex mixtures 258 

would shift performance into a truly background-limited regime (Figure 3E), a hypothesis that 259 

can be tested experimentally. 260 

Analyzing the Geometry of Glomerular Neural Manifolds 261 

The previous analyses established that neural variability and sparse coding strongly affect lin- 262 

ear decodability, but they do not address how these factors alter the structure of the underly- 263 

ing glomerular representations. In particular, it remained unclear whether weak target signals 264 

primarily reduce separability by increasing the magnitude of variability within each target rep- 265 

resentation, by increasing the number of dimensions along which the representation varies, or 266 

both. To address this, we used the neural manifold capacity framework 39–41 , which provides a 267 

geometric description of how easily neural representations can be separated. 268 

Conceptually, the point cloud of population responses to all odor mixtures containing a given 269 

target odor comprises a neural manifold. The framework quantifies both how much variability 270 

exists within a manifold and how easily different manifolds can be separated. Variability within a 271

Using this framework, we examined how neural variability and background complexity jointly 276

The framework further measures how the linear separability arises due to variability within each 281

The decoder and behavioral analyses showed that lowering target concentration impaired 285

altered representation geometry in fundamentally different ways. Under weak background and 288 

strong target conditions, increasing the number of background odors had relatively little effect 289

increasing background complexity substantially expanded effective dimension and radius while 298 

reducing manifold capacity (Figure 4B,E,H), indicating that strong backgrounds broadened the 299

third columns of the figure. We observe the stronger target concentration (Figure 4C,F,I) results 302 

in more “compressed” colored curves, meaning the impact of adding more background odors 303 

is diminished compared to weak target concentration (Figure 4A,D,G). This can be simply inter- 304 

preted as a stronger signal results in neural manifolds that remain compact and well separated 305 

even as background noise is added. 306

ing the first two columns of the figure. We found that holding target concentration fixed, both 308 

weak and strong background concentration produced a similar relationship between the back- 309 

ground complexity and effective dimension (Figure 4D,E). Thus, we can interpret the effect of 310 

adding background odors at any strength as increasing the number of dimensions along which 311 

the odor manifold varies. The magnitude of variability, however, depended on the concentration 312 

of background odors, and this is reflected in the increased effective radius of the manifolds at 313 

higher background concentration (Figure 4G,H). Together, these analyses provide a geomet- 314

reduces the separability of cue manifolds by increasing both the magnitude and dimensional- 316 

ity of background-induced variations. By contrast, weak background mixtures leave glomerular 317 

representations relatively compact and minimally distorted in terms of effective radius, while sub- 318 

stantially stronger backgrounds produce the manifold expansion and overlap characteristic of a 319 

background-limited regime. Lastly, the addition of background odors results in more dimensions 320

# DISCUSSION 326 

Odor detectability with decreasing concentration 327 

Our results show that odor discrimination performance declined systematically as target con- 328 

centration decreased, but remained surprisingly insensitive to background mixture complexity 329 

across the tested range of conditions. Although background odors are expected to interfere with 330 

perception through overlapping glomerular activation 19,22,42–44 , reducing target concentration did 331 

not selectively impair performance in more complex mixtures (Figure 2F). Instead, performance 332 

declined similarly across background conditions, indicating that weak target signals became lim- 333 

iting before background interference strongly disrupted discrimination. 334 

This result helps clarify how concentration-dependent limits on odor perception emerge in 335 

complex sensory environments. Detection thresholds for monomolecular odors are known to 336 

vary widely across many species, including humans 45–47 , presumably depending on the cognate 337 

receptors, but how these thresholds behave in complex mixtures has remained largely unex- 338 

plored. Our findings suggest that, under the conditions tested here, olfactory representations 339 

remain relatively robust to increasing background complexity even as target signals weaken. 340 

Rather than entering a strongly background-limited regime, behavioral performance was primar- 341 

ily constrained by the detectability of the target odor itself. Consistent with prior work, some 342 

background odors produced substantially greater masking of target representations than oth- 343 

ers when estimated from published glomerular datasets (Fig. S6). However, the degree of 344 

masking changed only weakly across target concentrations in the glomerular model, suggest- 345 

ing that the pronounced behavioral dependence on concentration cannot be explained solely by 346 

concentration-dependent changes in target-background masking. 347 

Speed-accuracy tradeoff 348 

The relationship between sampling duration and behavioral accuracy further supports the in- 349 

terpretation that performance was limited by weak sensory signals rather than by background 350 

interference. At high target concentrations, mice benefited from longer sampling times, consis- 351 

tent with a classical speed-accuracy tradeoff in olfactory decision-making 31,48,49 . Under these 352 

conditions, longer odor sampling likely allowed animals to accumulate additional sensory evi- 353 

dence, improving discrimination performance (Figure 2C-D). 354 

As target concentration decreased, however, the relationship between sampling duration and 355 

performance became progressively weaker. Although discrimination accuracy declined substan- 356 

tially at low concentrations, longer sampling no longer improved performance, suggesting that 357 

additional sampling provided little useful information in this signal-poor regime. Consistent with 358 

this interpretation, sampling times modestly decreased as target concentration declined (Fig- 359 

ure 2B), similar to observations in related odor mixture discrimination tasks 30 . Together, these 360 

results suggest that when target signals become weak, neural variability limits the quality of 361 

sensory evidence itself, such that extending sampling duration cannot substantially improve dis- 362 

crimination accuracy. 363

Target odor signal-to-noise limits performance 364 

The weak dependence of behavioral performance on background complexity suggests that, un- 365 

der our task conditions, odor discrimination was limited primarily by target detectability rather 366 

than by strong mixture interference. This result is not trivial given the known nonlinearities of 367 

olfactory coding. Neural responses throughout the olfactory system vary strongly with odor 368 

concentration 34,45,50 , and odor mixtures produce complex receptor-level interactions including 369 

suppression and antagonism 33,51,52 . Moreover, concentration-dependent odor identity decoding 370 

differs across olfactory bulb output channels, with tufted cells supporting more concentration- 371 

invariant decoding than mitral cells 53–56 . These observations, together with prior modeling show- 372 

ing that nonlinear mixture interactions can reduce component decodability 57 suggest that weak 373 

target odors embedded within mixtures could become increasingly vulnerable to masking by 374 

background activity. 375 

To understand why this did not occur behaviorally, we analyzed a biophysical model of glomeru- 376 

lar activity across different signal and background regimes. Decoder performance best matched 377 

behavior in a weak-background regime where target odors activated relatively sparse glomerular 378 

representations and background odors produced limited overlap with target-selective glomeruli. 379 

Under these conditions, discrimination performance declined primarily because Poisson-like 380 

neural variability progressively obscured weak target signals. In contrast, stronger background 381 

inputs produced the expected background-limited regime, where increasing mixture complexity 382 

substantially impaired decoding as target concentration decreased. 383 

These results suggest that sparse sensory representations help preserve odor discriminabil- 384 

ity even in complex mixtures. Because individual odorants activate relatively restricted subsets 385 

of glomeruli, accurate decoding can be achieved by selectively weighting a limited set of infor- 386 

mative inputs while largely ignoring irrelevant background activity. Consistent with this interpre- 387 

tation, sparse readouts better reproduced behavioral performance than dense decoders pooling 388 

broadly across glomeruli, and the resulting sparsity levels aligned with experimentally measured 389 

glomerular response sparsity 34,38 . This interpretation extends earlier work showing that olfactory 390 

bulb representations can support robust linear decoding of odor mixtures 22 across a broader 391 

range of target concentrations and background conditions. 392 

Importantly, our results indicate that early olfactory representations are already sufficiently 393 

informative to support robust odor mixture discrimination through simple linear decoding. This 394 

interpretation is consistent with our re-analysis of published OSN and piriform cortical bouton 395 

recordings 58 , as well as recent simultaneous recordings suggesting greater mixture discrim- 396 

inability in olfactory bulb than piriform cortex representations 59 . However, this does not imply 397 

that downstream nonlinear processing is unnecessary in all contexts. Rather, our results suggest 398 

that under the conditions studied here, olfactory bulb representations remain sufficiently separa- 399 

ble for simple linear readout, whereas stronger interference regimes may benefit from additional 400 

cortical transformations 60 . Together, these findings support a model in which downstream cor- 401 

tical circuits may refine, contextualize, or associate odor representations, while olfactory bulb 402 

representations remain sufficiently separable to support robust odor discrimination under the 403 

conditions examined here. 404 

Geometry of odor manifolds 405 

To understand how neural variability and background odors alter odor discriminability at the 406 

population level, we analyzed the geometry of glomerular activity manifolds generated by the 407 

model 41 . This framework provides a geometric description of how easily odor representations 408 

can be linearly separated and how different noise sources distort the underlying sensory repre- 409 

sentation. 410

Manifold analysis revealed that neural variability and background mixtures affected represen- 411 

tation geometry in qualitatively different ways. Poisson-like neural noise consistently reduced 412 

manifold separability by increasing the effective dimensionality and spread of odor representa- 413 

tions (Figure 4). In contrast, weak background mixtures produced comparatively modest geo- 414 

metric distortions, leaving odor manifolds relatively compact and well separated even as target 415 

concentration decreased. Only under stronger background conditions did increasing mixture 416 

complexity substantially expand manifold structure and increase overlap between odor repre- 417 

sentations, consistent with the emergence of a background-limited regime. 418 

This geometric interpretation closely mirrors the behavioral and decoding results. Decreasing 419 

target concentration reduced discrimination performance broadly across all background condi- 420 

tions, consistent with neural variability globally degrading separability of the target representa- 421 

tions. At the same time, the relatively weak effect of background complexity behaviorally cor- 422 

responded to the minimal manifold distortions produced by weak background mixtures in the 423 

model. Together, these results suggest that sparse olfactory representations preserve robust 424 

odor separability across complex mixtures until weak target signals and neural variability be- 425 

come the dominant limitations on discrimination performance. 426 

Future work 427 

Our results generate several experimentally testable predictions about how sensory variabil- 428 

ity and background structure shape odor discrimination. First, glomerular population record- 429 

ings across target concentrations should reproduce the behavioral concentration-performance 430 

relationship when analyzed using linear decoders. Second, trial-to-trial decoding variability 431 

should be dominated by neural noise aligned with the target representation rather than by large 432 

background-dependent distortions of the sensory code. Finally, sufficiently strong background in- 433 

puts or substantially weaker target concentrations should eventually drive the system into a truly 434 

background-limited regime characterized by increased representational overlap and reduced lin- 435 

ear separability. 436 

An important next step will be to determine how these principles extend to more naturalis- 437 

tic olfactory environments. Although our mixtures contained up to 16 odor components, natural 438 

odor scenes can contain substantially larger numbers of volatile compounds. However, many 439 

natural scenes may still possess relatively sparse effective structure, with only a subset of high- 440 

concentration odorants dominating sensory representations. Future experiments using stronger 441 

backgrounds or hierarchically structured “mixtures of mixtures” could help determine when back- 442 

ground interference becomes fully masking and whether the independence between target con- 443 

centration and background complexity persists under these conditions. 444 

More broadly, simultaneous recordings across olfactory bulb and cortical circuits during odor- 445 

guided behavior could clarify how representational geometry evolves across processing stages. 446 

In particular, relating manifold structure and decoder performance directly to behavioral variabil- 447 

ity may help constrain computational models of olfactory perception 41 . An important direction 448 

will be identifying the biophysical and circuit-level mechanisms that preserve separability in ol- 449 

factory representations despite background variability. One possible contributor is heterogeneity 450 

in glomerular response amplitudes and tuning widths, which theoretical and experimental studies 451 

in sensory cortex suggest can reshape representational geometry by increasing centroid sepa- 452 

ration or decorrelating manifold structure 61 . Similar mechanisms may contribute to maintaining 453 

compact and linearly separable odor representations in the olfactory bulb. 454

Concluding remarks 455 

In summary, our experiments reveal that the effects of target concentration and background com- 456 

plexity on odor discrimination are largely independent. Modeling identifies a regime of strong 457 

neural noise and weak background interference that reproduces this behavioral robustness, ex- 458 

plaining why faint targets remain detectable even in complex mixtures. By combining behavioral 459 

data, biophysical modeling, and manifold analysis, we show that glomerular representations of 460 

odor mixtures remain sparse and linearly decodable under challenging conditions. These results 461 

imply that the olfactory bulb provides a robust substrate for odor identity discrimination, while 462 

downstream cortical areas may primarily refine or contextualize this information, a direction for 463 

future investigation. 464 

Limitations of the study 465 

Our study has several important limitations. In the behavioral experiments, mice discriminated 466 

between two target odors embedded within artificial background mixtures. Natural odor environ- 467 

ments are often more dynamic, containing fluctuating concentrations, temporal structure, and 468 

correlations between odor sources that were not represented here. In addition, our task involved 469 

only a small number of behaviorally relevant odors. It remains possible that performance would 470 

be more strongly affected in environments containing larger numbers of salient odor sources that 471 

compete for behavioral relevance. 472 

Our modeling analyses focused on representations at the level of olfactory bulb glomeruli 473 

and on the performance of linear readouts. Although the modeled receptor sensitivity distri- 474 

bution is consistent with experimental characterization of olfactory receptors, we do not have 475 

a method to calibrate between simulated odor mixture and experimental odor concentrations. 476 

Thus, we empirically search for a concentration regime in the model where decoding matches 477 

behavior performance qualitatively and retrodictively compare the population response sparsity 478 

to glomerular response data. 479 

To capture the 0-background performance drops in the target-dominated regime, we needed 480 

neural noise in the response. We assumed Poisson-like neural noise model without correla- 481 

tions across glomeruli, though real glomerular responses are likely to display noise correlations. 482 

In terms of readout, downstream regions may transform glomerular representations through 483 

nonlinear computations, learning-dependent plasticity, recurrent network dynamics, and state- 484 

dependent modulation. Such processes could improve the robustness of odor representations 485 

or alter how information is extracted from glomerular activity, potentially influencing behavioral 486 

performance in ways not captured by our framework. 487 

Our manifold analyses focused on the geometry of static population representations and lin- 488 

ear separability. We did not examine how temporal dynamics within sniff cycles, representation 489 

learning, noise correlations, or cortical feedback reshape these manifolds. Incorporating these 490 

factors may reveal additional trends in the geometric features that contribute to odor discrimina- 491 

tion. 492 

Our results support the conclusion that neural noise, rather than background interference, 493 

is the primary factor limiting odor discrimination under the conditions examined here. However, 494 

under more complex odor scenes, olfactory processing may enter a different regime in which 495 

background interference becomes a dominant constraint on performance. The target-dominated 496 

regime identified here may therefore represent one portion of a broader continuum of olfactory 497 

operating conditions. 498

Experimental model and study participant details 748 

Eleven male adult Black6/J (000664) were trained on the behavioral task. All animals were 749 

obtained from Jackson Laboratories and maintained within Harvard University’s Biological Re- 750 

search Infrastructure (BRI). Mice were housed in an inverted 12 h light cycle and fed ad libitum. 751 

Animals were 2–12 months old at the time of the experiments. All experiments were performed 752 

in accordance with the guidelines set by the National Institutes of Health and approved by the 753 

Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Harvard University. 754 

Method details 755 

Behavioral Apparatus 756 

The olfactometer (S1 was custom built in house using a series of clear odor vials, soft tubing, 757 

and solenoid valves. The behavior chamber consisted of a custom built sound proofing box, an 758 

odor release port utilizing an IR beam break emitter and sensor, and two water releasing ports 759 

utilizing capacitive lick sensors and solenoid controlled water release. 760 

Odor port activation, odor release, and monitoring of licking and water rewards were con- 761 

trolled using computer interface hardware (data acquisition boards and I/O controllers, National 762 

Instruments) and custom software written in LabView. The mouse was continuously monitored 763 

using a web camera during behavior sessions. 764 

Odors Used 765 

The following odors were used as target odors: methyl tiglate and valeraldehyde. For supple- 766 

mental analysis (Figure S4) propyl acetate and allyl butyrate were used as targets. 767 

The following odors were used as background odors: pentyl acetate, isoamyl acetate, ethyl 768 

tiglate, propyl tiglate, methyl valerate, ethyl propionate, octanol, pentanol, heptanal, isobutyralde- 769 

hyde, hexanone, heptanone, methyl butyrate, ethyl butyrate, ethyl hexanoate, and hexanal. 770 

Training Protocol 771 

Water-restricted mice were trained in a freely moving two-alternative forced choice (2AFC) task 772 

to discriminate between two target odors mixed within variable odor backgrounds. Each trial 773 

began when the animal entered a central odor delivery port, releasing a 1-second odor pulse. 774 

Following odor presentation, mice had up to 20 seconds to respond by licking one of two side 775 

ports. Correct responses were rewarded with a single drop of water ( ∼ 2 μL). Once a choice 776 

was made, the trial ended, and a new trial began upon reentry to the odor port. Mice were not 777 

permitted to switch choices after selecting a port. 778 

Mice were trained in a procedure similar to that described by Rokni et al. 19 . During the initial 779 

phase of training, animals learned to discriminate between two monomolecular target odors in 780 

the absence of background odors. Once they achieved a performance criterion of ≥ 75% correct 781 

responses, background odors were introduced. 782

mixture. 789 

Background complexity was initially drawn from an exponential distribution favoring mixtures 790 

with few background components. Over successive training sessions, this distribution was grad- 791 

ually flattened until each level of background complexity, from 0 to 16 components, was equally 792 

probable. Under this uniform distribution, mice could encounter up to 65,536 unique background 793 

odor combinations. 794 

Mice were considered experts after reaching ≥ 75% accuracy under uniform background 795 

conditions. This typically took anywhere from 2-7 days to achieve after starting the uniform 796 

background condition. Following expert performance, mice were further tested with the training 797

fects. Mice were then tested on subsequently lower odor concentrations. Each mouse completed 799

Data Analysis 807 

(i) Sample and action time measurement: Sample time was defined as the total duration 808 

during which the odor port beam-break sensor was activated within a trial. This included multiple 809 

entries into the odor port, but only the periods when the mouse was inside the port contributed to 810 

the sample time. Action time was defined as the interval between the final odor port beam-break 811 

and the subsequent activation of either lick port. Periods between earlier sampling bouts were 812 

not included in the action time. 813 

(ii) Trial filtering: For analyses investigating how performance changed across increasing back- 814 

ground difficulty and learning (Figure 1), two mice were excluded because their training sched- 815 

ules differed from the standardized regime used for the remaining animals. Mice included in this 816 

analysis completed at least six days of trials on the most difficult background complexity at the 817 

training target odor concentration, and data from the first three training days were retained to cap- 818 

ture within-animal learning. In contrast, for analyses examining steady-state performance across 819 

concentrations and background complexities (Figure 2), the first three days of recordings at the 820 

”training” concentration were excluded. These sessions represented initial learning, whereas all 821 

subsequent sessions were considered ”test” days and reflected expert-level performance. 822

Comparing Behavioral and Decoder Performance 911 

To compare decoder performance in the model to behavioral performance, we summarized the 912 

effect of background odor complexity using a ratio-based metric. For each condition, we com- 913 

puted the ratio between performance at the highest and lowest background complexity. Be- 914 

havioral performance was defined as mean animal success rate, while model performance was 915 

defined as decoder accuracy. This metric provides a scale-independent measure of robustness 916 

to increasing background odor complexity. 917 

Because the concentration sets used in the behavioral experiments and in the model simula- 918 

tions differed, direct one-to-one concentration matching was not possible. Instead, we compared 919 

behavior and models using concentration-rank matching: behavioral and model conditions were 920 

independently ordered from highest to lowest cue concentration, and corresponding ranks were 921 

matched until one set was exhausted. This approach preserves the relative ordering of concen- 922 

trations while avoiding assumptions about equivalence between absolute concentration values 923 

across experiments and simulations. In addition, in the model simulations many of the highest 924 

concentration conditions yielded indistinguishable (flat) decoder performance curves, making 925 

them redundant for quantitative comparison; rank matching naturally accommodates this redun- 926 

dancy without over-weighting these conditions. 927

Capacity Analysis 1035 

To better characterize how neural response structure affects odor discriminability beyond simple 1036 

linear decoding accuracy, we applied the manifold capacity framework. We will first describe the 1037 

procedure we use to estimate the manifold capacity before providing a concise explanation of 1038 

the data-driven manifold capacity theory. 1039 

We independently generate 10,000 odor mixtures that contain one of two cue odors and a 1040 

random selection of up to 16 background odors. This time, we allow the backgrounds to be drawn 1041 

from up to 24 odors to mitigate the effect of only having a single mixture with 16 background odors 1042 

and 16 mixtures with 15 background odors. We generate glomerular responses for 400 glomeruli 1043 

under the same biophysical parameter distributions as the decoding analyses; we additionally 1044 

sample 10 replicates of the biophysical parameters to quantify the uncertainty in our analysis. 1045 

We consider the set of all neural responses to mixtures containing a given cue odor to be a 1046 

neural manifold associated with that cue odor. We arbitrarily label one manifold +1 and the other 1047 

−1. Then, using the “Geometry Linked to Untangling Efficiency” approach 41 , we estimate the 1048 

manifold capacity, effective manifold radius, and effective manifold dimensionality. In essence, 1049 

the effective manifold radius ( RM ) describes the extent of variability within each neural response 1050 

manifold, with smaller radii indicating tighter clustering (less variability). The effective manifold 1051 

dimensionality ( DM ) quantifies how many distinct directions of neural response variability are 1052 

relevant for discriminating between odor categories, with lower dimensionality representations 1053 

resulting in simpler discrimination. We schematically depict these measures in Figure S8. We 1054 

sweep across the same glomerulus response parameter sets as in the decoding analyses. We 1055 

focus our analyses on trends over varying background complexity and neural noise strength at 1056 

different background and cue concentrations. 1057
