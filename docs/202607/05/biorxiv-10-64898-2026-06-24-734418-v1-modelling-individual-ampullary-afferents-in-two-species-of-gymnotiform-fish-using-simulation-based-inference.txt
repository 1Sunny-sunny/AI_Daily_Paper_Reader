# Abstract 

Ampullary electroreceptors are widespread across aquatic vertebrates. The purpose of sensing exogeneous electric fields is conserved across species but the implementations differ and the encoding mechanisms remain incompletely understood. We compared baseline and stimulus-driven response properties of ampullary electroreceptor afferents in the weakly electric fish Apteronotus leptorhynchus and Eigenmannia virescens . We find that their activity is very well captured by an extended leaky integrate-and-fire June 24, 2026 1/41

model that generalizes across both species. The model shares similarities to a previous model of the tuberous electroreceptor afferents but further incorporates a low-pass pre-filtering and additional noise sources to reproduce the observed spectral response characteristics. The low-pass is essential to shape stimulus encoding in the high-frequency range. Accurate prediction of low-frequency stimulus encoding further requires two distinct noise sources: stimulus-independent white current noise and activity-dependent noise in the adaptation current, which is shaped by the adaptation time constant to yield pink noise dynamics. Using simulation-based inference, we trained a neural network to map model parameters to neuronal response features. This approach enables the generation of heterogeneous, biologically plausible model populations that may serve as a realistic input layer for studying neuronal processing on the next level. With this, we provide a unified and mechanistic model of ampullary electroreceptor encoding in these species and possibly beyond. The proposed model is another step towards a full model of the electrosensory periphery in these animals. 

# Author summary 

The ability to sense exogenous electric fields, i.e. passive electroreception, is widespread among aquatic animals. It plays a central role in prey detection and, in some species, also contributes to communication. To understand higher-order brain function, we also need to grasp the sensory periphery and ideally have models that provide naturalistic peripheral responses. Using simulation-based inference (SBI), we here develop a mechanistic model of passive electroreception that is valid for at least two species of electric fish. Through detailed analyses, we identify model components, such as a source of pink noise, that are essential for matching the model’s spectral response properties to those of the recorded cells. The main results of this work are the model itself and the trained inference network (here called the SBI network), which can now be used to create artificial but biologically plausible populations of sensory neurons that may serve as a realistic input layer for studying higher-order neuronal processing. Our work complements the existing models of the active electric sense and is a big step towards a full model of the electrosensory periphery in these animals. June 24, 2026 2/41

# Introduction 1

The ability to sense electric fields is widespread among aquatic animals and plays an important role in prey 2

detection. With the so-called passive electric sense, animals can sense exogenous electric fields that are 3

leaked into the water by muscle activity of, for example, prey. Passive electroreception is found in freshwater 4

animals such as catfish, sturgeon, or the mammalian platypus as well as in marine creatures such as sharks 5

and rays [1, 2]. Despite being widespread, the passive electric sense has been lost and re-invented several 6

times during evolution leading to different solutions serving the same purpose (for review e.g. [3, 4]). 7

Passive electroreception is mediated by primary electroreceptors that are located at the base of cutaneous 8

ampullary organs [4, 5]. Ampullary electroreceptor afferents innervate these organs, encode passive 9

electrosensory information in their spiking activity and carry it to the brain. We here study the response 10 

properties of such ampullary afferents in two species of gymnotiform South-American weakly electric fishes, 11 

namely the brown-ghost knifefish Apteronotus leptorhynchus and the glass knifefish Eigenmannia virescens 12 

that possess the passive and an active electric sense. These animals actively generate a surrounding electric 13 

field through discharges of their electric organ. In contrast to strongly electric fish, which employ their 14 

actively generated electric organ discharges for self-defense and prey-capture [6], weakly electric fishes use 15 

their actively generated field for prey-detection, navigation [7, 8], and communication [9]. 16 

Peripheral encoding as well as central processing of active electrosensory information has been in the 17 

focus of research on weakly electric fishes [10] but all fishes that have an active electric sense, are also 18 

equipped with a passive electric sense while the converse is not true [3]. Passive electrosensory stimuli, which 19 

arise from prey [11, 12], from the animal’s movement within the earth’s magnetic field in sharks [2, 13], or 20 

also during courtship signaling in E. virescens [14, 15] play an equally important role. The afferents of active 21 

and passive sense share some similarities, but there are also profound differences which invite for comparative 22 

studies. Ampullary afferents are, as their counterparts of the active system, spontaneously active but show 23 

comparatively low levels of intrinsic noise (see [16, 17], for the African mormyrid fish Gnathonemus petersii 24 

and the South-American gymnotiform fish A. leptorhynchus , respectively) and higher levels of nonlinear 25 

encoding in the weakly non-linear regime [18]. 26 

The afferents of either type project into the hindbrain where those of the active system trifurcate and 27 

synapse onto three adjacent maps of the electrosensory lateral line lobe (ELL) while those of the passive 28 

system project into a single map, the medial segment [10]. Pyramidal cells in the ELL integrate afferent 29 

inputs of populations of afferents [19]. The similarities and also the differences again ask for comparative 30 

studies of sensory information processing. A precondition for this, however, is a good grasp of the peripheral 31

electrosensory information, we will require good approximations of sensory responses to arbitrary stimuli, not 34 

necessarily step- or noise stimuli that may have been used during experimental characterization. In other 35 

words, good models for the electrosensory periphery are needed. 36 

Using simulation-based-inference (SBI) [20, 21] we here aim at training an artificial neuronal network that 37 

learns the mapping of model parameters to cellular response features. The SBI-network can then be used to 38 

retrieve parameter sets that define plausible models of ampullary afferents without the need of a fitting 39 

process. The underlying neuron model is based on an extended leaky integrate-and-fire model (LIF, [22 –24]) 40 

that shares some similarity to the model employed for afferents of the active system [25, 26]. Analysis of the 41 

model highlights specific properties of the passive system: we show that two different noise sources are 42 

needed to reproduce ampullary response features. One is an additive white current noise in the LIF 43 

integrator while the other is part of the activity dependent adaptation process where it is shaped into pink 44 

noise through the adaptation time-constant. Further, an additional low-pass pre-filter on the incoming signal 45 

is needed to shape the high-frequency decline of the stimulus-response coherence. These specificities are 46 

discussed in some detail. Our results further show that the SBI framework could be successfully employed to 47 

generate a single SBI-network from which naturalistic models of ampullary cells of at least two different 48 

species of gymnotiform electric fish can be constructed. The trained SBI-network allows building arbitrary 49 

populations of ampullary cells. Such populations can then be used as input layer for studying subsequent 50 

steps of sensory processing which renders it an invaluable tool for future studies. 51

## Experimental setup 74 

Fish were positioned centrally in the experimental tank, with the major parts of their body submerged into 75 

the water. Those body parts that were above the water surface were covered with paper tissue to avoid 76 

drying of the skin. Local analgesia was refreshed in intervals of two hours by cutaneous application of 77

## Electric field recordings 92 

For monitoring the unperturbed EOD, two vertical carbon rods (11 cm long, 8 mm diameter) in a head-tail 93 

configuration were placed isopotential to the stimulus. Their signal was differentially amplified with a gain 94 

factor between 100 and 500 (depending on the recorded animal) and band-pass filtered (3 to 1500 Hz 95

the electroreceptors, we measured the field with two additional silver wires spaced by 1 cm that were located 97 

next to the left operculum of the fish and orthogonal to the fish’s longitudinal body axis (amplification 100 98 

to 500 times, band-pass filtered with 3 to 1 500 Hz pass-band, DPA2-FX; npi-electronics, Tamm, Germany). 99 

This local EOD measurement recorded the combination of the fish’s own EOD and the applied stimulus. 100 

## Stimulation 101 

Electric stimuli were attenuated (ATN-01M, npi-electronics, Tamm, Germany), isolated from ground 102 

(ISO-02V, npi-electronics, Tamm, Germany) and delivered via two horizontal carbon rods (30 cm length, 103 

8 mm diameter) located 15 cm laterally on either side of the fish. 104 

The fish were stimulated with band-limited white noise stimuli with a cut-off frequency of 150 Hz. The 105 

stimulus intensity was given as the contrast, i.e. the standard deviation of the white noise stimulus in 106 

relation to the fish’s EOD amplitude. The contrast varied between 10 and 20 %. Only cells with at least 20 s 107 

of white noise stimulation were included for the analysis. 108

# Results 175 

The SBI approach consists of an initial brute-force step in which training data is assembled. Sets of model 176 

parameters are randomly chosen from prior distributions, the model is run for each parameter set, and 177 

respective response features are extracted. Afterwards, an artificial neural network is trained to learn the 178 

mapping between model parameters and response features. The first tasks are thus to identify which features 179 

are essential to describe the cellular responses and to know their distributions which we used to constrain the 180 

priors by excluding implausible simulation results. 181 

## Feature selection from experimental data 182 

Our experimental dataset consists of previously collected data from ampullary afferents recorded in E. 183 

virescens and A. leptorhynchus [15, 17]. The selection of cells used here is constrained by the availability of 184 

sufficiently long recordings of spontaneous activity and responses to noise stimuli at a given intensity (20% 185 

relative to fish’s self-generated field, see methods). This leaves us with a sample of 14 and 20 cells, 186 

respectively. For these, we extracted response features as depicted for single-cell examples in Fig 2 and S1 187 

Fig. 188 

Baseline activity 189 

From the spontaneous baseline activity, we extracted three characterizing features: B1: baseline rate , the 190 

average firing rate in the absence of a driving stimulus, B2: CV ISI , the coefficient of variation of the 191 

interspike interval distribution that describes the response regularity of the spontaneous activity, B3: SC ISI , 192 

lag-1 serial correlation of the interspike intervals that characterizes the dependence of successive spikes (Fig 2 193 

panels A1 – A3). 194

The responses to dynamic stimuli were further analyzed by means of the stimulus-response coherence and 203 

the transfer function [34]. The coherence describes the linear correlation between stimulus and responses and 204 

is a spectral measure varying between zero, no linear correlation, and one, perfect linear correlation. 205 

Deviations from unity are caused by noise, non-linear behavior of the system, or combinations thereof. The 206 

shape of the coherence spectrum was characterized by five features: C1: C0Hz , the coherence at zero 207 

frequency; C2: Cmax , the maximum coherence; C3: C120 −150 Hz , the average coherence at high frequencies 208 

(120 – 150 Hz); C4: Fmax , the frequency of the maximum coherence; and C5: Fcutof f , the cutoff frequency of 209 

the coherence spectrum. 210 

The transfer function (or first-order susceptibility) is the ratio of the cross-spectrum of stimulus and 211 

response and the stimulus’ power-spectrum. Its absolute value, the gain spectrum, describes the spectral 212 

sensitivity of the system. The shape of the gain spectrum was captured by eight features: G1 : G0Hz , the 213 

gain at zero frequency; G2: G1/2Fmax , the gain half-way between zero and maximum; G3: Gmax , the 214 

maximum gain; G4: Grate , the gain at average firing rate which, in weakly driven cells, can show a distinct 215 

peak; G5: G120 −150 Hz , the average gain at frequencies in the range 120 – 150 Hz; G6: F1/2Fmax the position 216 

of the gain halfway to the frequency of the maximum of the gain; G7: Fmax , the position of the maximum 217 

gain; and G8: Fcutof f , the cutoff-frequency of the gain spectrum. Feature G6 depends only on the position 218 

of the maximum and is redundant. We still kept it to emphasize the initial part of the transfer function 219 

during training. 220

## One fits all: Successful reproduction of cellular data for both species 238 

We then trained the SBI-network on a feature-set based on 12 million simulations using the final model with 239 

model parameters that were randomly chosen from prior distributions (see discussion of some detailed model 240 

considerations). Once the SBI-network is trained, it can be queried to return the parameter set that will 241 

most likely match the experimentally observed features best. For this, we applied the MAP 242 

(maximum-a-posteriori) approach provided by the SBI package: i.e. multiple samples are drawn from the 243 

trained posterior and used as starting points for a gradient-ascent optimization that searches for highest 244 

posterior density. Fig 4 compares experimental responses and simulation results obtained from the MAP 245 

estimate for the same A. leptorhynchus cell as in Fig 2. The same SBI-network was also queried to provide 246 

parameter sets to reproduce E. virescens ampullary cells. Even though the distribution of the same features 247 

were different in the samples we used here, the SBI-network still returns parameter sets that reproduce 248 

neuronal responses of either species similarly well (S2 Fig). Note that this involves no actual fitting to 249 

experimental data. It is only the vector of desired response features of baseline and stimulus-driven responses 250 

that is used to draw parameter sets from the SBI-network. Baseline features match almost perfectly. Also, 251 

responses to white-noise stimuli are very nicely reproduced. There are, however, also some parts of the 252 

responses that are not so well captured (between 400 and 450 ms in Fig 4 B or at ms 260 in S2 Fig B for an 253 

E. virescens ampullary afferent). The stimulus-response coherence as well as the gain spectrum are very 254 

well-matched in both example cells. 255

stimulus-driven responses are almost perfectly matched (Fig 5 A & B). The characteristics of the 257 

stimulus-response coherence are well-matched, but the simulations tend to overestimate the coherence at zero 258

# Discussion 320 

In the present account we have applied simulation-based inference (SBI) [21] to develop a model for 321 

ampullary electroreceptor afferents in gymnotiform electric fish. In the SBI approach, an artificial neural 322 

network is trained to learn the mapping between model parameters and response features, there is no actual 323 

fitting involved. The resulting SBI-network can be queried to provide model parameterization that will likely 324 

lead to the desired features. We show that SBI can be successfully applied to reproduce responses of 325 

20/41 

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 30, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.24.734418 doi: bioRxiv preprint

ampullary afferents in two species of South American weakly electric fish. With its availability, we lay 326 

important groundwork for studies on the integration of passive electrosensory information on higher 327 

processing steps. It becomes possible to create large populations of biologically plausible individual model 328 

neurons without going through a fitting process that provides specific solutions for individual previously 329 

recorded neurons (see also S2 Appendix). 330 

To successfully apply SBI, some preconditions must be met: (i) the model must be powerful enough to 331 

actually reproduce experimentally observed cellular responses and (ii) the selected response features must be 332 

effective descriptors of the neuronal response properties. In what follows, we will first discuss these two issues 333 

before we turn to the generalization capabilities of the model. 334 

## Model considerations 335 

The chosen model is based on a leaky integrate-and-fire model (LIF, Fig 1) inspired by models used to 336 

describe p-type afferents of the tuberous electroreceptor pathway (P-units) [18, 26, 32, 36]. Tuberous and 337 

ampullary signal pathways consist of similar building blocks: (i) primary electroreceptors are located at the 338 

base of cutaneous pits that differ in shape and signal transduction properties; (ii) primary electroreceptors 339 

are connected to respective afferent fibers via synapses in the form of large boutons or calical structures 340 

(ampullary and tuberous organs, respectively) [37]; and (iii) afferent fibers send action potentials along their 341 

axons to the ELL in the hindbrain. Accordingly, the models share some similarities but there are also 342 

differences. Ampullary cells encode the direct electrosensory input, not amplitude modulations of the 343 

self-generated field that P-units encode; hence, there is no non-linear amplitude-modulation extraction 344 

mechanism involved. In the following paragraphs we will highlight similarities and differences between the 345 

models. 346 

Activity-dependent adaptation current 347 

Much like P-units, ampullary afferents show pronounced spike-frequency adaptation in response to step 348 

stimuli (S6 Fig), i.e. cells respond initially strongly to a step and then rapidly adapt towards a steady-state 349 

response level. This is here modeled as an inhibitory adaptation current: i.e. with each fired action potential, 350 

the adaptation current is incremented and decays with a certain time-constant. This is the same 351 

implementation as in models by [26, 38]. Chacron et al. [32] yield a similar effect by transiently incrementing 352 

the spiking threshold in their model. Adaptation currents and dynamic thresholds, however, have distinctly 353 

different effects. While inhibitory adaptation currents act linearly and shift the neuron’s firing-frequency vs. 354 

stimulus intensity ( f − I) curve, the dynamic threshold acts divisively and changes the slope of the f − I 355

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 30, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.24.734418 doi: bioRxiv preprint

curve [39]. The shift induced by inhibitory adaptation current matches the observed effects of different 356 

adaptation levels on the f − I curves of P-units [40]. Inhibitory adaptation effectively acts as a high-pass 357

Coherence function is shaped by two noise sources 381 

Transfer function and coherence spectrum of ampullary afferents in both species show a distinct drop-off for 382

Fig 9 shows the impact of different noise sources on the stimulus-response coherence. Without any noise, 387

## Feature selection 403 

To train the SBI-network we defined a set of features that were extracted from spontaneous baseline 404 

responses as well as responses to band-limited white noise stimuli. The data was gathered in two species of 405 

South-American weakly electric fishes, namely the brown ghost knifefish Apteronotus leptorhynchus and the 406 

glass knifefish Eigenmannia virescens , which we recorded from in different contexts [15, 17]. Of all cells 407 

reported in the respective original papers, only a subset could be re-used in this study as only these were 408 

stimulated using the same stimuli. 409 

The choice of features may influence the final parametrization of the respective model cells. To create our 410 

set of models for the active, tuberous, electroreceptor afferents, P-units [18, 26, 45], the respective model was 411 

fitted to cells in which no white-noise stimulus was available but rather responses to step-stimuli were used 412 

to estimate f − I curves. These models reproduce white-noise responses well and also show the theoretically 413 

expected non-linear behaviors in the weakly-nonlinear regime [18, 46]. Some parameters, such as the dendritic 414 

low-pass filter that is also part of our ampullary model, is not well constrained by step-stimuli [35]. On the 415 

other hand, in this study, we did not consider step stimuli but focussed on those datasets that contained 416 

white-noise responses as dynamic stimulation is closer to natural stimulus situations [12] and noise 417 

stimulation allows for system’s characterization with much less recording time. On the downside, it may 418 

underestimate non-linearities due to linearizing effects of noise [18, 47, 48]. The models we retrieved from the 419

A linear system can be fully characterized by measuring its responses to white noise stimuli from which 422 

the transfer function (and the absolute of it, the gain) as well as the coherence function can be estimated. 423 

Multiplication of transfer function and the stimulus Fourier spectrum allows to predict the system’s overall 424

interspike interval distribution under baseline conditions. Thus, there are features that provide overlapping 427 

information, or may correlate. For the SBI process this is not a problem, since the SBI-network will learn the 428 

interdependencies and would even learn to ignore uninformative features [20, 21]. Thus, we would argue to 429 

rather have more than too little features. A drawback of this approach is that the retrieval of model 430 

parameters requires to provide desired values for all features. In the accompanying graphical tool (S2 431 

Appendix), the SBI-network can be used to retrieve model parameters and the tool assists by providing 432 

feature distribution from the training set that can be constrained by the user. 433 

## SBI approach does not require extensive datasets 434 

Experimental data provides the ground truth of modelling approaches. Yet, experimental data can be flawed 435 

as well and rigorous data curation is necessary. The curation processes applied here were hurtful as quite 436 

substantial amounts of recorded cells needed to be excluded for various reasons: (i) too little data to 437 

faithfully estimate the white noise responses, as they have not been the focus of the E. virescens recordings 438 

made in context of encoding of electrocommunication signals [15], (ii) instability during the recording of the 439 

baseline activity which usually is done in the first 30+ seconds after a recording could be established, or (iii) 440 

exclusion due to wrong stimulus conditions. In the end, our work is based on 14 cells recorded in E. virescens 441 

and 20 cells recorded in A. leptorhynchus which may seem not too impressive. For the SBI approach this is 442 

not critical: Experimental data is essential to establish the feature space and to learn whether features 443 

effectively constrain the model parameters. For the training set used to train the SBI-network, however, 444 

experimental data are of no relevance as it does not fit but learns how model parameters map to response 445 

features. Accordingly, the experimental data serve as tests to see if the parameters retrieved from the trained 446 

network actually lead to plausible results. As we have demonstrated, this is satisfactory; the important factor 447 

here is that the experimental example data are good representatives of the overall variety of cells we studied. 448

## Prediction power and applicability across species 449 

Even though the ability to sense exogenous electric fields is widespread and apparently conserved across 450 

species, this does not mean that ampullary electroreceptors are the same across species. On the contrary, 451 

passive electroreception has been lost and regained several times. Although these systems serve the same 452 

purpose, they differ with respect to response modes and morphology [4, 49]. In teleosts, these receptors are 453 

characterized by responses to anodal stimuli, i.e. firing rates increase when the outside becomes more 454 

positive relative to the inside. In contrast, their counterparts in non-teleost fish, including paddlefish, skates, 455 

and lampreys, increase firing in response to stimuli of the opposite sign [4]. What these systems share is a 456 

mechanosensory origin, and primary electroreceptors are connected to afferents via ribbon synapses [49]. 457 

They are all clearly low-frequency tuned and show spontaneous activity in the absence of any driving input. 458 

Spontaneous firing rates in the catfish Ictalurus nebulosus were found in the range 35 - 60 Hz [50] with a 459 

clear temperature dependence [51], in the range 45 ±7.5 Hz in the skate Raja eglanteria [52], 55 ±8.5 Hz 460 

reported for the paddlefish Polydon spatula [12]. In the African weakly electric fish Gnathonemus petersii we 461 

see higher firing rates in the range 73 ±23.8 Hz [16] which fits well to the range we here describe for E. 462 

virescens ampullary cells but is lower than we find for A. leptorhynchus (Fig 3 [17]). Spectral sensitivity 463 

scales along with firing rates in the sense that cells with higher firing rates encode stimuli in a wider range 464 

than those with lower firing rates, which is a generic feature of spiking neurons [35]. The question arises 465 

whether the model suggested here is of general applicability for ampullary afferents in other species. From 466 

inspections of the data published on ampullary afferents of G. petersii [16] we are very optimistic as all 467 

response features fall into the feature ranges we find in our experimental data and are covered by the 468 

training set. For the paddlefish, however, we are pessimistic, as the response spectra of these show the two 469 

oscillations [12] that we touched upon above and that cannot be reproduced by our model. Our model 470 

deviates from the model of paddlefish ampullary electroreceptors [12]. There, two oscillators are identified, 471 

the first oscillates at the spontaneous firing rate of the afferent and a second epitheliar oscillation of the 472 

ampullary canal. Our model only contains oscillation at the spontaneous firing rate which is implicitly 473 

created by interaction of offset current and membrane time-constant. As a consequence, our model will have 474 

limited generalization power for ampullary encoding in paddlefish and others which show two oscillations. 475 

The spectral sensitivity of primary electroreceptors in catfish was modelled with sequences of low-pass 476 

filters [41] which fits well to the dendritic low-pass filter applied here and, to our knowledge, no oscillations 477 

are described in the literature, which is promising. For the skate, we are lacking the required data. It would 478 

be nice to perform comparative studies to see how far the model carries across species. 479

## Conclusions 480 

With the present study we developed a phenomenological LIF-based model of ampullary electroreceptor 481 

afferents. The model is powerful enough to reproduce all response features that we observe in 482 

electrophysiological recordings made in two species of weakly electric fish, namely Eigenmannia virescens and 483 

Apteronotus leptorhynchus . We further provide descriptions and population statistics for these cells. Using 484 

the SBI framework we trained an artificial neuronal network, that has learned the mapping between model 485 

parameter and response features and can now be used to create ampullary model cells with desired response 486 

features. This is an invaluable tool for example to create populations of neurons that respects the level of 487 

heterogeneity of natural neuronal populations and thus fosters future studies. 488

S1 Appendix. Single Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise is not sufficient Our model contains two noise 525 

sources necessary to reproduce the low-frequency shape of the coherence spectrum (see Fig 9). The white 526 

noise added within the adaptation process, ξadapt , is turned into colored (pink) noise by the adaptation time 527 

constant [42]. Here we investigate whether a single Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise process ( OU-noise model) 528 

which would yield a colored noise directly, would be sufficient to reconstruct the experimental data. For 529 

comparison between the original, two-noises, and OU-noise model, we trained two separate SBI networks for 530 

the two different models that were based on the same number of training runs to exclude biases based on a 531 

larger training set. Both networks were then queried for most-likely models corresponding to each of the 532 

recorded cells. We here focus on three features, the serial correlation at lag 1 ( B3: SC ISI ), the 533 

zero-frequency coherence ( C1: C0Hz ), and the frequency of the maximum coherence ( C4: Fmax ). 534 

The 1st-lag serial correlation of the baseline ISI intervals is slightly elevated in the OU-noise models 535 

compared to the original models, all models are above the equality line (Fig 10 A, B3 , gray and orange dots, 536

distributed around the equality line, the OU-noise models are consistently above (Fig 10 A, feature C1 ). The 538 

frequency of the maximum coherence is most strongly affected and most OU-noise models underestimate the 539 

position of the best coherence (Fig 10, feature C4 ). Comparing the goodness of fit of the coherence to the 540

the corresponding original model. Increasing τ attenuates the high-frequency content of the noise. As a 550 

result, the low-frequency coherence is pushed down but at the same time, the higher frequency coherence is 551 

increased due to the improved SNR at higher frequencies (arrows). Using the version that best matches the 552 

low-frequency part, and increasing the overall noise strength does not help as the overall stimulus-response 553 

coherence is decreased Fig 10 F. We thus conclude that the white noise source in the adaptation process 554 

together with the additive current noise in the integrater are needed to resemble the characteristics of the 555 

ampullary cells. 556
