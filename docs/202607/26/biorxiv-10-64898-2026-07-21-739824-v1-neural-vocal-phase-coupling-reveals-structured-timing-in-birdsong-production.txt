# Abstract 

Understanding how neural population activity is temporally coordinated with behavior remains a central challenge in neuroscience. Songbirds provide a powerful model system for addressing this question because learned vocal production requires precise coordination among neural dynamics, temporally structured motor output, and auditory feedback. However, quantifying neural-vocal interactions is challenging because both neural and acoustic signals are rhythmic, noisy, and highly nonstationary. Here, we investigate neural-vocal coordination during spontaneous canary singing using simultaneous recordings of neural population activity in a forebrain region of the song system and vocal behavior. Using a phase-resolved cross-correlation framework combined with surrogate-based statistical validation, we quantify neural-vocal interactions in short and highly variable song segments. The coexistence of these regimes is consistent with the integrative role of the recorded region, which receives auditory input, contributes to premotor control, and participates in the neural circuitry supporting song learning and the ongoing maintenance of adult song. We further find that correlated and anticorrelated interactions coexist throughout singing, with anticorrelated interactions consistently concentrated around near-zero lags. These anticorrelations identify periods in which decreases in neural population activity are closely aligned with sound production, revealing biologically relevant information that is obscured by analyses performed over complete song renditions. Together, these results uncover a robust temporal structure linking neural population activity to vocal behavior and provide a broadly applicable framework for extracting transient neural-behavioral interactions from complex biological signals. 

# Author summary 

Modern neuroscience can simultaneously record the activity of large neural populations, yet extracting meaningful relationships between neural activity and behavior remains challenging because natural behaviors are highly variable. Songbirds provide a unique opportunity to study this problem because their learned vocalizations share key features with human speech while remaining experimentally accessible. Here, we analyzed simultaneous recordings of neural population activity and vocal behavior in freely singing canaries. Instead of averaging neural activity across entire songs, we examined brief time windows and combined local correlation analysis with statistical tests to identify reliable interactions between the brain and behavior. We found that these interactions switch among several preferred timing patterns: neural activity can precede vocal output, occur at nearly the same time, or follow acoustic events. This diversity is consistent with the combined roles of the recorded brain region. Our work provides an intuitive and broadly applicable framework for uncovering transient neural-behavioral interactions that remain hidden by traditional time-averaged approaches. Complex behaviors emerge from the coordinated dynamics of neural populations and the body across 2

multiple temporal scales. Recent advances in large-scale neural recordings and population-level analyses have 3

substantially improved our understanding of neural-motor interactions [1–4]. However, how neural 4

population dynamics are temporally coordinated with behavior remains poorly understood [5–7]. This 5

challenge is particularly significant when both neural and behavioral signals are rhythmic, noisy, and strongly 6

nonstationary, making it difficult to identify meaningful temporal relationships between brain activity and 7

behavior. Developing robust methods capable of extracting neural-behavioral interactions from such signals 8

remains an important challenge in neuroscience. 9

Songbirds provide a powerful model system for investigating neural coordination during learned motor 10 

behaviors because they are one of the few animal groups capable of vocal learning, a rare ability shared with 11 

humans and a small number of other species [8, 9]. As a consequence, songbirds have become a valuable 12 

animal model for studying the neural mechanisms underlying learned vocal communication [10]. In addition, 13 

since learning and vocal production depend on the precise interaction between neural activity and temporally 14 

structured motor output, they are a powerful model to investigate neural-behavioral coordination [11–13]. 15 

Among songbirds, canaries ( Serinus canaria ) are especially well suited for studying neural-vocal timing 16 

relationships as their songs are composed of rhythmic sequences of repeated syllables spanning a broad range 17 

of production rates, from approximately 3 to 30 Hz [14 – 18]. This broad temporal repertoire generates diverse 18 

vocal rhythms that facilitate the investigation of neural-behavioral coordination across multiple timescales 19 

within a single behavioral context. These vocal dynamics emerge from activity within a specialized neural 20 

network known as the song system, in which the forebrain neural nucleus HVC plays a central role in song 21 

production and sensorimotor integration during singing [19–21]. Previous studies have revealed temporally 22 

structured activity in HVC during vocal production [22–26], supporting the idea that neural dynamics are 23 

closely linked to the temporal organization of song [27, 28]. However, characterizing neural-vocal coordination 24 

remains challenging because conventional analyses often rely on averages computed over extended time 25 

periods, potentially obscuring transient and heterogeneous temporal relationships. 26 

More recently, computational approaches have increasingly focused on extracting temporally structured 27 

neural-behavioral interactions from high-dimensional and highly variable datasets, highlighting the need for 28 

analysis frameworks capable of resolving neural dynamics at behaviorally relevant temporal scales [6, 7, 29]. 29 

Recent computational methods have further emphasized the importance of phase-based and multivariate 30 

approaches for characterizing coordination between neural populations across multiple temporal 31

Different methodologies are available for quantifying the synchronization of two signals, X(t) and Y (t), 33 

and the results obtained can be qualitatively similar, or not, depending on the nature of the signals [32 –34]. 34 

Because of its simplicity, cross-correlation analysis has been extensively used; however, caution is needed 35 

when assessing the statistical significance of cross-correlation values [35]. The cross-correlation function, 36 

CXY (τ ), has been widely used to quantify the similarity between X(t) and Y (t − τ ) in signal analysis. For 37 

example, it has been used to analyze local field potentials recorded from different cortical regions [36]. The 38 

height and time shift of the main peak of CXY (τ ), i.e., the peak that has the largest deviation from the 39 

background level, has been used to estimate the degree of synchronization and lag between X(t) and 40 

Y (t) [36]. Correlation-based approaches have also been used to study synchronization in the cat visual 41 

cortex, including trial-by-trial variations in neuronal response latencies and transient episodes of oscillatory 42 

synchrony captured through sliding-window cross-correlation and surrogate-based controls [37, 38]. Similarly, 43 

lagged correlations have been used to characterize how neural responses relate to speech dynamics in the 44 

human auditory cortex [39, 40]. This approach relies on the assumption that CXY (τ ) has a well-defined, 45 

unambiguous peak, and that it is approximately symmetric, i.e., CXY (τ ) ≈ CY X (−τ ). However, this 46 

assumption does not hold if X(t) and Y (t) are non-stationary, or if the time interval analyzed is not long 47 

enough. Moreover, the estimation of the degree of synchronization and lag between X(t) and Y (t) from the 48 

height and time shift of the main peak of CXY (τ ) fails when the signals’ rhythmicity generates multiple 49 

peaks in CXY (τ ), as occurs, for example, in music-like time series. 50 

Alternative measures to quantify the synchronization of two signals include the phase locking value 51 

computed from the instantaneous phases of the signals extracted using the Hilbert transform [41]. However, 52 

the Hilbert technique only gives an accurate estimation of the phases if the signals are sufficiently narrow 53 

band and the duration of the analyzed time interval is sufficiently long. A related method based on 54 

coherence analysis [42] also requires that the signals are sufficiently narrow band. Statistical phase-locking 55 

indices have also been developed to detect transient synchronization episodes in oscillatory neural signals, 56 

even in noisy data; however, their reliability depends on the choice of surrogate data methods used for 57 

significance testing [43]. Another popular approach to infer correlations that reflect causal interactions is 58 

based on autoregressive models. As an example, the classic Granger causality quantifier [44] and its 59 

variants [45, 46] are popular tools for analyzing interactions between neuronal populations in brain circuits. 60 

However, autoregressive models have unknown hyper-parameters that have to be carefully selected [47], and 61 

can return spurious causal relationships when the signals analyzed are not stationary. A different class of 62 

methods relies on the analysis of the timing of events defined in the signals. Event coincidence analysis has 63

In this work, we develop a phase-resolved framework to investigate neural-vocal coordination during 67 

spontaneous canary singing. Our approach combines cross-correlation analysis with surrogate-based 68 

statistical validation and applies these tools to short sliding windows of simultaneously recorded neural and 69 

behavioral signals. Rather than relying on a single correlation estimate computed over an entire song, the 70 

method identifies statistically significant interactions at short temporal scales while preserving the temporal 71 

diversity of neural responses. Using this framework, we characterize the temporal organization of 72 

neural-vocal interactions during singing and show that neural-vocal coordination is not described by a single 73 

temporal relationship but instead involves multiple interaction regimes. More broadly, our results 74 

demonstrate the importance of temporally-resolved analyses for studying interactions between rhythmic and 75 

strongly nonstationary biological signals. 76

## Neural-vocal interactions cluster into discrete temporal regimes 136 

To determine whether neural-vocal interactions are associated with preferred temporal lags, we analyzed the 137 

distribution of τ ∗ values across birds and song segments. If lag values varied continuously, a broad 138 

distribution of τ ∗ values without clearly separated peaks would be expected. Instead, Fig. 5 reveals 139 

structured distributions with distinct peaks separated by regions of low probability density. To quantify this 140 

organization, we estimated the probability density of τ ∗ values using kernel density estimation and identified 141 

peaks and surrounding valleys as candidate temporal classes (see Methods). Across birds, the resulting 142 

distributions consistently revealed a small number of preferred lag values rather than a continuous 143 

distribution of lags. For example, the correlations of Birds 2 and 3 and the anticorrelations of all three birds 144 

exhibited a dominant class centered near τ ∗ ≈ 0, whereas the correlations of Birds 2 and 3 and the 145 

anticorrelations of Bird 1 also displayed multiple classes at distinct lag values spanning both positive and 146 

negative lags. Median values and interquartile ranges for all classes are reported in Table S1. 147 

The distributions also exhibited extended tails toward both positive and negative lag values. In several 148 

datasets, lag magnitudes exceeded |τ ∗| > 100 ms, indicating neural-vocal interactions operating over 149 

substantially longer timescales than those associated with the dominant classes. These large-lag events are 150 

visible both in the distributions of Fig. 5 and in the time-resolved analyses of Figs. S2 , where they are 151 

associated with song segments containing slowly repeated or long-duration syllables. Although relatively 152 

infrequent, their recurrent presence across birds suggests that the temporal structure of vocal behavior can 153 

support neural-vocal coordination over a broad range of lags. 154

Canary song provides a unique opportunity to investigate how neural population activity is coordinated with 175 

behavior across multiple timescales as learned vocal production generates rhythmic vocal patterns spanning a 176 

broad range of syllable repetition rates. Here we show that neural-vocal coordination is not characterized by 177 

a single temporal relationship, but instead is organized into multiple interaction regimes that appear during 178 

different portions of the song. A central result of this study is that the temporal lag between neural activity 179 

and vocal behavior is structured rather than continuously distributed. Within individual birds, τ ∗ values 180 

clustered around a small number of preferred lags, indicating that neural-vocal interactions are organized 181 

into discrete temporal regimes. These regimes included positive, near-zero, and negative lags, suggesting that 182 

different coordination patterns between neural activity and vocal behavior coexist during song production. 183 

Rather than remaining fixed throughout a song, these regimes appeared during specific song segments and 184 

persisted over short temporal intervals before transitioning to different interaction regimes. 185 

The coexistence of positive, near-zero, and negative lag regimes reveals multiple modes of neural-vocal 186 

coordination. Positive lags are consistent with neural activity preceding vocal output, whereas negative lags 187 

are consistent with neural activity following acoustic events. Near-zero lags indicate approximately 188 

synchronous neural and vocal fluctuations. Although the present analysis does not establish causal 189 

relationships, the coexistence of these distinct interaction modes suggests that neural-vocal coordination 190 

during singing cannot be described by a single temporal mechanism. In addition to near-zero interactions, 191 

two birds exhibited prominent correlation classes centered at lags of approximately 40-60 ms. While the 192 

present analysis does not establish causal relationships, these timescales are compatible with the temporal 193 

lags expected between activity in HVC and the generation of vocal output through downstream motor 194 

pathways. The reproducibility of these lag classes across birds suggests that neural-vocal coordination may 195 

be organized around preferred temporal scales rather than continuously distributed lags. 196 

Another prominent feature of the data was the recurrent presence of anticorrelated interactions. In 197 

particular, anticorrelated segments frequently clustered around τ ∗ ≈ 0 in all three birds. This observation 198 

suggests that neural-vocal coordination is not limited to periods in which neural and acoustic fluctuations 199 

vary in the same direction. Instead, coordinated interactions can also emerge through temporally structured 200 

anticorrelations. The consistency of this near-zero-lag regime across birds suggests that anticorrelated 201 

interactions constitute a robust component of neural-vocal coordination during singing. 202 

We also observed interactions associated with larger lag magnitudes, in some cases exceeding 203 

|τ ∗| > 100 ms. These events were typically associated with slowly repeated or long-duration syllables, 204

# Materials and methods 216 

## Birds 217 

We collected data from three adult male canaries ( Serinus canaria ), sourced from a local breeder. All 218 

recordings were performed at the University of Buenos Aires, Argentina during the spring-summer breeding 219 

season, a period of peak vocal activity in this species. Birds were housed individually in sound-attenuating 220 

boxes, maintained under a 14:10-hour light-dark cycle simulating summer daylight. Food and water were 221 

provided ad libitum . All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use 222 

Committee of the University of Buenos Aires (CICUAL-FCEN, UBA). All efforts were made to minimize 223 

animal suffering. 224 

## Surgical procedures 225 

Surgeries were performed under isoflurane anesthesia (1–1.5% in 100% O2; Richmond) using a stereotaxic 226 

frame, with the bird positioned at a standard 45 ° angle between the ear bar axis and the beak. Feathers and 227 

skin over the skull were removed, and small openings were made in the outer bone layer to allow access for 228 

craniotomies and implant anchoring. Fine wires were inserted between skull layers to serve as support posts. 229 

The main craniotomy ( ∼ 500 μm diameter) was made at coordinates +2.4 mm lateral and 0 mm anterior 230 

relative to the Y-point (the intersection of the midsagittal sinus and the transverse sinus, used as a 231 

stereotaxic reference in songbirds). After removing the dura, the electrode array was lowered into the brain 232 

using a hydraulic micromanipulator and protected with paraffin. A ground wire was placed in a secondary 233

## Electrode array and electrophysiological recordings 237 

We recorded extracellular neural signals in the telencephalic nucleus HVC, a premotor area critical for song 238 

production and learning, during spontaneous singing, simultaneously with audio, following the protocol 239 

described in [52]. Each bird was housed alone but intermittently exposed to auditory and visual stimuli from 240 

other canaries to encourage vocalizations. Recording sessions continued as long as reliable neural signals and 241 

frequent singing were observed. Audio and neural signals were continuously monitored and automatically 242 

recorded when sound amplitude crossed a manually set threshold corresponding to vocal output. 243 

Electrophysiological signals were acquired using custom-built tetrode arrays made from low-impedance, 244 

polyimide-coated tungsten wires (12.7 μm diameter, 99.95% purity; California Fine Wire), as per [53]. 245 

Tetrodes were mounted on a lightweight manual microdrive (a device that allows precise positioning of 246 

electrodes in the brain), adapted from the design described in [54]. Signals were amplified using an RHD2132 247 

16-channel amplifier and digitized at 30 kS/s using the Intan Technologies RHD2000 USB interface. Audio 248 

was captured with a 20 Hz-20 kHz electret microphone connected to a MAX4466 amplifier board and fed 249 

into an analog input of the same interface. 250 

## Data pre-processing 251 

For each analysis, we used the recording of an entire song, including a few seconds of silence before its onset 252 

and after its offset to capture baseline neuronal activity. The raw neural signal was high-pass filtered using a 253 

third-order Butterworth filter with a 300 Hz cutoff to isolate spiking activity. Multi-unit activity (MUA) was 254 

obtained using an amplitude threshold set at three times the background noise level, estimated as 255 

σ = |x|/0.6745, where x is the high-pass filtered signal and σ is a robust estimator of the noise standard 256 

deviation [55]. To obtain the MUA, we applied a Gaussian kernel to generate smooth activity traces per 257 

channel (bandwidth = 5 ms, timestep = 0.033 ms). These parameters were chosen by visual inspection to 258 

capture temporal patterns of neuronal activity (periods of activity-silence) while minimizing noise across all 259 

phrase types, (repeated sequences of syllables — the minimal vocal units of canary song), considering their 260 

different temporal scales (2 to 27 Hz, reflecting the range of syllable repetition rates across phrase types). 261 

This smoothed curve was multiplied by the spike count to obtain an estimate of the absolute spiking rate, 262 

combining the temporal structure of the activity with the total number of detected spikes. Traces from 263
