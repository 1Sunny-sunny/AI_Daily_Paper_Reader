# Image-grounded encoding models reveal distinct temporal profiles of naturalistic object and scene processing in the human brain 

Temporal profiles of object and scene processing

1 Department of Psychology, University of Amsterdam, 1018 WS, Amsterdam, The Netherlands

# Abstract 1

In real-world vision, the human brain needs to process large amounts of information to effectively interact with its 2

environment. It is well established that our visual system has specialized regions to process information efficiently, 3

such as scene-, face-, and object-selective areas, which can be uncovered using functional magnetic resonance 4

imaging (fMRI). However, functional localizers typically use artificially separated stimuli (e.g. scene backgrounds 5

vs. object cutouts), and fMRI signals are insensitive to potential temporal processing differences. Here, we identify 6

temporal signatures of object and scene processing in natural visual environments by building image-grounded 7

brain-predictive encoding models of human electroencephalography (EEG) responses. In a large set of high- 8

resolution natural images, we first separate object from scene information on a per-image basis, and then feed this 9

information to train separate deep neural network-based encoding models to predict EEG responses to the full, 10 

intact images. We find that encoding models that receive only object information consistently exhibit a delayed 11 

temporal encoding profile compared to models that only receive scene information. Control analyses confirm the 12 

robustness of this delayed object encoding, showing that consistent selection of object or scene information is 13 

needed to achieve high encoding performance. Using these distinct encoding profiles as processing templates , we 14 

reveal which image parts are ’seen’ by the brain as an object, versus a background scene element. These findings 15 

demonstrate that object and scene processing in the human brain unfold differentially over time and indicate that 16 

image-grounded encoding models are powerful tools for isolating components of naturalistic perception. 17 

# Significance 18 

The neural mechanisms underlying visual perception are typically studied by experimentally separating different 19 

kinds of information, for example presenting either isolated objects or scene backgrounds. Since information 20 

naturally co-occurs in the world — objects are typically embedded within scenes — this artificial separation 21 

reduces ecological validity. We leverage image-computable neural encoding models to identify distinct visual 22 

processing profiles within ecologically valid, natural images. Models that receive stimuli in which only objects 23 

are visible and scene information is masked predict EEG responses at later time points than models receiving the 24 

inverse, "scene-only" stimuli. By quantifying how items such as buildings, trees or the ground are encoded in EEG 25 

signals, we reveal which elements of visual environments drive neural responses to intact, real-world images. 26

# Introduction 27 

The human visual system can efficiently and rapidly process vast amounts of incoming visual information. The 28 

processed information forms the basis for a wide range of decisions, for example how to navigate the environment 29 

(Bonner and Epstein, 2017; Bartnik et al., 2025b), or how to use and manipulate objects (Bracci and Peelen, 30 

2013; Baldassano et al., 2017). To achieve this feat, the visual system is thought to process information in a 31 

highly parallelized manner (Ballard, 1986; Rousselet et al., 2004; Nassi and Callaway, 2009), dedicating distinct 32 

neural pathways and cortical areas to processing specific types of visual information (Zeki et al., 1991). 33 

One set of brain regions is involved in scene-specific processing (see Epstein and Kanwisher (1998); Epstein and 34 

Baker (2019); Bartnik and Groen (2023) for reviews). Scene-specific processing is thought to be important for a 35 

variety of perceptual and cognitive functions, ranging from extracting scene gist (Oliva, 2005) — distinguishing 36 

e.g. indoor and outdoor (Wilder et al., 2018) or man-made and natural environments (Groen et al., 2013) — 37 

to landmark-based wayfinding (Epstein and Vass, 2014), and orienting oneself in the environment (Marchette 38 

et al., 2014). Another set of regions has been implicated in object-specific processing (Kourtzi and Kanwisher, 39 

2001; Riesenhuber and Poggio, 2002), because they respond strongly to objects regardless of the scene context 40 

in which they appear (Grill-Spector et al., 2001; Rossion et al., 2003). Object-specific processing is thought to 41 

be important for e.g. tool use (Bracci and Peelen, 2013), finding food (Henderson et al., 2025), and other visual 42 

tasks that require recognition of or interactions with objects (DiCarlo et al., 2012). 43 

Two major gaps remain in our understanding of object- and scene-specific visual processing in the brain. First, their 44 

temporal processing trajectories remain poorly understood. While some object- and scene-specific components 45 

have been identified in electroencephalography (EEG) recordings (Harel et al., 2016), prior work has mostly 46 

focused on spatial localization of specialized brain regions using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). 47 

Since visual perception unfolds in milliseconds (Thorpe et al., 1996), it is important to elucidate to what extent 48 

cortical object and scene processing require different neural time scales, in addition to different cortical regions. 49 

Second, to identify specialized object and scene processing, prior research has typically relied on experimental 50 

paradigms that explicitly separate kinds of visual information across distinct stimulus conditions, for example 51 

contrasting pictures of isolated objects to those of scene environments devoid of objects (Epstein and Kanwisher, 52 

1998; Grill-Spector et al., 2001; Harel et al., 2016; Silson et al., 2016). In real life, humans rarely encounter 53 

isolated objects or stripped-down scenes; objects are embedded within scenes, and scenes contain multiple objects. 54 

Importantly, the brain is sensitive to these co-occurrences of objects and scenes in the real-world (Mack and 55 

Eckstein, 2011; Sadeghi et al., 2015; Bonner and Epstein, 2021), necessitating to study the separation of object 56 

and scene processing when both object and scene information are jointly presented as during real-world visual 57 

experience. 58 

Recently, deep neural networks (DNNs) have emerged as powerful tools to create image-computable encoding 59 

models that predict neural signals (e.g., Yamins et al., 2014; Güçlü and Van Gerven, 2015; Schrimpf et al., 60 

2018; Gifford et al., 2022). Such DNN-based encoding models can be used to identify the most predictive model 61 

architecture (Schrimpf et al., 2018; Conwell et al., 2024; Sartzetaki et al., 2024, 2025) or layer within a DNN (Güçlü 62 

and Van Gerven, 2015), and to determine what inductive biases, learning regimes or training data (Loke et al., 63 

2025) help predict neural signals (Doerig et al., 2023). Beyond varying the underlying DNN, image-computable 64 

encoding models further allow explicit manipulation of their visual inputs. For example, we have recently used 65 

DNN-based encoding to separate foveal from peripheral information to link the processing thereof to the temporal 66 

dynamics of EEG signals (Müller et al., 2026). Others have instead separated face from scene information to 67 

identify functional specialization in human visual cortex using fMRI (Ratan Murty et al., 2021). 68 

Here, we combine these two components to identify temporal profiles of object and scene processing in human 69 

EEG during perception of intact natural images, in which object and scene information are simultaneously present. 70 

Specifically, we use encoding models that receive as input only one type of visual information — pixels labeled 71 

as objects or pixels labeled as scene information — to identify the time points in visual processing during which 72 

these elements are encoded in EEG responses. With several control analyses, we validate that only the selection of 73 

distinct object or scene pixels, but no other spatial selections elicit high encoding performance. Using state-of-the- 74 

art image segmentation models, we go beyond the binary object-scene categorization and identify how individual 75 

object classes and scene elements are temporally encoded in the human brain. Using this approach, we derive 76 

temporal templates of object- and scene-specific visual processing and use these to characterize the typicality of 77 

the processing of objects or scene elements in the brain. 78

EEG experiment 88 

Experimental design To study the temporal dynamics of visual processing of the OADS natural scene stimuli, 89 

we used the electroencephalography (EEG) recordings from Müller et al. (2026). In that study, 31 participants 90 

were presented with a total of 4680 unique stimuli (702 per participant) in a rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) 91 

paradigm (Grootswagers et al., 2019; Gifford et al., 2022) ( Fig. 1B, left ). Each participant was presented with 92 

240 RSVP trials, in which 20 images were presented for 100 ms each in rapid succession, interleaved with 300 93 

ms grayscale images. Participants performed a target detection task on interspersed indoor scene images (max. 94 

1 indoor image per trial; half of all trials were target trials) and were asked to respond at the end of each trial 95 

via a button press. Target scenes were selected from a separate, custom dataset and were, together with the 96 

corresponding EEG responses, excluded from analysis. Stimuli were split into a training (66%, N=468) and a 97 

test set (33%, N=234). Training stimuli were repeated 5 times during the EEG experiment and test stimuli were 98 

repeated 10 times. Stimuli were presented on a 24-inch monitor with a resolution of 2560 ×1440 pixels and a 99 

refresh rate of 144 Hz. Subjects were seated 63.5 cm from the monitor and were using a head rest such that 100 

stimuli spanned 50 ×29.5 degrees of visual angle. More details about the experimental design can be found in 101 

Müller et al. (2026). 102 

EEG analysis EEG data was collected using a Biosemi 64-channel ActiveTwo EEG system (Biosemi Instrumen- 103 

tation) with an extended 10–20 layout. EEG recording was followed by offline re-referencing to external electrodes 104 

placed on the earlobes. Resulting EEG data was pre-processed according to our lab’s standard pipeline (Groen 105 

et al., 2012) using the MNE software package (Gramfort et al., 2013), and involved filtering (high-pass filter at 106 

0.1 Hz and a low-pass filter at 30 Hz), artifact rejection (removal of deflections > 300 μV ), epoch segmentation 107 

in -100 to 400 ms from stimulus onset, ocular correction using electro-oculograms (EOG) electrodes, baseline 108 

correction between -100 and 0 ms, and a current source density transformation. Specific details can be found 109 

in Müller et al. (2026). The resulting event-related-potential (ERP) data were averaged across repetitions per 110 

unique image, thus resulting in an ERP specific to each subject, electrode and image. 111

and the upper bound as the correlation between the average of the first split of 5 repetitions and the average of 118 

all 10 repetitions. Noise ceilings were computed separately for each subject, electrode and time point. 119 

Image annotations 120 

Manual object annotations Each OADS image was manually annotated using the free version of the Supervisely 121 

annotation tool ( https://supervisely.com ). Images were downsampled to 1374x918 pixels, converted to 122 

JPEG, and then uploaded to the web-based annotation tool. Multiple annotators (the authors and 9 undergraduate 123 

students from their lab) were instructed to identify all instances of each object class within each image and to 124 

draw the smallest rectangles that included all pixels belonging to the object (see Fig. 1A right, for a list of 125 

object classes). Areas that were cluttered with multiple overlapping objects were annotated with the special class 126 

"MASK" and these annotations were excluded from all analysis (i.e. these areas do not belong to the object image 127 

version but are instead included in the scene version). For an overview of the number of annotated pixels per 128 

object class and their spatial distribution see Fig. S1 . 129 

For each image, we used the manually created annotations to construct multiple stimulus versions that only show 130 

information of a certain type. Specifically, we created an Objects version, in which all pixels falling within any 131 

bounding box were kept intact, while all other pixels (falling outside of bounding boxes) were masked (set to 0 132 

for all color channels). Additionally, we created a Scene version, in which all pixels falling within any bounding 133 

box were masked (set to 0), while all other pixels were kept intact. Last, we also included a stimulus version that 134 

uses the original, full, intact image. 135 

Automated image annotations The manually created annotations are focused on object information and were 136 

used to globally distinguish between object and scene (i.e., non-object) information and between different kinds of 137 

objects. However, these annotations do not differentiate scene elements such as buildings, ground, or sky. While 138 

objects often have a largely convex shape and can thus be annotated using simple bounding boxes, scene elements 139 

such as buildings or the ground often have complex shapes and are difficult to capture using bounding boxes. 140 

Further, alternative annotations such as binary masks or complex polygons are laborious for human annotators. 141 

We thus opted to use a state-of-the-art scene segmentation model by Cheng et al. (2022) to obtain pixel-wise 142 

labels, covering 71 object and scene part classes ( Fig. 1C ). For each scene element X, we created an additional 143 

stimulus version, in which only pixels annotated as X were kept intact while all other pixels were masked. For an 144 

overview of the number of annotated pixels per scene element and their spatial distribution see Fig. S2 . 145 

Computational modeling 146 

To study how different image information is encoded in human EEG recordings, we built linearized encoding 147 

models based on the features of a pretrained deep neural network (DNN) to predict stimulus-evoked event-related 148 

potentials (ERPs) at each time point and electrode for each participant. For each of the stimulus versions (full 149 

image, objects-only, scene-only, and individual scene-parts), we built separate encoding models that each received 150 

stimuli in which only a specific subset of pixels was kept intact while the remaining pixels were masked ( Fig. 1B ). 151 

DNN feature extraction We separately extracted the features from a task-optimized AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al., 152 

2012) trained on object classification on ImageNet (Russakovsky et al., 2015) for each stimulus version, i.e., for 153 

the full images, the objects-only version (with masked scene-pixels), and the scene-only version (with masked 154 

object-pixels). Specifically, we extracted the output of all three max-pool layers ("features.2", "features.5", and 155 

"features.12") and used them to build separate encoding models to predict the same EEG data from participants 156 

viewing the full, intact stimuli. The resulting encoding performance for each stimulus version was subsequently 157 

averaged across layers. Layer-wise encoding performances can be found in Supplementary Fig. S3 ). 158 

Linearized encoding model To construct a single encoding model, we performed dimensionality reduction on 159 

DNN features and fitted a linear regression model, following standard approaches in EEG encoding (e.g., Gifford 160 

et al., 2022). We first flattened the extracted features for each stimulus and then applied principal component 161 

analysis (PCA), reducing the number of features to 100. Next, we fitted a linear regression model per participant, 162 

time point, and electrode, linearly mapping the features of the training set stimuli onto the measured ERP 163 

amplitudes to those stimuli. We evaluated the model’s cross-validated encoding performance by applying the 164 

fitted model to the features of the test stimuli and calculating the Pearson correlation (r) between the measured 165 

and the predicted ERP amplitudes on those stimuli. The cross-validated encoding performance was averaged 166 

across the 18 posterior electrodes (O1, O2, Oz, Iz, P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, PO3, PO7, POz, PO4, PO8, 167 

I1, I2, Pz) and participants, and then used to compare different models across time points in visual processing. 168

Encoding model comparison We use capitalization to refer to the different encoding models, such as "Objects" 169 

for the encoding model receiving object-only stimuli, "Scene" for the model receiving scene-only stimuli, etc. We 170 

compare models in two ways: based on their raw cross-validated encoding performance (r), or based on partial 171 

correlations. To compute partial correlations for two encoding models X1 and X2, we first obtained their predicted 172 

responses P1 and P2, and then computed the correlation between P1 and the measured ERP data while partialling 173 

out the variance that is explained by P2 and vice versa. 174 

Control analyses 175 

We here separate object from scene information in images directly by masking all pixels that lie either within 176 

(object information) or outside (scene information) the object bounding boxes. As a consequence, the used 177 

stimuli contain large black areas. To test whether this could lead to changes in the predictivity of EEG data that 178 

is unrelated to the distinction between object and scene information, we created control analyses in which we also 179 

masked large numbers of pixels, but without explicitly dividing object and scene information. 180 

Shifted bounding boxes The annotated object bounding boxes have two general properties: spatial coherence 181 

(i.e., the bounding box contains a contiguous set of adjacent pixels) and a semantic link to the visual information 182 

(i.e., the physical pixel values depict a meaningful object). First, we tested whether maintaining the spatial 183 

coherence but removing the semantic link affected the EEG predictivity. To do so, we systematically shifted the 184 

position of each individual bounding box away from its original location by 20, 40, 60 or 80% until there was no 185 

more overlap between the object label and the information the bounding box includes (100%). For each bounding 186 

box, the shifting direction was randomly chosen from left, right, up or down. We also included a version where the 187 

true bounding boxes were randomly placed within the image. By simply changing the position of each bounding 188 

box we maintained both the spatial coherence property of each individual bounding box as well as the average 189 

number of pixels that were masked for a given image. For each of these image versions, we then extracted the 190 

DNN features, and fitted new encoding models. 191 

Patch shuffling Next, we tested how EEG predictivity was affected by keeping the semantic link between 192 

selected pixels and visual object labels intact whilst manipulating the spatial coherence of masked pixels. To do 193 

so, we ran three additional analyses that are all based on patch shuffling (inspired by Biederman (1972), showing 194 

that a disrupted spatial layout leads impair object recognition performance). We split the image into multiple, 195 

equally-sized patches, and subsequently shuffled the order of the patches, while maintaining image extent and 196 

aspect ratio. With these analyses, we tested if the disruption of the global scene layout selectively affects scene 197 

or object processing profiles, similar to how it negatively affected behavior in the Biederman (1972) study. In the 198 

first analysis, we applied this patch shuffling to the full, intact image, thereby disrupting both object and scene 199 

information. Second, we applied the patch shuffling to the Scene version, i.e., after masking all pixels within the 200 

object bounding boxes. In this analysis, all pixels originally labeled as objects are removed, however, the black 201 

areas introduces by the bounding boxes are also split up. In the third analysis, we first applied the patch shuffling 202 

to the full image and only afterwards masked pixels at the original location of the intact bounding boxes. In this 203 

version, the bounding boxes applied to the stimuli are comparable to the ones used in the Object version, but 204 

some coherent object information is retained in the split image, as the location of the bounding boxes does not 205 

fully match the location of object-pixels. In all three analyses, we systematically increased the number of splits 206 

from 0 to 2, 3, 4, 10, 21, including both horizontal and vertical splits. The number of splits were chosen such 207 

that the image can be split into equally-sized patches, without padding. 208 

Foveal and peripheral information In our stimulus set, most annotated objects are positioned around the 209 

horizontal meridian (see Fig. S1 ). By design of the EEG experiment, the center of the images is also the point 210 

of fixation of the participants. Thus, information at the center of the images falls within foveal regions of the 211 

retina which, as we showed previously (Müller et al., 2026), dominates the EEG signal at posterior electrodes. To 212 

test whether this overlap of object information with foveal regions could explain the observed temporal differences 213 

in encoding profiles for the Object and Scene models, we included additional encoding models using stimulus 214 

versions in which either only foveal information was maintained ("Center"), while peripheral information was 215 

masked, or vice-versa ("Periphery"). Similarly to separating object from scene information, we separated foveal 216 

from peripheral information by either masking all pixels at the center of the image (1% of the total image area) 217 

or by keeping those pixels intact and masking all other pixels. For these images, showing either only foveal 218 

information or peripheral information, we then extracted the DNN features and fitted linear encoding models, and 219 

subsequently obtained the model predictions for stimuli in the held out test set. We then quantified the amount 220 

of shared variance explained by foveal and object information, and between peripheral and scene information, by 221 

calculating partial correlations (see section "Statistical analysis" below for more details). 222

Statistical analyses 242 

For all analyses comparing time point-wise significant difference of encoding performances (correlations or partial 243 

correlations) (between models or against 0; Fig. 2B, C and Fig. 5C, D ), we performed non-parametric cluster- 244 

level permutation t-tests across participants for each time point using alpha=0.05 (Maris and Oostenveld, 2007; 245 

Sassenhagen and Draschkow, 2019). For all analyses comparing time-averaged encoding performances (correla- 246 

tions or partial correlations) between models ( Figs. 2B, 5C,D ), we performed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across 247 

participants with FDR-correction (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). 248 

To compare whether encoding models show differences in the time point of maximum encoding performance, we 249 

used bootstrapping across participants, for robust estimations of peak encoding performances. Specifically, across 250 

10000 bootstrap iterations, we randomly sampled with replacement from the set of 31 participants, then averaged 251 

the encoding performances within the sample and identified the time point of the maximum, for each encoding 252 

model. We then compared the variance of the peak time points across encoding models against the variance of a 253 

null distribution by first shuffling the order of encoding models before computing the variance, yielding a p-value 254 

(number of iterations with equal or larger variance of the null distribution compared to the observed variance). We 255 

used this bootstrapping method to compare the peak time points of the partial correlations between the Object 256 

and Scene models ( Fig. 2C ), as well as to compare the peak time points of the models used shown in Fig. 3D 257 

(for the following order: Objects, Shifted 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100%, Random Bounding Boxes), and for the 258 

models shown in Figs. 4C, 4F, 4I (each for the following order: 0, 2, 3, 4, 10, 21). 259 

For the analysis in Fig. 6C , we tested how masking the pixels of individual object classes influenced the encoding 260 

performance compared to models receiving pixels from all object classes. We related the relative differences in 261 

encoding performance for each individual object class to the proportion of pixels that each class takes up. Similarly, 262 

we tested how the encoding performance changes between the Scene model and models that receive all scene 263 

information plus the pixels from one object class ( Fig. 6D ) as well we how removing individual scene elements 264 

obtained using automated image segmentation changes the encoding performance compared to using all scene 265 

information ( Fig. 7C-D ). For all three analyses, we quantified the relationship between proportion of masked 266 

pixels and relative change in encoding performance using a linear regression per participant. To test whether any 267 

object classes or scene elements form an outlier in this linear relationship, we performed a Wilcoxon signed-rank 268 

test for each object class and scene element, testing if the residuals across participants are significantly larger than 269 

0 (alpha=0.05; fdr-corrected across all object classes or all scene elements; outliers are highlighted with asterisks 270 

in the figures). 271 

# Results 272 

The goal of the current study was to identify temporal signatures of scene and object processing in the brain 273 

during naturalistic vision. To this end, we developed image-grounded encoding models which we applied to a 274 

large-scale dataset consisting of human EEG responses to high-resolution, natural images of real-world, urban 275 

environments. First, we manually created bounding box annotations for a set of 16 object classes, identifying for 276 

each image which objects are present, where they are located, and their spatial extent ( Fig. 1A ). Second, for 277 

each image, we divided pixels into two groups - objects or scene, whereby all pixels contained within the bounding 278 

boxes were defined as objects and all remaining pixels were defined as scene. Then, we used these two groups of 279 

pixels to create new stimulus versions that showed either only the object pixels, or only the scene pixels, while all 280 

other pixels were masked (set to 0; see Fig. 2A ). For each stimulus version, we then extracted the convolutional 281 

activation maps of a pre-trained DNN and fitted an encoding model (models referred to as "Objects" and "Scene") 282 

to predict EEG data recorded from human participants viewing the full, intact stimuli. For comparison, we also 283 

built encoding models for the original, unmanipulated images ("Full"). Encoding models were trained to predict 284

Temporally distinct processing of object and scene information 287 

The average cross-validated encoding performance (r) for the Full model rises quickly starting at 80 ms after 288 

stimulus onset and peaks at 110 ms, showing that the EEG responses can be robustly predicted from the DNN 289 

features (see Fig. 2B ). The Objects and Scene models have a lower encoding performance on average (see bar 290 

plot in inset). This is expected since parts of the image are masked, thus part of what participants saw on the 291 

screen during the experiment is not included in the encoding model. Importantly, the temporal dynamics of the 292 

encoding curves for Scene and Objects do not overlap exactly; at early time points ( ∼100 ms), Scene appears to 293 

explain more variance than Objects while at slightly later time points ( ∼150 ms) both models seem to perform 294 

equally well, followed by a period during which the Scene model has the best performance of the two. 295 

To directly compare the temporal profiles of scene and object encoding, we computed the partial correlations 296 

between models, thus removing their shared variance in predicting the EEG recordings. The resulting temporal 297 

differences are clearly pronounced, with scene information explaining more unique variance at early time points 298 

(permutation test, alpha=0.05: significant differences for 85-110 ms) and object information explaining a similar 299 

amount of unique variance at later time points (110-170 ms) in the ERP epoch (see Fig. 2C ). The time points 300 

of maximum encoding performance across subjects are significantly earlier for the Scene model compared to the 301 

Objects model (p=0.0114; see Methods for details of bootstrapping the time point of the peak). In the following, 302 

we refer to the peak encoding performance of the Scene model (around 85-110 ms) as the ’early window’ and 303 

to the peak encoding performance of the Object model (around 110-170 ms) as the ’late window’. These results 304 

suggest that scene and object information are processed across distinct neural timescales, following a coarse-to-fine 305 

processing hierarchy reflected in the temporal encoding profiles of EEG responses. 306

To test whether the Objects model encoding performance was robustly driven by the object-specific pixels inside 314 

the bounding box, we systematically shifted the bounding boxes, while maintaining the size, shape, and number 315 

of pixels of the original bounding box ( Fig. 3A ). This leads to a monotonic decrease of encoding performance 316 

relative to the object encoding profile for correct bounding boxes ( Fig. 3B ). Since these different control models 317 

all received a similar number of input pixels, this result indicates that the correctly annotated object information 318 

is required to obtain high encoding performance of EEG responses. 319 

We also trained a control model in which the original bounding boxes (i.e., with the same size and shape) 320 

were randomly positioned in the image. The performance of this model is reduced even further ( Fig. 3C ), to 321 

approximately 50% of the shifted model which no longer had any annotated object overlap (100% shift). This 322 

again demonstrates that different encoding models with equivalent numbers of input pixels, extracted from the 323 

same images and trained on the same EEG responses to those images, perform substantially differently depending 324 

on pixel selection. It also suggests that systematic spatial relations between bounding boxes that are somewhat 325 

kept intact when merely shifting the bounding boxes, but disrupted when randomly positioning them, contribute 326 

to the EEG encoding. 327 

Last, we asked whether shifting the bounding boxes - which stepwise removes object information and instead adds 328 

scene information to the input to the encoding model - has an effect on the time point of the peak encoding 329 

performance. As we found in Fig. 2C), scene information is encoded early while object information is encoded 330 

later in the EEG signal, and therefore a temporal shift of the encoding profile may be expected when including 331 

more scene background in the bounding boxes. However, gradually shifting the bounding boxes has no discernible 332 

effect on the latency of the peak encoding performance ( Fig. 3D ); trend analysis using linear contrast, one-sample 333 

t-test, df=30, t=-0.4302, p=0.6701). These results suggest that a fraction of scene information is insufficient to 334 

explain early EEG signals. Thus, in the following section we investigate how much of the intact scene information 335 

is necessary to explain scene processing. 336 

Scene encoding requires an intact spatial layout 337 

Above, we found that selection of object information leads to a later encoding performance compared to selecting 338 

scene information. We verified that the use of bounding boxes, introducing large masked areas, does not intrinsi- 339 

cally lead to the observed temporal shift: only bounding boxes that are correctly positioned on the original object 340 

information result in a high encoding performance in the late window. The results of this control analysis also 341 

suggested that more than just a fraction of the entire scene needs to be selected in order to explain EEG signals 342 

during the early window. Here, we asked if we can find the core property, of which disruption leads to selective 343 

decreases in scene encoding. 344

categorization performance for objects embedded in scenes, leading to the proposal that object processing is 346 

preceded by a scene template (global-to-local processing), which is consistent with our encoding results so far. If 347 

the early window truly reflects scene processing, the prediction is that shuffling should reduce this specific aspect 348 

of the encoding time course. We also predict that scene shuffling will shift encoding to the later object-based 349 

processing, i.e., that we can selectively shift encoding profiles by disrupting the scene template. 350 

To test these predictions, we implemented the scene shuffling test as originally performed by Biederman (1972) 351 

into our encoding modeling pipeline by training a new set of encoding models on input images with increasing 352 

degrees of fragmentation. By splitting up each entire stimulus into multiple patches and shuffling the positions 353 

of patches (see Materials and Methods), we disrupt the global scene layout, while ensuring that the same pixel 354 

values are present in the stimulus. We gradually increased the number of splits, thereby decreasing the size of 355 

each individual patch, leading to a stronger disruption of the scene layout and eventually of individual objects. 356 

We performed this analysis on three different stimulus sets, each accounting for a different potential confound. 357 

First, we used the intact, full stimulus, that contains both object and scene information ( Fig. 4A-C ). Next, to 358 

more precisely isolate scene processing, we mask pixels within the bounding boxes, which can be done either 359 

after or before the shuffling. Thus, second, we masked pixels at the location of the original bounding boxes after 360 

the patch shuffle, ensuring that the exact same number of pixels are masked compared to the Scene model in 361 

Fig. 2B , thus masking the same locations as in the original Scene model ( Fig. 4D-F ). Last, we masked pixels 362 

within bounding boxes before patch shuffling, ensuring that all pixels labeled as objects are masked ( Fig. 4G-I ). 363 

We find that scene shuffling indeed reduces encoding performance in the early window, associated with a global 364 

processing of the scene. With an increasing number of splits, the peak encoding performance systematically 365 

reduces ( Fig. 4B ), and shifts in time ( Fig. 4C , one-sample t-test, df=30, t=3.3717, p=0.0021). This confirms 366 

the impairment of scene processing. However, we do not perfectly isolate scene processing, because the shuffling 367 

also disrupts objects. When masking pixels within bounding boxes after shuffling, we still see a clear reduction 368 

of the early peak ( Fig. 4E ) and a shift of the temporal profile ( Fig. 4F , one-sample t-test, df=30, t=3.5741, 369 

p=0.0012), which we can now more safely attribute to a shift in global (scene-based) to local (object-based) 370 

processing. However, since the bounding boxes are applied after shuffling, some of the object information is still 371 

present in the encoding model input, limiting the precision of the isolation of scene processing. 372 

Last, we masked pixels within bounding boxes before shuffling ( Fig. 4G ), now ensuring that all pixels labeled as 373 

objects are masked. Average encoding performance reduces even more drastically for 21 splits compared to the 374 

previous control analysis (paired t-test, df=30, t=-8.1950, p<0.0001); Fig. 4H ) while the temporal profile does 375 

not shift significantly ( Fig. 4I , one-sample t-test, df=30, t=1.8132, p=0.0798), suggesting that we here disrupt 376 

both global and local (object-based) processing. Together, these results provide robust evidence that the different 377 

temporal encoding profiles depend on intact scene information during the early window and availability of intact 378 

local object information during the late window. 379 

Distinct encoding of object and foveal information 380 

In our stimulus set, objects were unequally distributed across the scene, reflecting their typical locations in the 381 

real-world (see Fig. 5A and Fig. S1 for heatmaps showing the frequency of appearances per object class for each 382 

pixel). Some object classes were overrepresented in the center of the image, which was processed in the fovea, 383 

while most scene information was present in the periphery. To test whether the observed differences in temporal 384 

profiles between object and scene information were driven by a systematic difference in fovea-periphery sampling, 385 

we calculated the partial correlation of the Objects model while removing the variance shared with another, new 386 

encoding model that only receives the information from the center of the image (see Fig. 5A , right and Methods). 387 

Similarly, we compute the partial correlation between the Scene model and an encoding model that only receives 388 

the information from the periphery of the image ( Fig. 5B , right; "Center" and "Periphery" encoding models 389 

taken from Müller et al. (2026)). We then compared the resulting partial correlations to those from Fig. 2C , 390 

i.e. comparing the Scene with the Objects model and vice versa. 391 

Fig. 5C shows the time course of the partial correlation for the Object model, while removing the overlap in 392 

predictions with the Scene model (green) or the Center model (red). At early time points, between 70-110 ms, 393 

partialling out the Center model predictions results in higher Object model encoding performance compared to 394 

the partial correlation with the Scene model. The same pattern can be observed for later time points (>150 ms) 395 

and holds true for the averaged encoding performance across the full time course ( Fig. 5C , inset). These results 396 

suggest that during most of the time course, except for a small window between 110 and 150 ms, the overlap 397 

between object encoding and scene encoding is larger than between object and foveal encoding, highlighting the 398 

small degree of overlap between foveal and object encoding. 399

is comparatively more reduced than when partialling out object information across the full time course (see 401 

Fig. 5D ), suggesting that scene information is encoded to some degree based on image information present in 402 

the periphery. The temporal specificity of the reduction of encoding performance when partialling out peripheral 403 

information to the early window (80 - 120 ms) is in line with our previous finding that EEG signals during this 404 

time window selective encode scene information. Importantly, however, the encoding performance for the Scene 405 

model controlled for peripheral sampling again does not fully reduce to chance, suggesting that scene encoding is 406

Templates for object and scene processing reveal typicality of object classes 409 

So far, we have found separate temporal encoding profiles for object and scene information within naturalistic, 410 

real-world environments. By testing various control models, we verified that these profiles are direct signatures of 411 

object and scene processing and require the selection of the intact object and scene information. However, these 412 

profiles were based on average performance across many object classes. Next, we asked whether it is possible to 413 

delineate distinct encoding profiles for individual object classes. 414 

For each individual object class, e.g., car, we built two new encoding models receiving either 1) images containing 415 

all objects except cars, or 2) images containing all scene information plus cars, but no other objects (see Fig. 6A ). 416 

We then compared the resulting encoding performance of these two models to that of the original Objects and 417 

Scene models, respectively (see Fig. 6B ). Considering the original Objects and Scene encoding time courses as 418 

representative ’templates’ of overall object- and scene-specific information processing, we reasoned that system- 419 

atic comparison of the new reduced (1) or enhanced (2) models to these templates could reveal class-specific 420 

information encoding, according to the following logic: if an object class was perceived and thus encoded much 421 

like any other object, then the removal of this object class should lead to a decrease in Object model encoding 422 

performance that is roughly proportional to the area that instances of this class take up in the images (measured 423 

as the proportion of pixels of one object class compared to all object classes). At the same time, adding this 424 

object class to the Scene model should not lead to a large change in encoding performance. In other words, if a 425 

specific object class was processed as a ’true’ object, its removal from the model should reduce overall Objects 426 

model encoding performance, but not affect Scene model performance. However, if an object class is perceived 427 

more like a part of the scene, the opposite pattern should be observed. 428 

We find that all but one object class follow the predicted linear relationship between the proportion of pixels and 429 

the decrease in encoding performance resulting from removing those objects from the Objects model ( Fig. 6C , 430 

left; R 2=0.89). Only when removing information labeled as "Tree" did encoding performance not decrease linearly 431 

with the proportion of pixels - instead it increased Object encoding performance. Additionally, the "Front door" 432 

class was identified as an outlier from this linear relationship (Z=75, p<0.0001; see Methods). Consistently, all 433 

but the same one class "Tree" followed a constant relationship between the number of pixels and the change in 434 

encoding performance resulting from adding specific object classes to the Scene model ( Fig. 6C , right; R 2=0.07). 435 

Inverse mirroring the observed increase in Object model performance due to removal of "Tree", adding "Tree" to 436 

the Scene model enhanced its encoding performance. This suggests that while trees (and front doors) were here 437 

labeled as objects, they are neurally processed as parts of a scene. 438

Scene processing template reveals decoupling of spatial extent and neural relevance 439 

Above, we considered individual object classes and defined scene information as all scene parts not contained 440 

within an object bounding box. However, just like objects, scene background can be subdivided into separate 441 

elements, such as buildings, sky or floor. Next, we investigated whether it is possible to identify individual 442 

processing signatures for such elements as well. 443 

Using a pretrained scene segmentation model Cheng et al. (2022), we obtained pixel-wise annotations for each 444 

image, now also including segmentations of scene parts (e.g., buildings, sky, or floor) in addition to objects. To 445 

verify the quality of annotations, we first selected those object annotation labels that were similar to the ones that 446 

were used for our original manual annotations, and compared the encoding performance of a model receiving pixels 447 

belonging to the automatically-segmented objects with the encoding performance of a model receiving the pixels 448 

belonging to the manually-annotated objects (see Supplementary Fig. S4 ). Both models yield similar encoding 449 

performances, although automated annotations lead to slightly lower encoding performance despite including more 450 

pixels. Still, this confirms that the automatically created segmentations were of comparable quality as our manual 451 

annotations. 452

encoding performances of removing the information of a specific scene part to that of a model receiving all scene 456 

information ( Fig. 7B ). As before, we related the difference in encoding performance to the proportion of removed 457 

pixels. 458

The human brain is thought to process incoming visual information efficiently through dedicated networks that 485 

are specialized for specific types of information, such as objects, faces, or scenes. While the spatial clustering 486 

of these networks into topographic regions has been studied extensively using fMRI, the temporal dynamics of 487 

specialized information encoding are less well understood. In addition, most prior research has relied on the 488 

use of artificial stimuli to separate individual visual components, limiting ecological validity. To identify whether 489 

the brain differentially encodes objects and scene elements over time we used a large-scale EEG dataset of 490 

temporally-resolved responses to large-field naturalistic images, and built neural encoding models of object and 491 

scene information processing. By separating object from scene information at the pixel level and fitting separate 492 

encoding models based on corresponding convolutional neural network features, we identified distinct temporal 493 

profiles of object and scene encoding as well as of individual object classes and scene elements, elucidating 494 

how individual components of real-world environments are encoded over the course of visual processing. Our 495 

results reveal that when humans perceive complex, realistic natural scenes, scene features are encoded earlier 496 

than object features, suggesting their representations involve distinct computational cascades. Our fine-grained 497 

analyses furthermore suggest that not all scene elements are ’perceived equally’: instead, certain image elements 498 

are overrepresented compared to others, disproportional to the number of pixels they occupy in the image. 499 

Temporal profiles of object and scene processing 500 

Previous work has suggested that the P2 ERP component can act as a marker of scene-specific processing (Harel 501 

et al., 2016). Here, we instead find that scene encoding peaks at 80-110 ms, preceding object encoding, consistent 502 

with theories of coarse-to-fine processing (global scene context precedes local object classification) (Bar, 2004; 503 

Hegdé, 2008) and behavioral studies demonstrating ultrarapid recognition of scenes from global image properties 504 

(e.g., Greene and Oliva, 2009). The difference in outcomes could be due to the use of naturalistic images, 505 

i.e. stimuli that contain both scene and object information, compared to the isolated stimuli for each condition in 506 

Harel et al. (2016), as well as the focus of the EEG analyses (ERP components versus encoding of DNN features). 507 

Prior studies have found substantial differences between naturalistic and artificial experimental conditions (Kayser 508 

et al., 2004; Hasson et al., 2010; Martin and Schröder, 2013; Talebi and Baker, 2012; David et al., 2004) and 509 

whether naturalistic stimuli should be preferred over artificial ones when studying the neural mechanisms underlying 510 

visual perception continues to be debated (Felsen and Dan, 2005; Rust and Movshon, 2005; Zhang et al., 2021). 511 

More recent work has leveraged representational similarity analysis (RSA) to investigate temporal differences in 512 

natural image information processing. Consistent with our results, Greene and Hansen (2020) found that image- 513 

computable low-level global scene features around 100 ms preceded encoding of high-level object features around 514 

170 ms in EEG responses. Similarly, using behavioral annotations, Mononen et al. (2025) showed that spatial 515 

structure predicts neural signals better at early time points compared to semantic content and vice-versa for later 516 

time points. Both these studies relied on non-spatially localized image descriptors to identify differences in neural 517 

dissimilarity across stimuli (i.e., each feature model used to construct a model comparison in RSA is based on 518 

the entire image), and thus did not differentiate between separate features within a single scene. In contrast, our 519 

image-grounded encoding models disentangle the encoding of scene information and object information within 520 

each unique stimulus (i.e., one feature per pixel), allowing for more precise tests of which specific image elements 521 

are driving variance in neural responses to natural images. 522 

Potential confounds for temporal encoding differences 523 

In our analyses, we addressed a number of factors that could have potentially confounded the temporal separation 524 

of scene and object information processing. First, we demonstrated that only the selection of object pixels, 525 

and no other spatial selection, leads to high encoding performance during the late window. Masking pixels in 526 

both randomly positioned bounding boxes and shifted bounding boxes led to a significant decrease in encoding 527 

performance. These results confirm that EEG signals around 150 ms selectively encode object information. 528 

Second, we showed that high encoding performance at early time points only arises when stimuli fed to the 529 

encoding model have an intact spatial layout. Consistent with long-established behavioral results (Biederman, 530 

1972), disrupting this spatial layout leads to an overall decrease in encoding performance and a temporal shift 531 

towards later time points. These results confirm that EEG signals around 100 ms selectively encode scene 532 

information. Additionally, the patch shuffle analysis provides an experimental mechanism for shifting the peak 533 

encoding performance from the early to the late window: increasing the number of splits on stimuli that still 534 

contain (some) intact object patches reduces early encoding performance more than late and systematically shifts 535 

the peak to a later time point. This temporal shift is absent when all object information was masked before 536 

shuffling the image patches. Together, these two analyses provide clear evidence for distinct temporal profiles of 537 

object and scene processing and an experimental mechanism for how to turn scenes into objects. 538 

Third, we investigated whether the separation of object from scene information coincides with the spatial separation 539 

of features between fovea and periphery. In the present EEG dataset, we previously found a temporal hierarchy 540 

in neural processing of peripheral and foveal information, with peripheral encoding (around 100 ms after stimulus 541 

onset) preceding foveal encoding (around 150 ms), coinciding exactly with our peak encoding windows for scene 542

exclusively driven by foveal vs. peripheral information sampling, respectively. This result is consistent with recent 549 

work showing that spatial biases do not fully account for fMRI response selectivity (Silson et al., 2024). 550 

Resolving the trade-off between controllability and ecological validity 551 

Typically, experiments targeting the neural mechanisms underlying (visual) perception face a trade-off between 552 

the controllability of experimental parameters, such as stimulus composition, versus their ecological validity. In 553 

their extremes, one excludes the other: a manually crafted visual stimulus, where each component (such as lines, 554 

circles, colors, etc.) is controlled by its own parameter, is highly unlikely to be found in nature - and these stimuli 555 

have drastically different statistical properties compared to natural stimuli (e.g., David et al., 2004). Contrarily, 556 

an image depicting a natural scene is a complex collection of lines, colors, and other components, that are difficult 557 

to experimentally manipulate without losing image naturalness (although state-of-the-art diffusion models may 558 

offer a new solution, see e.g., Garcia Cerdas et al. (2025)). Here, we propose that instead of considering (groups 559 

of) individual stimuli as separate experimental conditions, one can use image-computable encoding models that 560 

each receive different information from the same stimuli to form the experimental conditions. By using pixel-level 561 

annotations to manipulate the input to the models, rather than the stimuli presented to the participants, we gain 562 

back a form of experimental control thought to be lost when using natural stimuli. 563 

This method could be extended to experiments in virtual environments, further increasing the experimental control 564 

over visual information whilst increasing the level of immersion in the natural stimuli. This approach can also 565 

be employed in task-based paradigms, e.g. when manipulating covert attention using spatial cues or object-based 566 

priming, to identify how the encoding of the same natural, visual information changes under task influences. 567 

Finally, image-grounded encoding models can easily be applied to other brain measurement modalities (e.g., 568 

fMRI) to identify functional specialization, and expanded to other sensory modalities (e.g., audition). 569 

Limitations and suggestions for future work 570 

First, our stimuli exclusively consisted of Amsterdam street scenes, with limited diversity in object classes and 571 

scene parts. For example, there were few water-labeled pixels and encoding contribution those pixels was near- 572 

zero. Future research could determine whether these classes have a stronger encoding contribution in more diverse 573 

images depicting larger regions of those classes. Second, the indoor-detection task may have influenced the image 574 

features participants attended to — e.g., prioritizing information that helps differentiate indoor from outdoor 575 

scenes. However, since the object classes and scene parts we analyzed are both primarily found in outdoor scenes, 576 

we expect any task influences to similarly affect their processing. Furthermore, our prior work suggests limited 577 

influence of task instructions on early EEG responses to natural scenes (Groen et al., 2016; Bartnik et al., 2025a). 578 

Still, future research should validate if the same temporal differences occur across multiple tasks instructions and 579 

investigate how tasks and attention interact with the temporal distinction between object and scene processing. 580 

Conclusion 581 

We used deep neural network-based encoding models in combination with image annotations to delineate temporal 582 

dynamics of object- and scene-specific processing in EEG responses to ecologically-valid, natural scene images. We 583 

show that object encoding shows a delayed temporal profile compared to scene encoding and identify the unique 584 

contribution of individual object classes and scene elements to these profiles. These results suggest that object 585 

and scene processing in the human brain differentially unfold over time, in addition to being spatially segregated 586 

in specialized brain regions. Our image-grounded encoding modeling approach opens up new possibilities for 587 

disentangling how individual components of the real-world visual environments are encoded in the human brain. 588
