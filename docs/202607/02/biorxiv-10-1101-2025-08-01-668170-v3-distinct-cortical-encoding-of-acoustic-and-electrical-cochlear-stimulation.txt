Abstract 24 Cochlear implants are neuroprosthetic devices that restore hearing and speech comprehension to 25 profoundly deaf humans and represent an exemplar application of biomedical engineering and 26 research to clinical conditions. However, the utility of these devices in many subjects is limited, 27 largely due to lack of information about how neural circuits respond to implant stimulation. 28 Recently we showed that deafened rats can use cochlear implants to recognize sounds, and that 29 this training refined the responses of single neurons in the primary auditory cortex. Here we asked 30 how local populations of cortical neurons represent acute implant stimuli, using electrode arrays 31 we developed for cortical surface recordings for micro-electrocorticography (μECoG), a form of 32 intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG). We found that there was a coarse, non-random spatial 33 organization with limited evidence for consistent, sharply graded cochleotopy across recording 34 sites, relative to a clearer tonotopic spatial representation in normal-hearing rats. Single-trial iEEG 35 responses to acoustic inputs were more reliable than responses to cochlear implant stimulation, 36 although stimulus identity could be successfully decoded in both cases. However, the spatio-37 temporal response profiles to acoustic vs cochlear implant stimulation were substantially different. 38 Decoders trained on acoustic responses showed essentially zero information transfer when tested 39 on electrical stimulation responses in the same animals after deafening and cochlear implant 40 stimulation. Thus, while acute cochlear implant stimulation evoked spatially non-random cortical 41 activity with coarse cochleotopic structure, the dynamics of network activity were substantially 42 different from those evoked by acoustic stimulation, with possible implications for perceptual 43 similarity that remain to be tested. 44

Introduction 45 Cochlear implants are neuroprosthetic devices that restore hearing and provide users with an ability 46 to attain open-set speech perception without other aids such as lip-reading (Djourno and Eyries, 47 1957; Simmons et al., 1965; Michelson, 1971; House and Urban, 1973; Eddington et al., 1978; 48 Hochmair and Hochmair-Desoyer, 1981; Wilson et al., 1991; Clark, 2006; Svirsky, 2017). 49 Cochlear implants function by inserting a single and flexible shank of electrodes along the cochlear 50 spiral (generally 12-22 channels in human subjects), to stimulate primary afferent neurons of the 51 auditory system and bypass pathologies of deafness. Individual electrodes deliver trains of 52 electrical pulses, providing spectral cues as a function of location along the topographical axis of 53 the cochlear spiral, mimicking the encoding of sound in a functional and healthy cochlea. Over 1 54 million users have been implanted world-wide, making it the most widely adopted type of brain-55 computer interface, surpassing vagus nerve stimulators, deep-brain stimulation electrodes, and 56 artificial retinas (Hays et al., 2013; Dawson et al., 2016; Ayton et al., 2020; Zeng, 2022; Johnson 57 et al., 2024). Cochlear implants are thus the gold-standard for neuroprosthetic device use in terms 58 of performance, safety, and ability to fine-tune or personalize the programming of each device to 59 individual users. 60 61 Despite their wide adoption in human subjects over the last several decades, there is limited 62 understanding of how these devices restore functionality and the sense of hearing to users. One 63 long-standing question has been how the central auditory system responds to and interprets signals 64 resulting from electrical stimulation of auditory nerve fibers. It is unknown to what degree 65 emulating the patterns evoked by acoustic stimulation in normal hearing users is important for the 66 auditory system to interpret patterns of evoked neural activity and inform downstream auditory 67

Many structures of the central auditory system are organized tonotopically, following 68 the frequency alignment of the cochlear basilar membrane (Walzl and Woolsey, 1946; Merzenich 69 et al., 1973; Polley et al., 2007). This topographic mapping might facilitate the encoding of 70 auditory stimuli. Previous studies have demonstrated cochleotopic organization in auditory cortex 71 of cochlear implant animals (Bierer and Middlebrooks, 2002; Johnson et al., 2016). Here, our aim 72 was not to establish spatial organization per se, but to test whether acoustic and electrical 73 stimulation produce overlapping or transferable cortical population representations under a 74 common analysis framework. Previous psychophysical studies in human implant users have 75 provided indirect evidence for perceptual differences (McDermott, 2004) and similarities 76 (Vermeire et al., 2013), but direct neural evidence comparing representations in the same subjects 77 has been lacking. This question has profound implications for how we design and program 78 cochlear implants, or more generally other brain-computer interface devices. 79 80 Studies in non-human animals are required to understand the neural basis of cochlear implant use 81 and the relation to spatiotemporal representations in the auditory system. Responses to cochlear 82 implant stimulation have been studied in a number of species generally in non-behaving animals 83 under anesthesia (Klinke et al., 1999; Bierer and Middlebrooks, 2002; Middlebrooks and Bierer, 84 2002; Fallon et al., 2014; Johnson et al., 2016, Adenis et al. 2024), but occasionally in awake or 85 behaving animals (Vollmer and Beitel, 2011; Keating et al., 2013; Johnson et al., 2016). Over the 86 last decade we have examined cochlear implant responses in deafened rats (King et al., 2016; 87 Glennon et al., 2019), where we showed that animals can be trained to report the activation of 88 specific implant channels. Surgical procedure for iEEG recordings. All animal procedures were performed in accordance 121 with National Institutes of Health standards and were conducted under a protocol approved by the 122 NYU Grossman School of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. We used a 123 custom array consisting of 61 passive electrodes spaced 406 μm apart in an 8 x 8 grid, with three 124 corner electrodes omitted and the fourth corner electrode intentionally unexposed to test 125 encapsulation, reducing the number of active recorded sites to 60 (Insanally et al., 2016). iEEG 126 grids were fabricated using a standard flex -PCB processing technique by Microconnex Flex 127 Circuits (Snoqualmie, WA). All procedures and experiments were carried out in a sound-128 attenuation chamber and under anesthesia. 129 

A specific surgical protocol was developed for implantation of cortical surface iEEG in 130 rats. Sprague-Dawley rats 4–6 months old were anesthetized with ketamine (40 mg kg −1, 131 intramuscular injection) and dexmedetomidine (0.125 mg kg −1, intramuscular injection), or 132 pentobarbital (50 mg kg −1, intraperitoneal injection). The head was secured in a custom head-133 holder that left the ears unobstructed. A longitudinal incision was made along the midline to expose 134 the skull. Five bone screws were inserted into the skull around the point of entry of the electrode 135 array to help anchor the dental cement (C & B Metabond Quick! Luting Cement). After reflecting 136 the right temporalis muscle, a 5 mm × 5 mm craniotomy was made on the right temporal skull to 137 expose the brain, and a sterilized iEEG array was epidurally placed over the left hemisphere core 138 auditory cortex, located using vasculature landmarks. Behavioral training for tone and implant channel detection. Four animals with iEEG recording 189 electrodes were behaviorally trained to detect target tones. Rats were food restricted and trained 190 on a self-initiated, auditory go/no-go task (Froemke et al., 2013; Martins and Froemke, 2015; King 191 et al., 2016). Animals nosepoked in a designated port to initiate trials and are trained to nosepoke 192 in a different port if the target tone was presented (22.6 kHz, any intensity) or withhold from 193 nosepoking if a nontarget (foil) tone was presented (0.5-32 kHz excluding 4 kHz if 4 kHz was the 194 target, or 8-45.3 kHz excluding 22.6 kHz if 22.6 kHz was the target, at 0.5-1 octave intervals and 195 at any intensity). A sugar pellet reward was given for correct nosepokes within 2.5 s of target-tone 196 presentation, whereas a 7 s timeout was given if the animal incorrectly nosepoked for foil tones. 197 The four rats that achieved >70% target-tone hit rate and d’ ≥1.7 were included for further testing 198 and implantation. A second subset of three animals was behaviorally trained to detect cochlear 199 implant channel 3 (N=2) or channel 4 (N=1); procedures were the same for tones except that a 200 programmable clinical cochlear implant processor was used. These animals were trained for 3, 9, 201 and 13 days. 202

Processing of cortical iEEG recordings. All signal processing and analyses were performed 270 using custom MATLAB scripts (MathWorks, MA). To determine whether trials were functional or 271 contained artifacts, raw measurements were first filtered (2-150 Hz, 6 th order bandpass IIR filter) 272 and then root mean squared power (rms) was computed for each channel. All trials with rms 20-273 300 μV were considered functional; power less than 20 μV corresponded with pre-amplifier 274 saturation, while power greater than 300 μV consistently indicated intermittent corruption from 275 non-neural sources. Estimation of best frequency. All analyses of tone-evoked responses were restricted to the range 292 of 1.4-32 kHz tones. Best frequency was estimated by computing the center of mass of tone-evoked 293 responses at 70 dB SPL. The response m ass function was defined by the mean vector after 294 projecting all responses on a circular domain where vector angles are represented by tone 295 frequencies, to prevent biasing the center of mass towards the interior of the 1.4-32 kHz stimulus 296 range. 297

Tensor component analysis (TCA). We applied a canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition to a 3-312 dimensional model, enabling us to treat spatial and temporal aspects of iEEG recordings across 313 multiple trials as linearly independent (orthogonal) dimensions (Kolda and Bader, 2009; Williams 314 et al., 2018). Naive feature vectors were constructed by arranging all data into a three-dimensional 315 matrix composed of length-R post-stimulus responses, S responsive sites, and T stimulus trials 316 resulting in a R x S x T matrix. CP decomposition was performed (Bader et al., 2023) for 15 latent 317 factors, the number 15 chosen to exceed the number of tone or implant stimuli and to parallel the 318 data reduction performed by PCA. Then the resulting estimate of the T factors for each of the 15 319 components reduced our datasets into T (rows) x 15 (column) matrices. For cross-modal analyses, 320 TCA was used because it constrains latent factors into separable spatial, temporal, and trial 321 dimensions. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA). We trained a decoder for predicting stimulus identity from 327 single trial recordings by developing a supervised linear classifier . Each decoder was retrained and 328 retested 1,000 times to average decoder performance across randomizations for combinations of 329 trials used for training and test sets. Specifically, each bootstrap replicate was randomly partitioned 330 to include 13 trials per stimulus for each training set and the remaining trials being used to test the 331 decoder. LDA was applied to estimate the stimulus-likelihood map of the PCA- or TCA-332 compressed feature space. Naive feature vectors from the training set were normalized and 333 compressed according to the learned transformations, and the maximum likelihood stimulus (i.e., 334 the identity of the tone frequency or active cochlear implant electrode) was estimated for each 335 response. Absolute accuracy of the decoder was computed as the average accuracy over all stimuli 336 was estimated by the total proportion of correct predictions. In addition to absolute accuracy, we 337 also computed the mean of the error distances between the predicted and actual stimulus, i.e., 338 octaves or number of electrodes. 339 340

Reducing evoked responses using TCA preserves encoding of stimulus identity. 592 One challenge with using PCA is that the ordinations of dimensionality reduction are 593 unconstrained, because they are arbitrary linear combinations of the original variables. Discussion 747 Cochlear implants are the gold standard for success of brain-computer interfaces and 748 neuroprosthetic devices to safely activate the human nervous system and effectively restore 749 functional sensation (Hochmair et al., 2006; Wilson and Dorman, 2008; Clark, 2015; Svirsky, 750 2017; Glennon et al., 2020). While high levels of hearing and speech processing can be achieved 751 by some users, outcomes remain highly variable in terms of learning rates and peak performance, 752 especially in real-world conditions and even after controlling for age and durations of deafness 753 (Blamey et al., 2013). Invariably, all users require an adaptation period; speech perception 754 outcomes are initially poorer than later timepoints (Holden et al., 2013; Cusumano et al., 2017; 755 Caswell-Midwinter et al., 2022; Stronks et al. 2025), indicating both that acute encoding of 756 cochlear implant stimulation by the central auditory system is not optimal and that neuroplastic 757 processes are needed for maximizing outcomes. Current and future advances in engineering and 758 implant programming algorithms might help improve outcomes further, but there is a general belief 759 in the field that what is now required is an understanding of how the central nervous system 760 responds to peripheral electrical stimulation (Kral et al., 2002; Kral and Eggermont, 2007; Wilson 761 and Dorman, 2008; Moore and Shannon, 2009). 762 763 Our findings provide the direct neural evidence that, at the time of acute cochlear implant 764 activation, acoustic and electrical cochlear stimulation evoke cortical population responses with 765 poor representational transfer. This addresses a longstanding question in cochlear implant research: 766 whether electrical stimulation of the cochlea can evoke the same cortical representations as 767 acoustic stimulation. The lack of transferable representations suggests that cochlear implant users 768 may initially experience qualitatively different percepts that cannot be predicted from normal 769

Invasive recordings are not feasible for human cochlear 778 implant recipients, and thus decoding methods based on purely-invasive measures may be difficult 779 to translate into human subjects for optimizing implant programming and/or training for use of the 780 device. While our iEEG recordings are also invasive and under anesthesia, the principles of iEEG 781 analysis for cochlear implant responses developed here might be more applicable to non-invasive 782 EEG recordings or similar approaches more easily performed in humans. Grid electrodes allowed 783 us to take simultaneous advantage both of temporal and spatial features in the data, some of which 784 were more obvious in ERPs, others were more latent in HG signals or PCA/TCA factors. 785 786 Our results show that these features could provide accurate stimulus decoding even on single trials, 787 indicating that: 1) use of these methods in translational or clinical settings could aid personalization 788 and optimization of brain-computer interfaces, and 2) single-trial implant responses can be 789 adequately processed by auditory cortex and presumably downstream regions related to perception 790 and behavior. Longitudinal recordings during chronic implant use will be required to distinguish 830 between these possibilities. Previously we showed that deafened rats could learn to use a cochlear 831 implant to perform an auditory task, and that changes in cortical excitatory and inhibitory synapses 832 related to implant use outcomes. Our results suggest that refinement particularly of inhibitory 833 cortical responses might help increase inter-neuronal variance at the population level to increase 834 spatial organization, while decreasing intra-trial variability at the single neuron level to enhance 835 temporal organization and improve auditory perception. 836
