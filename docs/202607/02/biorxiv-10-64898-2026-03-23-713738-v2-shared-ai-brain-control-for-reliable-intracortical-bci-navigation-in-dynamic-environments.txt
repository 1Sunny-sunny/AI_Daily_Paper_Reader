# Abstract 16 

Intracortical brain–computer interfaces (iBCIs) can enable people with paralysis to 17 control assistive devices, but reliable operation in dynamic environments remains limited by 18 

fluctuations in decoded neural commands. Here we develop a confidence -modulated AI–brain 19 shared-control framework in which an artificial intelligence copilot adaptively integrates the 20 decoded neural commands with a probabilistic temporal prior to stabilize execution while 21 preserving user intent. In two macaques performing closed-loop virtual navigation tasks in 22 

complex environments , shared-control nearly eliminated execution -level failures, including 23 obstacle collisions and target overshoot, while maintaining the directional structure of neural 24 commands. Abrupt target changes revealed a boundary condition : temporal stabilization 25 transiently delays responsiveness when recent history was no longer predictive. Offline replay 26 showed that resetting the temporal prior eliminated this lag and restored performance, 27 demonstrating that the impairment was algorithmic rather than a failure of neural decoding. # Introduction 32 

Brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) aim to restore movement by translating neural activity into 33 

control signals for external devices. Both invasive and non -invasive systems now achieve 34 impressive performance in controlled laboratory settings, enabling cursor control, constrained 35 reaching, and basic manipulation 1,2 . However, clinical adoption will require these systems to 36 operate reliably beyond constrained laboratory settings, where assistive devices must move 37 through cluttered and changing environments while preserving the user’s evolving intended 38 actions. 39 

A major challenge for real -world BCI control is that decoded neural commands are 40 

variable. During continuous control, short -timescale fluctuations in decoded velocity may not 41 

reflect genuine changes in user intent, but they are nevertheless expressed directly in device 42 movement. In BCI-controlled assistive devices such as powered wheelchairs or robotic 43 manipulators , such fluctuations can accumulate into unsafe or inefficient behavior, including 44 

collisions, unstable trajectories or failures to reach the intended goal. Reliable BCI use therefore 45 

requires more than accurate decoding of movement intent: it requires mechanisms that stabilize 46 

the execution of decoded commands while remaining responsive to meaningful changes in 47 intent. 48 

One strategy is to combine neural control with AI assistance that stabilizes unreliable 49 commands and compensates for environmental constraints 3,4 . Shared-control systems can use 50 information about the environment to improve safety and reduce the burden of continuous 51 

control. However, excessive autonomy risks overriding the user’s intended actions and may 52 reduce the sense of agency that is central to the acceptance and long-term use of assistive 53

neurotechnology 5,6 . This creates a central design problem for shared-control BCIs 4,7 : assistance 54 

must correct unreliable execution without substituting the user’s decisions. 55 

Shared control has been explored in both non -invasive and invasive BCIs. In many non-56 invasive BCI systems, decoded commands are low-dimensional or discrete, and reliable 57 navigation often requires substantial autonomous support 3–5,7–10 . By contrast, invasive BCIs can 58 provide continuously varying control signals that contain rich information about intended 59 

movement. In this regime, the goal of assistance is not to determine the trajectory autonomously, 60 

but to stabilize noisy commands when intent is present yet unreliable. While shared -control 61 elements have been incorporated into invasive systems to improve robustness 11–16 , previous 62 

approaches rely on fixed blending or phase -specific assistance, with limited focus on 63 

continuously adapting assistance to fluctuations in decoded control reliability. Consequently, 64 

confidence -modulated continuous shared control for high-bandwidth invasive BCI navigation 65 under competing demands for stabilization and rapid responsiveness has not previously been 66 demonstrated. 67 

Here, we developed an AI–brain shared-control framework for in vasive BCI navigation 68 under dynamic environmental constraints and abrupt changes in intended goals. The controller 69 integrates decoded neural velocity commands with a probabilistic model of recent movement 70 

history and environmental constraints, allowing it to stabilize execution while preserving the 71 directional structure of the decoded command. We tested this framework in two macaques 72 performing closed-loop virtual navigation using intracortical BCI control (Saussus et al., 2026). 73 

The tasks imposed complementary demands on the controller: obstacle-avoidance tasks 74 required stabilization of noisy but goal-consistent commands, whereas a target-respawn task 75 required rapid updating after an abrupt change in goal. We show that AI –brain shared control 76 

markedly improves navigation reliability by suppressing execution -level failures such as collisions 77 and target overshoot, while leaving the decoded direction of intent largely intact. Rapid goal 78

# Results 84 

To evaluate how shared-control arbitrates between stabilization and responsiveness 85 

under decoder ambiguity, we examined three navigation tasks imposing opposing demands on 86 the controller in two nonhuman primates implanted with three 96-channel Utah arrays. In the first 87 

task, decoded velocities from the intracortical BCI controlled a sphere navigating toward a fixed 88 target while avoiding a static obstacle positioned between the start location and the goal (Fixed 89 

Obstacle). This task , previously performed under neural control alone 17 , provided a benchmark 90 for assessing whether shared-control stabilizes execution under stable environmental structure .91 

In a second task, the obstacle emerged unexpectedly during the trial (Appearing Obstacle), 92 allowing us to test stabilization under sudden environmental perturbations while the intended 93 goal remained unchanged. In a third task, the target location was reassigned mid -trial (Respawn), 94 requiring rapid abandonment of prior action history and responsiveness to an abrupt change in 95 goal intention. 96 

These tasks impose complementary demands: obstacle tasks require suppressing 97 

execution variability, whereas the respawn task requires rapid updating to goal changes. 98 

Demonstrating effective arbitration across both regimes provides a stringent test of adaptive 99 shared-control under ambiguity in the decoded control signal. Representative trials appear in 100 Supplementary Movies 1–3. 101

## Shared-control improves reliability of BCI navigation 102 

Shared-control significantly increased task success across all navigation tasks, with 103 

consistent gains across subjects and stable performance over months ( Figure 1 ). In the Fixed 104 

Obstacle task ( Figure 1 A), shared-control substantially improved performance in both monkeys, 105 yielding ~30% higher success rates compared to BCI-only control. This improvement was 106 

significant in both animals (paired Wilcoxon signed -rank (two -sided): Monkey 1: median Δ = 0.313, 107 

p = 4.88 × 10 -4 ; Monkey 2: median Δ= 0.268, p = 9.77 x 10 -4 ). Monkey 2, who was naïve to this task 108 

at the start of the experiments, showed a gradual improvement across sessions even in the 109 absence of assistance (Spearman’s ρ = 0.755, p = 0.01), whereas Monkey 1 —previously trained 110 

on this task (REF) —reached an average success rate of approximately 80% with shared -control, 111 matching its earlier No-Obstacle benchmark performance (BCI -only). 112 

Performance gains with shared-control persisted in the more challenging Appearing Obstacle 113 

task ( Figure 1 B), in which an obstacle appeared unexpectedly during navigation. In this task, 114 shared-control produced large improvements in success rates (Monkey 1: 67% vs. 39%; median 115 

Δ = 0.267, p = 3.91 × 10 -3 ; Monkey 2: 56% vs. 22%; median Δ = 0.356, p = 3.91 × 10 -3 ). Without 116 

assistance, Monkey 2’s performance remained near chance, underscoring that shared -control 117 was essential for successful navigation under these conditions. 118 

To assess the stability of shared-control benefits over time, we repeated the Appearing 119 

Obstacle task in Monkey 1 six months later ( Figure 1 C). Although baseline performance without 120 shared-control improved substantially over this interval (average success rate 57% vs. 39%), 121 shared-control continued to confer a significant benefit, increasing success by an additional 22% 122 

and restoring performance to approximately 80% —comparable to both the Fixed Obstacle task 123 and prior No-Obstacle benchmarks (without AI). 124

## Shared-control preferentially improves unreliable neural control 182 

We next examined how the benefit of shared -control depended on baseline BCI 183 

performance across target directions, pooling data across sessions, monkeys, and tasks ( Figure 184 

3). Although shared-control improved success rates for all targets, the magnitude of improvement 185 varied with baseline (BCI-only) performance. Specifically, shared-control gain followed an 186 inverted-U relationship: targets with very low (<10 –15%) or very high (>75–80%) baseline success 187 showed little additional improvement with shared-control, whereas targets with intermediate 188 baseline performance (~30– 60%) exhibited the largest gains (quadratic fit, R² = 0.6 5, quadratic 189 term p = 8.89 x 10 -6 ). 190

## Shared control reveals a limitation under rapid goal changes 269 

We next examined how the arbitration variable α evolved around abrupt task events and 270 

whether its dynamics help explain both performance gains and failures. As α reflects relative 271 

rather than absolute arbitration, identical values can correspond to different assistance strengths 272 across trials. Across tasks involving unexpected environmental changes, α exhibited a273 stereotyped, event-locked modulation ( Figure 5). a pattern that was consistent across monkeys and sessions. This modulation was tightly time -276 

locked to the true obstacle appearance and substantially larger than fluctuations observed 277 around pseudo-events placed at random times along the same trajectories (controlling for slow 278 within-trial trends in α), indicating that it reflects a transient disruption of the controller’s short -279

# Discussion 319 

Reliable and safe control remains a central barrier for invasive brain–computer interfaces, 320 particularly in dynamic environments where variability in decoded neural signals can produce 321 unstable or unintended device behavior. Here we show that confidence -modulated AI–brain 322 shared control improves the reliability of continuous intracortical BCI navigation by stabilizing 323 

how decoded commands are executed . This stabilization produced large and consistent gains in 324 obstacle-based tasks by suppressing execution -level failures, but introduced transient delays 325 when rapid target reassignment required the controller to abandon recent action history. These 326 

asymmetric effects reveal a core design constraint for shared -control BCIs: assistance must 327 stabilize unreliable commands without slowing true changes in user intent .328 

A central insight is that shared control improved performance primarily by stabilizing 329 

execution, rather than altering the directional content of the decoded command . Across tasks, 330 shared control nearly eliminated collisions and target overshoot, whereas trials in which the 331 decoded velocity consistently favored a non-cued target remained difficult. Conditioning 332 performance on the decoder-inferred choice state confirmed this distinction: assistance was 333 

most effective when decoded commands were at least partially aligned with the cued target, but 334 

provided little benefit when they consistently specified an incorrect target. Thus, the controller 335 converted noisy but goal-consistent commands into successful trajectories, while leaving 336 misdirected decoded commands largely unchanged. This indicates that shared control acted as 337 an execution -stabilizing layer, not as an autonomous intent-correction system. 338 

Previous shared-control approaches often resolved uncertainty by shifting control 339 

authority toward autonomy, either through fixed blending, phase -specific assistance or explicit 340 goal inference 10,13,14,18 . Such strategies can be effective when decoded commands are discrete, 341 low-dimensional or unreliable, but they may be less appropriate for high-bandwidth invasive BCIs 342 in which the decoded velocity carries meaningful moment-to-moment information about 343

intended movement. In this setting, assistance should not determine the trajectory on behalf of 344 

the user, but should regulate how noisy decoded commands are expressed in the effector. The 345 

present framework implements this principle by maintaining continuous expression of the 346 

decoded velocity while selectively suppressing execution -level instability induced by neural 347 variability or environmental constraints. Thus, for high-bandwidth BCIs, shared control may be 348 

most effective when it stabilizes action execution under uncertainty rather than substituting user 349 decisions. 350 

The arbitration variable α provides mechanistic insight by reflecting the consistency of 351 

recent control. When recent actions are consistent with task demands, α increases and 352 

strengthens temporal stabilization, reducing variability in execution. Abrupt deviations produce a 353 transient dip in α, indicating increased uncertainty. This mechanism explains both the 354 stabilization observed in obstacle-based tasks and the reduced responsiveness during goal 355 changes : temporal priors improve control when recent action history remains predictive, but can 356 impair responsiveness when that history becomes misleading. 357 

Rapid goal changes reveal this boundary condition directly. In the Respawn task, the 358 controller received no explicit signal of target reassignment and could update its posterior only in 359 response to changes in the decoded velocity. Under these conditions, the executed trajectory 360 

realigned more slowly, reflecting temporal inertia in the short -term action prior. Offline analyses 361 demonstrated that this interference was algorithmic: resetting the prior eliminate d the lag and 362 restored performance. Notably, abrupt task events were accompanied by reliable, event -locked 363 dips in α, suggesting that α may provide an online signal for detecting violations of recent action 364 consistency. Together, these findings show that stabilization and responsiveness are not 365 

independent objectives, but arise from the same temporal assumptions, which future controllers 366 

may adapt in a context -dependent manner when recent action history is no longer predictive. 367

This 370 suggests that shared control is most useful in an intermediate regime where decoded commands 371 contain goal-relevant information but are too variable to support reliable execution on their own .372

## Shared-control paradigm. 469 

Decoded velocity signals exhibit transient fluctuations that do not necessarily correspond 470 to genuine changes in user intent. In cluttered environments, even small deviations can 471 accumulate into obstacle collisions or other constraint violations. To mitigate this ambiguity, we 472 implemented a shared-control framework , RT-V2 20 , as a post-processor to the neural decoder 473 (Figure 6). The controller infers trajectory -level intent from decoded command, recent movement, 474 

and environmental context, and adjusts the executed velocity to promote stable, goal -directed, 475 and collision-free navigation. The system consisted of an offline -trained probabilistic intent 476 predictor and an online Bayesian inference mechanism operating at 50ms updates during closed-477 loop control. 478 

The intent predictor modeled a prior distribution 𝑝𝑝 (𝜉𝜉 |𝜁𝜁 , 𝑐𝑐 ), which represents candidate 479 future velocity trajectories 𝜉𝜉 conditioned on the environmental context 𝑐𝑐 and recent decoded 480 velocity history 𝜁𝜁 (last eight 50ms steps, 400ms total). This prior was parameterized as a 481 

trajectory -Gaussian Mixture M odel. The intent predictor was pre-trained using a large dataset of 482 

simulated trajectories, in which a virtual agent navigated towards targets while avoiding obstacles 483 

in randomized environments matching the task structure. Subsequently the predictor was fine -484 

tuned on BCI navigation trajectories to capture task -specific and BCI -related control dynamics. 485 

The model learned to predict plausible future trajectories given environmental context and past 486 motion history. Neural activity was not incorporated during prior training; only decoded 487 

kinematics and task context were used . The prior was conditioned on the set of five candidate 488 targets but did not encode which target was cued. At trial onset, the prior supported trajectories 489 toward all candidate targets; as motion history accumulated, the distribution progressively 490 

concentrated on trajectories consistent with recent velocity patterns and environmental 491 feasibility. Importantly, the instantaneous decoded velocity 𝑣𝑣 was not incorporated in computing 492 the prior. 493
