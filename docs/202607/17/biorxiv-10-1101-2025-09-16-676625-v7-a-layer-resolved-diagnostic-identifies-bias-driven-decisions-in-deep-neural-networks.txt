# Abstract 24 

25 

Modern AI systems can be accurate and confident, but this alone does not reveal whether a 26 

decision is well supported by the input. This creates a trust problem because confidence reports 27 

how decisive a model is, but not what supports that decisiveness. Here we show that neural -28 

network confidence can be decomposed into input -dependent feature support and input -29 

independent offset support. We formalize this decomposition through the Bias Dominance Index 30 

(BDI), a layer -resolved measure quantifying the relative contribution of input -independent 31 

offsets to the decision margin, revealing whether confidence is primarily feature -supported or 32 

bias -driven. Across convolutional neural networks, a vision transformer and a transformer 33 

language model, BDI shows that high confidence can coexist with bias -driven decisions. Layer -34 

resolved analyses map where bias -driven support across network depth. Perturbation analyses 35 

further show that the bias component can stabilize performance when readout weights are 36 

degraded. Finally, we operationalize decision composition into an acceptance rule that combines 37 

confidence and BDI for mechanism -aware auditing and triage. Together, these results position 38 

BDI as a general diagnostic of decision composition that distinguishes feature -supported from 39 

bias -driven decisions across model families. 40

# Introduction 44 

Deep neural networks (DNNs) increasingly operate as decision -making systems in settings 45 

where inputs are incomplete, ambiguous, or degraded – images are occluded 1, sensor streams are 46 

noisy 2,3 , and language inputs are underspecified 4,5 or adversarially framed 3,6 . In these situations, 47 

models often remain decisive, assigning high confidence to predictions even when the input 48 

provides limited support 7. This creates a practical trust problem. 49 

50 

For deployment, the central question is not only whether a prediction is confident, but what 51 

computational substrate supports that confidence. Confidence is expressed through the decision 52 

margin and a large decision margin can arise because the current input provides strong evidence, 53 

or because input -independent offsets, such as learned bias terms, normalization shifts, or learned 54 

baselines, contribute substantially regardless of the specific input 8. These offsets are a normal 55 

and useful part of neural -network computation, but they matter for trust because they can help 56 

sustain decisiveness even when input -dependent evidence is weak. The practical challenge is 57 

therefore not simply whether to trust a model globally, but when to trust a particular decision. 58 

This problem of trust requires diagnostics that go beyond performance and confidence to ask 59 

what supports the model’s decisiveness on each input. 60 

61 

A substantial body of work provides complementary tools for probing these issues. Uncertainty 62 

estimation and calibration relate confidence to error 9,10 , and attribution methods map outputs to 63 

influential input features or internal units 11 –14 . These tools are useful, but they address different 64 

aspects of model behavior. Calibration asks whether confidence is statistically warranted, and 65 

attribution asks which features influence the output. Neither directly explains what 66 

computational components support the model’s confidence on a given input. This distinction 67 

matters because a prediction can be both correct and confident while differing in whether its 68 

margin is primarily feature -supported or bias -driven. From a trust perspective, confidence alone 69 

would treat these cases as equivalent, even though their mechanistic basis differs. As a 70 

consequence , confidence can mask distinct forms of decision support. 71 

72 

Importantly, here bias denotes an intercept -like contribution to the logit margin that is 73 

independent of the current input. Therefore, the bias in the current analysis is an input -74

Addressing this issue requires moving beyond the magnitude of the decision margin to its 78 

composition, asking whether the margin is supported primarily by input -dependent features or by 79 

input -independent offsets. We formalize this decomposition through the Bias Dominance Index 80 

(BDI), a measure that quantifies the relative contribution of input independent offsets (i.e. bias) 81 

to the decision margin. For a broad class of neural networks, the decision margin can be 82 

decomposed into feature and bias components, enabling BDI to distinguish decisions in which 83 

confidence is primarily feature -supported from those in which bias disproportionately shape the 84 

output. Thus, BDI is a mechanism -aware diagnostic of margin composition . Crucially, the goal is 85 

not merely to restate that bias terms exist, but to use their relative dominance to identify bias -86 

driven decisions and improve trust .87 

88 

We extend the diagnostic across depth by partitioning each layer’s gradient -weighted local signal 89 

into feature and input -independent components. This yields a depth -dependent map of the 90 

decision margin, providing a profile of bias dominance across layers of a network. Additionally ,91 

we adapt the framework to multi -class models using a label -free decision margin defined by the 92 

top -1 versus top -2 logit difference, because deployment settings often lack labels enabling BDI -93 

based auditing of decisions without ground -truth annotations. 94 

95 

First, w e apply these diagnostics to a set of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on a 96 

controlled perceptual decision -making task. BDI reveals systematic decisions in which feature 97 

contributions are attenuated and the bias component disproportionately supports the margin, 98 

while layer -resolved BDI maps where this mode across network depth. Additionally, we test the 99 

mechanistic relevance of the bias component through perturbations that selectively degrade 100 

feature -carrying computation. We then establish that the BDI signature generalizes to other 101 

convolutional networks (AlexNet 15 and VGG -16 16 ), a vision transformer ( ViT -b/16 17 ), and a102 

transformer language model (BERT 18 ). Similarly in these networks, BDI flags bias -dominant yet 103 

confident decisions, mapping bias dominance in the network hierarchy, and provides a 104 

framework for auditing and stress -testing models under degraded feature and distributional 105

# Results 216 

BDI reveals decision -composition heterogeneity hidden by confidence 217 

To determine how internal computations support the decision margin, we trained five CNNs on a 218 

controlled dot -discrimination task in which each image contained intermixed red and blue dots 219 

and the model reported the dominant color (Fig. 2A). We previously used this same task in 220 

human participants, enabling direct comparison of performance and providing a bridge to human 221 

decision -making processe s19 .222 

223 

Across models, performance was above chance (Accuracy = 0.75 ± 0.0038; t(4) = 65.41, P = 224 

3.27 × 10⁻⁷) and comparable to human accuracy on the same task (Accuracy = 0.77 ± 0.0095; t = 225 

1.84, P = 0.07; Fig. 2B). Accuracy was above chance for both red -dominant (Accuracy = 0.75 ± 226 

0.035; t(4) = 7.91, P = 1.38 × 10⁻³) and blue -dominant trials (Accuracy = 0.72 ± 0.034; t(4) = 227 

5.65, P = 0.004), with no preference for either color (t(4) = 0.70, P = 0.52). 228 

229 

We then quantified how each model’s decision margin was constructed from the feature 230 

component versus the bias component by computing the Bias Dominance Index (BDI) at the last 231 

affine layer. Across all five models, the output -layer BDI ranged from weakly to strongly driven 232 

by bias (BDI IQR = 0.291 ± 0.036 ), revealing marked trial -to -trial heterogeneity in how the 233 

decision margin was supported (Fig. 2 C-G). Moreover, on average, 35.48 ± 8.71% of red -234 

dominant trials and 47.80 ± 14.05% of blue -dominant trials exceeded the bias -dominance 235

Bias support can be stabilizing rather than uniformly harmful 288 

Real -world decision systems must operate under noise and corrupted internal signals. We 289 

therefore asked whether the bias component contributes causally to performance when the 290 

feature component is degraded. For each test trial, we added Gaussian noise,  𝒩 (0, 𝜎 ), either to 291 

the neural activation vector or to the classifier weights mapping the representations to output 292 

logits, and quantified accuracy with and without the bias component ( bred = bblue = 0). Using both 293 

perturbations distinguishes sensitivity to representational noise (activation corruption) from 294 

sensitivity to readout uncertainty (weight corruption) .295 

296 

In a representative model (Model 4), weight noise impaired accuracy more than activation noise 297 

(P FDR < 0.001; Fig. 4 ). To confirm this observation, we tested whether the stabilization from 298 

them bias component depended on perturbation magnitude using a three -way ANOVA with 299 

noise type (activation vs weights) , noise level, and bias condition (with vs without) as factors. 300

BDI provides a label -free axis for auditing pretrained vision models 330 

To assess whether bias dominance extends beyond the controlled perceptual decision -making 331 

task, we evaluated ImageNet -pretrained AlexNet, VGG -16, and ViT -b/16 on Tiny ImageNet and 332 

Tiny ImageNet -C images. These models produce 1000 -way ImageNet logits. We therefore used 333 

this setting to determine whether BDI can be used in a label -free manner. Specifically, we 334 

quantified the decision support using a label -free margin defined as the difference between the 335 

highest and second -highest logits,  Δ𝑧 = 𝑧 (1) − 𝑧 (2), which does not require class labels. 336 

Competitor indices (1), (2) were determined per input by the model’s own ranking. We first 337 

focused on the relationship between BDI and confidence in the output layer because, in this layer 338 

the relationship between the feature component, and the bias component directly influences the 339

Confidence + BDI as a triage policy 469 

To translate decision composition into an operational audit rule , we evaluated a two -parameter, 470 

label -free acceptance policy that retains a prediction only when it is both decisive by standard 471 

uncertainty criteria and not bias -dominant by the decision -composition diagnostic. This 472 

motivates using BDI not only as a descriptive diagnostic, but as a label -free triage axis that 473 

targets decision mechanism rather than confidence alone. 474 

475 

Specifically, we accepted trials satisfying Confidence ≥ τ and BDI all ≤ γ, where τ sets a minimum 476 

confidence threshold and γ limits tolerance for bias -supported margins. In AlexNet, sweeping ( τ, 477 

γ) yields the fraction of predictions accepted ( Fig. 8A ). We used this grid to examine the 478 

mechanism -aware consequences of acceptance, since lowering γ does not simply reject low -479 

confidence predictions, but selectively excludes decisions whose margins are disproportionately 480 

supported by input -independent offsets, including cases that remain high -confidence or match 481 

the dataset label. This distinction is important because label agreement alone does not establish 482 

that a decision is trustworthy at the mechanistic level. A prediction can be correct while still 483 

deriving its decisiveness from weak input -dependent support. Thus, the confidence –BDI policy 484 

treats trust as a joint criterion in which accepted decisions should be both confident and feature 485 

supported. 486

# Discussion 538 

Modern neural networks are often evaluated by whether their predictions are accurate and 539 

confident. Here we show that these quantities do not fully determine whether a particular 540 

decision should be trusted. Addressing this issue requires moving beyond confidence and the 541 

magnitude of the decision margin to examine its composition. This distinction is important for 542 

trust because two decisions can have the same confidence value s, but their margin could be 543 

supported primarily by input -dependent features or by input -in dependent offsets (i.e. bias). To 544 

address this issue, we developed the Bias Dominance Index (BDI), a measure of whether the 545 

margin is supported primarily by input -dependent feature contributions or disproportionately by 546 

input -independent offsets internal to the model. BD I provide s a quantitative lens to see through 547 

the "confidence mask" of DNNs. Across large -scale vision and language models – AlexNet 15 ,548 

VGG -16 16 , ViT -b/16 17 , BERT 18 – BDI revealed that confidence can mask mechanistically 549 

distinct decisions, showing that predictions with similar apparent confidence may differ in 550 

whether their margins are feature -supported or bias -driven. 551 

552 

These findings have practical implications for trusting and auditing modern neural networks. 553 

Crucially, accuracy and confidence alone w ere not a sufficient description of the computational 554 

basis of a prediction. Our results indicate d that two decisions with similar confidence and similar 555 

label agreement can rely on different components . One may be primarily supported by feature -556 

dependent computation s, whereas the other may be disproportionately supported by bias . This is 557 

consistent with prior work showing that the bias terms can make substantial contributions to 558 

prediction accuracy. In certain settings, it has been shown that using only the bias terms can 559 

achieve roughly 30 –40% of full -model performance 20 .560 

561 

The influence of bias is not confined to the readout layer but can be mapped to early processing 562 

stages and persist through intermediate representations, highlighting that bias is a distributed 563 

> .CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license available under a was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint (which this version posted July 16, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.16.676625 doi: bioRxiv preprint

property of computation. In convolutional neural networks, the influence of bias was strongest in 564 

the early layers, indicating that internal priors shape the feature extraction process from its 565 

inception. 566 

567 

In biological perception, systematic biases are often interpreted as adaptive priors – shaped by 568 

natural stimulus statistics and evolutionary pressures –that help stabilize feature inference under 569 

uncertainty 21 . In convolutional neural networks, however, these early offsets may represent 570 

architectural shortcuts that allow the model to bypass complex feature synthesis when a prior is 571 

sufficiently strong 22,23 . Moreover, in the vision transformer architecture, bias was introduced 572 

primarily through layer normalization, pointing to normalization as a key locus where input -573 

independent offsets can be amplified across depth. More broadly, because modern architectures 574 

include multiple normalization and other affine operations, the set of loci where input -575 

independent offsets can enter expands, underscoring the need for diagnostic measures. Overall, if 576 

one aims to reduce the support of the bias component to the decision margin, effective 577 

interventions may need to target early representational dynamics and normalization 578 

mechanisms —not only the output -layer decision rule. 579 

580 

While bias in machine learning is often framed as an issue to be corrected 24 –26 , our perturbation 581 

analys is reveal s a more nuanced role. Under conditions of high noise or weight degradation, bias 582 

acted as support, preserving classification accuracy when evidentiary weights were 583 

compromised. This suggests that the bias in neural architectures may function as a learned 584 

internal prior, providing a baseline of robustness in volatile environments. The challenge for 585 

future research is to distinguish between beneficial priors that aid generalization and harmful 586 

biases that drive erroneous overconfidence. 587 

588 

Most deployment pipelines treat softmax confidence as a proxy for reliability and implement a 589 

one -dimensional acceptance rule, for example , accepting predictions only if confidence exceeds 590 

a threshold 27 . Our results suggest that confidence alone can conflate qualitatively different 591 

factors underlying decisions. Predictions can be equally confident yet differ in whether the 592 

decision margin is supported by input -dependent features or by input -independent bias. Instead, a 593 

two -parameter policy that jointly thresholds confidence and BDI could allow practitioners to 594

trade coverage against tolerance for bias -dominant margins. Because both quantities are 595 

computed from internal decision variables, such a policy is label -free and could be deployed 596 

when ground truth labels are unavailable or delayed. More broadly, this perspective reframes 597 

“reliability” as not only a question of margin magnitude (confidence) but also of margin 598 

composition (feature vs bias), providing an interpretable handle for auditing and triaging 599 

predictions that would otherwise pass confidence screening. 600

BDI can also be estimated with label -free inputs (top -1 vs. top -2) expanding the practical utility 602 

of this framework. BDI could be applied in high -stakes deployments (e.g., medical diagnostics 28 )603 

where ground truth is unavailable or delayed. In such settings, BDI could serve as a monitor that 604 

flags high ly confiden t decisions whose margins are primarily bias -supported. This may provide a 605 

complementary audit signal for identifying predictions that warrant additional review. In thi s606

Beyond machine learning, these findings may also inform computational neuroscience models of 610 

the visual cortex and decision -making. Our observation that bias -dominant margin support can 611 

emerge early and persist across depth may be a way to formalize how priors influence perceptual 612 

decisions when stimulus -linked support is weak. This framing may be relevant because CNNs 613 

have been widely used as mechanistic models for multiple aspects of human vision, including 614 

object recognition 29,30 , representational structure 31  and processing in the visual cortex 32 –34 .615 

616 

Moreover, i n prior work, humans performing the same dot -discrimination task used in the 617 

current analysis exhibited multiple brain activation patterns, including engagement of the Default 618

internally generated dynamics dominate over externally driven dynamics 35 . In this light, our 620 

results raise the possibility that these pattern shifts reflect changes in decision composition. 621 

Accordingly, we speculate that Default Mode Network engagement during decision -making may 622 

be preferentially associated with trials in which sensory features provide relatively little margin 623 

support and internal priors provide comparatively greater support, paralleling bias -driven 624 

decision margins in DNNs. While we are not able to test this relationship here, future work could 625

Several limitations of the present work should be noted. One limitation of the current BDI 629 

formulation is that it captures only the input -independent component, and not all forms of 630 

undesirable reliance in a decision. In particular, BDI does not by itself identify other spurious but 631 

input -dependent biases, including bias that may be encoded in the learned weights or feature 632 

representations rather than in explicit additive offsets. Future extensions of the BDI framework 633 

could therefore broaden the analysis from input -independent support to more general forms of 634 

non -evidence -grounded support. In addition, the current study focused primarily on ImageNet -635 

trained vision models, and future work should assess BDI on a wider range of state -of -the -art 636 

baselines, particularly for large language models, to provide a more detailed evaluation of 637 

specific model classes. Finally, the analysis was centered mainly on common corruptions and 638 

noise. Therefore, future studies should test BDI across a broader range of domains and under 639 

adversarial perturbations to determine whether bias dominance is systematically linked to model 640 

robustness. 641 

642 

By shifting the focus from input features to decision composition, we provide a foundation for 643 

auditing neural networks. Importantly, this framework can be applied to any model whose 644 

decision variables can be expressed as affine functions of internal representations . Moreover, 645 

future work tracking the developmental trajectory of BDI over training could pinpoint when a 646 

model transitions from learning input -driven structure to exploiting bias. Taken together, rather 647 

than merely rationalizing outputs post hoc, the BDI framework exposes the balance between 648 

input -linked support and model -intrinsic offsets, moving us toward AI systems whose decisions 649 

are not only accurate, but more readily interpretable. 650

The gradient -based procedure used to estimate layer -resolved BDI may be sensitive to local 745 

saturation 36 and therefore may understate support when derivatives are small 12 . BDI decomposes 746 

the current decision margin into input -dependent and input -independent support terms. By 747 

contrast, Integrated Gradients (IG) attributes the change in output from a baseline input to the 748 

current input along a path and therefore measures a different quantity. IG is not a direct 749 

replacement for BDI because the two methods attribute different quantities. Because IG is 750 

defined on baseline -relative differences, explicit input -independent offsets cancel from the 751 

attributed output difference and are therefore not directly recoverable as a separate bias -support 752 

term. Thus, an IG -based extension would not be a direct replacement for BDI, but rather a 753 

distinct path -integrated variant that may provide complementary information. Nevertheless, 754 

future analyses could evaluate if IG -weighted BDI provides additional information to aid in 755 

diagnosis and triaging. 756

Dot -discrimination task 769 

The convolutional neural network (CNN) was trained to perform a perceptual decision -making 770 

task, identifying whether red or blue dots were more frequent in a cloud of randomly distributed 771 

dots. Specifically, the CNN was trained to perform a modified version of a perceptual decision -772 

making task we have previously used in humans to understand the neural correlates of decision -773 

making 19 . Each stimulus consisted of either 90 red - or 100 blue -colored dots, presented within an 774 

imaginary circular aperture. Dot positions were randomized for every trial to minimize low -level 775 

visual regularities. For testing, 1,000 novel stimuli were created with equal number s of red - and 776 

blue -dominant trials. All images were generated using Python. 777 

778 

Dot -discrimination task CNN model, training and testing 779 

We used a CNN based on the AlexNet 15 architecture, modified for binary classification. 780 

Convolutional -layer weights were fixed to ImageNet -1K –pretrained parameters provided by 781 

PyTorch ( https://pytorch.org/ ), and only the fully connected layers were trained. This design 782 

keeps the feature -extraction stage stable while allowing the decision stage to adapt to the 783 

red/blue task. Using pretrained weights learned from diverse natural images to isolate decision -784 

level effects from feature learning. 785 

786 

The CNN was trained on randomly generated trials of the dot -discrimination task. Training was 787 

conducted in five batches of randomly chosen trials. Model optimization was performed using 788 

stochastic gradient descent as implemented in PyTorch. During training and testing, images were 789 

balanced for red and blue dominance. After training, the CNN was evaluated on 1,000 unique 790 

trials not included in the training set. Accuracy was assessed separately for red -dominant and 791 

blue -dominant images, as well as across all trials. In total five different CNN models were 792 

trained and tested. Training and testing were conducted in Google Colab on an NVIDIA A100 793 

GPU. 794
