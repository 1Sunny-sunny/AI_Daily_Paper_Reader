Abstract Single-photon miniscopes enable large-scale calcium imaging in freely behaving 14 

animals but are limited by out-of-focus background fluorescence that degrades image contrast 15 

and single-cell signal fidelity. Multiphoton approaches address this limitation but remain costly 16 

and complex. Here we introduce a lightweight (<3 g), low-cost structured illumination miniscope 17 

that achieves optical sectioning in freely behaving mice. Using HiLo imaging implemented with a 18 

simple Ronchi grating and time-multiplexed excitation, the system strongly suppresses 19 

background fluorescence while preserving the speed, field of view, and accessibility of widefield 20 

miniscopes, and supports optical-sectioned multi-plane imaging to increase neuronal yield. Using 21 

hippocampal recordings, we show enhanced region-of-interest (ROI)-based signal quality and 22 

spatial information readout, allowing simple ROI-averaged signals to approach the performance 23 

of offline algorithm-extracted signals. We further demonstrate a proof-of-principle closed-loop 24 

brain–machine interface enabled by rapid online signal extraction and real-time neural decoding. 25 

Together, these results establish structured illumination as a practical and accessible strategy for 26 

achieving high-contrast calcium imaging with miniaturized microscopes. 27 28 

## Introduction 29 

Miniaturized microscopes (miniscopes) are now widely used for large-scale calcium imaging in 30 

freely behaving animals, enabling population-level recordings that are difficult to obtain with con-31 

ventional optical systems ( Flusberg et al., 2008 ; Ghosh et al., 2011 ; Cai et al., 2016 ; Barbera et al., 32 

2016 ; Zong et al., 2017 ; Skocek et al., 2018 ; Zong et al., 2021 , 2022 ; Madruga et al., 2025 ; Madan-33 

gopal et al., 2025 ; Qian et al., 2026 ). Single-photon widefield (WF) miniscopes are especially popu-34 

lar because they are lightweight, inexpensive, and easy to deploy across a range of experimental 35 

settings ( Ziv and Ghosh, 2015 ). A key limitation of these systems, however, is the lack of intrin-36 

sic optical sectioning, which leads to out-of-focus background fluorescence that degrades image 37 

contrast and limits the fidelity of single-neuron signals. Computational approaches can partially 38 

mitigate these effects but operate post hoc, remain constrained by the quality of the acquired im-39 

ages, and can be computationally demanding, limiting their use in experiments requiring rapid 40

2011 ; Ford et al., 2012 ) is particularly well suited for miniaturized implementations, as it requires 54 

only two images per frame—one acquired under uniform illumination and one under structured 55 

illumination—enabling efficient reconstruction with modest hardware modification and limited 56 

computational overhead. 57 

Here we introduce a lightweight (<3 g), low-cost miniscope that implements HiLo structured 58 

illumination for calcium imaging in freely behaving mice. Using a simple Ronchi grating and dual-59 

LED excitation, the system reduces background fluorescence at acquisition while preserving the 60 

field of view, frame rate, and usability of conventional widefield miniscopes, and supports multi-61 

plane imaging to increase neuronal yield. Using in vivo hippocampal recordings, we show that HiLo 62 

substantially improves ROI-based signal quality and spatial information readout, allowing simple 63 

ROI-averaged signals to approach the performance of offline algorithm-extracted signals. Finally, 64 

we demonstrate a proof-of-principle closed-loop brain–machine interface, highlighting the poten-65 

tial of HiLo-based miniscopes for rapid online signal extraction, real-time neural decoding, and 66 

feedback experiments. 67 

## Results 68 

Implementation of a HiLo miniscope for optical sectioning 69 

To address the challenge of background fluorescence in widefield calcium imaging, we developed 70 

a miniaturized microscope that integrated optical sectioning via structured illumination ( Figure 1 A71 

and B). This approach used patterned illumination to selectively modulate in-focus signals while 72 

suppressing out-of-focus background fluorescence. Because out-of-focus fluorescence is only weakly 73 

modulated by the illumination pattern, HiLo reconstruction can preferentially retain the in-focus 74 

signal and reject diffuse background, thereby generating an optically sectioned image rather than 75 

simply enhancing contrast. The device was based on the principles of HiLo microscopy and em-76 

ployed dual illumination to acquire two complementary images using a CMOS camera: a uniformly 77 

illuminated widefield image (15 frames/sec) and a structured illumination image (15 frames/sec) 78 

generated by a Ronchi grating ( Figure 1 C and D). These paired images were combined to recon-79 

struct a final optically sectioned image (15 frames/sec) that preserved both high- and low-spatial-80 

frequency components of the in-focus signal, resulting in substantially enhanced contrast. 81 

System performance was first evaluated using a standardized optical resolution target (#55– 82 

622, Edmund Optics), which demonstrated a lateral resolution of 256 line pairs per mm (Group 8, 83 

Element 1; Figure 1 E, Figure 1 —figure Supplement 1 A and B). We further quantified the optical-84 

sectioning capability of the system using a thin fluorescent plane and obtained an axial full width 85 

at half maximum of 23 𝜇 m ( Figure 1 —figure Supplement 1 C). To assess performance in biologi-86 

cal tissue, we imaged brain slices from a Thy1-GFP mouse and observed a marked reduction in 87 

background fluorescence in HiLo-reconstructed images compared with time-multiplexed, frame-88 

matched widefield images ( Figure 1 F). These results demonstrated that the HiLo miniscope effec-89

Performance evaluation of the HiLo miniscope 93 

We next systematically evaluated the performance of the HiLo miniscope using simulated data and 94 

in vivo calcium imaging in freely moving mice. 95 

To generate simulated data, each image frame was constructed by combining three distinct 96 

components: an in-focus neural activity signal, a spatially localized out-of-focus background signal, 97 

and a global background signal ( Figure 2 A). The final widefield image was synthesized as a weighted 98 

sum of these three components. The spatial profiles of the neural activity and local background 99 

were modeled as Gaussian signals with different spatial scales to reflect their distinct contributions. 100 

The temporal dynamics of neural activity and local background across frames were derived from 101 

real hippocampal imaging data acquired with a widefield miniscope. This simulation allowed us to 102 

directly compare the recovered fluorescence signals with known ground-truth neural activity while 103 

independently modeling local and global background contamination. 104 

We then processed the simulated data using the same reconstruction algorithm employed by 105 

the HiLo miniscope to generate the final HiLo images ( Figure 2 B). Comparison of the simulated 106 

widefield and HiLo images revealed a substantial reduction in both local and global background flu-107 

orescence in the HiLo reconstructions, while the true neural activity signals were largely preserved 108 

(Figure 2 C). Direct comparison of correlations between the widefield and HiLo signals relative to 109 

the ground-truth activity showed that the HiLo method recovered neural signals with markedly 110 

higher fidelity than conventional widefield imaging ( Figure 2 D). These simulations therefore con-111 

firmed that HiLo reconstruction can suppress background contamination while preserving in-focus 112 

neural activity. 113

HiLo miniscope was mounted on the animals’ heads to record activity from the hippocampal CA1 re-115 

gion through a gradient-index (GRIN) lens. Uniformly illuminated widefield images and structured-116 

illumination images were acquired in an alternating, time-multiplexed manner to enable direct 117

Optical-sectioned multi-plane imaging increases neuronal yield 138 

To test whether the HiLo miniscope could support multi-plane or volumetric imaging, we combined 139 

acquisition-level optical sectioning by HiLo background suppression with voltage-controlled focal-140 

depth modulation through the electrowetting liquid lens already integrated into the miniscope 141 

design ( Figure 1 A). We first calibrated the relationship between applied voltage and axial focal 142 

position, confirming focal-depth modulation across an axial range of up to 450 𝜇 m ( Figure 3 —figure 143 

Supplement 1 A). We then validated this capability in Thy1-GFP mouse brain slices, where sequential 144 

imaging across focal depths produced high-contrast optical-sectioned image stacks spanning up 145 

to 132 𝜇 m ( Figure 3 A, Figure 3 —figure Supplement 1 B). These results showed that the liquid-lens-146 

based HiLo miniscope could perform optical-sectioned multi-depth imaging, thereby establishing 147 

the technical basis for multi-plane recordings in vivo .148 

For in vivo hippocampal recordings, we employed a two-plane configuration with the focal 149 

planes separated by 33 𝜇 m and alternated between planes at an imaging rate of 2.5 Hz ( Figure 3 B). 150 

Representative calcium traces extracted from neurons detected exclusively in each plane, as well 151 

as from neurons detected in both planes, exhibited clear and well-resolved activity patterns ( Fig-152 

ure 3 C), demonstrating reliable signal extraction across imaging depths under optical-sectioned 153 

multi-plane imaging conditions. 154 

Given the high density of pyramidal neurons in dorsal CA1, a subset of neurons was detected in 155 

both imaging planes. Quantification of this overlap showed that overlapping neurons constituted 156 

a minority of the total population imaged across planes ( Figure 3 D), indicating that each imaging 157 

plane contributed a largely distinct set of neurons. Signal quality differed modestly across depth. 158 

Neurons detected in the shallower plane exhibited higher mean SNR than those detected in the 159 

deeper plane, although robust calcium signals were obtained from both planes (deep vs. shallow: 160 

𝑝 = 0 .013 ; paired 𝑡 -test; Figure 3 E). Combining neurons from both planes substantially increased the 161 

total number of neurons recorded relative to either plane alone, yielding a larger overall population 162 

for downstream analysis (deep vs. shallow: 𝑝 = 0 .049 ; shallow vs. total: 𝑝 = 0 .001 ; deep vs. total: 163 

𝑝 = 0 .0004 ; paired 𝑡 -tests; Figure 3 F). 164 

Together, these results demonstrate that the HiLo miniscope not only improves image contrast 165 

and ROI signal quality through optical background suppression, but also leverages its integrated 166 

electrowetting liquid lens to perform optical-sectioned multi-plane imaging. This combination in-167

## Discussion 256 

The miniature structured illumination microscope described here addresses a central limitation 257 

of widely used single-photon miniscopes: the absence of optical sectioning at acquisition. By inte-258 

grating HiLo structured illumination into a lightweight, head-mounted design, the system improves 259 

image contrast and single-cell signal fidelity while preserving the simplicity, affordability, and ease 260 

of use that have driven the broad adoption of widefield miniscope imaging. Rather than replac-261 

ing existing approaches, it provides a complementary solution that bridges conventional widefield 262 

miniscopes and more complex multiphoton systems, enabling higher-quality population record-263 

ings in freely behaving animals. 264 

A key advantage of this design is that background fluorescence is suppressed during image ac-265 

quisition rather than corrected post hoc. While computational approaches can partially mitigate 266 

background contamination after acquisition ( Mukamel et al., 2009 ; Pnevmatikakis et al., 2016 ; Pa-267 

chitariu et al., 2016 ; Zhou et al., 2018 ; Lu et al., 2018 ; Bao and Gong, 2024 ), HiLo provides optical 268 

sectioning directly at the source, resulting in cleaner images before downstream processing. Our 269 

results show that this acquisition-level improvement is especially important for simple ROI-based 270 

signal extraction. HiLo substantially improved ROI-averaged calcium signals and allowed them to 271 

approach the quality of signals obtained with offline source-extraction algorithms. These findings 272 

suggest that optical sectioning can recover a substantial fraction of the information typically ob-273 

tained through computational processing while preserving a simple and efficient signal-extraction 274 

workflow. This acquisition-level improvement may be especially valuable in settings where rapid 275 

access to neural activity is desirable, including real-time neural decoding and closed-loop experi-276 

mental paradigms. 277 

These gains are achieved with minimal added hardware complexity. Using a simple Ronchi 278 

grating and time-multiplexed illumination, HiLo reconstruction requires only two images per frame 279 

and modest computational processing, facilitating miniaturization and compatibility with existing 280

Data simulation 428 

Simulation data (uniformly illuminated widefield images and structured illumination images) for 429 

the HiLo miniscope were generated using a method analogous to that employed in a prior study 430 

(Zhou et al., 2018 ). Both uniformly illuminated and structured illumination images comprise three 431 

components: in-focus neural activity (true signal), blurred out-of-focus neural activity (local back-432 

ground), and global background signal. In our simulations, in-focus and out-of-focus neurons were 433 

simplified as normalized Gaussian distributions with distinct dimensions and random spatial po-434 

sitions. The temporal dynamics of each neuron were introduced by multiplying the pixel values 435 

within the corresponding neuronal region by a real hippocampal neural activity dataset acquired 436 

via a widefield miniscope. A single frame from the identical dataset was directly adopted as the 437 

global background. The final widefield images were synthesized as a weighted sum of these three 438 

components, whereas the in-focus neurons for structured illumination images were multiplied by 439 

a one-dimensional periodic grid signal prior to summation with the other two components. Sub-440 

sequent to this, the final HiLo images were reconstructed from the simulated widefield and struc-441 

tured illumination images using the identical algorithm implemented in the HiLo miniscope. 442 

Neural activity extraction 443

neuronal signals were extracted using the CNMF-E (constrained non-negative matrix factorization 446 

for microendoscopic data) algorithm ( Zhou et al., 2018 ). Because widefield and HiLo imaging dif-447 

fer in signal intensity and signal-to-noise characteristics, separate CNMF-E parameter sets were 448 

used (widefield: min_corr = 0.90, min_pnr = 8; HiLo: min_corr = 0.85, min_pnr = 3). For multi-plane 449 

imaging experiments, neuronal signals were extracted independently in each imaging plane using 450 

Suite2p ( Pachitariu et al., 2017 ). Putative neurons were visually inspected based on the quality of 451 

their spatial footprints and calcium dynamics, and cells exhibiting unusually long decay kinetics or 452 

minimal activity were excluded from further analysis. 453

than 15 𝜇 m were considered candidate matches. Functional similarity was assessed by computing 472 

the Pearson correlation coefficient between calcium activity traces, with correlations greater than 473 

0.5 indicating functional correspondence. ROIs were classified as representing the same cell only 474 

if they satisfied both spatial and functional criteria. 475 

For ROI-based signal analyses, we used CNMF-E exclusively for ROI identification to ensure 476 

a standardized comparison across imaging conditions. However, because HiLo substantially im-477 

proves image contrast (Figure 2G), ROI identification can potentially be achieved using simpler 478 

segmentation approaches, including Suite2p, Cellpose, or other image-based methods. 479 

SNR of calcium signals 480 

To quantify the quality of extracted calcium signals, SNR was computed from the raw fluorescence 481 

traces. For CNMF-E-extracted signals, the raw temporal component ( 𝐶 raw ) was used rather than 482 

the denoised trace ( 𝐶 ). This choice was made because denoising procedures explicitly suppress 483 

noise fluctuations and alter the statistical properties of the signal, which can artificially inflate SNR 484 

estimates. Using 𝐶 raw preserves the residual measurement noise present after signal extraction 485 

and therefore provides a more direct assessment of signal quality. The SNR of calcium activity 486 

traces was computed using a peak-based approach designed to separate transient calcium events 487 

from baseline noise. For each fluorescence signal, candidate calcium transients were first detected 488 

using a peak-finding algorithm ( findpeaks , MATLAB) with a minimum peak prominence set to three 489 

times the standard deviation of the entire signal. Detected peaks with durations shorter than 0.2 s 490 

were excluded to remove brief fluctuations unlikely to correspond to calcium events. 491 

To estimate baseline noise, time windows surrounding each detected peak were removed from 492 

the signal. Specifically, for each peak, a window extending from 2 s before to 5 s after the peak time 493 

was excluded. For peaks occurring near the beginning or end of the recording, the exclusion win-494 

dow was truncated to remain within signal bounds. All excluded segments were removed, yielding 495 

a baseline signal composed of non-event activity. To further reduce contamination from residual 496 

calcium activity, baseline samples above the 80th percentile of the remaining signal distribution 497 

were discarded. The remaining values were treated as noise, and their standard deviation was 498 

used as the noise estimate. 499 

The signal amplitude was defined as the mean of peak values exceeding the 90 th percentile 500 

of the detected peak distribution. The SNR was computed as the ratio between this mean peak 501 

amplitude and the standard deviation of the baseline noise. If no valid peaks were detected in a 502 

trace, the SNR was defined as zero. 503

Open-field task 519 

Recordings were conducted after animals had fully recovered from surgery. Prior to data collec-520 

tion, mice were habituated to the open-field arena for 2–3 days, during which they freely explored 521

Animal position tracking 530 

Animal position was extracted from video recordings of open-field behavior using a deep learning– 531 

based pose estimation pipeline. A total of 300 video frames (resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels) were 532 

randomly sampled from recorded sessions and manually annotated using LabelMe ( https://github. 533 

com/wkentaro/labelme ). Three anatomical landmarks were labeled in each frame: the left ear, right 534 

ear, and body center. Annotated images were used to train an Ultralytics YOLO pose estimation 535 

model ( https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics ) using 10-fold cross-validation. Model training was 536 

performed for 200 epochs with a batch size of 16. Video reading and writing were accelerated us-537

video processing optimized for deep-learning workflows. To further improve tracking robustness, 539 

frames that were difficult to predict—such as those containing curled body postures or partial 540 

occlusions (e.g., cables crossing the animal)—were manually selected and added to the training 541 

set for model fine-tuning. The model was subsequently fine-tuned for an additional 50 epochs 542 

with a batch size of 16. Predicted keypoint trajectories were temporally smoothed using a one-543 

dimensional moving-average filter with a kernel size of 3. 544
