Abstract: Modern cochlear implants (CIs) primarily use pulsatile stimulation, which provides 14 

robust speech recognition in quiet but poorly represents temporal fine structure (TFS). Analog 15 

stimulation preserves the continuous acoustic waveform and may better encode temporal 16 

information, yet it has not been systematically evaluated in combination with modern current -17 

focusing strategies. We compared neural responses evoked by 100 pulses/s biphasic pulse trains 18 

and 100 cycles/s sinusoidal analog stimulation delivered through monopolar, bipolar, and tripolar 19 

electrode configurations in urethane -anesthetized guinea pigs. Following intracochlear 20 

implantation, multiunit activity was recorded from the tonotopic axis of the central nucleus of the 21 

inferior colliculus using 16 -channel silicon probes. Detection thresholds, spread of excitation, 22 

vector strength, sustained response percentage, and temporal response properties were quantified. 23 

Analog stimulation consistently evoked significantly lower activation thresholds than pulsatile 24 

stimulation while maintaining comparable spatial selectivity across stimulation modes; bipolar 25 

analog stimulation additionally produced a narrower spread of excitation. In contrast, analog 26 

stimulation generated lower vector strength, larger sustained response components, and a 27 

pronounced level -dependent polarity effect. At low stimulus levels, responses were dominated by 28 

the cathodic phase of the sinusoidal waveform, whereas increasing stimulus level recruited both 29 

waveform phases, producing synchronization at twice the stimulus frequency. These findings 30 

demonstrate that stimulation waveform strongly influences temporal coding while having 31 

relatively little effect on the spatial distribution of neural activation. Combining analog stimulation 32 

with current focusing reduced activation thresholds without sacrificing spatial selectivity and 33 

produced temporal response properties that differed fundamentally from those evoked by 34 

conventional pulse trains. These results provide a physiological basis for reexamining analog 35 

stimulation as an alternative strategy for cochlear implant sound coding. 36

Introduction 38 

Cochlear implants (CIs) are widely regarded as one of the most successful neural prostheses. 39 

Average CI user s achieve 70 –80% accuracy in open -set sentence recognition in quiet environments 40 

(1) , but challenges persist regarding understanding speech in nois e, pitch perception (2,3) , and 41 

music appreciation (4,5) . Hearing restoration through CIs relies on two core aspects : how the 42 

acoustic signal is translated into electrical patterns (coding strategy) , and how those patterns are 43 

spatially distributed within the cochlea through the electrode array (electrode configuration) .44 

CIs process sound frequency through a series of bandpass filters that selectively deliver stimulation 45 

to the multiple, tonotopically arranged electrodes. The most common type of sound coding strategy 46 

used in modern CI s employs p ulsatile stimulation and the Continuous Interleaved Sampling (CIS )47 

(6) strategy that temporally interleav es stimulation pulses across electrodes so that only a single 48 

electrode is active at a time , prevent ing electrical field summation and likely redu cing channel 49 

interaction . This pulsatile strategy has proven highly effective for speech perception in quiet ;50 

however, it provides only coarse (if any) representation s of intricate sound features such as 51 

temporal fine structure (T FS), thereby limiting performance in noisy environments or complex52 

tasks . In normal acoustic hearing, TFS cues are preserved for frequencies up to approximately 53 

1.5kHz, and are represented by the synchronized spiking of auditory nerve fibers, either with pure 54 

tones or amplitude -modulated tones (7) . The only temporal information provided by m ost CIS -55 

based strategies is slow changes in envelope amplitude and ignore s TFS because they use a fixed 56 

carrier rate (of at least 500 p ulses per second (pps) ). Over the last decade, alternative sound coding 57 

strate gies have been developed to represent some aspects of TFS, but their efficacy for complex 58 

stimul us perception has not been demonstrated (8,9) and the lack of TFS in other available sound 59 

coding strategies remains a fundamental limitation (10 –12) .60

In contrast, an alternative sound coding strategy based on Analog Stimulation (AS) , also called 61 

Simultaneous Analog Stimulation or SAS (13,14) , more faithfully represent s the acoustic 62 

waveform by delivering a continuous electrical signal (15) . In this strategy the envelope and all 63 

aspects of the T FS are encoded directly in the stimulus waveform , preserving the instantaneous 64 

amplitude variations of the filtered signal by translating them directly into a time -varying electrical 65 

waveform . This theoretically enables the auditory nerve to respond to the stimulus in a manner 66 

analogous to normal hearing. 67 

For a time, a subset of CI systems offered both CIS and SAS to recipients, allowing for direct 68 

clinical comparisons between the two strategies. A p lethora of early psychoacoustic studies found69 

that SAS, alone or combined with CIS, yielded overall compar ed speech perception performanc e70 

similar to CIS alone (as summarized in Stupak et al. 201 8 (16) ) and a meaningful subset of users71 

performed better with, and preferred , the analog stimulation strategy. For example, Koch et al.72 

reported that one third of patients preferred to use the SAS strategy after three months of implant 73 

experience ; although mean sentence scores were similar between groups, word recognition scores 74 

were significantly higher for SAS users than for CIS users (17) . Similarly, another study found75 

that half of subjects selected SAS as their preferred strategy, with the use of SAS result ing in higher 76 

overall patient performance (18) .77 

A promising but largely unexplored synergy would be the combination of analog stimulation with 78 

modern current focusing techniques such as bipolar and tripolar mode (19,20) . These techniques 79 

us e adjacent intracochlear electrodes in the linear array of the cochlear implant to spatially restrict 80 

the electrical field and likely reduce channel interactions. If the primary historic shortcoming of 81 

analog stimulation was the uncontrolled summation of simultaneous electrical fields across 82 

adjacent channels, then appropriately calibrated bipolar or tripolar configurations offer a principled 83

Inferior Colliculus Multi -Unit Activity (MUA) recordings and analysis. 178 

MUA were recorded in a similar fashion as in McInturff et al. 2023 , 2022 (30,31) . Briefly, r aw 179 

voltage signals from the IC recording probe s were sampled at 25kHz. An a verage of activity across 180 

all 16 recording channels was used as a global reference and subtracted from each single channel 181 

waveform to remove large artifacts present across channels ( e.g. heartbeat, breathing ). Waveforms 182 

were then stimulus -aligned. For pulsatile stimulation, s timulus artifacts were also minimized by 183 

zeroing the first 0.8ms during and following each pulse. For each channel, the average of the 184 

aligned waveform was then used as a local reference and subtracted from each individual 185 

waveform to remove local field potential ( LFP ) components still present in the signal. The 186 

resulting waveforms were then digitally filtered (5th order, Butterworth bandpass filter, 300Hz to 187 

3kHz). 188

channel as 4 x the standard deviation of voltage traces during the stimulus -off period (0.5s). Post -192

Data analysis 196 

D-prime : Offline analyses started with the characterization of thresholds and saturation levels, and 197 

“stimulation tuning curves” (STCs). We then conducted cumulative d-prime analysis of the PSTHs 198 

(d’) as previously described in Arenberg, Bierer and Middlebrooks 2010 (35) . Briefly, for each 199 

stimulus -level combination , a receiver -operating characteristic (ROC) curve was formed from the 200 

trial -by -trial PSTHs. For each electrode , the averaged PSTH at a given level was considered the 201 

signal condition while the background condition consisted of the PSTH for the level below . The 202 

area under the ROC curve (AUC) yielded the proportion of trials in which the current level was 203 

discriminable from the previous level . That proportion was z-score d and multiplied by √2 to give 204 

the discrimination index ( d′). Cumulative d’ -prime was finally defined by the su mmation of the 205 

current d’ and d’ -1 level. A log -linear correction was applied to avoid any extreme Area Under the 206 

Curve value that could bias the analysis (36) . Detection threshold was taken as the interpolated 207 

stimulus level at which cumulative d′ = 2 for each recording site .208 

Spread of Excitation : The spread of activation along the tonotopic axis of the IC was represented 209 

by stimulation tuning curves ( STCs ), w ith a response contour of d′ = 2 as a function of the depth 210 

along the recording probe (vertical axis) and stimulus current level (horizontal axis) . This iso -rate 211 

curve was then used to calculate t he Spread of Excitation (SoE) as follow s. The recording channel 212 

with the lowest level to achieve d’>2 served as the tip of the STC, then the SoE width was 213 

calculated as the distance (electrode span) between this iso -rate curve boundary when the response 214 

was +3dB above threshold .215

Statistical Analysis. 237 

All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB R202 5b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). 238 

Group d ata are presented as median ± interquartile range (IQR) given non -normal distributions 239 

(tested through Lilliefors tests) . To compare pulse and analog stimulation modes, rank -based 240 

permutation tests were used (10,000 permutations, α = 0.05, two -tailed). For threshold and spread 241 

of excitation measurements (one value per recording configuration), simple rank -based 242 

permutation tests were performed . For vector strength , Euclidian distances and angles (multiple 243 

values per configuration), hierarchical permutation tests were instead used where group 244 

assignments were permuted at the animal level to account for within -animal correlations to avoid 245 

pseudo -replication. This approach is conservative and controls the non -independence of 246 

measurements from the same animal. Note that w hile mixed -effects models are powerful for 247 

hierarchical data, they assume specific distributions (typically normal or log -normal). Preliminary 248 

analyses revealed strong deviations from normality that could not be corrected by standard 249 

transformations. Rank -based permutation tests provide a distribution -free alternative that 250 

maintains appropriate Type I error rates while accounting for the hierarchical structure through 251 

animal -level permutation. That approach was verified as appropriately conservative by comparing 252 

results with standard Mann -Whitney tests for the subset of data where independence could be 253 

assumed. All tests were Benjamini -Hochberg corrected when appropriate. 254

Discussion 351 

The present study evaluated for the first time the coupling of sinusoidal analog stimulation with 352 

current focus ing modes (Bipolar, Tripolar) with the objective of comparing IC responses to analog 353 

with more conventional pulsatile stimulation . Results replicated previous findings related to 354 

electrode configuration, namely that spread of activation narrowed (19,29) , and threshold of 355 

activation increas ed systematically with more focused mode s of stimulation (from monopolar, to 356 

bipolar, to tripolar ) (28,42) . Within mode of stimulation, analog stimulation always elicited 357 

responses with lower thresholds but similar spread of activation, apart from Bipolar , which was 358 

narrower with analog stimulation . Last ly , IC responses to analog stimulation differed from 359 

pulsatile in three ways: 1) lower synchrony, 2) higher sustained/tonic activity, and 3) a level -360 

dependent polarity effect. 361

compared sinusoidally amplitude -modulated biphasic pulse trains stimulation to unmodulated 366 

pulse trains (43) . Note that stimuli used in that study consisted of longer phase duration pulses of 367 

200μs compared to the 40μs used in the present study. Th ese methodological difference s make 368 

results of this previous study difficult to compare with t he present ones .369 

In the present study, thresholds for pulsatile stimulation were, on average, 17dB higher than for 370 

sinusoidal stimulation. Psychoacoustic studies have demonstrated that detection thresholds 371 

decrease systematically with increasing stimulus duration, reflecting the integration of stimulus 372

III.  Polarity -Dependent Responses, Apparent Frequency Doubling, and Implications for 462 

Pitch Coding 463 

A mechanistically informative finding of the present study is the strong polarity dependence of IC 464 

responses to analog stimulation and its interaction with stimulation level . At low stimulation levels, 465 

neural responses were predominantly evoked during the cathodic phase of the waveform, 466 

consistent with classical biophysical models showing that cathodic current preferentially 467 

depolarizes peripheral processes of AN fibers (55,77,78) . At higher stimulation levels, however, 468 

we observed robust spiking during both cathodic and anodic phases, leading to neural 469 

synchronization at twice the nominal stimulus frequency (i.e., an apparent 200 Hz response to a 470 

100 Hz sinusoidal waveform). This polarity dominance has been repeatedly observed in animal 471 

models (79 –81) . Briefly, it is known that SGNs peripheral processes are more sensitive to cathodic 472 

currents whereas their somas are preferentially depolarized by anodic currents. Since peripheral 473 

processes usually sit closer to the CI array, cathodic -first pulses are associated with lower 474 

thresholds. Those results prompted the idea that the difference in thresholds for cathodic and 475 

anodic stimuli, referred to as the polarity effect (PE) could be used to probe the presence or absence 476 

of peripheral processes (82) . Similarly, polarity -dependent recruitment shifts with increasing 477 

levels have been reported in the studies cited above, where higher current amplitudes reduce the 478 

asymmetry between anodic and cathodic excitation by recruiting additional neural elements, 479 

including central axons and nodes of Ranvier. 480 

Hair cells do not show synaptic activation to both phases of a pure tone, with only one direction 481 

of the cilia ry deflection being excitatory. Thus, sound -evoked neural responses only arise from 482 

one of the two phases of pure tone s (83,84) . On the other hand, it is well established that electric 483

IV.  Implications and future directions 507 

The present findings carry important implications for both auditory neuroscience and CI 508 

engineering and development . It is worth noting that the translation of focused stimulation to 509 

functional performance gains in CI users has been inconsistent. For example, bipolar or tripolar 510 

mode with pulsatile stimulation requires higher current to reach threshold and therefore more 511 

battery consumption (91) . A compounding issue is that focused stimulation is not necessarily 512 

beneficial at all electrode locations due to irregular local nerve survival patterns (92,93) . The 513 

observation that analog stimulation coupled with current focusing configurations , achieve d lower 514 

thresholds of neural activation suggests that it could improve stimulation efficiency, potentially 515 

reduce power consumption, channel interaction, and current spread, while preserving the temporal 516 

representation of sound . Current CI systems rely almost exclusively on brief biphasic pulses, 517 

largely for safety and technical simplicity. However, this design inherently sacrifices temporal 518 

energy integration efficiency and the encoding of temporal fine structure. Our results suggest that 519 

alternative stimulation paradigms employing analog waveforms may achieve equivalent neural 520 

activation with significantly lower peak currents, consistent with prior modeling and physiological 521 

studies (61,70) . Furthermore, the invariance of spatial tuning width with tripolar , and possible 522 

sharpening with bipolar and monopolar , indicates that such strategies need not compromise 523 

sp ectral resolution, a critical consideration for speech and music perception in CI users. 524 

Collectively, these findings reveal potential trade -offs between waveform structure, synchrony, 525 

and coding fidelity . Pulsatile stimulation ensures strong, temporally precise neural activation but 526 

promotes hypersynchrony. Analog stimulation reduces hypersynchrony and introduces level -527 

depend ent sustained dynamics that may more closely approximate acoustic activation patterns, yet 528 

it can generate complex nonlinear response transformations at higher levels that are still not fully 529
