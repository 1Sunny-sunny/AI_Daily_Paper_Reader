# Abstract 14 

Many everyday actions, such as handwriting, can be performed fluently across a wide range of scales and speeds. 15 

This flexibility suggests that motor behavior relies on abstract representations that transcend low-level motor or 16 

sensory variables. However, how such abstract representations are formed from individual movement 17 

experiences remains unclear. Here, we test the hypothesis that motor memory is organized not only by physical 18 

state variables, but also by an abstract coordinate of action progress: the normalized progression of an intended 19 

action from initiation to completion. Using a series of motor adaptation experiments that dissociate action 20 

progress from kinematic variables and absolute time, we show that learned outputs are retrieved according to 21 

their position within an action. This progress-based coordinate spans the unfolding course of a planned action, 22 

whether a single reach or an action sequence, and operates continuously rather than as a simple early–late 23 

distinction. Together, these findings identify action progress as an abstract organizing coordinate through which 24 

the motor system may encode movement experiences as abstract motor memories, offering a framework for 25 

understanding the flexible generalization of human motor behavior. 26

# Introduction 34 

The idea that skilled actions depend on abstract motor representations has a long history in motor control 35 

research. Skilled behaviors often preserve a core spatiotemporal structure despite changes in movement scale 36 

or speed, a property known as scale–time invariance (1–3). For example, handwriting exhibits consistent letter 37 

geometry and characteristic velocity profiles across changes in size and speed (4), and gait shows stable relative 38 

timing across walking speeds (5). These observations have motivated classical accounts such as generalized 39 

motor programs and schema theory, which propose that skilled actions preserve invariant structural features 40 

while allowing execution parameters—such as timing, force, or scale—to vary across contexts (5,6). 41 

More recently, motor abstraction has attracted renewed attention, as this classical idea is now being 42 

tested as a functional and neural mechanism for learning and generalization. Behavioral studies have shown that 43 

abstract-level motor training can causally support the early stages of learning novel movement patterns (7). In 44 

parallel, neurophysiological work has identified action symbols—recombinable representations of discrete units 45 

of motor behavior—in the ventral premotor cortex (8). Together, these findings suggest that abstract motor 46 

representations are not merely theoretical constructs for explaining well-learned skills, but functional 47 

components of the motor system that actively support learning and generalization. 48 

However, a key question remains: how such abstract, generalizable representations are formed from 49 

individual movement experiences. Here, we address this representational gap by proposing that motor memory 50 

is not merely tied to the features of individual movements, but also organized by action progress—an abstract 51 

internal coordinate that spans the unfolding course of an intended action, from initiation to completion. 52 

From a control perspective, the framework of dynamical movement primitives proposes that 53 

expressing control commands as a function of a normalized progression variable—from movement onset to 54 

offset—enables faithful reproduction and robust generalization of discrete movements across scales (9). 55 

Consistent with this view, neural population activity has been shown to evolve along similar trajectories from 56 

movement onset to offset, largely independent of movement speed or position (10, 11). Motivated by this 57 

convergence, we reasoned that the relative position along such trajectories—namely, action progress—may 58 

provide a natural coordinate for organizing abstract motor memory. 59 

To test this representational hypothesis, we turned to motor adaptation, where patterns of adaptation 60 

and their generalization provide a window into the structure of the underlying memory (12). A large body of 61 

research—particularly studies using force-field adaptation paradigms—has emphasized that motor adaptation 62 

is highly specific to the individual movement features experienced during learning (13–18). A widely held view 63 

is that adaptation is supported by motor primitives tuned to limb state variables such as position and velocity: 64 

learning acquired in a specific state is expressed most strongly when the system revisits nearby states and decays 65 

as the state diverges. Although this framework provides a clear computational account of local generalization 66 

around trained states (13,19), it remains unclear whether movement state alone is sufficient to explain how 67 

motor memories are organized into abstract forms that support broader generalization across actions. In this 68 

sense, action progress is not intended as an alternative to state-based organization, but as a higher-order 69

coordinate that may operate alongside physical state variables. 70 

We tested this hypothesis by asking whether motor memories are expressed according to action 71 

progress, how this progress coordinate is defined over a planned action, and whether it functions as a continuous 72 

dimension. If action progress provides an abstract coordinate for motor memory, learned outputs should be 73 

retrieved according to their relative location within an action, even when physical state variables or absolute 74 

time differ. Moreover, the coordinate’s relevant range should also be defined not by a single movement element 75 

alone, but by the planned action unit. We first tested progress-dependent generalization within a single discrete 76 

reach, then asked whether the coordinate can span a double-reach sequence separated by a brief pause. Finally, 77 

using a button–reach–button sequence that allowed reach timing to be treated parametrically, we tested whether 78 

motor memories are organized within a continuous progress coordinate and whether its boundaries are defined 79 

by the planned sequence as a whole. 80

# Results 83 

Generalization across reaching distances within a single movement 84 

Experiment 1 examined how motor memory acquired within a single point-to-point reaching movement is 85 

generalized to reaches of other distances. Typical motor adaptation studies utilize a curl force field in which 86 

force is applied in a consistent direction throughout the single reach. In contrast, the present study used an S-87 

shaped force field with the direction reversed at the midpoint of the reach (14, 20), which requires participants 88 

to learn both leftward and rightward compensatory forces within a single reach. This design allowed us to 89 

determine where within the movement each learned force component was retrieved and thereby test whether 90 

motor memory is expressed along an action-progress coordinate within a single movement. 91 

Participants (n = 16) moved a cursor to a visual target using a manipulandum. They were randomly 92 

assigned to one of two groups: Group 1 practiced reaches of 8 cm (Fig. 1a; n = 8), and Group 2 practiced reaches 93 

of 16 cm (Fig. 1b; n = 8). During the learning session, participants adapted to the S-shaped perturbation, the 94 

magnitude of which gradually increased over trials. The subsequent test session examined how the learned 95 

motor memory generalized to an untrained reaching distance. To do so, probe trials were interleaved once every 96 

five perturbation trials. For each probe trial, the hand trajectory was constrained to a straight path between the 97 

start position and the target using a force channel (clamp channel). The lateral force exerted against the channel 98 

wall was used as an index of the retrieved motor memory (21). 99 

Figures 1c and 1d present the retrieved force profiles measured during the test session for both learned 100 

and unlearned reaching distances. For learned distances, participants expressed clear compensatory force 101 

components in opposite directions in the first and second halves of the movement, as required to counteract the 102 

S-shaped perturbation. The peak forces in the first and second halves of the movement were significantly 103 

directed opposite to the corresponding force component of the perturbation (t-tests, p < 0.001; learned panels in 104 

Fig. 1c–d). Importantly, a similar force pattern was observed when participants performed the unlearned distance. 105

Even though the reaching distance changed, the retrieved force again reversed between the first and second 106 

halves of the movement, and the peak forces in the first and second halves of the movement were significantly 107 

directed opposite to the perturbation (t-tests, all p < 0.002; unlearned panel in Fig. 1c–d). Thus, the learned force 108 

pattern generalized across reaching distances while preserving its relative organization within the movement. 109 

This pattern cannot be explained by a purely velocity-dependent account. The first and second halves 110 

of the reach involved nearly identical hand velocities, yet participants retrieved opposing force components in 111 

these two halves. If motor memory were indexed solely by velocity, the memories acquired in the two halves 112 

should have interfered with each other or been retrieved similarly. Instead, participants generated appropriately 113 

opposite forces in the two halves of the movement, indicating that velocity alone cannot account for observed 114 

memory expression. 115 

A position-dependent account was also insufficient. If the two opposing force components were tied 116 

to absolute limb position, then the same compensatory force should be retrieved whenever the hand passes 117 

through the same spatial location, regardless of the reach distance. However, this was not observed. For example, 118 

in Group 2, the first 8 cm of the trained 16-cm reach exhibited a predominantly rightward compensatory force. 119 

However, when the same participants performed the untrained 8-cm reach, the force reversed within that same 120 

spatial region, with rightward force in the first half and a leftward force in the second half (see the force profile 121 

for the unlearned distance in Fig. 1d). Thus, at overlapping limb positions, the retrieved force differed depending 122 

on whether that position occurred in the first or the second half of the movement. To quantify this discrepancy, 123 

we compared the lateral force between the learned and unlearned movements within a shared spatial window of 124 

60–75 mm (Fig. 1e inset), corresponding to the second half of the 8-cm reach and the first half of the 16-cm 125 

reach. The retrieved force differed significantly between reaching distances in both groups (Student’s t-tests: 126 

Group 1, t(7) = 5.062, p = 0.002; Group 2, t(7) = 4.866, p = 0.002; Fig. 1e), further arguing against position-127 

dependent retrieval alone. 128 

Taken together, these results show that neither position-dependent nor velocity-dependent encoding 129 

alone is sufficient to explain the generalization observed in Experiment 1. Additional analyses further showed 130 

that accounts based on other individual physical variables were also insufficient. The force profiles could not 131 

be explained by memory tied to absolute elapsed time: when trials were divided into fast and slow subsets based 132 

on the time taken to reach the movement midpoint, the force profiles did not follow a fixed temporal pattern but 133 

instead were better aligned after temporal scaling (Supplementary Fig. 1). This pattern is consistent with force 134 

retrieval following the relative progress of the movement, rather than absolute elapsed time. The observed force 135 

profiles were also inconsistent with an account based solely on acceleration magnitude (Supplementary Fig. 2). 136 

Thus, the findings are more parsimoniously explained by a relative coordinate, such as progression through the 137 

reach, rather than by any single absolute-valued kinematic or temporal variable. 138

Progression-based organization in double-reach sequences. 140 

Experiment 2 asked whether the progress coordinate can span an entire sequence when two reaches are planned 141

as a unified action, using a double-reach task in which participants briefly stopped at an intermediate point (Fig. 142 

2a). If each reach is planned independently, motor memory should be organized within separate progression 143 

cycles for each component reach. However, if two reaches are planned as a unified action, motor memory should 144 

instead be organized over a single progression cycle spanning the entire sequence. 145 

Previous studies have shown that participants can learn opposing curl force fields when each force 146 

field is associated with the direction of a lead-in movement performed immediately before the main reach (22) 147 

or a follow-through movement performed immediately after it (23) (Fig. 2b). These contextual effects are 148 

typically interpreted as showing that different preceding or subsequent movements cue the retrieval of distinct 149 

motor memories (22, 23). Here, we reinterpret those findings from the perspective of action progress. If the 150 

entire double-reach sequence is represented as a single unified action, then opposite force fields assigned to the 151 

same portion of the sequence should interfere, whereas force fields assigned to different portions of the sequence 152 

should be mapped to different locations along a sequence-level progress coordinate and should therefore 153 

interfere less. 154 

To test this idea, we assigned 32 participants to two groups (Fig. 2b-c). During the learning session 155 

(Fig. 2d), Group 1 participants (Fig. 2b; n = 16) performed either lead-in trials or follow-through trials in which 156 

opposite force fields were associated with two different directions of the same contextual movement type, 157 

meaning that the opposing perturbations were experienced within the same progression range of the sequence 158 

(high-interference condition). Group 2 participants (Fig. 2c; n = 16) performed both lead-in and follow-through 159 

trials, and opposite force fields were associated with different trial types, meaning that the perturbations were 160 

experienced in different progression ranges (reduced-interference condition). Thus, the critical distinction 161 

between the groups was whether the two opposing force fields occupied overlapping or separated portions of a 162 

putative sequence-level progress coordinate. 163 

Group 1 participants exhibited large deviations from a straight path throughout learning, whereas 164 

Group 2 participants showed gradual compensation for the perturbations and achieved straighter movements 165 

(Fig. 2e). Figure 3a presents the learning curves for the opposing force fields in each group. To quantify learning, 166 

we measured the magnitude of lateral deviation at the peak velocity during the perturbed main movement. As 167 

predicted by the action-progress framework, Group 2 participants—who experienced the two force fields in 168 

different progression ranges—showed facilitated adaptation compared with Group 1 participants, for whom 169 

both force fields were associated with the same progression range. 170

Although Group 2 showed faster learning than Group 1, this group difference alone does not establish 180

the preceding and following movements available as potential contextual cues, whereas each subgroup of Group 183

we examined memory retrieval in two error-clamp probe conditions (Probe 1 and Probe 2; Fig. 2c) that contained 186

Critically, these probes allowed us to test whether motor memories organized by action progression could be 190 

retrieved even when state information provided by the immediately preceding and following movement 191

Figure 3d shows the lateral force profiles measured in the channel trials. In both probes, the force 193

lateral force for each reach relative to its value at reach onset (inset of Fig. 3e). The peak values are summarized 195

that negative values indicate opposite-signed forces across the two reaches. This ratio was significantly negative 197 

in both Probe 1 (median [interquartile range, IQR] = −0.369 [−1.169, −0.286]; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 198 

0.001) and Probe 2 (median [IQR] = −0.268 [−0.473, −0.186]; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001), 199 

confirming that the retrieved force was reversed between the first and second reaches in both probe conditions. 200 

A particularly critical comparison is between the second reach of Probe 1 and the first reach of Probe 201 

2. These two movements are kinematically identical (Supplemental Fig. 4) and occur at the same limb position 202 

where the force field had been experienced during learning, differing only in whether the movement occurred 203 

in the early or late portion of the double-reach sequence. If motor memory were determined solely by state-204 

dependent variables (e.g., position or velocity), the retrieved forces should be identical. Similarly, if the learning 205 

advantage in Group 2 merely reflected the availability of multiple contextual cues, this account would not 206 

specifically predict opposite retrieved forces between these kinematically matched reaches. Instead, the 207 

measured forces had opposite signs (Fig. 3g), indicating that distinct motor memories are learned and retrieved 208 

according to where the movement occurred within the sequence-level progress coordinate. 209

A parametric approach to the sequence-level organization of motor memory 224 

Experiment 2 showed that motor memory differs between the early and late portions of a double-reach sequence. 225 

However, this result leaves open whether memory is organized along a continuous progress coordinate, or 226 

whether it simply differs between two discrete contexts: the first and second reaches. Experiment 3 addressed 227 

this question using a button–reach–button task (Fig. 4a) in which participants performed a left-hand button press 228 

(B₁), a right-hand reach (R), and a second left-hand button press (B₂). By controlling the timing of the three-229 

action sequence with a progress bar (Fig. 4b), this task allowed us to vary parametrically the progression value 230 

at which the reach occurred within the overall sequence. 231 

We first asked whether learning opposing force fields is facilitated when the two force fields are 232 

separated across different action-progress values rather than assigned to the same action-progress value. 233 

Participants (n = 32) were randomly assigned to two groups. In both groups, the direction of the curl field was 234 

associated with one of two B₁–R intervals (250 or 500 ms), but the mapping from these intervals to progression 235 

within the full button–reach–button sequence differed between groups. In Group 1 (Fig. 4d), the total button-236 

to-button duration was fixed at 750 ms such that the reach occurred at different progression values depending 237 

on the B₁–R interval: 2π/3 for the 250-ms condition and 4π/3 for the 500-ms condition. In this group, progression 238 

provided an additional cue for force-field direction alongside the absolute B₁–R interval. In contrast, in Group 239 

2 (Fig. 4e), the total duration was scaled with the B₁–R interval (500 ms vs. 1000 ms) so that the reach always 240 

occurred at the same progression value (π) despite different absolute intervals. Thus, for Group 2, the force-241 

field direction could be predicted only from the absolute timing variables (B₁–R, R–B₂, and total duration), not 242 

from progression. The CW and CCW force fields were presented in pseudorandom order for both groups. 243 

Learning was facilitated in Group 1 relative to Group 2 (Fig. 4f). The error decay rate did not differ 244 

significantly between groups (Mann–Whitney U test, z = 1.715, p = 0.086; left panel in Fig. 4g), whereas the 245 

error reduction ratio was significantly larger in Group 1 than in Group 2 (Mann–Whitney U test, z = 2.544, p = 246 

0.011; right panel in Fig. 4g). These results demonstrate that separating opposing force fields across different 247 

progression values facilitates learning during training. 248

We next asked whether the learned memories were expressed as a graded function of the progress 249 

coordinate. Probe trials sampled not only the two trained conditions but also an intermediate condition, allowing 250 

us to examine how memory expression varied across the coordinate. Figure 5a illustrates the forces exerted in 251 

the probe trials. The compensatory force size was quantified as the impulse of the lateral force from movement 252 

onset to peak velocity, normalized to the impulse of the ideal force (computed as b ⋅v) over the same interval 253 

and expressed as a percentage (Fig. 5b). In Group 1, the compensatory force size was systematically modulated 254 

by progression (one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), F(2,80) = 184.8, p < 0.001): the 255 

two trained conditions elicited opposite compensatory forces, whereas the intermediate condition produced an 256 

intermediate level of forces (post hoc comparisons with Holm correction; Early vs. Middle: t(15) = −10.62, p 257 

< 0.001; Middle vs. Late: t(15) = −10.94, p < 0.001). This graded pattern indicates that motor memory was 258 

not merely assigned to two discrete contexts but was organized along a continuous sequence-level progress 259 

coordinate. 260 

Group 2 exhibited a qualitatively similar pattern across the trained and intermediate absolute-time 261 

conditions (one-way repeated measures ANOVA, F(1.5, 21.75) = 77.19, p < 0.001 with Greenhouse-Geisser 262 

correction) with significant differences between adjacent conditions (post hoc comparisons with Holm 263 

correction; Early vs. Middle: t(15) = −8.00, p < 0.001; Middle vs. Late: t(15) = −6.10, p < 0.001). However, 264 

overall memory expression was stronger in Group 1: the compensation ratio, computed by averaging sign-265 

aligned compensatory forces in the trained conditions, was significantly higher in Group 1 than in Group 2 266 

(Student’s t-test, t(30) = 4.872, p < 0.001; Fig. 5c). Thus, action progress provided a more effective organizing 267 

coordinate than absolute timing alone. 268 

Because Group 2 still expressed opposing memories, we next examined whether this reflected true 269 

organization by absolute timing or unintended separation along action progress. Even when the task was 270 

designed to place the reach at the same progress value in Group 2, participants’ timing variability could shift 271 

the realized reach position along the progress coordinate. To examine this possibility, a progression-separation 272 

index was computed by binning sequence progression into 100 bins, accumulating the force-field exposures (+1 273 

for CW, −1 for CCW), and summing the absolute values across bins (see Methods, Eq. 3). Higher values 274 

indicate greater separation of the two force fields along progression. 275 

Figure 5d shows an example Group 1 participant with a high separation index, reflecting clear 276 

segregation of the two fields along progression. In contrast, some Group 2 participants expressed both force 277 

fields at nearly the same progression value (Fig. 5e, left), whereas others introduced small but systematic 278 

progression shifts that yielded partial separation (Fig. 5e, right). Across participants in both groups, the 279 

progression-separation index strongly predicted the degree of memory expression. A linear regression 280 

constrained through the origin indicated a positive relationship between the separation index and the 281 

compensation ratio (β = 0.056, r² = 0.487; Fig. 5f, left panel). 282

Overall, Experiment 3 shows that motor memory can be organized within a continuous progress 312 

coordinate. Moreover, the comparison between the button–reach–button and button–reach tasks indicates that 313 

this coordinate is defined by the structure of the sequence as a whole, rather than by the absolute timing between 314 

adjacent action elements alone. Absolute B₁–R timing can contribute to memory organization, but it provides a 315 

weaker organizing signal than sequence-level action progress. 316

Discussion 319 

A hallmark of the motor system is its ability to flexibly reuse motor memories acquired from previous movement 320 

experiences to meet new behavioral demands. This flexibility suggests that motor memories are not stored 321 

merely as responses tied to the specific kinematics or timing of individual movements but can also be organized 322 

within a more abstract representational structure. However, it remains unclear what abstract coordinate system 323 

supports this form of organization. Here, using motor adaptation experiments, we provide converging behavioral 324 

evidence that action progress can serve as an abstract, continuous coordinate for organizing motor memory 325 

within the structure of a planned action. 326 

327 

Action progress as an abstract coordinate for motor memory 328 

Motor memories are often described as being supported by primitives tuned to limb state variables, such as 329 

position and velocity, producing state-dependent generalization (13–18). This framework has been powerful for 330 

explaining force-field adaptation, including local generalization and interference when two perturbations 331 

overlap in state space. Here, our aim was not to challenge the importance of physical-state coordinates, but to 332 

test whether they are sufficient to explain generalization across changes in movement scale. 333 

Experiment 1 provided this test by asking whether a learned force pattern would transfer across 334 

reaching distances. Participants learned to compensate for an S-shaped force field and expressed a similar S-335 

shaped force pattern even when reaching over distances different from those experienced during learning (Figs. 336 

1c-d). The acquisition of an S-shaped pattern argues against a purely velocity-based account, because 337 

participants expressed oppositely directed compensatory forces within a single reach despite comparable 338 

velocity magnitudes across the two movement halves (14). The transfer across reaching distances further 339 

showed that force expression at overlapping spatial positions depended on where those positions occurred within 340 

the progression of the action (Fig. 1e), indicating that position alone was also insufficient. Additional analyses 341 

showed that neither acceleration magnitude nor absolute elapsed time alone provided a sufficient explanation 342 

of the observed force profiles (Supplementary Figs. 1–2). 343 

We cannot rule out more complex alternatives in which multiple physical variables, or model-free 344 

updates of motor policy (24), account for aspects of the data. However, such accounts would require the motor 345 

system to flexibly combine and reweight these variables across changes in movement scale. It is unclear how 346 

this strategy would naturally give rise to the generalization observed here. These considerations motivate an 347 

action-progress account, in which the learned force pattern is organized not only by physical movement 348 

variables, but also according to progression within the intended action, allowing it to be retrieved according to 349 

the same progression in a new movement situation. 350 

A related alternative is a gain- or scaling-based account, in which the learned relationship between 351 

movement state and motor output is rescaled according to changes in movement distance or speed (25). In the 352 

present case, such an account could describe the transfer across reaching distances as a rescaling of the learned 353 

force pattern from the trained movement distance to the test movement distance. However, this account leaves 354

open how the motor system selects which previously learned state-dependent memory should guide the current 355 

movement and, within that memory, how the relevant part should be aligned and rescaled to the current 356

reference to a particular previously learned state-specific memory. Thus, although state-dependent and gain- or 359 

scaling-based mechanisms may contribute to the observed transfer, the action-progress account provides a 360 

parsimonious way to describe the component of generalization that depends on where the movement lies within 361 

the intended action. 362

Sequence-level progression 364 

If action progress provides an organizing coordinate for motor memory, it should not be limited to the internal 365 

progression of a single reach, but may also apply to multiple movement elements organized as a single planned 366 

action. Experiment 2 tested this possibility using a double-reach task (22–23) in which both the first and second 367 

targets were presented before movement onset, allowing participants to know the final goal of the sequence in 368 

advance (Fig. 2a–c). 369 

Learning was facilitated when opposing force fields were assigned to different portions of the 370 

sequence, but impaired when both fields were experienced within the same portion (Fig. 3b–c). Although this 371 

learning advantage could partly reflect the availability of both preceding and following movements as contextual 372 

cues in Group 2, the subsequent probe trials provided a more direct test: kinematically identical reaches elicited 373 

opposite force outputs depending on whether they occurred early or late within the sequence (Fig. 3d–g). This 374 

result provides stronger evidence for progression-dependent retrieval at the sequence level. 375 

This interpretation does not imply that any series of movements is automatically organized into a 376 

single progression cycle. Karniel and Mussa-Ivaldi showed that participants failed to learn alternating opposing 377 

force fields in sequential reaching tasks, suggesting that movement order alone is not sufficient to switch 378 

between opposing dynamics (26). A key difference from our task is that, in their task, each reach was triggered 379 

by the presentation of a new target only after the previous target had been acquired, likely encouraging 380 

participants to plan each movement separately. By contrast, our double-reach task encouraged participants to 381 

plan and execute multiple movement elements as a single action unit. Thus, sequence-level progression should 382 

not be viewed as an inevitable property of any movement sequence, but rather as a representational coordinate 383 

that may emerge when task structure encourages multiple movement elements to be organized as a single 384 

planned action. 385

Action progress as a continuous memory dimension 387 

A key limitation of Experiment 2 is that progression-dependent retrieval could, in principle, reflect a categorical 388 

distinction between the first and second reaches rather than a continuous coordinate. Experiment 3 addressed 389 

this issue using a button–reach–button paradigm in which the reach occurred at parametrically varied 390

interpretation is difficult to reconcile with previous evidence that purely visual contextual cues are relatively 414 

ineffective in separating opposing motor memories (29, 30). Thus, the difference between the button–reach– 415 

button and button–reach tasks is more consistent with the notion that the final button press, as an action element, 416 

helped define the reach’s relative position within the unfolding action sequence. 417

therefore needed to determine whether progression-dependent memory expression is specific to action 422 

sequences or reflects a broader task-event coordinate. 423

Conceptual implications and future directions 431 

Our findings extend the concept of motor abstraction from abstract representations of movement patterns to the 432 

abstract coordinates by which motor memories are organized and retrieved. Previous work has shown that 433 

abstract motor representations exist and can support the learning and generalization of novel movements beyond 434 

the conditions in which they were acquired (7,8). Yet motor memory has traditionally been described as a state-435 

dependent process (13–18), in which memories are linked to physical variables such as position and velocity. 436 

This raises a fundamental question: how can memories learned through specific movements be flexibly reused 437 

when future actions differ in scale, timing, or sequence context? The present results identify action progress as 438 

an abstract organizing coordinate that complements physical-state representations. Importantly, this coordinate 439 

may provide a basis for organizing individual movement experiences directly into an abstract form, rather than 440 

reconstructing abstract motor memory only retrospectively from memories tied to separate physical states. This 441 

interpretation resonates with recent theoretical accounts of categorization, which propose that categorization is 442 

not an end stage of perception but a process that shapes signal processing from the outset through predictive, 443 

action-oriented abstractions (32). In a similar way, action progress may structure motor learning as an action 444 

unfolds, supporting both the construction and retrieval of abstract motor memory according to its functional 445 

position within an intended action. Thus, this study provides a framework for understanding how abstract motor 446 

memories support flexible motor control. Because the present study focused on motor adaptation, an important 447 

future direction is to determine whether similar progress-based coordinates also contribute to other forms of 448 

motor learning, such as de novo skill acquisition and reward-based learning. 449 

The continuity revealed in Experiment 3 also has an important implication for flexible generalization. 450 

Unlike a categorical cue, a continuous progress coordinate allows learned outputs to vary smoothly with the 451 

relative position of a movement within a planned action. This continuity fits naturally with neural population 452 

dynamics accounts in which movement-related neural activity evolves along continuous, phase-like trajectories 453 

in neural state space (10, 11). However, evidence from rapid movement sequences suggests that motor cortex 454 

does not simply fuse successive elements into a single holistic dynamical cycle; instead, individual elements 455 

are generated through partly separate dynamics, with preparation for upcoming elements overlapping with 456 

ongoing execution (33, 34). Thus, sequence-level action progress may depend on higher-order population-level 457 

mechanisms that represent the structure and progression of an action sequence, consistent with evidence for 458 

ordinal-rank subspaces in prefrontal cortex and progression-dependent encoding geometries in posterior cortical 459 

populations (31, 35, 36). These representations may interact with cerebellar and basal ganglia circuits to 460 

organize multiple movement elements into appropriately timed and sequentially ordered actions, as dysfunction 461 

of these systems impairs the rhythmic and sequential organization of movement in cerebellar ataxia and 462 

Parkinson’s disease (37-41). 463

Finally, the present study bridges motor adaptation and sequence learning within a common 464 

framework. Motor adaptation and sequence learning have traditionally been treated as distinct paradigms in 465 

motor learning research (12). Sequence learning, in particular, has often been discussed in terms of hierarchical 466 

organization and chunking of movement elements (42-44). The present findings connect these traditions by 467 

showing that adaptation memories for a reach can depend on where that reach occurs within the structure of a 468 

planned action sequence. This perspective is important because everyday motor behavior rarely consists of 469 

isolated movements; rather, it is composed of action elements embedded within structured goals, sequences, 470 

and contexts. Future work should examine how progress-based coordinates are formed as action sequences are 471 

learned, and how hierarchical circuits involving motor cortical areas, higher-order cortical regions, and 472 

subcortical structures define planned action units and organize memory retrieval within them. 473

# Materials and Methods 476 

Experimental Design 477 

This study tested whether motor memory is organized by action progress rather than by the specific movement 478 

states experienced during learning. We used three experiments. Experiment 1 examined whether motor learning 479 

generalized across movement scales in single reaching movements according to action progress. Experiment 2 480 

tested whether distinct motor memories were associated with different progression ranges in a double-reach 481 

sequence. Experiment 3 examined whether motor memory was organized along a continuous sequence-level 482 

progression in a button–reach–button task. Participants were assigned to predefined groups, force-field 483 

directions were counterbalanced across participants, and motor memory was quantified using force output 484 

measured in error-clamp probe trials and learning traces in perturbation trials. Prespecified exclusion criteria 485 

were based on movement initiation, task timing, movement execution, and completion of the session. 486

experiments as follows: Experiment 1 (Group 1: 8 (4 women); Group 2: 8 (4 women)), Experiment 2 (Group 1: 490

Group 2: 18 (10 women); Group 3: 16 (8 women)). Participants were compensated ¥2,000 per hour for their 492

Experiment 3 666 

Experiment 3 established whether motor memory is organized along a continuous sequence-level progression. 667 

Procedure: Participants performed a sequence comprising three consecutive actions: pressing a keyboard key 668 

with the left index finger (B₁), smoothly reaching forward with the right hand toward a target located 4 cm away 669 

(R), and pressing another key with the left index finger (B₂). Participants were instructed to pre-plan the timing 670 

of these actions and execute them as a continuous, well-timed sequence. 671 

A horizontal progress bar displayed on a screen indicated the temporal structure of the task (Fig. 5b). 672 

Five vertical reference lines were drawn on the bar, corresponding to −750 ms, −375 ms, 0 ms (the timing of 673 

the first button press), the timing of the reach, and the timing of the second button press. The start position for 674 

the reach was located just below the line corresponding to the reach time. After participants held the cursor at 675 

the start position for approximately 1 s, the progress bar appeared for 1 s to allow them to understand the 676 

temporal structure of the upcoming trial. The bar then began to fill from left to right in pink, starting from the 677 

leftmost line. The interval of 375 ms corresponded to a horizontal distance of 3.9 cm on the display. When the 678 

bar had filled with pink to the third line, it stopped, and the participants were instructed to imagine its 679 

continuation and perform the movement such that the pink bar would overlap with each reference line at the 680 

appropriate timing. During the practice session, small feedback markers were presented above the progress bar 681 

in real time at the timing of each button press and the onset of the reach (defined as 8 mm of movement). 682 

Participants were encouraged to adjust their performance so that these feedback markers aligned with the 683 

reference lines during the trials. If the peak velocity of the reaching movement deviated from the prescribed 684 

range of 0.3–0.4 m/s, the word “slow” or “fast” was displayed as feedback on the screen after the trial. A trial 685 

was considered successful if the timing error for each action was within 150 ms of the target timing. 686

Data analysis 743 

The handle position and force data were sampled at a rate of 500 Hz and filtered using the 4th-order zero-lag 744 

Butterworth filter with a cut-off frequency of 10 Hz. Data analysis was performed using MATLAB v.2020b 745 

(Mathworks). For all analyses, the lateral force profile was defined as the lateral forces exerted in the probe 746 

trials, subtracting the average baseline force from the clamp channel in the baseline session. 747 

Data analysis for Experiment 1. Movement onset was determined as the time when the handle velocity reached 748 

5% of the peak velocity. The relationship between the lateral forces and applied forces was analyzed by aligning 749 

all trials at the movement onset and averaging them across repetitions. Because the force field direction was 750 

counterbalanced across participants, force data were flipped as necessary so that the perturbation direction in 751 

the first half of the movement corresponded to the negative direction across participants. The direction of the 752 

retrieved lateral force was quantified in the early and later parts of the movement. The maximum value of the 753 

lateral force exerted in the direction opposing the perturbation within each range of the progression was taken 754 

as an index of motor memory expressed in the appropriate compensatory direction. To assess force output at 755 

comparable spatial locations across groups, the integrated lateral force was computed over a 6–7.5-cm region 756 

(Fig. 1e inset). These measures enabled the determination of whether learned motor memory was expressed in 757 

a state- or progression-dependent manner. 758 

To examine whether the force profiles could be explained by memory tied to absolute elapsed time, 759 

probe trials were divided within each participant into fast and slow subsets (Supplemental Figure 1). 760 

Specifically, the eight trials with the shortest and longest times to reach the midpoint distance were labeled fast 761 

and slow trials, respectively. Force profiles were then averaged within each subset. To quantify temporal scaling, 762 

the fast-trial force profile was temporally scaled and correlated with the slow-trial force profile, and the scaling 763 

factor that maximized this correlation was identified for each participant. 764

corresponded to 0, π and 2π, respectively. b, Experimental paradigm motivated by previous studies (Howard et 1014

Supplemental Figure 1 | Absolute-time-dependent representation cannot simply explain the 1102 

generalization in experiment 1. 1103 

To test whether the probe-trial force profiles in Experiment 1 could be accounted for by memory tied to absolute 1104 

elapsed time, trials were divided within each participant into fast and slow subsets. Specifically, the eight trials 1105 

with the shortest and longest times to reach the midpoint distance—4 cm in the 8-cm condition and 8 cm in the 1106 

16-cm condition—were labeled fast and slow trials, respectively. The force profiles were then averaged within 1107 

each subset. a–b, Position (a) and force (b) profiles for the fast and slow trial subsets in Group 1. If force 1108 

generation were tied to absolute elapsed time, the temporal evolution of the force profiles should not differ 1109 

between the fast and slow trial subsets. However, the force profiles instead appeared to scale with movement 1110 

duration. c, Scaling analysis of force profiles. To quantify temporal scaling, the fast-trial force profile was 1111 

temporally scaled and correlated with the slow-trial force profile. The left panel shows the correlation as a 1112 

function of the scaling factor, and the right panel shows the scaling factor that maximized the correlation for 1113 

each participant. The peak scaling factor was significantly greater than 1 (one-sample t-test; 8-cm reaching: t(7) 1114 

= 6.116, p < 0.001; 16-cm reaching: t(7) = 8.216, p < 0.001), indicating that the force profiles were stretched in 1115 

time rather than generated according to a fixed absolute-time pattern. d–f, Same analysis for Group 2. Position 1116 

(d) and force (e) profiles for the fast and slow trial subsets, and the temporal scaling analysis (f). The peak-1117 

correlation scaling factor was significantly greater than 1 (one-sample t-test; 8-cm reaching: t(7) = 4.580, p = 1118 

0.003; 16-cm reaching: t(7) = 6.284, p < 0.001). These results suggest that force was not generated based on 1119 

absolute elapsed time, supporting the idea that the memory was retrieved according to relative temporal 1120 

progression within the movement. 1121
