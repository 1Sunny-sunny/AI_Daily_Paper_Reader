# Learning Regularization Structure for Biosignal Template Estimation 

Yonathan Murin, Alexandre Gramfort 

Abstract —Estimating event-locked templates from bio-signal recordings via regularized least-squares requires choosing both the regularization structure and its magnitude, choices that are typically made heuristically. We develop a data-driven framework based on Stein’s Unbiased Risk Estimate (SURE) that jointly optimizes both. By parameterizing the regularization operator as a convolution kernel, our method learns the penalty structure directly from the data, combining smoothness enforcement with ridge-like shrinkage in a way that cannot be achieved by scaling a fixed difference operator. While standard SURE assumes white noise, biosignal noise exhibits temporal autocorrelation. We therefore extend SURE to colored noise by replacing its scalar trace term with a structured correction based on the noise covariance matrix. For AR(1) noise, this correction requires only two parameters, the noise variance and the lag-1 autocorrelation, both estimable from pre-event baselines. Cross-modality valida-tion on auditory event-related potentials, P300 brain–computer interface data, and ECG morphology demonstrates consistent gains compared to alternative methods, all at K = 5 events per class - the regime most relevant for rapid calibration and personalization. 

Index Terms —Template estimation, Tikhonov regularization, SURE, colored noise, event-related potentials, EEG, ECG, biosig-nal processing 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Estimating event-locked templates from noisy biosignal recordings is a foundational challenge spanning multiple biomedical modalities, including EEG [1], MEG [2], ECG [3], EMG [4], [5], [6], and fMRI [7]. Such templates are known as event-related potentials (ERPs) in EEG, event-related fields (ERFs) in MEG, or hemodynamic response functions (HRFs) in fMRI. Across these domains, rapid calibration from few (K) events is highly desirable for personalization and bedside monitoring, making template estimation quality in the low-data regime a critical practical concern. The classical approach of signal averaging improves the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) proportionally to √N [8], but fails when short inter-stimulus intervals cause adjacent responses to overlap temporally, in-troducing systematic bias [9]. While single-trial denoising frameworks address trial variability [10], [11], [12], [13], they fundamentally do not resolve this overlap problem. To handle overlapping events, an alternative approach mod-els the continuous recording as a linear superposition of event-locked templates: X = DΦ + W, where D is adesign matrix encoding event timings, Φ contains the target templates, and W represents noise [14], [15], [7], [16], [17],

[18]. Though the least-squares solution naturally deconvolves overlapping contributions, implementing Tikhonov regulariza-tion [19] introduces the difficult challenge of parameter se-lection. The regularization structure (e.g., smoothness penalty, ridge, or a combination) and its magnitude are typically chosen heuristically, which can severely degrade estimation quality in the low-data regime where optimal regularization dictates performance. Template estimation via regularized least squares is a linear inverse problem with several classical parameter selection strategies, including GCV [20], the L-curve method [21], and Morozov’s discrepancy principle [22]. Alternatively, Stein’s Unbiased Risk Estimate (SURE) [23] targets the mean-square error (MSE) directly, offering a powerful framework for data-driven optimization [24], [25], [26], [27]. Two limitations, however, restrict the applicability of these methods to biosignal template estimation. First, all of them (including Empirical Bayes frameworks [28], [29], [30], [31]) optimize only a scalar magnitude for an a-priori chosen penalty operator, leaving the penalty structure (smoothness, ridge, or a combination) as a heuristic choice. Second, standard SURE assumes independent white noise, an assumption routinely violated by the tempo-rally correlated, 1/f -type noise ubiquitous in bandpass-filtered biosignals [32]. The second issue, colored residuals in a linear deconvolution model, has been studied extensively in the closely related general linear model (GLM) framework for fMRI inference. Friston et al. [33] captured the residual autocorrelation with an autoregressive (AR) process and pre-whitened both the data and the design matrix, and Worsley and Friston [34] refined the resulting corrections for statistical testing. Pre-whitening, however, transforms the design matrix and complicates the mapping between regularization in the whitened and original domains; an issue that is acute when the regularizer itself is the object being optimized. We instead leave the data and design matrix untouched and modify the risk estimator: an exact correction to the SURE trace term under an AR(1) noise model, expressed through the compact P ×P matrix D⊤ΣW D

(rather than a full T ×T observation-space covariance), recov-ers an unbiased risk estimate using only two noise parameters estimable from pre-event (e.g., pre-stimulus) baselines. In this work, we develop a data-driven framework for opti-mizing Tikhonov regularization in event template estimation. Our contributions are as follows: 1) We derive a SURE-based objective to optimize the reg-ularization kernel structure, not just its magnitude , by parameterizing the operator as a convolution matrix. The learned kernels uniquely combine smoothness enforce-ment with ridge-like shrinkage. arXiv:2607.23232v1 [eess.SP] 25 Jul 2026 2

2) We extend SURE to colored noise via a structured covariance correction ( D⊤ΣW D) that accounts for event timing geometry while avoiding the computational burden of full observation-space matrices. 3) We validate the method across auditory ERP, P300 BCI, and ECG datasets, demonstrating consistent improve-ments over Empirical Bayes in the critical low-data regime ( K = 5 ). To our knowledge, this is the first work to optimize the structure of a regularization kernel via SURE while account-ing for temporally correlated noise through an event-timing-dependent covariance structure. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion II formulates the template estimation problem. Section III presents the proposed SURE-based optimization framework, including the colored-noise extension. Section IV provides a simulation study while Section V validates the method on auditory ERP, P300 BCI, and ECG data. Section VI discusses practical considerations, and Section VII concludes. II. C. Kernel Parameterization 

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 hold for any regularization ma-trix Γ, which brings the question: What type of regularization matrix one should use? Since Γ acts independently on each column of Φ (i.e., on each template waveform), and each column is a one-dimensional signal along the temporal axis, we parameterize Γ as a convolution matrix defined by a kernel 

h. Then, the optimizers solving for Γopt find not only the magnitude of the regularization but also its spectral shape. To control the number of free parameters in our optimization problem we further impose the following constraints:  

> •

Kernel length Q: controls the filter order (typically Q∈{3, 5}).  

> •

Symmetry : constraining h to be symmetric halves the number of free parameters and ensures a zero-phase frequency response. For example, a symmetric kernel of length 3 has the form 

h = [ a, b, a ] with 2 free parameters, compared to the fixed second-order difference [1 , −2, 1] . The optimization in (10) is performed over these kernel coefficients using a general-purpose optimizer (e.g., L-BFGS-B). A key finding (Sections IV and V) is that the learned kernels consistently have non-zero DC gain , meaning they provide ridge-like shrinkage in addition to smoothness enforcement. This is in contrast to the standard practice of using only temporal regularization via finite differences, which have zero DC gain by construction. Empirical Bayes, GCV, and L-curve can only optimize the scalar magnitude λ for a fixed penalty structure; they cannot discover this combined structure. 

D. Noise Parameter Estimation 

Theorem 2 holds for any known covariance ΣW , but practi-cal use requires a parametric model with few estimable param-eters. Bandpass-filtered biosignal noise is well characterized by a first-order autoregressive process, whose covariance is fully determined by two scalars: the marginal variance σ2 and the lag-1 autocorrelation φ. AR(1) models have a long track record for capturing temporal autocorrelation in neuroimaging, notably in fMRI where they were adopted to correct GLM statistics [33], [34]. In the biosignal setting, the AR(1) model captures the dominant effect of bandpass filtering (temporal smoothing that correlates adjacent samples), while remaining estimable from short pre-stimulus baseline segments. 

Variance ( σ2). We extract pre-stimulus baseline segments (e.g., 100 ms before each event onset), concatenate them, and compute the sample variance. These segments precede the event-locked response and thus contain only ongoing noise. 

AR(1) coefficient ( φ). From the same baseline segments, we compute the lag-1 sample autocorrelation and take the median across channels for robustness. Typical values for bandpass-filtered biosignal data range from φ ≈ 0.88 (P300 dataset, 1–20 Hz at 128 Hz) to φ ≈ 0.98 (auditory ERP dataset, 1– 40 Hz at 600.6 Hz), reflecting the ratio of passband to sampling rate. A. Learned Penalty Structure Across Modalities 

Figure 8 compares the normalized frequency response of kernels learned by SURE(kernel) on each dataset. The learned structures differ substantially across modalities, reflecting the distinct spectral characteristics of each signal:  

> •

Simulation and ECG : the learned kernels closely resem-ble scaled versions of the standard [1 , −2, 1] difference operator, with high Nyquist-to-DC gain ratios (178 ×

and 120 ×, respectively). This indicates that smoothness enforcement dominates, consistent with well-defined, rel-atively sharp template features (Ricker wavelets and QRS complexes).  

> •

Auditory ERP : the kernel exhibits a moderate Nyquist-to-DC ratio (2.9 ×), combining smoothness with a non-trivial ridge component. The auditory ERP spans abroader frequency range than the P300, and the learned kernel reflects this intermediate structure.  

> •

P300 : the kernel is dominated by ridge-like shrinkage (Nyquist-to-DC ratio of 0.2 ×), penalizing overall ampli-tude more than high-frequency roughness. This is consis-tent with the slow, smooth character of the P300 wave, where the primary estimation challenge is amplitude rather than shape. These differences cannot be captured by a scalar-λ method. The ability to learn the penalty structure automatically is a unique advantage of the proposed framework.

C. Noise Estimation: A Design Choice 

SURE requires noise parameters as input, both a weakness (extra inputs required) and a strength (the user can adapt the noise model to the application). We recommend:  

> •

AR(1) colored : default for biosignal data with temporal autocorrelation ( φ> 0.5), especially at low K (≤ 20 ).  

> •

Pairwise : for white or near-white noise, for ECG (where adjacent-beat contamination biases baseline estimates), or when many events are available. Emp. Bayes avoids this input requirement, which makes it easier to deploy but less flexible when the noise model is well characterized. 

D. Computational Considerations 

Building D⊤ΣW D requires O(P 2N 2

> events

) operations, aone-time cost per dataset. For the auditory ERP dataset with 

P =480 and Nevents =145 , this takes approximately 1 second. Each SURE evaluation requires O(P 3) for the matrix solves, the same cost as the white-noise version since the M computa-tion dominates. SURE(kernel) with a symmetric 3-tap kernel has 2 free parameters and typically requires ∼50 optimizer iterations, for a total of ∼100 matrix inversions of size P×P .This is comparable to Emp. Bayes (which also iterates) and faster than GCV with dense grid search. The method is most practical for low-to-moderate channel counts: for the P300 dataset ( P = 204 ), SURE(kernel) runs in approximately 30 s, while for the 60-channel auditory ERP dataset ( P = 720 ) it takes approximately 10 min. For high-channel-count record-ings, spatial dimensionality reduction (e.g., xDAWN [39]) can be applied before template estimation to reduce P .

E. Limitations 

Several limitations should be noted. First, the AR(1) noise model is a first-order approximation. Bandpass-filtered noise has oscillatory autocorrelation structure that AR(1) captures only approximately. Higher-order AR models could provide better approximation at the cost of additional parameters, but we found AR(1) sufficient for the datasets considered. Second, for real-data evaluation the reference template is estimated from all available events, introducing evaluation noise at high K. This explains why improvements diminish as K approaches the total number of events. Third, template non-stationarity (trial-to-trial variability) is not modeled. Our framework assumes a deterministic template, which is standard in the literature but may underestimate the effective noise variance in paradigms with substantial habituation or learning effects. Finally, the method applies to temporal regularization only and is complementary to spatial filtering methods such as xDAWN [39]. Joint spatio-temporal optimization is a promis-ing direction for future work. 9

VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a data-driven framework for optimizing Tikhonov regularization for event template estimation in biosignals. By parameterizing the regularization operator as a convolution kernel and minimizing an unbiased estimate of the MSE, the proposed method jointly determines both the structure and magnitude of the regularization from the data. The colored-noise extension bridges SURE theory and biosignal practice, enabling principled regularization optimiza-tion for temporally correlated noise with only two additional parameters. Cross-modality validation demonstrates consistent improve-ments over Empirical Bayes at K = 5 events per class: 53% on auditory event-related potentials, 27% on P300 brain– computer interface data, and 28% on ECG beat morphology. The method is most beneficial precisely where it is most needed - when events are scarce and regularization matters most. # S6 Sensitivity to the AR(1) Coefficient φ

The colored-noise SURE (Theorem 2 of the main paper) requires an estimate ˆφ of the AR(1) lag-1 autocorrelation. To assess robustness to mis-estimation of ˆφ, we perturb the baseline-derived estimate on the P300 dataset (subject 1, K =5 , 10 reps) by factors {0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, 1.2} (clipped at 0.99 for stability) and re-run the full SURE(kernel) pipeline at each setting. The baseline-derived value on this dataset is ˆφ ≈ 0.873 .

Results. Table 2 summarizes the template distance under each perturbation, alongside the Empirical Bayes and white-noise SURE baselines. Two observations stand out: 1. Robust to mis-estimation. The colored SURE template distance varies by only ∼30% across the full perturbation range ( 0.5× to 1.5×), and always stays below both Emp. Bayes and the white-noise SURE. 2. Asymmetric in the direction of error. Performance is essentially flat for overestimation (factor 

≥ 1.0) but degrades more under underestimation (factor < 1.0). This is expected: overesti-mating φ leads to a more conservative (heavier) regularization, which fails gracefully; under-estimating it can leave the estimate under-regularized. A practical implication is that a crude ˆφ estimate ( ±20 % ) is sufficient to retain most of the colored-SURE benefit. Note that the reference template is itself an Emp.-Bayes estimate (regular-ized); a fraction of the apparent improvement at large φ comes from convergence in regularization strength to the reference, not necessarily from better template recovery. The qualitative robustness conclusion is unaffected. Figure S5 plots the same data graphically.

φ; they are absorbed by the regularizer S in the solve and have no observable effect on the SURE cost or the estimated kernel.

# S8 Additional Template Waveforms 

This section provides additional template visualizations not shown in the main paper. All figures use the same plotting convention: thick light-grey reference (from all available events) with mean and ±1 std shaded bands across 20 random subsamples for each method at K =5 .

ECG PVC beats. Figure S7 shows the premature ventricular contraction (PVC) class on the same MIT-BIH record 106 as the Normal beat shown in the main paper. Unlike the sharp QRS of the Normal beat, the PVC waveform is intrinsically broad and smooth, so all three methods produce essentially indistinguishable mean templates and comparable uncertainty bands. This is a useful negative control: when the template carries no sharp feature for over-regularization to damage, SURE(kernel) neither helps nor hurts relative to Empirical Bayes and simple averaging. 

EEG auditory left and right. Figures S8 and S9 show templates for the two auditory event classes (left- and right-ear stimuli) used in the main paper’s auditory ERP section (Sec. V-A). The expected N100 deflection around 100 ms is visible on the L condition; the R condition is dominated by noise on these channels at K =5 .
