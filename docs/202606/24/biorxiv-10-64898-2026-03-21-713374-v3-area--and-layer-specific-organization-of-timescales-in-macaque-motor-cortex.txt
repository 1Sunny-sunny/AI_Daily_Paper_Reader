# Abstract 10 

Hierarchy in the brain emerges across spatial and temporal scales, enabling transformations from 11 

rapid sensory encoding to sustained cognitive control, and this organization is well established in 12 

sensory systems. In contrast, the hierarchical organization of the primate motor cortex remains 13 

debated, partly due to its agranular architecture and the absence of clear layer -constrained input -14 

output projections. In particular, the relative hierarchical position of the dorsal premotor cortex 15 

(PMd) and the primary motor cortex (M1) cannot be resolved from anatomy alone. To investigate 16 

their relative organization, we adopted a unique multimodal approach using timescales derived 17 

from both single -unit spiking activity (SUA) and local field potentials (LFPs) in macaques 18 

performing a delayed -match -to -sample reaching task. We found convergent evidence for inter -19 

areal temporal organization, with longer SUA timescales and smaller LFP aperiodic spectral 20 

exponents in M1. Across cortical depth, however, temporal dynamics depended on signal 21 

modality. LFP autocorrelation timescales were systematically longer in deep layers of M1, and 22 

this was accompanied by smaller LFP spectral exponents in deep layers in both areas. In contrast, 23

# Introduction 38 

Hierarchy in the brain emerges across both spatial and temporal scales, enabling distributed yet 39 

coordinated processing of information across distinct regions. Cortical hierarchy is classically 40 

illustrated in the primate visual system (Felleman and Van Essen, 1991) , where receptive fields 41 

expand in size and computational complexity from orientation -selective responses in V1 (Hubel 42 

and Wiesel, 1968) to abstract object representations in higher visual areas like inferotemporal 43 

cortex (Tanaka, 1996; DiCarlo, Zoccolan and Rust, 2012) . Motor behavior likewise depends on 44 

multilevel transformations that convert sensory inputs into planned and executed actions. 45 

However, unlike sensory cortices, the organizational principles of the primate motor system 46 

remains less well understood, partly due to the agranular architecture of the motor cortex and the 47 

absence of clear layer -constrained input -output pathways that typically define feedforward and 48 

feedback projections  (Shipp, 2005; Barbas and García -Cabezas, 2015) .49 

The relative relationship between two motor areas involved in upper limb movements, the 50 

dorsal premotor cortex (PMd) and primary motor cortex (M1), has been debated. Early anatomical 51 

studies positioned M1 below PMd in the cortical hierarchy, interpreting PMd to M1 projections as 52 

descending feedback (Felleman and Van Essen, 1991; Shipp, 2005) . Recent dual anterograde tract -53 

tracing studies  (Ninomiya et al. , 2019)  have shown layer -specific connectivity patterns between 54 

PMd and M1. In particular, PMd projections terminate predominantly in the superficial layers of 55 

M1, whereas M1 projections target both superficial and deep layers of PMd, suggesting a higher 56 

position for M1. These findings highlight the limitations of inferring motor hierarchy solely from 57 

anatomical connectivity. 58 

Another line of work suggests that intrinsic temporal properties of neuronal activity may 59 

provide an alternative functional marker of cortical hierarchy  (Murray et al. , 2014) . Intrinsic 60

timescales refer to the temporal window over which neuronal activity integrates information and 61 

are typically estimated from the decay constant (τ or tau) of the autocorrelation of single -neuron 62 

spiking activity (SUA). Across the brain, timescales exhibit a hierarchical gradient. At the cortical 63 

level, they increased from sensory areas to higher -order regions like prefrontal cortex  (Murray et 64 

al. , 2014; Chaudhuri et al. , 2015; Cavanagh, Hunt and Kennerley, 2020; Wang, 2021) , whereas at 65 

the subcortical level, the input structures of the basal ganglia exhibited longer timescales than a 66 

downstream nucleus  (Nougaret et al. , 2021) . Longer timescales support sustained information 67 

integration required for cognitive processes including working memory, decision -making, and 68 

motor planning  (Cirillo  et al. , 2018; Wasmuht  et al. , 2018; Spitmaan  et al. , 2020; Golesorkhi  et 69 

al. , 2021; Manea  et al. , 2022; Zeraati  et al. , 2023; Trepka  et al. , 2024) . Importantly such temporal 70 

organization across cortical areas has been shown across species  (Zeisler et al. , 2025) , and using 71 

different signal modalities, including spiking activity (SUA), intracranial electroencephalography 72 

(iEEG) recordings  (Cusinato et al. , 2023) , electrocorticography (ECoG)  (Gao et al. , 2020) , and 73 

functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)  (Manea et al. , 2022) .74 

Timescales have not been systematically used to probe relative organization within the 75 

motor system (see , however  (Morales -Gregorio et al. , 2025) ). Moreover, whether any laminar 76 

differences in temporal dynamics exist within a cortical area remains unknown . One theoretical 77 

proposal suggests that timescales should differ across layers, with longer timescales for neurons 78 

in superficial layers due to their enhanced recurrent connections (Cavanagh, Hunt and Kennerley, 79 

2020) . However, such laminar predictions have not been directly tested using single -neuron and 80 

LFP autocorrelations, both of which are direct measures of neuronal (population) timescales. A 81 

recent study  (Halgren et al. , 2021)  reported that the LFP aperiodic spectral exponents ( exp )82 

decrease with cortical depth. This result was interpreted as evidence for longer timescales in 83

Voytek, 2017) , in which larger spectral exponents indicate longer timescales. However,  (Cusinato 85 

et al. , 2023)  directly examined the relationship between timescales and aperiodic spectral 86 

exponents in iEEG data, and reported that lower exponents were associated with longer 87 

autocorrelation -derived timescales across regions, consistent with an inverse relationship between 88 

exponent and timescales. 89 

Here, we addressed three questions: (1) Do intrinsic timescales differ between M1 and 90 

PMd, and if so, what would these differences suggest about their relative hierarchical position? (2) 91 

Do timescales differ between superficial and deep layers? and (3) Do neurons with different 92 

timescales have different encoding properties during motor planning and execution? To answer 93 

these questions, we analyzed acute laminar recordings of SUA and bipolar LFPs from two male 94 

macaques performing a delayed -match -to -sample (DMS) arm -reaching task. First, we found that 95 

SUA -derived timescales were longer and LFP aperiodic exponents smaller in M1 than in PMd, 96 

suggesting a higher position for M1 in the cortical hierarchy. Second, LFP -derived measures 97 

exhibited systematic layer -dependent differences consistent with longer timescales in deep layers. 98 

In contrast, SUA timescales did not differ across layers. Third, neurons with longer tau exhibited 99 

more stable representations of movement direction during planning and execution. 100

# Results 102 

We investigated intrinsic temporal dynamics in PMd and M1 of two macaques performing a DMS 103 

arm -reaching task (see Fig. 1A). To maintain comparable task structure across animals while 104 

reducing task demands, Monkey T performed sessions using a reduced set of three target positions, 105 

randomly selected on each session, whereas Monkey M performed the full set of four target 106 

positions. Neural activity (SUA and LFP) was recorded using acute laminar probes and single -tip 107 

electrodes (see Fig. 1B). For SUA, timescales (decay constant tau) were estimated using the 108 

autocorrelation method described by  (Fontanier et al. , 2022) . Larger tau values indicate slower 109 

decay of temporal correlations and longer temporal integration (see Fig. 1C/D). For LFPs, bipolar 110 

derivations were computed across cortical depth to obtain local signal specificity in each channel 111 

(see Fig. 1E). While previous work has linked the aperiodic spectral knee to intrinsic timescales 112 

(Gao et al. , 2020) , reliable knee estimation was obtained in only ~23% of channels in our dataset. 113 

We therefore adopted two complementary LFP -based approaches . First, LFP timescales were 114 

estimated from signal autocorrelation (ACF) using the timescales toolbox from Voytek and 115 

colleagues ( (Cellier et al. , 2025) , timescales toolbox ; see Fig. 1F - bottom). Second, ape riodic 116 

exponents have been proposed to reflect underlying E/I balance and have been linked to timescales 117 

(Gao, Peterson and Voytek, 2017; Halgren et al. , 2021; Cusinato et al. , 2023) . Spectral exponents 118 

were estimated using FOOOF method (fitting oscillations and one over f,  (Donoghue et al. , 2020) 119 

; fixed mode) in the 55 -95 Hz range to capture the linear region of the spectrum, while avoiding 120 

contamination from knee frequencies (up to ~45 Hz). This enabled us to obtain robust estimates 121 

of spectral decay ( exp ) across all channels (Fig 1F - top). 122

Cortical organization based on temporal dynamics between M1 and PMd 123 

Recording sites were classified as PMd or M1 based on a functional categorization. In a recent 124 

study using this dataset  (Nougaret et al. , 2024) , we described that a low beta band rhythm is 125 

dominant in posterior (M1) LFPs and reflects movement preparation and postural dynamics, while 126 

a high beta band rhythm is dominant in anterior (PMd) LFPs and reflects spatiotemporal attention. 127 

This clear functional organization was used to delineate a functional border between M1 and PMd 128 

at the transition between low and high beta dominance (see Fig. 1B). 129 

Timescales have been proposed as a marker of cortical hierarchy across brain areas. To test 130 

whether such organization is present within the motor cortex, we compared spike and LFP 131 

timescales (tau) and LFP aperiodic spectral structure ( exp ) between these functionally defined 132 

PMd and M1 areas. SUA timescales were longer in M1 than in PMd, see Fig. 2A (top). All 133 

statistical results are shown in Table 1. At the level of individual monkeys, this difference also 134 

reached significance in monkey M (see Supplementary Table 1). In contrast, LFP autocorrelation 135 

timescales did not differ between M1 and PMd (Fig. 2B (top)), though a marginal difference 136 

emerged if considering only distinct task epochs (preGo epoch, see control analyses below). 137 

However, LFP aperiodic spectral exponents were significantly lower in M1 than in PMd (Fig. 2C, 138

To determine whether these PMd -M1 differences depended on task -related neural activity, 142 

we performed several control analyses. Previous studies have shown that although timescales can 143 

vary with behavioral demands, their relative ordering across cortical regions remains stable 144 

(Manea et al. , 2023) . We therefore estimated timescales from two task epochs: preSC1, before 145 

directional information was provided with the cue, and preGo, while directional information was 146 

actively being maintained in working memory (see Materials and Methods). We additionally 147 

utilised the SUA to estimate tau from the full -trial epoch in correct trials only, thus excluding error 148 

trials, longer pauses in behavior and the intertrial intervals. Control analyses confirmed that both 149 

spike - and LFP -derived measures across task epochs were correlated (all p < 0.0001; see 150 

Supplementary Fig . 1D -F and Supplementary Fig 2C -D), supporting the robustness of these 151 

measures. Consistent with the main analysis, the relative PMd/M1 temporal organization was 152 

preserved across epochs, with M1 generally exhibiting longer timescales and lower aperiodic 153 

exponents than PMd. These differences reached significance for spike -derived tau during the 154 

preSC1 baseline epoch (p = 0.01), for LFP -tau during the preGo epoch (p = 0.03), and for aperiodic 155 

exponents across all epoch comparisons (all p < 0.01; Supplementary Fig . 1A -C; Supplementary 156 

Fig . 2A -B). 157 

Together, these results indicate a temporal gradient across the motor cortex, with longer 158 

SUA timescales and flatter aperiodic exponents in M1 relative to PMd, that is robust across task 159 

epochs. 160

# Discussion 234 

Here, using a multimodal approach combining spiking activity and LFPs, we investigated the 235 

hierarchical organization of motor cortical areas and layers through the lens of timescales. We 236 

report three main findings. First, both neural timescales and LFP spectral exponents suggested that 237 

M1 is placed above PMd in the temporal hierarchy. Second, at laminar resolution, LFP -derived 238 

measures showed consistent layer -dependent differences. Spectral exponents were significantly 239 

smaller in deep than superficial layers in both M1 and PMd, while LFP -derived autocorrelation 240 

timescales were longer in deep layers in M1. In contrast, spiking activity did not show any laminar 241 

differences in temporal dynamics. Third, probing the functional relevance, we found that neurons 242 

with longer tau exhibited more stable representations of memorized movement direction during 243 

the planning phase in PMd and during execution in both areas. 244 

Timescales have been proposed as an intrinsic organizational principle of the brain. Prior 245 

work has shown that estimation of tau from different signal types often converge on similar 246 

hierarchical ordering across cortical areas. Our simultaneous observations across SUA and LFP ,247 

in two areas and layers, suggest that tau from different signals captures distinct aspects of neural 248 

population activity. Neural timescales derived from single unit activity reflect the duration over 249 

which the firing activity of individual neurons remains correlated over time. The timescales 250 

obtained from autocorrelation of LFP signals capture the temporal persistence of population -level 251 

activity, where longer timescales reflect more sustained, slow -varying fluctuations within the local 252 

circuit. Aperiodic exponents quantify the slope of scale -free activity, which has been proposed to 253 

reflect, amongst other factors, the excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance within local populations. 254 

Larger exponents correspond to a steeper suppression of higher frequency power. The inverse 255 

relation observed between timescales and aperiodic exponents suggests that the mechanisms 256

Together, these three measures provided complementary windows into the temporal 261 

organization of the motor cortex, both across areas and within the laminar microcircuit. 262 

Cortical hierarchy between PMd and M1 in light of temporal dynamics 263 

The concept of anatomical hierarchy is well established in sensory systems, where laminar 264 

projection patterns define the direction of information flow. Feedforward projections terminate in 265 

layer IV to provide driving input to higher -order areas, whereas feedback projections avoid layer 266 

IV and exert modulatory influences on lower areas. In contrast, the motor cortex lacks a distinct 267 

granular layer IV, complicating hierarchical inference based solely on laminar projections. 268 

Functionally, M1 appears to occupy the terminal stage of the motor hierarchy, as it provides the 269 

densest corticospinal output and low -intensity stimulation readily evokes movements, which 270 

would predict a feedforward projection from PMd to M1. However, available anatomical evidence 271 

presents a paradox - projections from PMd to M1 display feedback -like termination patterns, 272 

suggesting a modulatory rather than purely driving influence (Shipp, 2005) . A more recent dual 273 

anterograde tracing study  (Ninomiya et al. , 2019)  demonstrated that PMd projections terminate 274 

primarily in deeper portions of L2/3 and weakly in layer 1 of M1, while M1 sends projections back 275 

to superficial and deep layers of PMd, supporting an ascending influence from PMd to M1 and 276 

placing M1 higher in the motor cortical hierarchy. Similarly  (Barbas and García -Cabezas, 2015) 277 

proposed that M1 is the most structurally specialized motor area, exhibiting extensive myelination, 278 

restricted long -range connections, and elaborate cortical architecture, whereas premotor areas 279

Timescales that quantify the temporal window over which neurons sustain information, 283

Using this temporal framework, we found that M1 neurons exhibit longer timescales than PMd, 285 

supporting its hierarchical placement above PMd, consistent with anatomical evidence from 286 

anterograde tracing studies  (Ninomiya et al. , 2019) . At the population level, timescales have also 287 

been estimated from LFP signals  (Gao et al. , 2020) . Although identifying a knee frequency in the 288 

power spectrum was feasible in only a subset of our channels, alternative estimates based on LFP 289 

autocorrelation and spectral decay yielded complementary measures of temporal dynamics across 290 

areas. Aperiodic spectral exponents were significantly smaller in M1 than PMd. This pattern aligns 291 

with recent intracranial iEEG findings showing that spectral exponents flatten as timescales 292 

increase along sensory hierarchies  (Cusinato et al. , 2023) , thus corroborating a higher position in 293 

the hierarchy for M1 compared to PMd. However, LFP autocorrelation timescales did not differ 294 

significantly between the two areas, suggesting that spectral scaling and temporal persistence of 295 

population fluctuations may reflect dissociable aspects of local circuit dynamics. 296

engaged, and preGo, when directional information had to be actively maintained in working 301 

memory during motor planning. Despite the markedly different cognitive demands of these 302

Modality -dependent laminar organization of temporal dynamics 318 

Neural timescales exhibit substantial heterogeneity within cortical areas, reflecting diversity in 319 

local microcircuit dynamics. Such variability is thought to support flexible computation, allowing 320 

neural populations to simultaneously process fast -changing inputs and integrate information over 321 

longer temporal windows  (Wasmuht et al. , 2018; Cavanagh, Hunt and Kennerley, 2020; Stern, 322 

Istrate and Mazzucato, 2023) . Theoretically, part of this diversity has been proposed to be 323 

expressed also across layers, with longer timescales for neurons in superficial layers due to their 324

In the motor cortex, this question is further complicated by the absence of a clearly defined 327

cortical columns rather than strict laminar segregation. Recordings in PMd  (Opris et al. , 2011; 332 

Opris, 2013)  introduced the concept of functional ‘minicolumns’, demonstrating stronger 333 

synchronization between superficial (L2/3) and deep (L5) neurons within the same column than 334 

across adjacent columns. More recently, another study  (López -Galdo et al. , 2025)  proposed that 335

representations over time. Consistent with this framework, we did not observe significant laminar 340

LFP -derived aperiodic exponents were furthermore systematically smaller in deep relative 352

(Gao, Peterson and Voytek, 2017; Ameen et al. , 2025) . In that framework, stronger inhibitory 356 

activity, associated with slower, longer -lasting synaptic currents, is predicted to increase the 357 

temporal autocorrelation structure of population activity (longer timescales), resulting in a steeper 358 

spectral slope (larger exponent). Conversely, relatively stronger excitation is associated with flatter 359 

spectra (smaller exponent) and reduced temporal correlations (i.e. shorter timescales). However, 360 

(Halgren et al. , 2021)  did not directly estimate timescales from LFP autocorrelation, leaving this 361 

interpretation untested. Our direct measurements stand in contrast to this interpretation. Smaller 362 

exponents in deep layers co -occurred with longer LFP autocorrelation timescales, consistent with 363 

the empirical finding that aperiodic exponents inversely mirror autocorrelation -based timescales 364 

across brain regions  (Cusinato et al. , 2023) . Together, these LFP -derived measures converge on 365 

the interpretation that deep layers exhibit longer temporal integration at the population level. These 366 

findings diverged from theoretical predictions of longer timescales in superficial layers (Cavanagh, 367 

Hunt and Kennerley, 2020) , and suggest that spectral slope alone may not provide a reliable proxy 368 

for temporal persistence. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly estimate timescales 369 

across cortical layers using multiple complementary signals and measures. 370

recording site, whereas LFP signals primarily arise from synaptic inputs and dendritic processing, 374 

largely dominated by excitatory post -synaptic potentials. In layers L5/L6, pyramidal neurons 375 

extend apical dendrites toward superficial layers, spanning much of the cortical column. Bipolar 376 

LFP signals at a particular depth will integrate synaptic inputs predominately at that depth, possibly 377 

highlighting laminar differences in temporal dynamics of apical/distal and basal/proximal 378 

dendrites at the population level that remain invisible at the level of individual neuron spiking 379

Timescales shape temporal dynamics of movement direction encoding during 381 

planning and execution 382 

Timescales are believed to structure the temporal dynamics of neural coding, with long -383 

tau neurons supporting stable representations and short -tau neurons supporting more transient 384 

ones. Evidence from the prefrontal cortex links longer timescales to sustained working memory 385 

representations  (Murray et al. , 2014; Cavanagh et al. , 2016, 2018) , whereas  (Cirillo et al. , 2018) 386 

reported that longer -tau neurons in PMd showed stronger response selectivity during a delay period 387 

in which monkeys were preparing their future spatial response. In our study, we examined two 388 

motor cortical areas, PMd and M1 , which are associated with movement planning and execution. 389 

Although these regions are often considered motor output areas, numerous studies have shown that 390 

PMd, in particular, plays an active role in movement planning and decision formation prior to 391 

movement initiation  (Confais  et al. , 2012; Nougaret  et al. , 2024; López -Galdo  et al. , 2025) . Our 392

Behavioral setup and task 449 

A detailed description of the task design and data collection procedures can be found in a previous 450 

study  (Nougaret et al. , 2024) . Two monkeys were trained to perform a visuomotor delayed -match -451 

to - sample (DMS) task with fixed cue order and a Go signal, using their right arm. They performed 452 

arm -reaching movements in one of four diagonal directions from a central starting position. The 453

presented on a vertical computer monitor (LCD; 75Hz) positioned in front of them, at 57 cm (Fig. 455 

1A). 456 

At the start of each trial, the display showed the handle’s position (small white square, 0.4 457 

cm radius), a central fixation spot (yellow flashing disc, 0.45 cm radius), and four possible 458 

peripheral targets (red circular outlines, 1.5 cm radius) located 9 cm diagonally from the center. 459 

The monkey initiated a trial by positioning the cursor within the central fixation spot. Once the 460 

cursor was correctly placed, the fixation spot stopped flashing (though it remained visible), 461 

accompanied by a 50 ms tone. After maintaining the central hold for 1000 ms, a selection cue 462 

(SEL) appeared on the screen for 300 ms. The SEL was a large polygon (~3 cm radius) in one of 463 

three colors (blue, green, or pink), indicating the color to attend to, for that trial. Following a 1000 464 

ms delay, three spatial cues (SC1, SC2, SC3) were presented sequentially, each for 300 ms and 465 

each followed by a 1000 ms delay. These SCs were small colored discs (0.9 cm radius), always 466 

presented in the temporal order blue -green -pink, and each could appear in any of the four 467

Preprocessing and PSD computation of bipolar LFP 675 

The detailed preprocessing of the raw signals are mentioned in an earlier study  (Nougaret et al. ,676 

2024) . The raw neural signals were processed offline to extract the local field potential (LFP). 677 

First, a zero -phase 4th -order Butterworth filter with a 250 Hz cut -off frequency was applied (using 678 

the butter and filtfilt functions in MATLAB) to low -pass filter the data. The LFP was then high -679 

pass filtered at 1Hz, and power -line noise was removed using a 4th -order Butterworth band -stop 680 

filter (49.5 - 50.5 Hz). The filtered signals were then downsampled to 1 kHz and saved for further 681 

analysis. To ensure data quality, trials containing noticeable artifacts, such as those caused by teeth 682 

grinding, static electricity, or heartbeat signals, were identified through visual inspection and 683 

excluded (12.3% of all trials in monkey T and 5.1% in monkey M). 684 

LFPs represent the low -frequency components of neural signals recorded by electrodes, 685 

reflecting the summed activity of a population of neurons. Since LFPs are a population measure, 686 

they can often capture shared fluctuations across nearby electrodes, particularly in laminar probes 687 

where contacts are in proximity. Such shared modulations can arise from common synaptic inputs 688 

to the recorded area or from volume conduction effects, reflecting non -local neural activity. To 689 

isolate locally generated activity, we applied a bipolar derivation approach, a referencing method 690 

that estimates local signals by subtracting the potentials recorded at two adjacent electrodes 691 

referenced to a common ground. Since our recordings included probes with inter -contact spacings 692 

of 100, 150, or 200 μm, we first interpolated the signals from the 150 μm probes to obtain a 693 

uniform 100 μm depth resolution before computing bipolar derivations. For these interpolations, 694 

the signal at each intermediate depth was estimated as a weighted sum of the two adjacent 695
