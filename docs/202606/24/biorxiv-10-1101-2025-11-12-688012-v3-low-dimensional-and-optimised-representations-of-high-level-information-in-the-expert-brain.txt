Low-Dimensional and Optimised Representations of High-Level Information in the Expert Brain 

Andrea I. Costantino 1,2,* , Artem Platonov 1, Felipe Fontana Vieira 1,3 , Emily Van Hove 1,2 , Merim Bilali´ c4, Hans Op de Beeck 1,2

> What transforms a novice into an expert? Decades of research show that expertise relies on domain-specific knowledge, but a neural account of this transformation has remained fragmentary: we lack an understanding of what information expert representations encode, how they are structured for efficient use, and where in the brain they reside. Using chess as a model system, we combine neuroimaging with multivariate pattern analysis to reveal three principles of the expert brain. Expertise drives a shift in representational content, from surface visual features to high-level, relational information. It is accompanied by a structural change to low-dimensional, optimised representation: codes become more compact and better organised, yet retain the detail needed for precise evaluation. Finally, the representational load shifts from sensory-specific cortices to domain-general frontoparietal networks. The expert brain packs more into less, concentrating richer knowledge into fewer, better-organised representations that support the rapid, flexible decisions of mastery.

Introduction 1

What makes a novice into an expert? This longstanding question sits at 2

the intersection of neuroscience, cognitive science, and artificial intelli-3

gence 1–4 . Across diverse domains such as mathematics 5,6 , language 7,4

motor skills 8–10 , and board games 11–13 , extensive experience equips 5

individuals with domain-specific knowledge, which serves as the foun-6

dation for fast, accurate, and flexible decision-making 14 . Decades 7

of cognitive research have established how such knowledge supports 8

expert performance at the behavioural and computational levels. Yet, 9

we still lack a precise account of its neural basis: specifically, what 10 

information expert representations encode (the content ), how that in-11 

formation is organised for efficient readout (the structure ), and where 12 

in the brain such representations are expressed (the neural substrate ). 13 

Research on expertise has revealed that expert performance rests much 14 

more on knowledge than on raw general ability 15,16 . Years of fo-15 

cused exposure tune perception and memory to a domain’s recurring 16 

structure 3,17 . Co-occurring features are grouped into chunks 11 and 17 

richer templates 18 that bind surface cues to deeper relations and typ-18 

ical courses of action. When familiar constellations appear, they 19 

trigger these stored structures, which rapidly unpack the relevant de-20 

tails and predictions 11,17,18 , functioning like an extension of work-21 

ing memory 19 . This enables experts to quickly grasp a situation’s 22 

underlying structure while simultaneously preparing appropriate re-23 

sponses. Novices, in contrast, organise by appearances and get stuck 24 

on particulars. Expert cognition is therefore qualitatively different: 25 

recognition-driven and relational, powered by knowledge that supports 26 

both a quick recognition and fine-grained analysis when needed 20,21 .27 

Neuroimaging studies confirm that expertise involves a functional re-28 

organisation of neural resources toward domain-specific and memory-29 

based processing, which enable access to knowledge for fast, accurate 30 

decisions 20,22 . Across domains, these systems are engaged more 31 

strongly (and often bilaterally) in experts than in novices 21 . The pre-32 

cise loci vary with stimulus type, mapping where domain knowledge is 33 

stored and read out. In chess, experts preferentially recruit lateral tem-34 

poral and parietal regions for object identities and functions but invoke 35 

scene/navigation systems for pattern recognition 23–27 . Radiological 36 

expertise, with its holistic processing at its core, is characterised by 37 

ventral occipito-temporal selectivity 28,29 . In higher-level calculation, 38 

professional mathematicians engage a bilateral frontoparietal-temporal 39 

network 5,30 , while mental calculators draw on episodic memory com-40 

ponents in the temporal lobe 31 . When computation is externalised, as 41 

with the abacus, experts rely on visuospatial and premotor simulation 42 

circuits related to instrument manipulation 32 . Finally, in motor exper-43 

tise, a premotor-parietal action-observation network (AON) allows for 44 

efficient anticipation from kinematic cues 8,22 .45 

These studies mostly rely on univariate contrasts. They highlight 46 

regions with increased activation but do not reveal the structure or 47 

content of the encoded information. We know which regions are more 48 

active in experts, but not how they represent domain-specific knowl-49 

edge. To close that gap, we move from localisation to representational 50 

geometry, investigating how domain knowledge is represented. Ad- 51 

dressing these questions requires analytical methods that go beyond 52 

region-wise activation. 53 

Multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA)–including decoding and repre- 54 

sentational similarity analysis (RSA)–offers a framework for probing 55 

the informational content and structure of neural activity 33 . These 56 

methods allow us to quantify not just whether two conditions elicit 57 

different responses, but also how stimuli are internally represented and 58 

organised in representational space 34–36 . While MVPA methods have 59 

been used with success in vision 35,37,38 , language 39,40 , and memory 60 

> 41,42

, they remain underused in research on expertise. 61 

Here, we apply these methods to the domain of chess, a model system 62 

in cognitive science and artificial intelligence 43,44 , with objective skill 63 

rating (Elo) and well-controlled stimuli that jointly engage low-level 64 

perceptual processing (board layout, piece identity) and high-level 65 

cognitive operations (strategy, planning, goal evaluation). We combine 66 

behavioural measures with univariate fMRI, MVPA (decoding/RSA), 67 

and manifold-based dimensionality analyses (Fig. 1). We then ask not 68 

only where experts and novices show different average activations, 69 

but also what information expert representations encode (content), 70 

how that information is organised for efficient readout (structure), and 71 

where in the brain these codes are expressed (neural substrate). This 72 

representational approach moves beyond localisation or activation- 73 

based accounts by characterising the internal geometry of neural codes 74 

rather than their magnitude alone. 75 

Crucially, the answers to these questions are not predetermined by 76 

existing theory or data. Expert knowledge could either expand or com- 77 

press neural codes: richer distinctions among board states could yield 78 

higher-dimensional representations, while abstraction over shared re- 79 

lational structure could collapse them onto fewer dimensions. Chunk 80 

and template theories describe content enrichment without prescribing 81 

a specific geometry 11,17,18 .. The representational locus could remain 82 

in domain-specific cortex, as prior neuroimaging has consistently 83 

localised expertise effects to category-selective regions (fusiform, tem- 84 

poral, parahippocampal) 20,21,45 . And if expert representations do 85 

compress, that compression could sacrifice fine-grained detail for 86 

speed, producing fast but coarse evaluation. Whereas classical exper- 87 

tise theories specify the shift from perceptual to relational content, the 88 

present study asks how that shift is neurally realised: whether exper- 89 

tise compresses or expands neural codes, whether its locus shifts from 90 

domain-specific to domain-general systems, and whether compression 91 

preserves or discards the detail that expert performance requires. 92 

Using this multilevel framework, we reveal three principles. First, 93 

expert representations prioritise relational, goal-relevant informa- 94 

tion over surface features (what/content). Second, they are more 95 

compact but still easier to distinguish because they preserve fine 96 

detail (how/structure). Third, expert representations are expressed 97 

most strongly across domain-general frontoparietal control systems 98 

(where/substrate). These properties provide a representational account 99 

of expertise: compressed, separable codes that retain decision-critical 100 

details and enable rapid, flexible performance. By clarifying how rich 101

Results 105 

We compared twenty long-time rated tournament chess players (ex-106 

perts) with twenty chess-literate hobby players (novices). Both experts 107 

and novices performed a domain-specific 1-back task during fMRI: 108 

on each trial they judged which of the two most recent boards was 109 

more strategically advantageous for White, pressing one of two buttons 110 

(two-alternative forced choice; Fig. 2D for procedure, for more details 111 

see Methods). 112 

The stimulus set was built so that the same boards could be grouped 113 

under three complementary labels (Fig. 2A): (i) perceptual (visually 114 

matched pairs that share surface layout and piece identities), (ii) strat-115 

egy (recurring relational motifs such as queen-rook ladder mates or 116 

bishop-forcing patterns), and (iii) checkmate (checkmate vs. non-117 

checkmate). By “relational” we mean properties defined over relations 118 

among pieces and legal move constraints that realise a goal (e.g., at-119 

tack/defence structure and forced move sequences that constitute a 120 

mate); “strategy” refers to the specific relational motif implementing 121 

that goal. In contrast, “perceptual” similarity refers to surface lay-122 

out/identity irrespective of whether the legal relations yield a solution. 123 

One could think of perceptual as “the board looks the same,” while 124 

strategy/relational as “the same plan and constraints apply,” even if 125 

pieces are shifted to nearby squares (e.g., a queen–rook ladder plan 126 

still works one file over). 127 

To dissociate these sources of similarity, each checkmate had a per-128 

ceptually matched non-checkmate counterpart created by a minimal 129 

intervention, typically repositioning one or two pieces, that disables 130 

the mating line (Fig. 2B, red circles). Checkmate/non-checkmate pairs 131 

are perceptually alike but relationally distinct, whereas boards within 132 

the same strategy share a relational motif regardless of surface details. 133 

In other words, strategy pairs preserve the same attack–defence rela- 134 

tions by design, while checkmate pairs deliberately break exactly one 135 

critical relation (e.g., an extra flight square/defender) to flip checkmate 136 

to non-checkmate while holding appearance constant. 137 

These categorical labels yield theoretical representational dissimilarity 138 

matrices (RDMs) that specify our model predictions for behavioural 139 

and neural geometry (Fig. 2C): low dissimilarity for same-label pairs 140 

within each model (perceptual, strategy, checkmate) and higher dis- 141 

similarity otherwise. Variance partitioning confirms that the three 142 

RDMs are largely orthogonal, each capturing a distinct dimension of 143 

board similarity (Supplementary Sec. S1). We use these models to 144 

test what information is represented via behavioural RSA and fMRI 145 

RSA/decoding. 146 

Our analyses follow three questions (Fig. 1). We first ask what infor- 147 

mation is represented by aligning behavioural RDMs (from 1-back 148 

value preferences) and neural RDMs (multivoxel pattern distances) 149 

with model RDMs for perceptual, strategy, and checkmate struc- 150 

ture (Fig. 2C). We then ask how these representations are organised– 151 

quantifying effective dimensionality with the participation ratio and 152 

testing readout (linear separability) with multivariate decoding. Fi- 153 

nally, we ask where these signatures reside by combining ROI and 154 

searchlight analyses with meta-analytic network and term maps to 155 

situate effects within broader functional systems. 156

What Is Represented: Perceptual to Relational Content 157 

Behavioural representational geometry. We first characterise the 158 

representational geometry of participants’ choices during the fMRI 159 

task by conducting a behavioural representational similarity analy-160 

sis (RSA). For that purpose, we used participants’ responses in the 161 

1-back chess evaluation task, in which they judged which of the two 162 

most recent boards was more advantageous for White. These choices 163 

implicitly reflect the dimensions guiding participants’ internal repre-164 

sentations of chessboard value. We computed behavioural RDMs for 165 

each group, derived from pairwise stimulus choices (see Section 4.7.1). 166 

We then tested whether these behavioural RDMs were significantly 167 

correlated with model RDMs reflecting three candidate dimensions of 168 

board similarity: visual similarity , checkmate status , and strategy (Fig. 169 

2). 170 

Expert directional preference matrices and the derived behavioural 171 

RDMs exhibit a clear block structure by Checkmate, with sub-structure 172 

by Strategy (Fig. 3A; orange vs. blue axis labels; saturation indicates 173 

strategy). The selection frequencies further echo this structure: experts 174 

show systematic preferences for checkmates and particular strategic 175 

motifs rather than flat, uniform counts (Fig. 3C). The MDS embedding 176 

mirrors these patterns: expert configurations show partial separation 177 

of Checkmate (orange) from Non-checkmate (blue) boards, indicating 178 

an organised preference geometry (Fig. 3B). 179 

In contrast, novices’ preferences are not significantly predicted by 180 

any of the tested model dimensions. Novices’ directional matrices 181 

and behavioural RDMs lack the coherent block structure observed in 182 

experts (Fig. 3A), and selection counts are flatter and less differentiated 183 

by checkmate status across stimuli (Fig. 3C). The MDS for novices 184 

is diffuse, with no clear separation between checkmates and their 185 

matched non-checkmates (Fig. 3B). 186

RSA reveals strong expert-novice differences for dominant dimensions 294 

such as strategy and checkmate status, but this method is inherently 295 

limited to capturing features that drive the overall representational ge- 296 

ometry 46 . To assess whether subtler chess stimuli properties are also 297 

encoded, we applied multivariate decoding across eight dimensions 298 

(e.g., number of moves to checkmate, board difficulty; see Supple- 299 

mentary Sec. S3). RSA detected robust group differences in multiple 300 

ROIs for 3 dimensions, partial effects for 3 more, and none for the 301 

remaining 2. In contrast, decoding revealed expert advantages for 7 302 

of 8 dimensions across widespread regions. These findings indicate 303 

that expertise supports a rich, sparser and more expressive representa- 304 

tional space, where both dominant task-relevant properties and finer 305 

distinctions coexist. 306

Where Information Is Encoded: Domain-General Networks 396 

To investigate which cognitive domains are preferentially engaged in 397 

chess experts, and how these domains relate to the representational 398 

changes that we observed, we examined the spatial correspondence 399 

between group-level brain activity and representational patterns and 400 

meta-analytic maps derived from Neurosynth. Specifically, we as- 401 

sessed the degree to which Experts and Novices recruited predefined 402 

functional networks by correlating group-level z-statistic maps with 403 

seven term-based meta-analytic association maps: Working Memory , 404 

Memory Retrieval , Navigation , Language Network , Face Recognition , 405 

Object Recognition , and Early Visual . These networks include both 406 

domain-general processes–most notably working memory and mem- 407 

ory retrieval–and more domain-specific or perceptual systems that 408 

have been implicated in prior studies of visual and cognitive expertise 409 

(see Sec. 4.8.1 for a more detailed rationale). 410 

We applied this correlation framework to both univariate second-level 411 

t-maps and multivariate RSA maps, offering complementary perspec- 412 

tives on how chess expertise modulates brain function. The univariate 413 

analysis captures differences in overall activation strength, while the 414 

RSA-based analysis reflects differences in local representational ge- 415 

ometry (see Sec. 4.8) across three model-based stimulus dimensions: 416 

Checkmate, Strategy, and Visual Similarity. This procedure allowed 417 

us to characterise how differences in activation patterns and represen- 418 

tational geometries align with canonical functional networks, thereby 419 

bridging the gap between computational and cognitive changes ob- 420 

served in expertise. 421

Discussion 541 

How does extensive learning reshape neural representations? Rather 542 

than merely identifying where expertise resides in the brain, we asked 543 

how extensive training transforms the internal structure of neural codes 544 

and what (which content) is encoded in the expert brain. Using chess 545 

as a controlled, theory-rich domain, our findings reveal expertise- 546 

driven transformations in the content, structure, and neural substrates 547 

of representations, characterised by optimisation along high-level, 548 

goal-relevant dimensions. We interpret these transformations as a 549 

form of network-code inversion , providing a unified account that inte- 550 

grates classic cognitive theories, efficient coding, and modern manifold 551 

theory. 552 

Experts prioritised relational content over surface features. Be- 553 

haviourally, expert choices were more reliable (Supplementary 554 

Sec. S2) and better predicted by high-level, relational properties of the 555 

stimuli (Fig. 3). In-scanner board preference profiles reinforce this 556 

dissociation: within visually matched pairs, experts strongly preferred 557 

checkmate over non-checkmate boards, whereas novices preferred 558 

both at similar rates, with their choices governed by shared perceptual 559 

features (Supplementary Sec. S2). This divergence was mirrored in 560 

neural geometry: representational geometry in experts aligned more 561 

strongly with the same relational models in frontal and parietal cor- 562 

tices, whereas novices showed weaker or absent alignment (Fig. 4). 563 

The behavioural and neural results indicate a qualitative shift in what is 564 

encoded with expertise: away from appearance and toward high-level, 565 

relational properties. Rather than encoding perceptual properties, ex- 566 

pert representational resources are allocated to behaviourally relevant 567 

dimensions (in line with efficient coding principles 48 ) to prioritise 568 

relational structure (in line with chunking and template theories 17,43 ). 569 

A perceptual-to-relational feature gradient analysis further confirmed 570 

this dissociation, with the vast majority of explained variance in expert 571 

preferences attributable to relational rather than perceptual features 572 

(Supplementary Fig. 3). 573

they do not specify the form or brain basis of this shift. Our re-576 

sults show that the shift is realised through low-dimensional, detail-577 

preserving codes in domain-general frontoparietal systems. This adds 578 

neural and computational precision to existing theories. 579 

Expertise alters not only what information is represented, but also how 580 

that information is structured in the brain. Using participation ratio 581 

as an effective dimensionality estimate, we found that expert neural 582 

codes were significantly more compressed across visual, parietal, and 583 

prefrontal regions compared to novices (Fig. 6; Sec. 2.2). Yet this 584 

compression did not entail information loss: regions with the largest 585 

drop supported the highest decoding of high-level, relational features 586 

(Supplementary Sec. S3). Fine-grained analyses showed reliable classi-587 

fication of subtle properties even within lower-dimensional manifolds 588 

(Supplementary Sec. S3). These findings suggest that compression is 589 

not incidental but instrumental in expertise: a representational strat-590 

egy that improves separability, reduces noise, and enhances access 591 

to task-relevant features. By putting most of the useful signal into 592 

the few dimensions that matter, expertise yields representations that 593 

are paradoxically both more compact and more separable. A similar 594 

“dimensionality paradox” has recently been documented in short-term 595 

task learning, where practice simultaneously increases separability 596 

along task-relevant axes and compresses along irrelevant ones 49 . Our 597 

findings suggest that this dual optimisation, initially observed over 598 

days of laboratory training, crystallises into a stable representational 599 

regime after years of real-world expertise. 600 

This pattern matches manifold views of population coding and 601 

efficient-coding principles: learning rotates and compresses the rep-602 

resentational space so that most variance lies along task-relevant di-603 

mensions, making behaviourally important states easier to separate 604 

while dampening nuisance variability 48,50–52 . Just as the efficient 605 

coding principles predicted that V1 neurons would develop Gabor-like 606 

receptive fields optimised for natural image statistics 53,54 , in cognitive 607 

domains like chess, a similar logic would lead to a representational ba-608 

sis that captures key structural properties of board configurations. The 609 

result is a flexible geometry that supports rapid readout, abstraction, 610 

and generalisation. It reflects a reorganisation from high-dimensional, 611 

entangled spaces to low-dimensional, task-tuned manifolds aligned 612 

with task demands. 613 

Despite being more compact, expert representations retained fine-614 

grained, task-specific detail. In focused analyses of checkmate po-615 

sitions (Supplementary Sec. S3), expert activity patterns reliably en-616 

coded subtle features (e.g., tactical motif, moves-to-mate) even though 617 

these properties did not dominate the global similarity structure (Sup-618 

plementary Fig. 7). These variables were linearly decodable, indicat-619 

ing that detail is embedded in a sparse, multiplexed format: a few 620 

dominant dimensions capture abstract, global structure, while smaller, 621 

partly independent subspaces carry tactical motifs. 622 

This organisation aligns with mixed-selectivity and factorised-coding 623 

accounts 50,52 , and proves particularly helpful in resolving the breadth-624 

depth trade-off in expertise: global dimensions support rapid evalua-625 

tion, while local subspaces preserve detail for deeper inference. Put 626 

differently, it illustrates a pragmatic paradox of expertise–more is 627 

packed into less, yet the details that matter remain readily accessible. 628 

Meta-analytic spatial correlations indicated that expertise reshapes 629 

both the engagement and representational role of large-scale brain 630 

systems (Sec. 4.8). Experts’ activation patterns showed stronger in-631 

volvement of domain-general networks (working memory, navigation, 632 

retrieval–chiefly frontoparietal) whereas novices aligned more with 633 

sensory and language-related systems (face/object regions, early vi-634 

sual) (Fig. 7). Pattern-similarity analyses mirrored this divergence: in 635 

experts, domain-general systems carried high-level relational informa-636 

tion, while in novices, task-relevant information remained anchored 637 

in domain-specific networks (Fig. 8). If stronger frontoparietal in-638 

volvement merely reflected greater control demand, one would expect 639 

it to be more pronounced in novices, for whom the task was harder. 640 

Instead, novices aligned more strongly with perceptual and language- 641 

related systems, whereas the frontoparietal signature tracked expert 642 

relational coding. This frontoparietal locus converges with recent evi- 643 

dence that practice reshapes task representations toward conjunctive, 644 

task-tailored codes 49,55 . The present findings extend this picture by 645 

showing that, in established expertise, the cortical systems carrying 646 

these restructured codes are domain-general frontoparietal networks 647 

that encode domain-specific relational content in a compressed, multi- 648 

plexed format, a level of representational specificity that has not been 649 

characterised in practice-based paradigms. 650 

This divergence likely reflects distinct cognitive and computational 651 

strategies. Experts appear to transition from a “lazy” learning regime, 652 

characterised by generic, sensory-bound coding, to a “rich” learn- 653 

ing regime, where domain-general networks are actively reshaped 654 

into optimised, task-aligned representational systems 56 . This richer 655 

representational regime need not replace the stronger engagement of 656 

domain-specific cortex observed in experts 20–22 . Rather, improved 657 

perceptual templates in ventral/temporal regions might supply cleaner 658 

evidence to domain-general systems. Extracting the most informative 659 

latent (relational) variables probably depends on precise mid-level fea- 660 

ture representations. This in turn would align with findings of greater 661 

activation in experts’ domain-specific regions 6. 662 

In this sense, the change is less a switch of areas than a reallocation 663 

of representational content: domain-general networks encode domain- 664 

specific information in a structured, compressed, behaviourally op- 665 

timised format, while domain-specific cortex continues to deliver 666 

high-quality inputs. Together with the what and how results, the where 667 

pattern shows that expertise involves a systematic reallocation and 668 

repurposing of neural resources. The end-result is a fundamental reor- 669 

ganisation in the anatomical substrates and computational architecture 670 

of expert cognition. It marks a shift from reactive, perceptual encoding 671 

to proactive, schematic reasoning. This reorganisation is based on 672 

tuned perceptual scaffolding and reconciles prior reports of higher 673 

activation in domain-specific cortex 20,22 with the present representa- 674 

tional locus in domain-general systems. These results complement 675 

recent evidence suggesting that the rostrolateral prefrontal cortex sup- 676 

ports relational categorisation during initial laboratory learning 57 . Our 677 

results reveal the stable end-state of years of real-world expertise: a 678 

coordinated reorganisation of content, structure, and locus across the 679 

entire frontoparietal system, not just a single prefrontal focus during 680 

acquisition. 681 

Several of these findings have no behavioural analogue and could not 682 

have been anticipated from existing theories of expertise. Effective 683 

dimensionality is a property of neural geometry, not of choice be- 684 

haviour. The cortical locus of representational content (frontoparietal 685 

vs. sensory) is invisible to any behavioural measure. The multiplexed 686 

structure (global compression with local detail preservation in orthogo- 687 

nal subspaces) cannot be inferred from reaction times or accuracy. The 688 

co-occurrence and mutual constraint of these properties (the regions 689 

that carry relational content are the same ones that show compres- 690 

sion and belong to domain-general networks) is what establishes the 691 

network-code inversion as a mechanistic account rather than a de- 692 

scriptive restatement of known behavioural effects. Novices, for their 693 

part, were actively engaged with the task and applied internally con- 694 

sistent evaluation strategies organised along perceptual rather than 695 

relational dimensions (Supplementary Sec. S2), confirming that the 696 

expert-novice contrast reflects a difference in representational content, 697 

not in task engagement. Moreover, estimated eye-movement patterns 698 

did not differ between groups (Supplementary Sec. S7), ruling out 699 

gaze strategy as a confound. 700

be needed. First, while we assessed robustness with cross-validated 702 

and convergent analyses (see SI), our inferences are based on cross- 703 

sectional fMRI in a single, theory-rich model system, chess. It is 704

Keeping these limitations in mind, the current findings converge on 732 

a theoretical framework for understanding how expertise changes 733 

neural representations. Across behavioural, univariate, and multivari-734

separable representations that preserve fine detail), and where it is 737

this coordinated change as a network-code inversion : novices rely on 739

apply generic perceptual and cognitive processes to the task of chess. 742 

Experts invert this pattern, recruiting domain-general systems associ-743

them to represent domain-optimised content in a compact, structured, 745 

and task-optimised format. 746 

Put simply, expertise packs more into less. This shift reflects a fun-747 

damental change in the internal representational geometry. Instead 748 

of increasing activation within existing circuits, the expert brain con-749

of the task. This system is low-dimensional and task-tuned: its domi-751

partly independent subspaces preserve the tactical detail needed for 753 

appropriate responses. 754 

This representational realignment grounds classic chunk/template ac-755

the experts. 807

Experimental Task and Design 848 

Familiarisation Task 849 

To promote recognition of the stimuli, assess participants’ ability to 850 

detect checkmate configurations, and reduce eye movements during 851 

scanning, we implemented a two-stage familiarisation procedure (Fig. 852 

2D). First, participants were asked to complete an online task hosted 853 

on Pavlovia 48 to 24 hours prior to the scanning session, in which 854 

each of the 40 chessboard stimuli was presented in randomised order. 855 

Participants were instructed to enter the correct move in standard chess 856 

notation if they identified a checkmate sequence, or to skip the board 857 

otherwise. This task, performed without time constraints, allowed us 858 

to verify participants’ understanding of the stimuli while encouraging 859 

detailed inspection of each board. 860 

On the day of the scan, participants were again shown all 40 stimuli 861 

during a 10-minute pre-scan review period. Boards were presented one 862 

by one and could be viewed freely, without any task or time pressure. 863 

This second exposure was designed to reinforce memory of the stim-864 

uli’s visual layout and strategic content, thereby minimising the need 865 

for visual search during scanning and reducing potential oculomotor 866 

artefacts. Together, these procedures ensured that participants entered 867 

the scanner with well-encoded representations of each board, allowing 868 

us to more precisely examine neural responses associated with the 869 

encoded chess-relevant properties. 870 

fMRI Task 871 

During scanning, participants performed a 1-back board evaluation 872 

task designed to engage domain-specific cognitive processes while 873 

remaining accessible to both expert and novice players. On each trial, a 874 

single chessboard (approximately 8° of visual angle) was presented for 875 

2.5 seconds, followed by a 1-second inter-stimulus interval. On every 876 

trial, participants judged which of the two most recent boards presented 877 

a more strategically advantageous position for White, indicating their 878 

choice by pressing one of two buttons (two-alternative forced choice): 879 

one for the current board and one for the previous board. The button-to-880

Regions of Interest (ROIs) 933

Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) 986 

We used representational similarity analysis (RSA) 33 to characterise 987 

the geometry of stimulus representations in both behaviour and brain. 988 

This approach allowed us to assess how neural patterns in different 989 

brain regions reflect the structure of the experimental dimensions, 990 

and whether this alignment differs across groups. Specifically, we 991 

constructed representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) at the level 992 

of individual participants and regions of interest (ROIs), and compared 993 

them to theoretical model RDMs derived from stimulus properties. 994 

Theoretical RDMs 995 

To formalise the predicted representational structure associated with 996 

each experimental condition, we constructed binary theoretical RDMs 997 

for Checkmate , Strategy , and Visual similarity . Each model RDM 998 

encoded pairwise dissimilarities between stimuli based on their cat-999 

egorical labels. For instance, in the Checkmate model, all boards 1000 

labelled as checkmate were considered similar to each other and dis-1001 

similar from all non-checkmate boards, resulting in a matrix with zeros 1002 

along within-category pairs and ones for across-category comparisons. 1003 

The same logic was applied to the Strategy and Visual similarity mod-1004 

els, where board identities were defined according to shared tactical 1005 

motifs or matched visual configurations, respectively. These RDMs 1006 

thus provide idealised predictions of how stimuli should be organ-1007 

ised in representational space if neural or behavioural responses are 1008 

structured primarily along a given dimension (see Fig. 2C). Interpret-1009 

ing alignment with a model RDM therefore reveals which stimulus 1010 

feature–relational, strategic, or perceptual–dominates the underlying 1011 

representational geometry. 1012 

Behavioural Representational Similarity Analysis 1013 

From the raw trial-level behavioural data obtained during scanning, we 1014 

first derived pairwise preference comparisons between stimuli. Each 1015 

time a participant judged one board more favorable than another, we 1016 

recorded the preferred and non-preferred stimuli. Using these pairwise 1017 

comparisons pooled across all participants within each group, we 1018 

computed two group-level matrices (one for experts, one for novices): 1019 

a directional preference matrix and a symmetric behavioural RDM 1020 

(See Fig. 3A). 1021 

The directional preference matrix captured the signed difference in 1022 

how often one board was chosen over another. Specifically, cell (i, j) 1023 

reflected the net number of times stimulus i was preferred over stimulus 1024 

j. A positive value indicated a preference for i over j, while a negative 1025 

value indicated the reverse. Because the 1-back task compares only 1026 

consecutive boards, different stimulus pairs are compared different 1027 

numbers of times depending on the randomised presentation sequence. 1028 

To prevent pairs compared more often from accumulating higher raw 1029 

counts independently of preference consistency, we normalised each 1030 

entry by the total number of comparisons for that pair: 1031 

RDM norm (i, j) = |count (i > j) − count ( j > i)|

count (i > j) + count ( j > i) (1) Values range from 0 (perfectly tied preferences) to 1 (perfectly con- 1032 

sistent preference direction); pairs with zero comparisons were set 1033 

to 0. The directional preference matrix is reported in raw counts 1034 

in Fig. 3A to convey the group-level preference structure, while the 1035 

count-normalised behavioural RDM is what we use for all subsequent 1036 

behavioural RSA analyses. 1037 

To further explore stimulus-level preference profiles, we computed 1038 

the selection frequency for each stimulus in all comparisons (Fig. 1039 

3C). Specifically, we counted how often each board was chosen as the 1040 

preferred in a pair. These frequencies were visualised as bar plots, with 1041 

the bars coloured by checkmate status and shaded by strategy identity. 1042 

This visualisation allowed us to detect stimuli that were consistently 1043 

favoured or avoided (i.e., they had a higher or lower perceived value), 1044 

regardless of the specific board they were paired with. 1045 

Finally, we assessed the degree to which behavioural preferences 1046 

aligned with theoretically motivated models of board similarity (Fig. 1047 

3D). For each theoretical RDM (checkmate, strategy, visual similarity), 1048 

we computed Pearson correlations between the lower triangles of the 1049 

group-level symmetric behavioural RDM and each model RDM. To 1050 

quantify the reliability of each correlation, we used a nonparametric 1051 

bootstrap procedure (10,000 resamples over RDM pairwise measures) 1052 

to compute 95% confidence intervals around the correlation coeffi- 1053 

cient. This procedure was repeated separately for experts and novices, 1054 

enabling direct comparisons of the extent to which each group’s pref- 1055 

erences reflected structural properties of the stimuli. All p-values were 1056 

corrected for multiple comparisons across the three model tests within 1057 

each group using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) 1058 

procedure at α = 0.05. 1059

Before computing correlations, voxels were cleaned using three criteria 1173

S1 Orthogonality Across RDMs 2

To quantify the degree of overlap and separability among representa-3

tional structures in our dataset, we first computed pairwise Spearman 4

correlations between the flattened upper triangle of each model RDM 5

(excluding the diagonal), providing a measure of shared variance in 6

representational structure. We then computed partial Spearman cor-7

relations, which estimate the relationship between two RDMs while 8

controlling for variance explained by the third. For instance, the partial 9

correlation between the Checkmate and Strategy RDMs, controlling 10 

for Visual Similarity, reflects their unique association independent of 11 

the shared structure with the Visual RDM. 12 

To further disentangle representational overlap, we further conducted a 13 

variance partitioning analysis based on nested linear regression models. 14 

Each RDM was treated in turn as a dependent variable, with the 15 

remaining two serving as predictors. We fit a full multiple linear 16 

model using both predictors to explain variance in the target RDM, 17 

and two reduced models each omitting one predictor. The unique 18 

variance explained by each predictor was calculated as the difference 19 

in R2 between the full model and the corresponding reduced model. 20 

Shared variance was defined as the portion of R2 explained by both 21 

predictors jointly, beyond their individual contributions, and residual 22 

variance captured the unexplained portion. Formally, given a target 23 

RDM Y and two predictors X1 and X2, we define: 24 

R2full = R2(Y ∼ X1 + X2)

R2reduced 1 = R2(Y ∼ X2)

R2reduced 2 = R2(Y ∼ X1)

Unique X1 = R2full − R2reduced 1

Unique X2 = R2full − R2reduced 2

Shared = R2full − Unique X1 − Unique X2

Residual = 1 − R2full 

(1) Any negative values arising from this decomposition due to noise or 25 

multicollinearity were set to zero for interpretability. This approach 26 

provides a formal quantification of the unique and shared variance 27 

attributable to each representational model, allowing us to assess the 28 

degree of orthogonality between theoretical representational geome- 29 

tries. 30 

The variance partitioning analysis revealed that the three RDMs are 31 

well-separated and largely independent (see Supplementary Fig. 1), 32 

with each predictor explaining predominantly unique rather than 33 

shared variance. This orthogonality validates the theoretical distinctive- 34 

ness of the representational structures captured by the Check , Strategy , 35 

and Visual RDMs, indicating that they reflect genuinely different as- 36 

pects of chess board representation. The predominance of unique 37 

variance over shared variance supports the use of these RDMs as 38 

independent predictors in subsequent analyses, as they capture comple- 39 

mentary rather than redundant information about stimulus similarity 40 

structure. 41

for the group difference, we evaluated ∆ = rExperts full − rNovices full across 112 

matched resamples (see Supplementary Table 3). Expert reliability 113 

was significant ( rfull = 0.459 , p = .002 , 95% CI [0.392 , 0.515 ]), while 114 

Novices did not reach significance ( rfull = 0.040 , p = .488 , 95% CI 115 

[−0.069 , 0.151 ]). The group difference was significant ( ∆rfull = 0.419 ,116 

p = .002 , 95% CI [0.288 , 0.536 ]). Between-group correlations were 117 

near zero ( rfull = −0.040 , p = .589 , 95% CI [−0.178 , 0.078 ]), indi-118 

cating that expert and novice representations are organised differently 119 

rather than reflecting a simple strengthening of a common layout. 120 

Reconciling pairwise and marginal reliability. The pairwise RDM 121 

split-half analysis shows non-significant reliability for novices ( rfull =122 

0.040 , p = .488 , 95% CI [−0.069 , 0.151 ]), suggesting that their fine-123 

grained pairwise dissimilarity structure is not consistent across individ-124 

uals. However, the marginal (board-level) split-half analysis reveals 125 

significant reliability for both groups (Experts: rfull = 0.928 , p = .002 , 126 

95% CI [0.892 , 0.950 ]; Novices: rfull = 0.565 , p = .014 , 95% CI 127 

[0.175 , 0.808 ]). These two results are not contradictory. The pairwise 128 

RDM captures the full 40 × 40 matrix of relative preferences between 129 

specific boards; because the 1-back task compares only consecutive 130 

stimuli, each specific pair is observed a limited number of times, mak- 131 

ing the pairwise structure inherently noisy. The marginal preference, 132 

by contrast, aggregates each board’s selection frequency across all its 133 

pairings, averaging out opponent-specific variability. At this coarser 134 

level, novices clearly agree: boards that share visual features with their 135 

matched counterpart are preferred at similar rates regardless of check- 136 

mate status (within-pair r = 0.86 , p < . 001 , 95% CI [0.67 , 0.94 ]), and 137 

this shared perceptual evaluation is consistent across individuals. The 138 

expert-novice difference therefore lies not in the presence or absence 139 

of a shared strategy, but in which dimension organises that strategy. 140 

The board preference feature analysis below confirms this directly: 141 

novice preferences are systematically predicted by visual complexity 142 

features (officer count, image entropy, edge density), whereas expert 143 

preferences are predicted exclusively by checkmate status. This dis- 144 

sociation between reliable marginal and unreliable pairwise structure 145 

in novices reflects a deeper property of their evaluative strategy. Ex- 146 

pert evaluation is governed by a single categorical feature (checkmate 147 

status), which determines the outcome of every head-to-head compari- 148 

son in the same way, producing consistent pairwise orderings across 149 

individuals. Novice evaluation, by contrast, depends on multiple con- 150 

tinuous visual features (officer count, image entropy, edge density) 151 

that agree on the global board ranking (busier boards are generally 152 

preferred) but can conflict on specific pairwise comparisons: two 153 

novices may weight different features when resolving a close com- 154 

parison, leading to divergent pairwise outcomes despite convergent 155 

marginal preferences. The pairwise RDM split-half is therefore more 156 

sensitive to low-dimensional, categorical strategies than to shared but 157 

higher-dimensional continuous strategies. This asymmetry parallels 158 

the neural dimensionality finding: expert neural codes are compressed 159 

onto fewer, task-relevant dimensions, while novice codes are spread 160 

across a higher-dimensional, perceptually-driven feature space. 161 

Board preference feature drivers. To identify which objective 162 

board properties drive preferences in each group, we extracted 8 fea- 163 

tures for each of the 40 boards, ordered along a gradient from purely 164 

perceptual to deeply relational: (1) image entropy (Shannon entropy of 165 

the grayscale pixel intensity histogram, H = − ∑ pi log 2 pi, 256 bins), 166 

(2) edge density (proportion of pixels exceeding the 90th percentile of 167 

Sobel gradient magnitude), (3) piece count (total pieces on the board), 168 

(4) officer count (non-pawn, non-king pieces), (5) centre occupation 169 

(pieces within the central 16 squares), (6) king advantage (opponent 170 

king exposure minus own king exposure), (7) attack advantage (white 171 

attack coverage minus black attack coverage), and (8) checkmate 172 

status (binary indicator of forced checkmate). Image-level features 173 

(1–2) were extracted from the stimulus images; board-level features 174 

(3–8) were computed from the board position via FEN notation using 175 

python-chess. 176

selection frequency were computed per group. Significance was as- 178

S3 Brain Decoding 253 

To assess whether local voxel patterns encoded linearly decodable 254 

information about chess-relevant categories, we conducted multivariate 255 

classification analyses using support vector machines (SVMs). These 256 

analyses evaluated the discriminability of activation patterns across 257 

different conditions within each region of interest (ROI), providing a 258 

functional readout of category-selective information. 259 

Classification analyses were implemented in MATLAB R2024b us- 260 

ing the CoSMoMVPA toolbox 1. As explained in Sec. 4.6, we used 261 

the unsmoothed beta estimates of the 40 conditions as the input of 262 

these analyses. For each subject and run, multi-voxel patterns were 263 

extracted from predefined ROIs, including bilateral cortical mask from 264 

the Glasser atlas 2 (See section 4.5 for more details). All analyses were 265 

performed separately for each ROI and participant. 266 

For each ROI, a linear SVM classifier ( @cosmo_classify_svm ) was 267 

trained to discriminate between condition labels derived from the 268 

experimental design. Classification targets were constructed from 269 

categorical regressors: Checkmate (2 classes: checkmate vs. non- 270 

checkmate), Strategy (10 classes: 5 checkmate-specific and 5 non- 271 

checkmate-specific strategy types), and Visual similarity (20 classes: 272 

one per matched pair). The data were partitioned using an n-fold cross- 273 

validation scheme across runs (leave-one-run-out), with balanced folds 274 

ensured via cosmo_balance_partitions . Within each fold, voxel 275 

patterns were labeled with their corresponding class and used to train 276 

and test the classifier. 277 

Decoding accuracy was defined as the mean classification performance 278 

across cross-validation folds. ROIs with fewer than 6 usable voxels 279 

were excluded to ensure minimum feature dimensionality. Accuracy 280 

scores were computed independently for each participant, regressor, 281 

and ROI. 282

means: experts trend toward higher PR than novices in Early Auditory 377 

(∆PR = + 1.87 ) and Auditory Association ( ∆PR = + 0.26 ), although 378 

neither survives FDR correction at the per-region level (Fig. 6, Sup-379 

plementary Table 8). The same anatomical inversion is recovered 380 

independently by the unsupervised PCA decomposition (Fig. 5A, bot-381 

tom): PC1 (52.6% of variance) carries near-uniform positive loadings 382 

on the visual, parietal, and lateral prefrontal regions where experts com-383 

press, while PC2 (13.1% of variance) is bipolar, with positive loadings 384 

concentrated in insular and frontal opercular cortex (the largest PC2 385 

loading, +0.49 ), auditory cortex (Early Auditory +0.43 ; Auditory 386 

Association +0.26 ), medial temporal cortex ( +0.39 ), and posterior 387 

opercular cortex ( +0.26 ). The single FDR-significant region in which 388 

experts show higher PR than novices — the insular and frontal oper-389 

cular cortex ( ∆PR = + 4.49, t(37 .3) = + 3.07 , pFDR = .015 , Cohen’s 390 

d = + 0.97 , 95% CI [+ 1.53 , +7.44 ]) — is the strongest member of 391 

this same set; auditory cortex follows the same direction at smaller 392 

magnitude. 393 

This secondary axis spans regions implicated in language and inner-394 

speech (auditory, insular, frontal opercular), memory retrieval (medial 395 

temporal), and multi-sensory integration (posterior opercular). Read-396 

ing our data conservatively, expert chessboard evaluation appears to 397 

engage these regions in a more differentiated, higher-dimensional 398 

regime — complementing the dominant compression we report in 399 

visual, parietal, and prefrontal cortex — even though the canonical 400 

chess task contains no auditory stimulation. The cognitive mechanism 401 

behind this secondary axis is not adjudicated by the present design 402 

(candidates compatible with the chess-expertise literature include ver-403 

bal labelling of strategic patterns during evaluation and multi-sensory 404 

recruitment in long-term experts), and we therefore flag it as an obser-405 

vation that warrants targeted follow-up rather than as direct evidence 406 

for any specific account. 407 

S5 Participation ratio is not predicted by ROI size 408 

To assess how regional dimensionality relates to anatomical size, we 409 

computed the participation ratio (PR; 3,4 ) within each cortical ROI 410 

for each participant. We then averaged PR values across partici- 411 

pants within each group (Experts and Non-Experts) and calculated 412 

the difference (Expert minus Non-Expert) per ROI. Finally, we vi- 413 

sualized and quantified the relationship between PR and ROI size 414 

by plotting these measures against each other and computing Pear- 415 

son correlation coefficients across the 22 cortical ROIs (see Fig. 8). 416 

PR did not significantly scale with ROI size in Experts ( r = 0.18 , 417 

p = .426 , 95% CI [−0.26 , 0.56 ]), Novices ( r = 0.33 , p = .135 , 95% CI 418 

[−0.11 , 0.66 ]), or the Expert −Novice difference ( r = −0.15, p = .506 , 419 

95% CI [−0.54 , 0.29 ]), indicating that the reported PR effects are not 420 

explained by anatomical ROI extent. 421 

S6 Skill-Gradient Analysis 422 

To assess whether the observed neural differences scale continuously 423 

with skill, we tested for graded relationships between skill measures 424 

and key neural metrics within the expert group and across all partic- 425 

ipants. For Elo analyses, we computed both Pearson and Spearman 426 

correlations. For familiarisation accuracy, we used Pearson correla- 427 

tions with neural metrics averaged across the 22 ROI groups. The 428 

“mean” neural metrics reported below were obtained by averaging each 429 

measure (PR, RSA model fit, or decoding accuracy) across the 22 430 

bilateral Glasser ROI groups before correlating with skill. We also 431 

tested correlations at the individual ROI level (22 tests per measure); 432 

these were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini- 433 

Hochberg FDR procedure at α = 0.05 . No individual ROI survived 434 

FDR correction for any measure. 435 

Elo and neural measures (experts only). Within the expert group 436 

(n = 20, Elo range: 1751–2269), Elo rating correlated with mean 437 

Checkmate RSA model fit (Pearson’s r = 0.47 , p = .039 , 95% CI 438 

[0.03 , 0.75 ]; Spearman’s ρ = 0.43, p = .061 , 95% CI [−0.02 , 0.73 ]). 439 

Ten of 22 individual ROIs showed uncorrected positive trends, but no 440 

individual ROI survived FDR correction. Neither mean participation 441 

ratio (Pearson’s r = −0.03 , p = .913 , 95% CI [−0.46 , 0.42 ]), Strategy 442 

RSA (Pearson’s r = 0.37 , p = .113 , 95% CI [−0.09 , 0.70 ]), nor de- 443 

coding accuracy showed significant Elo correlations. The restricted 444 

Elo range (518 points) limits statistical power for detecting continuous 445 

effects within this group. 446 

Familiarisation move accuracy and neural measures (all partic- 447 

ipants). Across the full sample ( n = 38 ), familiarisation move ac- 448 

curacy on the 20 checkmate boards served as a stimulus-specific 449 

skill proxy, directly indexing how well each participant could eval- 450 

uate the exact boards presented during fMRI. Move accuracy corre- 451 

lated strongly with checkmate decoding (Pearson’s r = 0.71 , pFDR < 452 

.001 , 95% CI [0.50 , 0.84 ]), strategy decoding (Pearson’s r = 0.54 , 453 

pFDR < . 001 , 95% CI [0.27 , 0.73 ]), strategy RSA (Pearson’s r = 0.59 , 454 

pFDR < . 001 , 95% CI [0.33 , 0.76 ]), checkmate RSA (Pearson’s r = 455 

0.44 , pFDR = .008 , 95% CI [0.14 , 0.67 ]), and mean participation ratio 456 

(Pearson’s r = −0.39 , pFDR = .017 , 95% CI [−0.63 , −0.08 ]; negative 457

S7 Groups cannot be inferred from estimated eye-471 

movements 472 

To investigate whether estimated eye-movement patterns differ sys-473 

tematically between chess Experts and Novices, we performed two 474 

separate decoding analyses using support vector machines (SVM). 475 

The first analysis used the estimated gaze coordinates (x, y), while the 476 

second used the corresponding displacements from the centre of the 477 

screen. These estimates were derived from functional MRI data using 478 

BidsMReye (), a BIDS-ready version of DeepMReye 5.479 

We included the full sample of 40 participants (20 Experts and 20 480 

Novices). For each participant, all available task runs were used; each 481 

run was treated as an independent sample. The input features for 482 

each sample were constructed by flattening the time series of the gaze 483 

coordinates or displacement values within a run, resulting in a subject-484 

run-level feature vector. This yielded a feature matrix X ∈ Rn×d ,485 

where n is the number of runs and d is the number of timepoints 486 

times two (for x and y) or one (for displacement). Binary class labels 487 

y ∈ { 0, 1}n indicated expertise (expert = 1, novice = 0), and subject 488 

identifiers were recorded to define cross-validation groups. 489 

We assessed classification performance using stratified group 490 

k-fold cross-validation with k = 20 folds, as implemented in 491 

StratifiedGroupKFold from scikit-learn . This procedure en-492 

sured that samples from the same participant were never split between 493 

training and test sets (i.e., we used 19 subjects per group as training 494 

set and one subject per group as test set). Folds were stratified by class 495 

to preserve expert/novice balance. 496 

Within each fold, we trained a support vector classifier with a linear 497 

kernel using a pipeline that included z-scoring of input features via 498 

StandardScaler . The model was trained on all runs from all training 499 

participants and evaluated on the runs from the held-out participants. 500 

For each test sample, we recorded the predicted class label and the 501 

posterior probability for being classified as an expert. This process 502 

was repeated for all folds, and the predicted labels and probabilities 503 

were aggregated across folds. 504 

For each participant, runs from all folds in which that participant 505 

appeared in the test set were aggregated to produce a single subject-506 

level out-of-fold accuracy. Statistical significance was assessed with 507 

a two-sided one-sample t-test on these 40 subject-level accuracies 508 

against a null hypothesis of 0.5 (chance). Pooled run-level accuracy 509 

with Wilson 95% confidence intervals is reported descriptively. Neither 510 

feature set yielded significant decoding: for gaze coordinates, mean 511 

subject-level accuracy was 0.534 (95% CI [0.475 , 0.594 ], t(39 ) = 512 

1.162 , p = .252 , Cohen’s d = 0.18 ); for displacement, 0.462 (95% 513 

CI [0.415 , 0.509 ], t(39 ) = −1.620 , p = .113 , Cohen’s d = −0.26 ). 514 

Results are reported in Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary 515 

Table 14. 516 

S8 Subcortical Representational and Decoding Analysis 517 

To test whether the expertise-related representational effects observed 518 

in cortex extend to subcortical structures, we performed an exploratory 519 

analysis using the Cole-Anticevic Brain-wide Network Partition (CAB- 520 

NP; 6). We extracted the 358 subcortical parcels from the CAB-NP 521 

CIFTI atlas, grouped them into 9 bilateral anatomical ROIs (Hippocam- 522 

pus, Amygdala, Caudate, Putamen, Pallidum, Thalamus, Nucleus Ac- 523 

cumbens, Cerebellum, Brainstem), and resampled the resulting atlas 524 

into MNI volumetric space (2 mm isotropic) using nearest-neighbour 525 

interpolation. Voxels overlapping with the cortical Glasser atlas were 526 

excluded to ensure strictly non-overlapping cortical and subcortical 527 

regions. 528 

We then applied the same multivariate pipeline used for cortical ROIs 529 

(Sec. 4.7.3 and Supplementary Sec. S3): RSA correlations with the 530 

three model RDMs (Checkmate, Strategy, Visual Similarity) and SVM 531 

decoding of the same three dimensions, with identical cross-validation, 532 

balancing, and statistical procedures. Group differences were tested 533 

with Welch’s t-tests, FDR-corrected across the 9 subcortical ROIs. 534 

No subcortical ROI showed a significant expert-novice difference in 535 

either RSA or decoding after FDR correction (Supplementary Fig. 11; 536 

smallest pFDR = .090 ). Uncorrected trends were observed in the 537 

cerebellum for checkmate decoding ( ∆M = 0.035, t(27 .7) = 2.59 , 538 

p = .015 , pFDR = .135 , Cohen’s d = 0.82, 95% CI [0.007 , 0.063 ]) and 539 

strategy decoding ( ∆M = 0.013 , t(35 .1) = 2.72 , p = .010 , pFDR = 540 

.090 , Cohen’s d = 0.86, 95% CI [0.003 , 0.023 ]), but neither survived 541 

correction for multiple comparisons. 542 

These results suggest that the expertise-related representational reor- 543 

ganisation characterised in the main analyses is predominantly cortical. 544 

The null result in subcortical regions does not rule out subcortical con- 545 

tributions to chess expertise, but it indicates that the representational 546 

effects detectable with the present sample, task, and analytical ap- 547 

proach are concentrated in cortical systems. Subcortical involvement 548 

may be more closely tied to procedural learning, memory consol- 549 

idation, or reward processing, aspects that the current task (board 550 

evaluation rather than active play) may not strongly engage. 551

S9 Stimulus Set Details 552 

This supplementary section provides a comprehensive overview of the 553 

40 chessboard stimuli used in the experiment, each labeled and visual-554 

ized in Supplementary Fig. 12 and reported in Supplementary Table 1. 555 

Stimuli were constructed to systematically vary along three key dimen-556 

sions: whether they depicted an imminent checkmate (Checkmate vs. 557 

Non-checkmate), the tactical strategy leading to the checkmate (Strat-558 

egy Type), and perceptual similarity (Visual Pairing). The chessboards 559 

shown throughout this manuscript and Supplementary Information 560 

were rendered from the stimulus positions (Forsyth–Edwards Notation; 561 

Supplementary Table 1) using the open-source chessnut piece set by 562 

Alexis Luengas, distributed under the Apache License 2.0. 563 

Checkmate boards (C/NC) depicted configurations in which a forced 564 

mate sequence for White against Black existed, resolvable in four or 565 

fewer moves. Each checkmate board (denoted “C” in the figure) was 566 

paired with a non-checkmate counterpart (“NC”) that preserved the 567 

overall structure and piece configuration but introduced a minimal 568 

intervention—typically the repositioning of one or two pieces—to neu-569 

tralize the mating sequence while maintaining close visual similarity. 570 

Strategy types (SY) were defined according to both piece involvement 571 

and underlying tactical features, reflecting qualitative distinctions 572 

in how mating sequences are structured. These include queen-rook 573 

combinations (SY1 and SY6), involving coordinated linear tactics; 574 

supported attacks with minor pieces (SY2 and SY7), where auxiliary 575 

knights or bishops play a crucial role in sustaining the queen mate; 576 

minor piece mating nets (SY3 and SY8), relying on spatial confinement 577 

by knights and bishops; bishop-driven forcing moves (SY4 and SY9), 578 

emphasizing long-range control; and one-move checkmates (SY5 and 579 

SY10), which require minimal calculation and serve as structurally 580 

simple baselines. 581 

Visual pairing (P) designates stimuli matched primarily for percep-582 

tual similarity while holding relational structure constant. For each 583 

position, we also recorded the total number of pieces, the count of 584 

legal moves, the side of the board occupied by the defending king 585 

(left = 0, right = 1), and the dominant tactical motif characterising the 586 

checkmate sequence. Where applicable, the number of moves required 587 

to reach checkmate is also noted. 588 

S10 Run-Matched Control Analysis 589 

Although the number of functional runs did not differ significantly 590 

between groups (Experts: M = 8.4, SD = 2.0; Novices: M = 9.5, 591 

SD = 1.4; Welch’s t(33 .3) = −1.94 , p = .061, Cohen’s d = −0.61 , 592 

95% CI [−2.15 , 0.05 ], Experts −Novices), novices completed slightly 593 

more runs on average. To rule out the possibility that this imbalance 594 

contributes to the reported expertise effects, we repeated the ROI- 595 

based RSA and participation ratio analyses after equalising the run 596 

distribution across groups. All expert data were preserved. For novices, 597 

8 subjects were deterministically assigned a cap of 6 runs (matching the 598 

8 experts who completed 6 runs), yielding identical run distributions 599 

in both groups ( 8 × 6 + 12 × 10 = 168 runs per group). All statistical 600 

procedures (Welch t-tests, FDR correction) were identical to the main 601 

analyses. 602 

The participation ratio results were unchanged: all 14 originally FDR- 603 

significant ROIs remained significant, and classification of experts vs. 604 

novices from dimensionality profiles remained highly accurate (ROI 605 

space: cross-validated accuracy = 80 .0% , permutation p = .0006 ; 606 

PCA-2D: cross-validated accuracy = 85 .0% , permutation p = .0001 ; 607 

stratified 5-fold CV, n = 40 independent participants, 10,000 label 608 

permutations; Supplementary Table 16). For RSA, all originally sig- 609 

nificant ROIs retained significance, and several ROIs that had been 610 

borderline in the original analysis crossed the FDR threshold once 611 

the novice data advantage was removed: three additional ROIs for 612 

the Checkmate model and two for the Strategy model (Supplemen- 613 

tary Table 15). No ROI showed the reverse pattern (significant in 614 

the original but not in the run-matched analysis). The Visual Similar- 615 

ity model, which does not differ between groups, remained entirely 616 

non-significant in both analyses (0 ROIs). This pattern is expected: 617 

because novices contributed more data to the original analysis, their 618 

within-group estimates were more precise, producing slightly lower 619 

t-statistics for the group contrast. Equalising run counts removes 620 

this advantage, yielding marginally stronger expert-novice differences. 621 

These results confirm that the original expertise effects are, if anything, 622 

conservative estimates, and that the slight run imbalance does not 623 

inflate or drive the reported findings. 624

To identify finer-grained anatomical regions showing significant differ-711

combined approach allowed us to combine the spatial specificity of 722 

the Glasser atlas, which allows for more precise estimates of local 723 

activation patterns, with the broader and more familiar anatomical 724 

nomenclature of the Harvard-Oxford atlas. 725
