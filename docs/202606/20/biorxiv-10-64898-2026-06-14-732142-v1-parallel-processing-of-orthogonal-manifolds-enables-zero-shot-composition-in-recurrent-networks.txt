Abstract 11 

Animals flexibly combine learned behaviors into novel actions without practicing their 12 

combinations, yet the computational mechanisms that enable independently acquired 13 

computations to be expressed in parallel remain unclear. Here we show that feedback 14 

geometry during learning determines whether recurrent dynamics can be recombined 15 

through zero-shot parallel composition. Using recurrent networks trained by a local 16 

predictive plasticity rule, we found that distinct feedback vectors embed independently 17 

learned computations in separable dynamical subspaces, allowing novel input combinations 18 

to co-activate these components and generate composite outputs without joint training. In 19 

contrast, aligned feedback vectors, as well as networks trained by backpropagation through 20 

time, exhibited accurate single-task performance but failed to support parallel composition, 21 

demonstrating that task acquisition and future reusability are dissociable properties of 22 

learning. A combined input evoked a single composite population trajectory, whose 23 

projections onto feedback-shaped task subspaces recovered the independently learned 24 

component dynamics. The same principle reproduced additive reach-posture geometry 25 

observed in motor cortex and generalized to higher-dimensional movement primitives. 26 

These results identify feedback geometry as a computational principle by which learning 27 

systems structure recurrent dynamics for future compositional reuse. 28

Introduction 30 

Animals exhibit remarkable behavioral flexibility, enabling them to rapidly adapt to novel 31 

situations without learning every possible behavioral contingency 1. Such flexibility requires 32

computations remain available for simultaneous expression when new combinations are 34 

encountered 2,3 . A neural circuit may acquire each component accurately yet embed them in a 35 

geometry that prevents their interference 4–7 . Task acquisition and future compositionality are 36 

therefore distinct computational properties. 37 

Population geometry offers a natural framework for understanding this distinction 8.38 

Individual neurons often exhibit multiplexed responses to different task variables 9,10 , yet 39 

population activity often represents these variables in separable or nearly orthogonal 40 

subspaces 5–7,11–14 , allowing multiple signals to coexist within a shared population. In motor 41 

cortex, for example, movement-related dynamics and posture-related activity occupy 42 

separable subspaces, so that changes in posture shift the reach manifold without disrupting 43 

its internal dynamical structure 15 . Similar forms of separable geometry have been observed 44 

for task variables 6,12,16 , contexts 17,18 , preparatory states 19 , and movement-related dynamics 20,21 

45 

across cortical areas. These findings suggest that neural manifolds may provide a substrate 46 

for flexible computation. However, most studies have characterized population geometry 47 

after learning is complete. How plasticity creates geometries that support not only individual 48 

task performance, but also future composition, remains unclear. 49 

50 

This problem is difficult to isolate in conventional models of compositionality. Many models 51 

achieve combination through modular architectures, explicit factorization, joint multitask 52 

training, baseline control, or direct exposure to combined conditions 2,3,22,23 . In these settings, 53 

the geometry needed for combination is either imposed by design or shaped by experience 54 

with the relevant combinations. Biological circuits, however, often acquire behavioral 55 

components across separate learning episodes and later encounter novel situations in which 56 

those components must be expressed together 24,25 . Moreover, compositionality is often 57 

studied in settings where components are arranged sequentially over time 2,3,26,27 , whereas 58 

many behaviors require multiple computations to be expressed in parallel 28 , as when 59 

movement goals and body posture must be represented simultaneously during reaching. 60 

Whether a recurrent circuit can support such zero-shot composition therefore depends on 61 

how independently acquired computations are embedded during learning. 62

Here we study this problem as zero-shot parallel composition, in which multiple learned 64 

computations must be expressed simultaneously within the same recurrent population. This 65 

setting isolates a general requirement for compositional learning: acquired dynamics must 66 

not only solve the tasks on which they were trained, but also remain structured so that they 67 

can be reused in novel combinations. Using recurrent networks trained by a local predictive 68 

plasticity rule, we show that task-specific feedback vectors embed independently acquired 69 

dynamics in separable subspaces that preserve them for later simultaneous expression. 70 

Combined inputs can then co-activate these feedback-shaped dynamical components and 71 

produce composite outputs without training on the combined condition. By contrast, aligned 72 

feedback vectors or standard backpropagation through time allow individual task 73 

acquisition but fail to support zero-shot composition. We establish this principle in an 74 

abstract temporal-generation task, identify a population-level mechanism based on 75 

projections onto feedback-shaped task subspaces, and show that the same mechanism 76 

reproduces motor cortex-like reach-posture geometry and extends to higher-dimensional 77 

multi-output movement-like primitives. 78

# Results 80 

Zero-shot parallel composition of independently learned primitives 81 

We first define the computational problem of zero-shot parallel composition. As an intuitive 82 

example, a dancer may learn one movement component, such as spinning, and separately 83 

learn another, such as jumping (Fig. 1A). Zero-shot parallel composition asks whether these 84 

learned components can later be expressed together, as in a spinning jump, without prior 85 

experience of that combination (Fig. 1B). 86 

More generally, we considered a recurrent network that learns two distinct computations in 87 

separate training blocks and is then tested on a novel condition requiring both computations 88 

to be performed simultaneously. Because the network is never exposed to this combined 89 

condition during training, successful performance requires the learned computations to 90 

remain reusable beyond their original training conditions. This setting differs from 91 

conventional multitask learning, in which networks may be trained directly on task 92 

combinations, and from sequential composition, where computations unfold over time 93 

rather than being expressed in parallel. 94

Feedback geometry determines zero-shot composition 101 

We reasoned that a recurrent circuit could support such composition if independently 102 

learned computations were embedded into orthogonal subspaces of population activity. 103 

Such an organization would preserve each computation while allowing multiple learned 104 

dynamics to be expressed in parallel within the same recurrent population. Building on 105 

previous work showing that distinct feedback signals can organize learned dynamics into 106 

orthogonal task subspaces 29 , we tested whether feedback geometry determines whether 107 

independently learned computations remain reusable during zero-shot composition. 108 

109 

To this end, we used a sine-wave generation task in which a recurrent network received one 110 

of two distinct constant inputs, S1 or S2 (Fig. 2A). Each input was associated with a different 111 

sinusoidal target output. The two input-output mappings were trained separately, and the 112 

network was never exposed to trials in which both inputs were presented simultaneously. 113 

Learning was implemented using a local predictive plasticity rule in which feedback signals 114 

constrained recurrent synaptic updates through a locally computed prediction error 30 . We 115 

compared two conditions: a different-feedback condition, in which each task was associated 116 

with a different feedback vector (Diff FB), and a same-feedback (Same FB) condition, in which 117 

both tasks shared an identical feedback vector. Although learning was somewhat slower in 118

Together, these results indicate that feedback geometry is crucial to determine whether 155 

independently acquired computations remain available for later composition. Distinct 156 

feedback vectors enabled the network to reuse learned computations under a novel 157 

combined input, whereas aligned feedback supported individual task learning but impaired 158 

their simultaneous expression. 159

A single composite trajectory recovers independently learned dynamics 161 

We next asked how feedback geometry enables a single recurrent network to express two 162 

independently learned computations simultaneously. If distinct feedback signals embed the 163 

corresponding computational dynamics into different subspaces of the network, each 164 

computation may remain independently executable even when the inputs are combined. To 165 

test this possibility, we defined each task-specific subspace as the leading 10 principal 166 

components of the recurrent activity evoked by the corresponding single-input condition 167 

(Methods). We then examined how network activity under the combined-input condition 168 

was represented within these task-specific subspaces. 169

In summary, population-level decomposition of the combined responses revealed the 223 

mechanism underlying zero-shot composition. Successful performance on the novel 224 

combined-input condition did not require additional training. Instead, each learned 225 

computation was preserved within a feedback-defined task subspace, and the combined 226 

response emerged through the concurrent expression and integration of these task-specific 227 

dynamical components. These results show that feedback geometry shapes recurrent activity 228 

into separable subspaces that can be co-activated during composition. 229

Feedback-guided learning reproduces additive reach-posture geometry 271 

observed in motor cortex 272 

Having established the mechanism for parallel processing in an abstract temporal-generation 273 

task, we next asked whether feedback-driven learning could account for geometric structures 274 

previously observed in motor cortex. During reaching from different initial postures, 275 

movement-related dynamics and posture-related activity occupy approximately separable 276 

subspaces, such that posture shifts the reach-related trajectories without distorting their 277 

internal dynamical structure 15 . We therefore asked whether a similar additive 278 

representational structure could emerge through separate learning of movement and posture, 279 

without exposure to their combinations. 280

We trained recurrent networks to learn movement and posture readouts in separate tasks, 281 

with each task associated with its own output and feedback signal (Fig. 4A, B). In the 282 

movement task, the network generated two-dimensional reach trajectories toward one of 283 

eight target directions. In the posture task, the network maintained one of three scalar 284 

posture outputs. During training, movement and posture inputs were never presented 285 

simultaneously, and distinct feedback vectors guided learning for the movement and posture 286 

readouts. The trained network successfully acquired both component tasks (Fig. 4C). During 287 

movement-only trials, the movement readouts generated direction-specific reach trajectories, 288 

whereas during posture-only trials, the posture readout converged to the corresponding 289 

target level while movement outputs remained near zero. 290 

291 

We then tested zero-shot composition of reaching and posture by simultaneously presenting 292 

movement and posture inputs. The network generated reach trajectories that preserved both 293 

their directional selectivity and temporal structure across all posture conditions (Fig. 4D). To 294 

examine the resulting population geometry, we visualized population activity within a 295 

feedback-defined subspace corresponding to the two movement dimensions and the posture 296 

dimension. Combined reach-posture trajectories formed approximately in parallel subspaces, 297 

in which the dynamical structure of reaching was preserved within each subspace while 298 

posture produced a uniform shift along the posture axis (Fig. 4E). This geometric 299 

arrangement qualitatively recapitulates the additive population structure observed in motor 300 

cortex, where posture and movement are represented in separable subspaces and combined 301 

with minimal interference. 302 

303 

In summary, feedback-driven learning reproduced a motor cortex-like reach-posture 304 

geometry without requiring exposure to combined reach-posture conditions during learning. 305 

The same principle that enabled zero-shot composition in the abstract sine-wave generation 306 

task also generated an experimentally observed form of additive population geometry. These 307 

results suggest that feedback geometry provides a unifying mechanism linking the 308 

acquisition of independent computations to their later composition in novel tasks. 309

354 

We next examined whether these composite outputs reflected selective recruitment of the 355 

corresponding primitive-specific subspaces. As in the previous analysis, we defined a 356 

subspace from the population activity evoked by each primitive presented alone and 357 

projected combined-input activity onto these subspaces (Supplementary Fig. 4). For every 358 

pairwise combination, activity was more strongly projected onto the subspaces 359 

corresponding to the presented primitives than onto the subspace corresponding to the 360 

absent primitive (Fig. 5D). Thus, zero-shot composite movements were accompanied by 361 

selective reuse of the learned primitive-specific dynamical subspaces. 362 

363 

In summary, feedback-driven learning enabled zero-shot composition in a multi-output 364 

setting. Novel composite outputs emerged through selective recruitment of subspaces 365 

associated with the constituent primitives, whereas subspaces corresponding to absent 366 

primitives remained weakly recruited. Therefore, the feedback-geometry principle 367 

generalized from abstract temporal patterns and reach-posture composition to the 368 

compositional reuse of coordinated dynamical primitives distributed across multiple output 369 

dimensions. 370

Discussion 372 

We show that the geometry of neural population activity reflects not only what a circuit has 373 

learned, but also how the learned computations can later be reused. Networks with matched 374 

individual task performance differed in their ability to express those tasks together, 375 

demonstrating that task acquisition and future combinability are distinct computational 376 

properties. In our model, this dissociation was controlled by learning-related feedback 377 

geometry. Distinct feedback vectors embedded task-specific dynamics into separable 378

These findings suggest a broader functional role for neural manifolds. Separable (or 383 

orthogonal) subspaces are often viewed as a mechanism for representing multiple variables 384 

within the same population while minimizing interference 14,32–34 . Our results suggest that 385 

such geometry can preserve computations as reusable dynamical components for future 386 

behavior. In successful networks, combined inputs did not trigger switching between 387 

previously learned trajectories. Instead, they generated a single population trajectory whose 388 

projections onto task-specific subspace recovered the independently learned dynamics. Thus, 389 

manifold geometry served not merely as a representational scaffold but as a substrate for 390 

compositional computation. 391

The comparison with BPTT-trained networks further supports this interpretation. Standard 393 

RNNs trained on the same individual tasks learned the component mappings accurately but 394 

generalized poorly to the combined condition 35 . Thus, successful zero-shot composition 395 

cannot be explained by task acquisition alone. Instead, it depends on how learning embeds 396 

computations within the recurrent state space. The systematic feedback-angle manipulation 397 

strengthened this conclusion (Fig.3D). Performance remained high when feedback vectors 398 

were sufficiently separated but declined as they became aligned, indicating that feedback 399 

geometry continuously controls the degree to which learned dynamics can be expressed in 400 

parallel. 401 

402 

The population-level mechanism identified here may map directly to experimentally 403 

observed neural geometry in motor cortex. During reaching from different postures, 404 

movement-related dynamics and posture-related activity occupy approximately orthogonal 405 

subspaces, allowing posture to shift the reach manifold without distorting its internal 406 

structure 15 . Our model reproduced this additive reach-posture organization despite never 407 

being trained on combined reach-and-posture conditions. This suggests that such geometry 408 

may reflect not only the statistics of experienced movements but also the structure of 409 

plasticity signals that shape circuit dynamics during learning. The same principle extends to 410

413 

These results make several experimental predictions. At the population level, tasks acquired 414 

under distinct feedback, teaching, reward, or contextual signals should become embedded 415 

in more separable subspaces than tasks acquired under shared feedback signals 37 . This 416 

separation should predict future combinability more strongly than single-task performance. 417 

At the circuit level, disrupting a task-specific feedback pathway during learning should 418 

impair later zero-shot composition without necessarily affecting performance of the 419 

individual task. More generally, the model predicts that the geometry of learning-related 420 

feedback pathways constrains which learned computations can later be jointly expressed. 421 

422 

Several limitations remain. The tasks studied here involve additive forms of parallel 423 

composition, and the networks rely on linear readouts with externally specified input 424 

combinations. More complex forms of compositional behavior, including conditional routing, 425 

multiplicative interactions, or hierarchical sequencing, may require additional mechanisms 426 

beyond the principle demonstrated here. In addition, the feedback vectors in our model were 427 

preconfigured rather than learned. Biophysically, such vectors may correspond to projection 428 

patterns from upstream areas 38–41 . Thus, our model isolates the computational consequence 429 

of feedback geometry, rather than its biological origin. How such feedback pathways are 430 

acquired or reorganized to support future combinability remains an open question. 431 

432 

Together, our results suggest that learning-related feedback signals shape recurrent 433 

dynamics in ways that determine whether independently acquired computations can later 434 

be expressed together. 435

Low-rank mean-field theory of zero-shot parallel composition 3

We developed a low-rank mean-field description of the trained recurrent network 1. Our prior 4

work has shown that each learned computation is represented by a structured low-rank 5

mode embedded in the recurrent connectivity 2.6

Our analysis relies on a Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT). Intuitively, distinct feedback 7

vectors enhance the two learned computations to be embedded in separate directions of the 8

recurrent dynamics. As a result, the network can activate one computation without 9

substantially disrupting the other. In mean-field terms, this corresponds to statistically 10 

orthogonal low-rank modes whose collective variables are approximately decoupled, 11 

allowing the zero-shot composition to drive both computations in parallel. By contrast, 12 

aligned feedback vectors embed the two computations in overlapping modes, producing off-13 

diagonal coupling between collective variables and thereby causing cross-talk during 14 

combined-input conditions. 15
