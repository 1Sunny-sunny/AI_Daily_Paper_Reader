# Visual consequences of saccades explain early cortical response dynamics during natural vision 

## Richard Schweitzer ∗,1, Olaf Dimigen 2, and Christoph Huber-Huber 1

Department of Experimental Psychology, University of Groningen, Netherlands During natural vision, each saccadic eye movement evokes a large cortical poten-tial known as the lambda response. Traditionally, lambda responses have been considered feedforward responses to new visual input arriving at fixation. In con-trast, here we show that they are best explained by the retinal shifts induced by the saccade itself. We first recorded EEG data for thousands of saccades during free viewing of natural scenes and then replayed the same retinal image shifts on a high-speed display during fixation. Temporal response dynamics were virtually identical for real saccades and replayed motion, and most strongly aligned to sac-cadic peak velocity. A computational model based only on saccade trajectories, 1/f natural-scene statistics, and known human spatiotemporal sensitivity parsi-moniously reproduced these dynamics. Our results show that saccade intervals should not be viewed as a gap in visual processing, but as a structured sensory event that shapes human cortical activity during natural viewing. 

Keywords: lambda wave; saccades; visual evoked potential; EEG; eye tracking; fixation-related potentials; replay; natural vision 

Introduction 1

In natural environments, humans incessantly make eye 2

movements. About three to four times per second, their 3

active vision triggers brain responses denoted as lambda 12 

responses. Traditionally recorded with electroencephalog-13 

raphy (EEG), they typically peak around 50-100 ms after 14 

saccade offsets at occipital sensors. First reported in the 15 

1950s (Evans, 1953; Gastaut, 1951), lambda responses 16 

have been the subject of numerous studies. There is am-17 

ple evidence that they reflect the feedforward sweep of in-18

Results 72 

Across a total of four separate experimental ses-73 

sions, twelve human observers performed a simple object-74 

reporting task (Figure 1A) while EEG and video-based 75 

eye-tracking (ET) measurements were conducted. In the 76 

first two sessions, observers freely explored a set of 1980 77 

color images retrieved from a widely used natural-scene 78 

database (Allen et al., 2022, see Stimuli for details) and, 79 

with a probability of 1/8 on each trial, were prompted 80 

to indicate whether a certain object category was either 81 

present or absent in the most recent scene. The purpose 82 

of this viewing condition, henceforth Saccade condition, 83 

was to record a large number of free-viewing saccades 84 

and to measure lambda responses during natural active 85 

vision. In the third and fourth sessions, the critical Replay 86 

viewing condition was presented, in which – during fixation 87 

– the same scenes rapidly moved across the screen sim-88 

ulating the previously recorded eye-movement trajectories 89 

for a given scene and observer (see Replay trajectories). 90 

Importantly, reproducing motion at saccadic speeds re-91 

quires presentation systems with high temporal resolution 92 

(e.g., Duyck, Wexler, Castet, & Collins, 2018; Schweitzer 93 

& Rolfs, 2020), which is why we applied a modern OLED 94 

monitor capable of variable frame rates up to 480 Hz (Fig-95 

ure 1B; see also Figure S1 for timing evaluations). A total 96 

of 143888 pairs of real and replayed saccades entered the 97 

analysis, a data set large enough to perform a highly pow- 98 

ered, direct comparison of lambda responses with moving 99 

and fixating eyes to isolate potential mechanisms specific 100 

to active vision. 101

Some important conclusions can be made at this point. 224 

Clearly, lambda responses are not specific to active sac- 225 

cades, but could be elicited by displaying the visual con- 226 

sequences of saccades to the fixating eye. Even though 227 

lambda responses following active saccades exhibit larger 228 

amplitudes, the functions by which their amplitude and es- 229 

pecially latency vary with saccade size were virtually iden- 230 

tical in active and passive viewing conditions. This sug- 231 

gests that the systematic variation of response latencies 232 

is not, as previously suggested, caused by active predic- 233 

tive processes but by bottom-up processing of the visual 234 

change. Finally, lambda responses were locked to neither 235 

saccade onset (positive slope) nor offset (negative slope) 236 

but to a time point during the saccade – providing a first 237 

intuition that their timing is related to the dynamics of sac- 238 

cadic visual consequences. 239

Discussion 447 

More than 70 years after their discovery (Evans, 1953; 448 

Gastaut, 1951), here we provide a model-based explana- 449 

tion of how lambda responses are generated. We col- 450 

lected several thousands of saccade epochs of EEG data 451 

during active free-viewing of natural images and com- 452 

pared them to an experimental condition where those sac- 453 

cades’ visual consequences were replayed during fixation. 454 

We did not only find virtually similar lambda-response dy- 455 

namics in the two conditions, but the same response dy- 456 

namics emerged from a purely early-visual computational 457 

model. Using this modeling as a catalyst, we extracted 458 

the basic principles needed for the observed lambda- 459 

response dynamics to arise and confirmed their empirical 460 

predictions, establishing the new hypothesis that lambda 461 

responses are most parsimoniously explained by the vi- 462 

sual dynamics that naturally occur as consequences of 463 

saccades. 464 

First, the dynamics of lambda responses were virtually 465 

identical in Saccade and Replay conditions, regardless of 466 

whether waveforms were computed from saccade offset- 467 

locked or onset-locked aggregates. This finding demon- 468 

strates that the observed scaling of lambda-response la- 469 

tency was in fact not a result of extra-retinal mechanisms 470 

involved in active vision, but must be linked to what is 471 

common among conditions: Bottom-up, feedforward vi- 472 

sual processing. It should be noted that, despite our best 473 

efforts to keep the retinal input as comparable as pos- 474 

sible, there is no perfect replay condition, because it is 475 

impossible to reproduce either the movement of the pre- 476

Second, measured dynamics of lambda responses, in-514 

cluding similarities and differences between viewing con-515

visual processing. The model was neither built for specif-518

al., 2025) nor fitted in any way to the data. Neither model 520 

nor simulations included every potentially important as-521 

pect of the human visual and oculomotor system. For in-522 

stance, the model did not include the SF-specific reduction 523 

of sensitivity at increasing eccentricities (e.g., Pointer & 524 

Hess, 1989) or contrast-dependent latencies of V1 simple 525 

cells (Reich et al., 2001). Moreover, simulations did not 526 

include visual structure around the presentation screen. 527 

Simulated saccade trajectories also did not include post-528 

saccadic oscillations that are prominent in video-based ET 529 

(Schweitzer & Rolfs, 2022) and have been shown to reli- 530 

ably affect visual localization judgments (Deubel & Bridge- 531

replayed saccades (Schweitzer, Doering, Seel, Raisch, & 533 

Rolfs, 2025). The model only needed human observers’ 534 

saccade dynamics, their stimulus display, and especially 535 

the psychophysically estimated spatiotemporal sensitivity 536 

profile of the human visual system (Kelly, 1979), to pro- 537 

duce the phenomenon of latency scaling of the lambda re- 538 

sponse. The computational model also qualitatively repro- 539 

duced amplitude differences between Saccade and Re- 540 

play conditions, suggesting that it would be premature to 541 

attribute this difference solely to the difference in active 542 

and passive viewing. 543 

Third, and finally, we proposed a parsimonious and 544 

visual-only explanation for the latency dynamics of lambda 545 

responses that approximated the measured latencies very 546 

closely. The core idea is that saccades introduce consid- 547 

erable velocities to the retinal image, thereby greatly mod- 548 

ulating visual spatiotemporal sensitivity. On the one hand, 549 

high SFs are rendered invisible as saccadic velocities in- 550 

troduce TFs unresolvable by the visual system (Castet, 551 

Jeanjean, & Masson, 2002; Schweitzer, Doering, et al., 552 

2025). On the other hand, low SFs are boosted as sac- 553 

cadic velocities shift them into their optimal TF range (Burr 554 

& Ross, 1982). It is fair to say that saccade-induced reti- 555

why they are considered highly important for the visual ex- 558 

traction of low-SF information (Boi, Poletti, Victor, & Rucci, 559 

2017). The larger the saccade, the higher the power of 560 

luminance modulation in the low-SF domain (Mostofi et 561 

al., 2020), and preliminary evidence confirms that larger 562 

saccades also lead to higher contrast sensitivity to low- 563 

SF stimuli, notably in both real and simulated saccades 564 

(Howard Li, Cox, Victor, & Rucci, 2025). For viewing natu- 565 

ral scenes, whose spectral content is subject to the ubiq- 566 

uitous 1/f power law (Tolhurst et al., 1992), this is critical: 567 

Larger (and thus faster) saccades are capable of boost- 568 

ing lower SFs which have higher power in the visual im- 569 

age, thus producing greater overall transients (cf. Fig- 570 

ure 3F). This mechanism, which is a mere consequence 571 

of the spatiotemporal sensitivity profile of the human vi- 572 

sual system (Kelly, 1979), explains the increase and satu- 573 

ration of lambda-response amplitudes with saccade size: 574 

Transients increase as saccadic velocities gradually en- 575 

hance low-SF bands, but also saturate as the saccadic 576 

main sequence saturates. Importantly, the strength of the 577 

saccade-induced visual transient should not only vary be- 578 

tween saccade sizes but also as a function of any given 579 

saccade’s velocity profile, that is, it should be strongest 580 

around the time of the peak velocity. In other words, 581 

as the latency of the peak velocity scales with saccade 582

saccade-induced visual transients not only enables the 637

they were found to be largely similar in real and simulated 639 

saccades. Given their reliable measurement, as well as 640 

tractability via computational modeling, lambda responses 641 

may prove a valuable tool for understanding visual pro- 642

endowing the present computational model with justified 644 

assumptions about the generation of neurophysiological 645 

signals in the brain may pave the way towards predict- 646

MEG, or intracranial techniques. 648

Appendix A Supplementary Text Evaluation of stimulus timing 1343 

To ensure that stimuli were presented correctly, we performed extensive timing tests with two photodiodes and 1344 

DB-25 (parallel port) connector wired to an Arduino Due which sampled analog input at 1000 Hz. We devised a test in 1345 

which a flickering grating, that is, with alternating low and high polarities, was presented with upon each screen refresh, 1346 

based on which we were able to make four critical assertions. First, we ascertained the correct setup of the variable 1347 

screen refresh rate set to 480 Hz. In a sample of 37,890 refreshes, the inter-flip interval (IFI) amounted to on average 1348 

2.12 ms (range [2.10, 2.34]). In other words, we achieved an effective frame rate of 472 Hz, notably without "dropping" 1349 

frames – which would occur with classic fixed refresh rates and cause at least a doubling of the IFI. Second, to verify G-1350 

sync functionality, we introduced additional wait times (1.04–6.25 ms in steps of 1.04 ms) between stimulus rendering and 1351 

flip. As wait times exceeded the nominal frame duration (i.e., 2.08 ms) IFIs increased linearly with wait times ( β = 1.00 ,1352 

t = 1251.3 , p < .001 ) while a constant delay of 0.43 ms ( β = 0.43 , t = 122.8 , p < .001 ) incurred, likely due to stimulus 1353 

preparation and drawing (Figure S1A). Clearly, neither slope ( β = 0.001 , t = 0.96 , p = .338 ) nor intercept ( β = −0.002 ,1354 

t = 0.59 , p = .553 ) were affected by stimulus polarity. This behavior is expected from G-sync’s capability of flipping 1355 

without having to wait for the monitor’s vertical retrace (Poth et al., 2018). Third, as shown in Figure S1B, photodiode 1356 

responses clearly scaled with the administered wait times, showing that IFIs determined by the timestamps returned 1357 

by the ’flip’ command corresponded to physical display durations, thereby underlining the feasibility of OLED monitors 1358 

for vision research (see also Dimigen, Badea, Simon, & Span, 2025). Fourth, we determined the video delay of the 1359 

system, which should amount to approximately one refresh cycle at a frame rate of 480 Hz, that is, 2.08 ms. Indeed, a 1360 

cross-correlation analysis of the trigger (’off’ for low polarities and ’on’ for high polarities) and photodiode signals revealed 1361 

a maximal correlation at 2 ms (Figure S1C), providing good evidence that triggers and physical displays were closely 1362 

aligned when the video delay was taken into account. 1363 

SF-specific model-response dynamics 1364 

Our modeling approach allowed looking at the saccade onset-locked latency dynamics of individual spatial 1365 

frequency (SF) channels (Figure S5A). Model response latencies in channels selective to 0.25 cycles per degree of 1366 

visual angle (cpd) hardly increased with increasing saccade duration (reference condition; 0.25 cpd: β = 0.10 , t = 1.75 ,1367 

p = .096 ), but slopes significantly increased at higher SFs (treatment-coded difference to reference; 0.5 cpd: β = 0.20 ,1368 

t = 2.46 , p = .023 ; 1.0 cpd: β = 0.71 , t = 8.69 , p < .001 ; 2.0 cpd: β = 0.82 , t = 10.10 , p < .001 ; 4.0 cpd: β = 0.76 ,1369 

t = 9.33 , p < .001 ). As a comparison, measured lambda response latencies time-locked to saccade onset increased with 1370 

saccade duration at a slope of 0.36 (cf. Figure 1G). Note that, for saccade-onset locked signals, slopes of 0 represent a 1371 

locking of lambda responses to saccade onset, whereas slopes of 1 represent a locking to saccade offset. The results 1372 

thus suggest that positive model responses of lower SF channels were driven by saccade-related visual events, whereas 1373 

channels selective to higher SF were driven by fixation-related visual events. Normalized waveforms shown in Figure S5B 1374 

confirm this notion: The low-SF (i.e., 0.25-cpd) channel showed saccade onset-locked increases in activity, while activity 1375 

decreased in the high-SF (i.e., 2.0-cpd) channel. Moreover, post-saccadic activity increases were only observed in the 1376 

high-SF channel where their onset latency clearly scaled with saccade duration. From these results it becomes evident 1377 

that SF bands were variably affected by the same saccade-induced visual shift. 1378
