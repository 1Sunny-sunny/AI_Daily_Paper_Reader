Abstract 

The ultimate goal of sensory coding is to extract and represent the cues required for adaptive motor output. This suggests that sensory codes and behavioral out-comes may align, and a variety of studies have argued that both biological and engineered sensory systems represent stimuli similarly when they play similar roles in behavior. However, the extent to which behavioral demands determine sensory coding throughout the brain is largely unknown. Here we propose that behavioral alignment is a general principle that organizes sensory representations, and we show that carefully measured behavior can predictively account for visual encoding across the entire zebrafish brain. We discover population codes that represent visual motion stimuli according to the optomotor responses elicited by them, indicating that information required for behavioral selection is explicitly encoded in sensory populations. Brain-like neuronal responses result when sensory codes are optimized for efficiency and aligned to behavior. These results provide a paradigm for understanding sensory representations through the behaviors they drive.

Adaptive behavior requires animals to perceive stimuli and flexibly select actions based 2

on what they sense in the environment. Sensory systems facilitate this process by 3

encoding various features of the stimulus through patterns of neural activity. Some 4

of these features correspond to simple physical properties of the stimulus, but more 5

abstract features could better align with behavioral outcomes (Figure 1). However, 6

there is no consensus on the specific features that sensory systems encode, let alone 7

the normative and mechanistic principles that determine the encoding. 8

One prominent perspective is that sensory coding constructs an internal repre-9

sentation that is as informative as possible about the external world [1–3]. Based on 10 

this view, the efficient coding hypothesis proposes sensory codes that are optimized 11 

to minimize the redundancy of sensory encoding or maximize the mutual information 12 

between neural responses and natural stimuli. This framework has predicted sparse 13 

and decorrelated sensory codes similar to those observed in many experimental studies 14 

[4–14]. However, the successes of efficient coding largely pertain to peripheral sensory 15 

systems. This is perhaps unsurprising considering that the brain’s primary function is 16 

behavior, and sensorimotor pathways should discard sensory information when that 17 

information is not behaviorally relevant [15, 16]. 18 

An alternate perspective supposes that sensory coding is optimized to support 19 

specific behavioral tasks [1, 17–21]. This paradigm has found much recent success by 20 

predicting brain-like representations in multiple layers of sensory processing [22–29]. 21 

For example, when deep neural networks are trained using machine-learning methods 22 

to perform image classification, the hidden layer units can linearly predict neuronal 23 

data across multiple regions of the ventral stream. During the training process of these 24 

networks, hidden layers gradually extract task-relevant features from the input and 25 

task-irrelevant information is compressed [30–32], effectively aligning the representa-26 

tions to the behavioral output. Therefore, the brain-like representations of hidden 27 

layers appear to meet particular demands of the task. 28 

In many ethological behaviors, such as hunting, escaping, and courtship, the task 29 

is sensorimotor, fundamentally requiring that sensory cues drive specific motor out-30 

puts [33, 34]. However, the extent to which the motor demands of ecological behavior 31 

predict sensory coding throughout the brain has not been investigated. One prominent 32 

behavior is the optomotor response (OMR), which stabilizes an animal’s position in its 33 

environment by generating locomotor actions that counteract optic flow from involun-34 

tary movements [35–40]. This innate behavior develops in the larval stage of zebrafish, 35 

wherein the fish is still translucent and small enough to permit cellular-resolution opti-36 

cal imaging of almost all its around 100,000 neurons [41, 42]. Moreover, neural coding 37 

of optomotor stimuli in several visual areas of the zebrafish brain has been suggested 38 

to be organized according to behavioral outcomes [43–45], a promising observation 39 

that motivates a comprehensive analysis of how sensory coding is organized at the 40 

whole-brain scale to support this critical sensorimotor behavior. 41

# 2 Results 42 

2.1 Behavioral output and sensory codes in the optomotor 43 

response of zebrafish larvae 44 

To understand sensory coding through its sensorimotor task, we first characterized 45 

the motor output of a sensorimotor behavior. In particular, we tracked larval zebrafish 46 

swimming during the optomotor response, a visuomotor behavior wherein fish gener-47 

ate locomotor actions triggered by optic flow (Figure 2a). The presented stimuli can 48 

be summarized into two categories of binocular visual motion: translational motion 49 

and rotation-like motion (Figure 2b). Those stimuli combined eye-specific monocular 50 

motion patterns, either drifting gratings or rotating windmills, into binocular ones, 51 

such that the fish could experience various stimuli and generate diverse behavioral 52 

responses. 53 

We quantified this sensory-induced behavior by how often the fish swam in each 54 

possible direction. For larval zebrafish, the motor output of OMR is discrete swim-55 

ming events called bouts (Figure 2c). Therefore, we binned swimming directions into 56 

nineteen 10 °-wide intervals and computed the bout frequency for each bin (Figure 2d). 57 

Compared to spontaneous swimming, translational visual motion elicited swimming 58 

towards the stimulus direction and suppressed that towards the opposite direction. 59 

In addition, nasal motion paired with backward motion elicited more turns facing 60 

the stimulus direction and suppressed opposite turns more effectively than tempo-61 

ral motion, consistent with previous findings [35]. However, this pattern was reversed 62 

when combined with forward motion, with nasal motion triggering fewer turns towards 63 

the stimulus direction and more turns towards the opposite direction compared to 64 

temporal motion. This observation shows that sensorimotor computation in OMR is 65 

not simply summing the contributions of monocular visual motion. 66 

We identified sensory neurons as those that responded reliably across trials to at 67 

least one visual stimulus. Using whole-brain calcium imaging, we extracted regions of 68 

interest (ROIs) and their fluorescence traces from 12 fish with expressing GCaMP6s 69 

[46] in nearly all neurons (Figure 2e-h). Sensory ROIs were distributed broadly across 70 

the brain (Figure 2g,h; Extended Data Figures 1,2) and were anatomically distinct 71 

from motor-related neurons that correlated with individual bouts (Extended Data 72 

Figure 3). Consistent with previous studies, sensory ROIs in optic tectum were mostly 73 

tuned to motion along body axis, whereas those in pretectum and anterior hindbrain 74 

were more tuned to leftward or rightward motion [35, 43]. The sensory responses of all 75 

selected ROIs are summarized in Figure 2i, sorted based on their preferred directions. 76

responses 78 

A major goal of sensorimotor computation is to format sensory signals in a way that 79 

motor systems can read out to generate behavior [1, 17, 18]. This suggests that sensory 80 

signals close to motor output might encode sensory stimuli similarly to their impact 81 

on behavior. We thus asked whether we could interpret the sensory codes measured 82 

through the zebrafish brain in terms of the measured patterns of stimulus-evoked 83

We first learned a group of behavioral elements using sparse dictionary learning on 86

extracted behavioral elements, we examined each element’s weight profile over pre-93 

sented visual stimuli (Figure 3c). These elements had interpretable structure across 94 

the stimulus set: left vs. right motion (1 and 6), translational vs. rotational motion (2 95 

and 5), clockwise vs. counterclockwise rotation (3), and backward vs. lateral motion 96 

(4). In addition, we computed the cosine similarity between each behavioral element 97 

and the direction-specific bout frequencies (Figure 3d). This revealed that these ele-98

turning from a motor perspective. 100 

Most ROIs needed multiple behavioral elements to fully explain their activity. To 101 

see this, we first tested whether single ROI responses were aligned with a single behav-102 

ioral element. For each ROI, we projected its response vector onto each behavioral 103 

element (Figure 3e). The component of the sensory response along each behavioral 104 

element quantified how much of the sensory response could be explained by the behav-105 

ioral element. We found that a single behavioral element was usually not enough to 106 

explain single-ROI responses (Figure 3f). However, the set of behavioral elements as 107 

a whole could accurately reconstruct these single-ROI responses (Figure 3g; Extended 108 

Data Figure 4). Thus, the response level of a single ROI reflects a combination of mul-109 

tiple behavioral elements, rather than uniquely representing one element. A related 110 

analysis based upon principal components analysis produced similar results (Extended 111 

Data Figure 4), but the behavioral basis functions produced by principal component 112 

analysis were less interpretable and less aligned to single ROI activity. 113 

2.3 Behavior-aligned stimulus representations in sensory 114 

populations 115 

Having shown that the behavioral elements largely explain sensory activity, we next 116 

asked a stronger question: are stimulus representations aligned between sensory popu-117 

lations and behavioral output? If sensory codes align with behavior, then populations 118 

of sensory neurons should represent stimuli similarly when they produce similar behav-119 

iors [47]. To illustrate this point, we first considered neural network models where 120 

raw sensory inputs are transformed into behavioral outputs through multiple feedfor-121 

ward layers of neural representation. We focused on deep linear neural networks (i.e., 122 

multilayer perceptrons wherein all activations are the identity function), because the 123 

representational structure of each hidden layer can be understood comprehensively 124 

using analytical methods [48] (see Methods). When we trained a deep linear network 125 

to transform random sensory cues into the behavioral responses (Figure 4b), we found 126

To explore whether this concept can explain the experimental data, we developed 132 

a quantitative framework to determine if the pair-wise stimulus correlations of sen-133 

sory population codes match behavior. This framework is similar to representational 134 

similarity analysis [47], but it differs by quantifying the alignment between a given 135 

correlation matrix and the observed data using the log-likelihood of the data under 136 

a Gaussian model with zero mean and covariance defined by the specified correla-137 

tion matrix (Figure 4a, Methods). For instance, if we define the Gaussian distribution 138 

with stimulus correlations from the behavioral response, then a sensory population 139 

with high likelihood would exhibit similar neural responses to stimuli that elicit sim-140 

ilar behavioral responses, and vice versa. This framework successfully captured the 141 

layer-by-layer transformations observed in our deep linear neural network model, with 142 

deeper layers exhibiting higher log-likelihood as their correlation matrices became 143 

aligned to the output (Figure 4d). 144 

We therefore proceeded to apply our method to the experimental data. As above, 145 

we used the stimulus correlation matrix of the behavioral responses (Figure 5a) to 146 

define the Gaussian distribution in the proposed framework. We then estimated like-147 

lihoods for sensory populations across twelve anatomical brain regions that contain 148 

the most sensory neurons [49]. Among these regions, the estimated likelihood was sig-149 

nificantly higher in the pretectum, habenula, tegmentum, and the reticular formation 150 

in rhombomeres 1-2 (Figure 5b, Extended Data Table 1). Sensory populations in the 151 

hindbrain showed especially strong behavioral alignment in terms of the estimated 152 

likelihood. Visualizing the stimulus correlation matrices also supported these findings, 153 

as these five regions exhibit stimulus correlation patterns closely matching those of 154 

the behavioral responses (Figure 5c,d). In contrast, the optic tectum and torus semi-155 

circularis exhibited strong monocular patterns, while the thalamus, hypothalamus, 156 

and posterior tuberculum did not display clearly interpretable patterns (Extended 157 

Data Figure 6). These sensory populations still encode visual motion stimuli and may 158 

support other behaviors that are guided by optic flow information. Overall, these 159 

results indicate that specific sensory populations encode behavioral responses, and 160 

that similarities in behavior are encoded in similarities in sensory responses. 161 

2.4 Efficient coding of stimuli driving behavior 162 

We next asked what principles are needed to predict single ROI responses. Given 163 

that sensory population codes exhibit stimulus correlations similar to the behavioral 164 

output, we assumed that the neural activity could be transformed into the behavioral 165 

responses through a linear readout (Figure 6a). Moreover, we modeled the readout 166 

weight matrix as random and Gaussian to preserve the stimulus correlations across 167 

the transformation (Supplementary information). Many representations are consistent 168 

with these assumptions, and, following the many successes of efficient coding theory, 169 

we hypothesized that biology would use an efficient representation that minimizes 170

can be understood in term of their behavioral alignment. 193 

# 3 Discussion 194 

The present study demonstrated that the zebrafish visual system encodes stimuli in 195 

a manner aligned with the behavioral responses they elicit. Our study provides a 196 

conceptual framework for understanding recent work suggesting that sensory neurons 197 

in the zebrafish respond to physically diverse stimuli that trigger similar directed 198 

locomotion [35, 43, 44, 50]. It also provides a new perspective on the widespread 199 

encoding of valence in diverse brains [51–53], as attractive stimuli drive approach 200 

behaviors and aversive stimuli drive avoidance behaviors. How do animals benefit from 201 

these behavior-aligned sensory codes? One potential benefit is to avoid mistakes in 202 

action selection. Since stimuli that drive distinct actions are well separated as sensory 203 

codes, even when environmental or internal noise distorts sensory representations, the 204 

noise is unlikely to result in large behavioral differences. 205 

Behavior-aligned sensory coding may provide a new normative principle of sensory 206 

encoding. Here we presented a proof-of-concept optimization model that could predict 207 

single-neuron activity patterns by hypothesizing that the population code is efficient, 208 

non-negative, and aligned to behavior (Figure 6). This normative model was not fit 209 

to neural data, suggesting that the model captures critical behavioral objectives and 210 

mechanistic constraints shaping neural computation. Previous normative models of 211 

sensory coding based on efficient coding predict that sensory codes should adapt to 212 

changing sensory statistics of the animal’s environment [54–57]. The behavior-aligned 213

making tasks, sensory representations of task-relevant stimuli changed with behavioral 216 

performance [58–60], and such changes did not arise from passive exposure to the 217 

stimuli alone [61, 62]. These results might reflect the alignment of learning-shaped 218 

sensory representations and stimulus-driven behavior. 219 

Our proposed method to quantify the behavioral alignment of sensory population 220 

codes could be extended in important ways. Our method utilized the behavioral cor-221 

relation among the stimuli to model a probability distribution of behavior-aligned 222 

representations and assessed population codes based on their likelihood. This likeli-223 

hood measurement conceptualizes the observed neural activity as samples from an 224 

explicit generative model, and the resulting comparisons are closely related to rep-225 

resentational similarity analysis (RSA) [47, 63]. For instance, our method measures 226 

the alignment of global structures, as also captured by RSA [64–66]. However, our 227 

likelihood-based framework also allows us to consider alternate generative models and 228 

could enable flexible extensions to model alternative response distributions and noise 229 

correlation between neurons. For example, Fig. 6 shows a non-Gaussian generative 230 

model that explained single neuron activity better than its Gaussian alternative, and 231 

it would be interesting to extend our method to this case and future improved models. 232 

Although this study focused on a simple sensorimotor behavior, the proposed 233 

framework has the potential to extend to more complex and flexible behaviors. While 234 

the importance of behavior in shaping sensory codes has been recognized previously 235 

[19–22], we went further by carefully measuring the animal behavior to explain sen-236 

sory activity based upon it. Moreover, we found that coarse behavioral measures 237 

could obscure important details of the code (Extended Data Figures 9, 10). Going 238 

forward, recent advances in behavioral quantification could allow for richer descrip-239 

tions of behavior across the animal’s natural behavior repertoire [67–69], and the 240 

motor commands generating it might provide a particularly insightful representation 241 

that quantifies how ecological motor behaviors are read out from sensory codes [70]. 242 

We therefore anticipate that more comprehensive investigations of animal behavior 243 

and motor control will deepen our understanding of sensory systems, and future work 244 

should map sensorimotor circuits based upon the gradual or abrupt emergence of 245 

behaviorally aligned representations across the the cell types and brain regions that 246 

transform sensation into behavior. 247

All experiments were performed with 6–8 days post-fertilization (dpf) zebrafish larvae 261

4.2 Experimental details 268 

4.2.1 Free-swimming behavioral experiments 269 

Fish were placed in a 15 cm shallow glass dish (Duran group, watch glass, nr. 216-0281) 270 

on a clear acrylic support covered with a diffusive screen and illuminated from below 271 

using an array of IR LEDs. Freely swimming larvae were monitored using a high-speed 272 

camera (Ximea) running at 180-200 fps, equipped with a 8 mm lens (Edmund Optics) 273 

and an 830 nm long pass filter (Edmund Optics). The visual stimuli were displayed 274 

from below using a microprojector (Asus P2E or optoma 1050ST) and a cold mirror 275 

(Edmund Optics). An open-source Python software, Stytra [73], was used to track fish 276 

swim dynamics and to generate the closed-loop optic flow motion. The visual stimuli 277 

were presented below on a 10 cm by 10 cm screen, covering the 10 cm diameter circle 278 

of the dish that was filled with water. The grating patterns were either parallel or 279 

perpendicular to the body axis of fish, depending on the stimulus, and moved at 10 280 

mm per second with the spatial period of 1 cm. The windmill patterns were centered 281 

on the fish fish, had 12 arms and moved at 36 degrees per second. To independently 282 

stimulate each eye, a 1.25-cm-width black bar was presented below the fish. The whole 283 

set of visual stimuli was presented 40 repetitions, in a different random order each 284 

repetition. Each trial of one stimulus contained 5 s of a static pattern and 10 s of 285 

motion. During the presentation of all visual stimuli, 3-min pauses were added (after 286 

10, 20, 30 repetitions) to record spontaneous swimming. The extracted position and 287 

orientation were further analyzed with custom-written software in Python. 288 

4.2.2 Whole-brain imaging experiments 289 

Animals were embedded in 2.2% low-melting-point agarose (Thermo Fisher) in a cus-290 

tomized lightsheet chamber with two glass coverslips sealed on the side and a square 291 

of transparent acrylic on the bottom for behavioral tracking. The chamber was filled 292 

with water from the fish facility system and agarose around the tail was removed to 293 

enable unconstrained tail motion. To record whole-brain neural activity, a custom-built 294 

lightsheet microscope was used [74]. A 488 nm wavelength laser source (modulated 295 

laser diodes; Cobolt) was used to produce a pair of approximately 1.5-mm laser beams 296 

with two Galvo scanners (Thorlab) at 500 Hz. The two laser beams were separately 297 

conveyed through the glass windows on the fish chamber. A custom-written Python 298

4.3 Data analysis 307 

4.3.1 ROI segmentation 308 

ROIs and their fluorescence traces were extracted from the raw 4D image data using 309 

the Suite2P package [76]. A detailed introduction of the algorithms used in this package 310 

can be found in [76, 77]. Compared to the default setting, we made the following 311 

changes to make it suitable for one-photon lightsheet imaging data – subpixel: 5; 312 

max overlap: 0.95; high pass: 60; nbinned: 4000; sparse mode: False; diameter: 6. After 313 

the initial ROI extraction, we did not use the morphology-based ROI filtering in 314 

Suite2p. Instead, we discarded the ROIs with their aspect ratios larger than 1.25 315 

and with their sizes larger than 350 pixels or smaller than 50 pixels. In addition, 316 

all ROIs outside the brain were removed. These steps helped remove non-cell ROIs 317 

that were artifactually generated. The raw traces of ROIs were preprocessed by a 318 

linear detrending to compensate drifting and a low-pass filter of 0.5 Hz to remove the 319 

scanning laser beam artifact. 320 

4.3.2 Brain registration 321 

All images were registered to the Zebrafish Brain Browser reference [49] using an 322 

open-source Python package, ANTsPy [78] (https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTsPy). 323 

The registration included three steps. First, we manually labeled around 30 points 324 

in both brain stacks to compute an initial affine transformation. The labeled points 325 

were selected across the brain and around clear structures such as the border between 326 

midbrain and hindbrain, the anterior commissure in sub-pallium, and the shell of 327 

interpeduncular nucleus. We then used the TRSAA method to perform an automatic 328 

affine transformation for refinement. Two parameters were changed from the default 329 

setting – aff sampling: 5; flow sigma:0. Finally we used the SyNOnly method to perform 330 

diffeomorphic transformation with the following parameter changes – syn samping: 331 

20; flow sigma: 1; reg iterations: [20,15,10]. The brain region labels were also adopted 332 

from the anatomical atlas of [49]. 333

vector for each ROI was also normalized by its L2 norm to equalize the weight of ROIs 364 

in the population analysis. 365 

4.3.4 Behavioral quantification 366 

Since the swimming behavior of larval zebrafish can be separated into discrete 367 

swimming bouts, we used an open-source Python package [80] (https://github.com/ 368 

portugueslab/bouter), to extract the bouts based on the swimming velocity. The bouts 369 

extracted from the freely swimming behavior were categorized into nineteen uniformly 370 

spaced direction categories from -95 ° to 95 ° . Therefore, the bout frequency was com-371 

puted as the number of bouts within each 10 ° bin divided by the motion duration of 372 

each visual stimulus, with both bouts and motion periods discarded when the fish was 373 

within 1 cm of the dish border. 374 

The behavioral response matrix, M , was defined by the bout directions (19 rows) 375 

under 14 stimuli (14 columns). We first preprocessed the raw bout-frequency data to 376 

reduce noise. For the self-symmetric stimuli (FF, BB, dark), the bout frequency was 377 

averaged over each pair of symmetric turning directions (e.g., -10 ° and 10 ° ). Except 378 

the self-symmetric stimuli, for a pair of mirrored stimuli (e.g., FR and LF, LL and 379 

RR), the bout frequency was averaged over each pair of symmetric turning directions 380 

(e.g. -10 ° in FR/LL and 10 ° in LF/RR). We then subtracted the spontaneous bout 381
