Perception of visual time is transiently distorted around saccadic eye movements, yet the 26 

neuronal mechanisms constructing perisaccadic representations of time remain unclear. Here, 27 

we investigate how perisaccadic temporal information is encoded and read out in extrastriate 28 

cortex, by combining electrophysiological recordings in V4 and MT neuronal population in 29 

macaque monkeys under high spatiotemporal resolution visual stimulation, and a statistical 30 

modeling framework capturing perisaccadic response modulations at single-trial precision. Our 31 

analyses show that perisaccadic neuronal responses systematically shift the temporal 32 

representation of presaccadic stimuli within receptive fields –biasing them toward earlier times, 33 

and also reduce temporal sensitivity for presaccadic stimuli –impairing discrimination of stimulus 34 

onset times. Model-based readout using time-varying spatiotemporal sensitivity maps in neuronal 35 

ensembles enables quantitative characterization of these effects and identifies their specific 36 

neuronal response components at millisecond resolution. In silico manipulations further 37 

demonstrate a causal role of representational bias in reducing temporal sensitivity. These findings 38 

suggest that extrastriate cortex implements an active encoding strategy to stabilize presaccadic 39 

temporal information by favoring the most recent reliable input, revealing a fundamental tradeoff 40 

between temporal precision and robustness that supports a continuous visual percept across 41 

saccades. This finding also establishes a general role for extrastriate populations in constructing 42 

the perception of visual time. 43

## Introduction 44 

Time perception, the cognitive capacity to quantify duration, is a ubiquitous function essential for 45 

motor control and decision-making in humans and nonhuman primates (NHPs) 1–5. Behavioral 46 

studies demonstrate that subjective time is susceptible to contextual factors, attention, and 47 

predictability 6,7 . Neural investigations suggest that temporal processing is implemented across 48 

widely distributed networks. In NHPs, areas such as the Lateral Intraparietal area (LIP) exhibit 49 

ramping activity encoding elapsed time and reflecting evolving temporal judgments and spatial 50 

attention allocation 8,9 . Additionally, studies in humans using transcranial magnetic stimulation 51 

confirm a causal involvement of visual cortices V1 and the middle temporal area (MT) in both the 52 

encoding and working memory maintenance of temporal information in the hundreds of 53 

milliseconds range 10 .54 

Actions, such as movement preparation or performing manual tasks, have been shown to 55 

significantly influence our subjective perception of time 11,12 . Saccadic eye movements, which are 56 

rapid shifts in gaze, are a prime example of actions that induce temporal distortions. These quick 57 

ballistic movements are crucial for directing our gaze to areas of interest, allowing us to focus on 58 

different parts of a scene multiple times per second 13 –15 . Studies have shown that visual 59 

perception of brief stimuli, presented close to the time of a saccade, can be distorted in several 60 

ways 16 –18 . One well-documented distortion is mislocalization, a change in the perceived location 61 

of a perisaccadic stimulus 19 –25 . Temporal distortions also occur around saccades, including 62 

phenomena of order inversion 26 –28 , temporal compression (underestimation of stimulus duration 63 

or interval) 16,18,29 –34 , and chronostasis (overestimation of stimulus duration or interval) 35 –40 .64 

These perceptual distortions are accompanied by alterations in visual responses around the time 65 

of saccades 41 . Studies showed that receptive fields (RFs) actively shift perisaccadically 42 –44 , a 66 

possible neural substrate for mislocalization. Furthermore, visual sensitivity decreases across 67

To examine the link between perisaccadic visual responses and time perception, our research 75 

utilizes a model-based approach. We use a time-varying extension of the classical generalized 76

designed to precisely characterize the dynamics of visual information encoding on the millisecond 79 

timescale, enabling temporally-precise tracing of perisaccadic sensitivity changes from neural 80 

data. Classically, the perisaccadic stimulus-response relationship has been studied using limited 81 

variations in the times and locations of visual stimuli preselected by the experimenters. However, 82 

the prevalence of reported effects often varies across studies. These discrepancies likely arise 83 

from experimental paradigms limited by coarse sampling of space or time, which fail to capture 84 

the continuous dynamics of the perisaccadic transition. To alleviate the subjectivity of this 85 

approach, we present white noise probes with high temporal precision and covering a wide range 86 

of space, and use statistical frameworks to provide an unbiased assessment of the computations 87 

underlying the neuron’s response generation. The model, fit to electrophysiological data, provides 88 

a mathematical description of the computations that the neuron applies on a stimulus to generate 89 

its response, often referred to as the neuron’s processing “kernels”, which characterize the 90

modulations, we fit the GLM-based model described above 53,55 to the recorded neuronal 100

time of presaccadic stimuli toward earlier times. The observed temporal bias suggest that stimuli 108

extending the most recent stable presaccadic information up to just before the saccade onset. 110

## Results 120 

Characterizing the spatiotemporal sensitivity of extrastriate neurons across saccades 121 

To investigate temporal processing across saccadic eye movements, we conducted 122 

electrophysiological recordings of single-unit activity from neurons in the extrastriate visual areas 123 

V4 and MT of macaque monkeys. Across 108 recording sessions, we obtained data from 300 MT 124 

and 147 V4 neurons in four adult male rhesus macaques. Neural activity was recorded using 16-125 

channel linear array electrodes, and spikes were sorted offline. The monkeys performed a visually 126 

guided saccade task designed to probe perisaccadic visual responses (Fig. 1a). Before, during, 127 

and after a visually guided saccade, brief visual probe stimuli (white squares, 0.5 ×0.5 degrees of 128 

visual angle (dva); 7 ms duration) were presented within a 9 ×9 grid of spatial locations covering 129 

the fixation point (FP), saccade target (ST), and the neurons’ estimated RFs both before and after 130 

the saccade. The probe grid dimensions and spacing were individually adjusted in each recording 131 

session to ensure adequate coverage of the neuron’s pre - and post-saccadic RFs and 132 

surrounding visual space. Every 7ms, location of the probe was chosen randomly from the 9x9 133 

grid of possible locations. Figure 1b shows the grid arrangement and RF locations for neurons in 134 

an example ensemble, and the firing rate of three sample neurons to the same probe in the fixation 135 

and perisaccadic period is shown in Fig. 1c. This high-density visual stimulation protocol allowed 136 

us to sample neuronal responses with fine spatial and temporal precision, thereby capturing the 137 

rapid changes in visual sensitivity that occur around the time of saccades. 138 

In order to investigate the mechanism of perisaccadic temporal distortions, we leveraged a 139 

statistical encoding model that quantitatively characterizes the neuron’s input -output relationship 140 

as varying across saccades (Fig. 1d) 53 . This model captures the neuron’s sensitivity map with 141

A model-based readout of perisaccadic temporal sensitivity 176 

To conduct all our analyses at the population level, we grouped neurons into 15 ensembles, each 177 

containing at least 10 neurons recorded under similar RF, ST, and probe grid configurations; of 178 

these, 6 were V4 ensembles and 9 were MT ensembles. For each ensemble, the RF area is 179 

defined as the center of mass of the RF center of all ensemble neurons plus the 8 surrounding 180 

probes. The distribution of all neurons’ RF centers across all ensembles is shown in 181 

Supplementary Figure 2. For each particular time and delay, we constructed a population kernel 182 

matrix consisting of the kernel values at that specific time and delay, across RF probe locations 183 

and all neurons in an ensemble (examples in Fig. 1e). These population kernel matrices for probes 184 

in the RF area are the basis for subsequent analyses. We first quantified ensembles’ temporal 185 

sensitivity, i.e. the ability to discriminate between visual events occurring at nearby time points. 186 

We reasoned that greater similarity between ensemble kernels at different delays relative to 187 

stimulus onset would result in reduced temporal sensitivity. To quantify this, we computed the 188 

correlation between the population kernel matrices at a specific delay and other delay bins at 189 

each time to saccade onset (Fig. 1e, see Methods section). The correlation was measured for 190 

each delay bin and repeated at different times to saccade spanning the pre-, peri-, and post-191

We hypothesize that the observed reduction in temporal sensitivity could arise from one of two 230 

potential mechanisms (schematically illustrated in Fig. 4a, b). First, it could result from increased 231 

noise (high frequency fluctuations) in the temporal representation during the perisaccadic period, 232 

reflecting a degradation of the precision with which stimulus timing is encoded. Alternatively, it 233 

could reflect a systematic temporal bias, whereby neural representation of perisaccadic stimuli at 234 

each timepoint is temporally shifted toward certain timestamps, which may lead increased 235 

representational similarity and thereby a reduction in the ability of the response to discriminate 236 

between different timepoints. To differentiate between these possibilities, we conducted a second 237 

set of analyses aimed at characterizing the temporal mapping properties of neural population 238 

activity during the perisaccadic interval. 239

Model-predicted bias in the representation of time information across saccades 240 

Assuming that temporal encoding is incorporated into ensemble kernels during fixation, we 241 

investigated how this representation could give rise to perceived time around saccade onset. To 242 

assess how temporal representations are altered around the time of saccades, we examined the 243 

correlations between perisaccadic and fixation kernels across delays. These correlations served 244 

as a measure of how neural representations at different time points were mapped onto one 245 

another. We measured the similarity between kernels at each time relative to saccade onset and 246 

those in the fixation window by computing the correlation between the ensemble kernel at a 247 

specific delay and time and the ensemble kernel at multiple delay bins in the fixation period (-248 

500:-300 ms from saccade onset) (Fig. 1d). The correlation was measured for each delay bin 249 

across time to saccade onset. Thus, at each time to saccade onset, we have a matrix of 250 

correlation values, each element corresponding to the correlation between delays of 0-200 ms 251 

(5ms bin) and those in the fixation window. Examples of these correlation maps are shown for a 252 

sample ensemble in Fig. 4c. Next, we measured the similarity between each 300 ms sliding 253 

window of these maps by computing the correlation between each map in this sliding window and 254 

its central map, meaning that we have a scalar value of correlation associated with each time to 255 

saccade onset (see Methods section). Treating these scalars as weighting factors for each bin of 256 

time in a sliding window, and then taking sum of all the time to saccade bins in the sliding window 257 

results in the perceived time to saccade. The time to saccade corresponding to the central map 258 

is considered as the real time. Figure 4d shows an example plot for real vs. perceived time for an 259 

ensemble. The average of mapping between perceived and actual time across 15 ensembles is 260 

shown in Fig. 4e. Left histogram in Fig. 4e depicts the distribution of perceived time to saccade. 261 

From these results, we observed that time windows just before saccade onset were systematically 262 

mapped to earlier times, such that the distribution of perceived time is no longer uniform 263 

perisaccadically. To measure this phenomenon, we identified the most prevalent perceived time 264

The role of representational bias in the perisaccadic readout of visual time 289 

The SVGLM framework allows us to identify and manipulate specific components of the neural 290 

representation to determine their contribution to particular perisaccadic perceptual changes. 291 

Using this in silico approach, we systematically identified and eliminated individual STUs to 292 

characterize their role in generating specific perisaccadic temporal effects. We leveraged this 293 

feature to test the two hypotheses illustrated in Fig. 4a, b, and to identify subsets of STUs that 294 

were either responsible for generating temporal bias in the neural representation (’bias -relevant’), 295 

or associated with increased temporal variability (’noise -relevant’). 296 

To identify bias-relevant STUs, we computed an index reflecting the difference between the 297 

stimulus kernels of each delay bin and the average kernel of delays 40:100 ms at each time to 298 

saccade. STUs were classified as bias-relevant if removing them significantly altered this index 299 

(see Methods). Figure 6a shows how the kernel weights evolve during the perisaccadic period 300 

across delay values. The prevalence map of identified bias-relevant STUs for an example 301 

ensemble of neurons is shown in Fig. 6b. Figure 6c shows the average prevalence map across 302 

all neurons, illustrating that most of the bias-relevant STUs are in the perisaccadic window. After 303 

replacing the perisaccadic weights of these bias-relevant STUs with their fixation values, we 304 

recomputed both the temporal bias and time difference over time and delay values. As expected, 305 

after replacing bias-relevant STUs, there was a significant reduction in temporal bias compared 306 

to the full model (-50:50 ms window, -6.23±3.21; p=2.66×10 -2 , Fig. 6d). Figure 6e shows the time 307 

difference map after removing the bias-relevant STUs, and Figure 6f demonstrates the mean time 308 

difference over delay of 40:60 ms, no longer showing a significant time difference 309 

(0.80±1.77, p=1.10×10 -3 ), demonstrating that the same STUs drive the changes in both 310 

perisaccadic sensitivity and temporal bias. We can also test the effect of removing these bias-311 

relevant STU modulations on the model-predicted spiking of neurons to synthetic probe 312

## Discussion 349 

Saccades induce a range of distortions in the perception onset time or duration 11 , including the 350 

subjective lengthening of perceived durations (chronostasis) 35 , and the compression of temporal 351 

intervals 29 . These phenomena challenge the notion of a stable and continuous internal clock 56,57 ,352 

pointing instead to a dynamic and context-dependent construction of subjective time. In the 353 

present study, we used a combined electrophysiological and computational framework to 354 

investigate the link between perisaccadic modulations of neuronal responses in extrastriate cortex 355 

and temporal perceptions. Our results show a reduction in temporal sensitivity (the ability to 356 

discriminate between different stimulus onset times), and a bias toward perceiving stimulus onset 357 

as earlier. These findings suggest that presaccadic shifts in perceived timing play a key role in 358 

maintaining a smooth and continuous visual experience. First, they transiently hold on to the 359 

visual information available just before the saccade, effectively stabilizing the presaccadic scene 360 

until new input is processed post saccade. Second, they reduce temporal sensitivity, filtering out 361 

high-frequency and potentially unreliable temporal fluctuations. Together, these findings 362

of the visual scene, despite the jitters and uncertainty inherent in saccadic eye movements. 364 

We found that the onset of stimuli presented -100:0 ms relative to saccade onset is biased toward 365 

earlier times inside the RF area of neurons. This phenomenon suggests that presaccadic visual 366 

signals have shifted perceptual latency such that stimuli presented just before a saccade may be 367 

perceived as stimuli that were available earlier. This effect reflects a bias in perceived timing, 368 

which could lead to apparent temporal expansion, similar to chronostasis. In saccadic 369 

chronostasis, there is subjective temporal lengthening or overestimation of the duration of a visual 370 

stimulus 35,39 . It is shown that the overestimation is not proportional to overall stimulus duration 37 ,371 

and subjects typically overestimate the time they have seen the saccadic target by about 120 372 

ms 35 . Our observed backward shift in perceived stimulus onset prior to a saccade, however, offers 373 

a potential replay-like mechanism that preserves the most recent stable presaccadic temporal 374 

representation, being temporally distinct from postsaccadic chronostasis. Rather than simply 375 

freezing the visual scene, this active process may serve to preserve the most recent stable 376 

presaccadic temporal representation and mitigate perisaccadic uncertainty. By shifting incoming 377 

signals to earlier time points, this presaccadic temporal bias establishes a temporal prior, which 378 

favors the most recent reliable presaccadic input, enabling an extrapolation of this information 379 

forward in time. This inferential strategy, supported by the temporal prior, could facilitate an active 380 

reconstruction of the presaccadic visual scene, and contribute to continuity and stability in 381 

temporal perception across saccades. In addition, we showed that during the time window of -382 

45:0 ms before saccade onset, the temporal sensitivity significantly declines. We hypothesize that 383 

this perisaccadic sensitivity reduction can be attributed to the previously reported effect of early 384 

saccadic suppression 53,54,58 –60 and late enhancement of response 54 , which were also observed for 385 

a similar dataset; to elaborate, the suppression of early onset times and the enhancement of late 386

Experimental paradigm and electrophysiological recording 429 

All procedures complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were 430 

approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Utah. Four 431 

adult male rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) performed a visually guided saccade task. 432 

Monkeys fixated on a central point (FP) while a peripheral saccade target (ST) appeared 10 –15 433 

degrees of visual angle (dva) away. After a randomized interval (700 –1100 ms), the FP 434 

disappeared, cueing the monkey to saccade to the ST. A liquid reward was delivered after 435 

maintaining fixation on the ST for 560 –750 ms. In most sessions (85 out of 108), the ST location 436
