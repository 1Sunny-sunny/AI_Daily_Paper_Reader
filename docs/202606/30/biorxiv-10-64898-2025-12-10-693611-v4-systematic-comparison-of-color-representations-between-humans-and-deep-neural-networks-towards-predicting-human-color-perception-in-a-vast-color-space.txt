# Systematic comparison of color representations between humans and deep neural networks: towards predicting human color perception in a vast color space 

Nipun Ravindu Wickramanayaka 1 and Masafumi Oizumi 1* 

1 Graduate School of Arts and Science, The University of Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan * c-oizumi@g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp 

# Abstract 

The representational structure of large-scale human color perception remains incompletely understood. While classical studies measured numerous color pairs, these measurements compared only similar colors, and exploring the global relationships among thousands of colors has been infeasible due to the time costs of psychophysical experiments. Given these constraints, deep neural networks (DNNs) have attracted attention as a promising tool for providing proxies or predictions of human perception beyond the scope of psychophysical experiments. However, it remains unclear which DNNs possess embeddings that geometrically align with human color perception. Furthermore, it is unclear which learning paradigm enables DNNs to acquire a color representation that aligns with that of humans. Here, we systematically investigate which learning paradigm enables DNNs to produce a color representation that is structurally congruent with that of humans, with a focus on three types: self-supervised learning (SSL) that trains on images alone, supervised learning (SL) that trains on images with category labels, and contrastive language-image pre-training (CLIP) that trains on image-text pairs. We compared the embeddings of DNNs with the human similarity judgments of 93 colors using a rigorous unsupervised method termed Gromov-Wasserstein Optimal Transport (GWOT). Our results show that, while each learning paradigm acquires color representations that strongly align with human data at the fine-item level in early layers, only CLIP sustains such a representation at the output. Furthermore, when we leveraged a key advantage of DNNs and investigated the representational structure of 4096 colors, the early layers of each learning paradigm and the output of CLIP consistently converged on their own characteristic structures. These structures present plausible predictions for the large-scale human color representation. Our work demonstrates an approach for exploring unknown territories of human perception through the use of computational models validated in a limited empirical space, and provides predictions for future large-scale psychophysical experiments.

# Introduction 1

Understanding the detailed representational structure of human color perception is a 2

fundamental challenge in vision and cognitive sciences. While the biological foundations 3

of trichromatic color vision are well-established [1, 2], the precise geometric structure 4

through which humans organize the vast spectrum of perceivable colors remains 5

incompletely understood. Specifically, although previous studies have investigated the 6

geometric structure of human color perception through a variety of psychophysical 7

experiments, including behavioral judgments [3 – 12], electroencephalography (EEG) [13], 8

magnetoencephalography (MEG) [14], and functional magnetic resonance imaging 9

(fMRI) [15–17], these investigations have typically faced a critical trade-off: studies 10 

evaluating a large number of colors have mostly captured local relationships, focusing 11 

on perceptual differences between adjacent or similar colors [3, 4, 6, 7], whereas studies 12 

aiming to capture global relationships are restricted by the practical limitations of 13 

testing human subjects to limited stimuli sets numbering in the dozens [5, 8–17]. A 14 

recent study broadened the scale of investigation by successfully mapping the intricate 15 

global similarity structure of a 93-color stimulus set that included different tones and 16 

achromatic colors [18]; nevertheless, the representational structure for even larger color 17 

spaces, such as those spanning thousands of colors, remains largely unexplored. 18 

One powerful methodology for addressing these experimental limitations is the use of 19 

deep neural networks (DNNs). DNNs have emerged as powerful tools in computer 20 

vision [19–25]; they have achieved remarkable performance across a wide range of 21 

perceptual tasks [26–30] and are increasingly regarded as effective computational 22 

proxies for the human visual perception [31 –36]. In the field of color science, DNNs have 23 

also been applied to study diverse phenomena such as color selectivity [37–41], color 24 

categorization [42, 43], color constancy [44], color illusions [45], perceptual 25 

coherence [46], and the organization of color naming [47]. The use of DNNs enables 26 

experiments in ways that are not feasible with human subjects. 27 

However, which DNN models exhibit embeddings whose global geometry aligns with 28 

human color perception across a broad color space remains to be established. Previous 29 

studies on comparisons of the global geometry of DNN and human color representations 30 

have successfully captured correlations using stimulus sets of approximately 20 31 

colors [48–50]. A recent study made progress by comparing a larger set of 93 colors 32 

more rigorously, showing that the responses of the state-of-the-art multimodal large 33 

language model GPT-4 [51] are consistent with human color similarity judgments [52]. 34 

However, the study was unable to clarify whether the internal representations of GPT-4 35 

are naturally consistent with human color perception. 36 

Furthermore, learning paradigms that facilitate the acquisition of a color 37 

representation that is geometrically consistent with that of humans have not been 38 

identified. Even the study above [52], which provides the most direct insight to date, 39 

showed only that the responses of the unimodal large language model GPT-3.5 had 40

comparing the embeddings of DNNs trained under distinct learning paradigms. 48 

Specifically, we analyze two categories of models: vision-only models and 49

comparison allows us to assess which type of learning experience is effective for 59 

developing a representational structure that is consistent with that of human color 60 

perception, namely the use of visual information alone, visual information with 61 

categorical labels, or visual information grounded in rich language. 62 

Of note, a rigorous comparison of these models requires a more sensitive method 63 

than standard correlation-based analyses [56–58]. Although such analyses can capture 64 

coarse-grained similarities, they are limited by an inherent ambiguity: a high correlation 65 

score cannot distinguish whether the alignment is due to a broad, group-to-group 66 

correspondence or to a fine-grained, one-to-one correspondence between individual 67 

elements [57, 59]. To overcome this limitation and directly assess the fine-grained 68 

geometric structure of the representations, we employed Gromov–Wasserstein Optimal 69 

Transport (GWOT) [60, 61]. This powerful unsupervised framework allows for sensitive 70 

comparison of representational geometry [18, 33, 52, 59, 62–65], making it particularly 71 

suitable for detecting subtle differences across models. 72 

Our analysis reveals that, while all three learning paradigms can acquire a color 73 

representation that aligns with human similarity judgments at early layers, only the 74 

CLIP paradigm sustains this alignment at the output. Notably, this divergence at the 75 

output is relatively clearer than that of the supervised comparison, where all three 76 

paradigms exhibited substantially high correlations with human data. Furthermore, by 77 

exploiting the unique advantage of DNNs to operate beyond human experimental 78 

constraints, we probed the geometry of a vast 4096-color representation and found that 79 

the second layer embeddings of the three learning paradigms converged upon a 80 

relatively simple, undistorted ring-like structure, while the output embeddings of CLIP 81 

paradigm converged upon a relatively complex geometry, namely a hollow, distorted 82 

ring-like structure with specific protrusions and indentations. These discoveries offer 83 

tangible computational predictions for the large-scale organization of human color 84 

perception, and pave the way for future, large-scale psychophysical validation. Our 85 

“select-and-predict” method thus demonstrates an approach for generating novel and 86 

nontrivial hypotheses about human color perception that are presently beyond the reach 87 

of direct empirical investigation. 88

# Methods overview 89 

## Analysis framework 90 

Our study employs a comprehensive analytical framework to systematically compare the 91 

structure of color representations between human behavioral data and various deep 92 

neural networks (DNNs) in order to probe which learning paradigms enable DNNs to 93 

develop internal representations that are structurally analogous to those of human color 94 

perception. As briefly illustrated in Figure 1, this process involves two primary stages. 95 

The first stage is the preparation of representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) 96 

for both the human behavioral data and the various DNN models, which form the basis 97 

of our comparison (Fig. 1A). To establish the human-derived RDM, we first constructed 98 

a matrix of the rates from the discrete similarity judgments collected in the previous 99 

study [18] for 93 colors selected from the Practical Color Coordinate System 100 

(PCCS) [66]. We applied multidimensional scaling (MDS) to this matrix to produce a 101 

set of continuous vector embeddings that are directly comparable to the embeddings of 102 

DNN, from which we then computed the RDM as the pairwise Euclidean distances 103 

between the 93 embeddings. For the DNN-derived RDMs, we first extracted the 104 

embeddings for the same 93 colors from models trained under three distinct learning 105 

paradigms: self-supervised learning (SSL) trained on images alone; supervised learning 106 

(SL) trained on images with category labels; and contrastive language-image 107 

pre-training (CLIP) trained on images paired with rich textual descriptions. The RDM 108 

for each model was then computed from the pairwise Euclidean distances between these 109 

93 embeddings. 110 

In the second stage, we compared the human-derived RDM with each of the 111 

DNN-derived RDMs using two complementary methods (Fig. 1B). The first method is a 112 

supervised comparison which is based on a fixed one-to-one correspondence between the 113 

colors. For this, we computed the Pearson correlation between the RDMs, known as 114 

conventional Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA). We refer to this comparison 115 

as supervised because it uses the externally provided identity labels of the colors to fix 116 

the one-to-one correspondence in advance. Specifically, each color in the human RDM is 117 

matched to the same color in the DNN RDM on the basis of these labels, and the 118 

Pearson correlation is then computed between the two RDMs under this label-defined 119 

correspondence [59]. This supervised comparison is a standard and widely used 120 

approach in neuroscience [56–58], but is limited by an inherent ambiguity: a high 121 

correlation score cannot distinguish between a coarse, group-to-group correspondence 122 

(in our case, color category to color category alignment) and a fine-grained, one-to-one 123 

correspondence (in our case, color to color alignment) [59]. To capture these 124 

finer-grained structural properties, our second method is a more rigorous unsupervised 125 

comparison using Gromov-Wasserstein Optimal Transport (GWOT) [60, 61]. This 126 

method finds an optimal probabilistic mapping, or “transportation plan,” that 127 

minimizes the difference between the internal distance structures of two point clouds (in 128 

our case, the two sets of color representations ). This alignment process is entirely 129 

unsupervised, as it does not use the identity labels of the items being compared (in our 130 

case, the colors). This in turn allows for the direct comparison of their intrinsic 131 

geometric properties, which cannot be measured by correlation alone. After the 132 

alignment is complete, we then quantified the accuracy of the resulting alignment by 133 

calculating a “top-1 matching rate” score, which represents the percentage of colors for 134 

which the transportation plan correctly assigns the highest mapping probability to its 135 

corresponding color in the other structure. This dual-analysis framework provides a 136 

multi-faceted evaluation of which learning paradigms produce human-like color 137 

representations. 138

## Human data used in this study 139 

We used human data collected in a previous study [18]. In that study, similarity 140 

judgments were collected for all possible pairs of 93 solid-colored circles presented on a 141 

uniform grey background. Consequently, the color perception investigated in the present 142 

study is explicitly limited to the context of this specific task: judging pure color 143 

similarity using uniform patches on a uniform background. 144 

## Selection of DNN models for comparing learning paradigms 145 

To investigate the learning mechanism that enables DNNs to produce internal 146 

representations that are consistent with human color perception, this study 147 

systematically compares the color representations of DNN models trained under three 148 

distinct learning paradigms that represent different types of available information. We 149 

evaluated a total of 22 DNN models: two CNN-based and four Transformer-based 150 

self-supervised (SSL) models trained on images alone, two CNN-based and three 151 

Transformer-based supervised (SL) models trained on images with category labels, and 152 

two CNN-based and nine Transformer-based CLIP models trained on images paired 153 

with rich textual descriptions. The models are detailed in the Materials and methods 154 

section. The results of 93 colors for all 22 models are presented in the Supporting 155 

information. An illustrative subset of this larger group are shown in the Results section 156 

to facilitate a clear and controlled comparison of the paradigms. 157

the alignment with human data differs across architecture sizes). 174 

## Selection of input images for DNN models 175 

For the main analysis, we used uniform color patches as input images to the DNN 176 

models. Although such simple inputs may not be ideal for probing the internal 177 

representations of complex DNN models in general, they yielded the strongest alignment 178 

with human data among the input formats we tested. Specifically, when we used 179 

circular color patches on a grey background as input images, the alignment with human 180 

data was relatively lower (Fig. S11). Based on these results, we used uniform color 181 

patches for the main analysis, since our study aims to identify DNN models whose color 182 

representations align with those of humans, and these inputs provided stronger 183 

alignment with human data. 184 

## Selection of the dimensionality for MDS visualization 185 

For the visualization of the representational structures using Multidimensional Scaling 186 

(MDS), we presented the 2-dimensional results in the main results and the 3-dimensional 187 

results in the Supporting information. We chose the 2-dimensional format for the main 188 

figures because static 3-dimensional plots can be misleading depending on the viewing 189 

angle, making distance relationships difficult to interpret. It should be noted, however, 190 

that a 2-dimensional result reflects the compression of one of the axes of the 191 

3-dimensional result. For example, in the human data, the axis related to lightness and 192 

chroma was compressed in two dimensions, making two dark colors with opponent hues 193 

appear among the most distant from each other (Fig. 2F); in three dimensions, this 194 

apparent distance was resolved, and the distance between two dark colors with 195 

opponent hues became smaller than that between two light colors with opponent hues 196 

(Video S1). To present such richer information, we provided the 3-dimensional MDS 197 

results as rotating videos in the Supporting information (Video S1– S6). 198

We first qualitatively compared the structure of color representations through a visual 203 

inspection of the representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) (Fig. 2A-E). In the 204 

human RDM, colors belonging to the same or similar hues are represented as being close 205 

to each other, resulting in a block-diagonal-like structure; conversely, colors with 206 

dissimilar hues are represented as being far apart (Fig. 2A). The RDMs from all three 207 

learning paradigms are visibly distinct not only from the simple structure of the 208 

baseline RGB model (Fig. 2B) but also from each other (Fig. 2C-E), indicating that 209 

each learning process transforms the input color space to acquire its own unique 210 

representational structure. While models from all three paradigms exhibited a 211 

block-diagonal-like structure to some extent, visual inspection of the RDMs alone was 212 

insufficient to determine which model was most similar to the human data (Fig. 2A-E, 213 

S1). 214 

The representational structures showed clearer qualitative similarities and 215 

dissimilarities when visualized using 2-dimensional Multidimensional Scaling (MDS), 216 

which revealed that the structure of the CLIP paradigm was visually similar to the 217 

human data (Fig. 2F-J). The human 93-color representation formed a wide, complex 218 

ring structure with intricate tones, and incorporated many achromatic colors (Fig. 2F). 219 

In contrast, although the chromatic colors sampled from PCCS form a distinct ring-like 220 

for each tone, the RGB baseline resulted in a filled-in structure when all 93 colors, 221 

including multiple tones and achromatic colors, were plotted together (Fig. 2G). Among 222 

the DNNs, the CLIP model exhibited a wide, complex ring-like structure similar to the 223 

human one, with intricate tones and achromatic colors (Fig. 2J). This tendency was 224 

consistent regardless of architectural size (Fig. S2I-K). The MAE model also formed a 225 

wide, complex ring-like structure with intricate chromatic colors (Fig. 2H), but this 226 

trend was dependent on architectural size (Fig. S2C-E). In contrast to these paradigms, 227 

the SL models failed to form a clear ring-like structure (Fig. 2I, S2F-H). To better 228 

understand these qualitative findings of varying degrees of similarity across the human 229 

and model representations, we conducted a quantitative analysis to objectively assess 230 

the degree of similarity between the representational structures. 231

DNN models, this finding is limited by an inherent ambiguity; it cannot distinguish 247 

whether the alignment is due to a coarse, group-to-group correspondence (in our case, 248 

alignment of colors within the same hue as represented in the block diagonal structure 249 

in RDMs) or a fine-grained, one-to-one correspondence (in our case, same color-to-same 250 

color alignment) [57, 59]. To resolve this ambiguity, we directly assess their geometric 251 

structure using the unsupervised alignment method, Gromov-Wasserstein Optimal 252

the Gromov-Wasserstein Distance (GWD). Two RDMs (Fig. 4A and B) are used as 259

the solution that achieved the lowest GWD (Fig. 4C), which corresponds to the optimal 262 

transportation plan shown in Fig. 4D. 263

structural congruence with the human data. The resulting optimal transportation (OT) 265

## Complementary roles of the supervised and unsupervised 316 

## comparisons 317 

A side-by-side examination of the Pearson correlation (Fig. 3) and the GWOT-based 318 

top-1 matching rates (Fig. 5) indicates that the supervised and unsupervised 319 

comparisons provide complementary information about the alignment between the color 320 

representations of humans and DNNs. On the one hand, the supervised comparison 321 

based on correlation reveals gradual differences that the unsupervised comparison did 322 

not discern. Since the top-1 matching rate reflects whether the fine-grained, one-to-one 323 

correspondence is achieved, a low matching rate does not necessarily mean that a 324 

representation is far from the human color similarity structure. For layers where the 325 

top-1 matching rate remains near the chance rate, or for layers where it fluctuates 326 

sharply (Fig. 5, S4C, D), the correlation scores reveal, in a more gradual manner, how 327 

closely each representation resembles the human color similarity structure relative to 328 

the RGB baseline (Fig. 3, S4A, B). On the other hand, the unsupervised comparison 329 

based on GWOT reveals fine-grained, one-to-one structural alignment that the 330 

supervised comparison cannot discern. While all three learning paradigms included 331 

architectures that aligned with human data at early or intermediate layers, only the 332 

CLIP paradigm sustained this alignment at the output (Fig. 5, S3B, S4C, D). This 333 

divergence was not apparent in the supervised comparison, where the output 334 

embeddings of all three paradigms exhibited substantially high correlations with human 335 

data (Fig. 3, S3A, S4A, B). Rather than one being superior to the other, the two 336 

comparisons are complementary, and combining them provides a more complete 337 

characterization of the alignment between the color representations of humans and 338 

DNNs than either could alone. 339

# Discussion 454 

In this study, we aimed to provide predictions of human color perception across a vast 455 

color space by systematically investigating the conditions under which DNNs acquire a 456 

color representation that mirrors human color perception. Our results revealed that the 457 

early layers of the three learning paradigms and the output of the CLIP paradigm 458 

acquire color representations that align with the 93-color human perception (Fig. 5). 459 

We found that the second-layer embeddings of the three learning paradigms and the 460 

output embeddings of the CLIP paradigm that successfully aligned with human data 461 

also developed remarkably consistent and robust color representations in a vast color 462 

space (Fig. 7B-D, I, J, O, P, Q-S, Fig 8), allowing us to make novel, testable predictions 463 

about the large-scale structure of human color perception. Here, we discuss the 464 

implications of these findings with a focus on four key areas: the effect of language on 465 

color representations of DNNs, the reason why the output embeddings of the models 466 

without rich linguistic input fail to maintain human-like color representations, the use of 467 

DNNs as computational proxies for predicting large-scale human color perception, and 468 

future prospects for validating and extending these DNN-based predictions. 469 

## Effect of language on color representations of DNNs 470 

The findings that the early layers of the three learning paradigms converged on a 471 

relatively undistorted ring-like structure (Fig. 7B-D, H-J, N-P, 8) whereas the output of 472 

the CLIP paradigm converged on a ring-like structure with specific protrusions and 473 

indentations (Fig. 7Q-S, 8) lead us to consider the possibility that these distortions 474 

June 29, 2026 15/44 

.CC-BY 4.0 International license available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 29, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.10.693611 doi: bioRxiv preprint reflect the influence of language on color representations. We draw this inference 475 

because the learning paradigms that align with each other at their early layers include 476 

MAE (Fig. 6, 8), which forms representations from visual information alone without 477 

any linguistic input or supervisory signals, suggesting that the influence of language on 478 

these representations is limited. In contrast, the output of the CLIP paradigm forms 479 

representations through the integration of visual information with rich linguistic 480 

descriptions, suggesting that the influence of linguistic information is substantial. This 481 

language-driven interpretation connects our findings to the long-standing debate on 482 

whether language shapes human color perception [72 –79], with prior studies addressing 483 

this question including investigations using DNNs [42–44, 47, 52]. If the influence 484 

suggested by our findings holds, it would lean toward the relativist view that language 485 

can shape color representations [72, 74, 75, 77, 79]. However, this raises an important 486 

open question: since the CLIP models analyzed in our study were predominantly 487 

trained on English-language descriptions, how would their color representations differ if 488 

they were trained on other languages? Future studies could address this question by 489 

comparing CLIP models trained on diverse languages. 490 

Turning our focus to the early layers, the shared representational structure across 491 

the three learning paradigms allows for an intriguing developmental hypothesis: these 492 

early representations might correspond to the color perception of pre-linguistic infants. 493 

As discussed above, these early layers likely form representations with limited linguistic 494 

influence, potentially serving as a precursor to the adult color representation refined by 495 

language [80–84]. This linguistically refined representation of adults would correspond 496 

to the output of the CLIP paradigm. Under this view, the 93-color human data used in 497 

this study [18] may reflect both this fundamental pre-linguistic representation and the 498 

subsequent linguistically refined representation of adults. Testing this hypothesis would 499 

require comparing the early layers of the three learning paradigms with color 500 

representations of pre-linguistic infants or children. However, the detailed 501 

representational structure of infants or children in a large color space remains 502 

unexplored. Although a recent study reported that children as young as 3 years old 503 

already possess a color representation that resembles that of adults in a limited 9-color 504 

set [12], this coarse similarity does not guarantee alignment in a larger color space. 505 

Accordingly, attempts to determine whether language acquisition is indeed necessary for 506 

forming the adult-like color representation require obtaining large-scale color structure 507 

data from children or infants and comparing them with the early layers of these learning 508 

paradigms. This endeavor would yield significant insights into the developmental 509 

trajectory of human color perception. 510 

## Reason why the output embeddings of the models without rich 511 

## linguistic input fail to maintain human-like color representations 512 

A possible explanation for why models without rich linguistic input fail to maintain 513 

human-like color representations at the output (Fig. 5) lies in the known processing 514 

dynamics of DNNs. A recent study has demonstrated that color information is 515 

prominently represented in early layers of DNNs but progressively diminishes in deeper 516 

layers, as the network increasingly prioritizes higher-level, task-specific features such as 517 

object categories [37 –41, 85]. Our layer-by-layer results (Fig. 5) are consistent with this 518 

pattern: models with MAE and SL paradigms achieve strong alignment with human 519 

color similarity structure in early or intermediate layers, but this alignment is lost at the 520 

output where representations are shaped by their respective training objectives of pixel 521 

reconstruction and object classification. In contrast, the CLIP paradigm employs a 522 

contrastive objective that aligns the output of the image encoder with that of the 523 

language encoder [55], and since linguistic descriptions include human-defined color 524

paradigms appear capable of forming such representations at their early layers. 530 

Another possible explanation for why MAE and SL models fail to align with human 531 

data at the output (Fig. 5) is their significantly smaller training dataset sizes. 532 

Specifically, the volume of the ImageNet dataset [67] falls short of that of the datasets 533 

used to train CLIP models [68–71] by more than an order of magnitude. Notably, this 534 

size difference is not accompanied by a difference in color, as the hue distribution of 535

models in the main results (Fig. S12). Because scale, unlike color, remains uncontrolled, 537 

training MAE and SL models on datasets of a comparable scale would allow for a more 538 

rigorous comparison between the learning paradigms. However, due to the massive 539 

computational resources required for such a large scale analysis, we leave this 540 

investigation for future research. 541 

## DNNs as computational proxies for predicting large-scale human 542 

## color perception 543 

While perceptually uniform color spaces such as CIELAB [86, 87] have been 544 

foundational in color science, previous studies have suggested that they fall short when 545 

mapping global relationships across a broader range of colors [88–90], and our findings 546 

support this view (Fig. S5). Although these classical models were based on a large 547 

number of human color judgments, the measured color pairs were typically local, 548 

restricted to those that were adjacent [6] or along specific axes [3, 4]. Consequently, 549 

these color space models were primarily optimized to capture perceptual color 550 

differences between similar colors, ensuring that small Euclidean distances in the space 551 

correspond to differences in human color perception. Therefore, there is no guarantee 552 

that the distances between colors positioned far apart in these spaces reflect human 553 

perceptual dissimilarities [88 – 90]. Indeed, our evaluation demonstrated that these color 554 

space models did not achieve top-1 matching rates as high as those of DNNs when 555 

compared against the human behavioral data capturing global relationships (Fig. S5). 556 

Thus, color space models based on local optimization have limitations in exploring the 557 

large-scale, global structure of human color perception. Furthermore, modifying these 558 

spaces to achieve global uniformity via traditional psychophysical methods is 559 

impractical due to the combinatorial explosion of required measurements. 560 

To overcome the methodological bottleneck of exhaustively measuring an enormous 561 

number of color combinations, human-aligned DNNs could offer a powerful 562 

computational approach. In contrast to these color space models, some DNN 563 

representations showed strong alignment with the human behavioral data capturing 564 

global color relationships (Fig. 5). By utilizing these human-aligned models as 565 

computational proxies, we generated testable predictions of the perceptual maps of a 566 

vastly expanded space comprising 4,096 colors (Fig. 7B-D, I, J, O, P, Q-S). Evaluating 567 

these DNN-generated large-scale predictions against actual human data could help 568 

address the outstanding issues of global non-uniformity in current models and guide the 569 

development of globally robust perceptually uniform color spaces. 570

## Future prospects for predicting human color perception with 571 

## DNNs 572 

The first future prospect involves empirically testing the plausibility of our prediction 573 

for the vast, 4096-color representation suggested by the human-aligned DNN models 574 

(Fig. 7 B-D, I, J, O, P, Q-S). In order to validate, we naturally need to compare our 575 

results with human data. This would require psychophysical experiments comparing the 576 

vast number of color pairs necessary to construct the 4096-color representation, which is 577 

infeasible for an individual subject to conduct. Therefore, one potential approach is to 578 

collect data from a group of people, as utilized in the previous research [18]. Although 579 

computationally and logistically demanding, empirical validation of this large-scale 580 

prediction remains a critical next step for future research. 581 

The second important future direction is to investigate more naturalistic and 582 

nuanced color representations. A limitation of the current study, consistent with much 583 

foundational research in this area, is the use of idealized, single-color patches. This 584 

controlled approach is necessary for isolating variables, but does not capture the 585 

complexities of color perception in natural environments. Human color perception is 586 

heavily influenced by context, such as changing lighting conditions [91], the influence of 587 

surrounding backgrounds [92], and the complex interplay of multiple colors [93]. Future 588 

work should therefore extend this research to examine whether the human-like 589 

alignment of DNN models holds under these more realistic conditions. 590 

# Materials and methods 591 

## Human Behavioral Data 592 

Human behavioral data were drawn from a large-scale dataset collected from 426 593 

color-neurotypical participants in a previous study [18]. In the experiment, participants 594 

performed a color similarity judgment task in which they were asked to rate the 595 

perceived similarity between pairs of 93 color stimuli selected from the Practical Color 596 

Coordinate System (PCCS) [66]. For each pair, ratings were provided on a scale from 0 597 

(“very similar”) to 7 (“very different”). The human dissimilarity matrix was computed 598 

as the pairwise Euclidean distances between 20-dimensional color embeddings estimated 599 

from the participants’ similarity judgments [18]. 600 

## DNN Models Used in This Study 601 

We used three types of deep neural network (DNN) learning methods in this study: 602 

self-supervised learning, supervised learning, and CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image 603 

Pre-training). A brief description of each learning paradigm is provided below. 604 

Self-Supervised Learning models: Self-supervised learning (SSL) is a framework 605 

where models learn representations from raw data by solving pretext tasks without 606 

relying on supervised signals. For our study investigating color representation, it was 607 

crucial to select models that had not been trained with color augmentation, as such 608 

techniques could artificially degrade or alter the intrinsic color information learned by 609 

the model. Our selection was therefore guided by this constraint, and lead us to focus 610 

on masked image modeling (MIM) models [53, 54, 94]. In this framework, the model 611 

learns by masking random patches of the input natural image and reconstructing the 612 

missing raw pixel values. These models learned representations exclusively from the 613 

input images themselves, without any reliance on labels or linguistic information. 614 

Supervised Learning models: Supervised learning (SL) is a paradigm where models 615 

learn to classify or predict outputs from the input natural image [22, 25]. These models 616 

were trained on labeled datasets of natural images. Although the labels were not 617

primarily focused on color information, some inherently included color descriptors (e.g., 618 

“black swan”, “red wine”), indirectly providing the models with color-related information 619 

during training. 620 

CLIP models: CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining) models learn to 621 

associate natural images and text by jointly training on large-scale image-text pairs [55]. 622 

The architecture consists of separate image and text encoders that project visual and 623 

linguistic features into a shared embedding space, where the model is trained to 624 

maximize the similarity of matched pairs via a contrastive objective. Consistent with 625 

the other learning paradigms, we used only the image encoders for our analysis. These 626 

models were trained with free-form natural language descriptions that objectively 627 

describe the visual content of an image, typically as a single short sentence or phrase, 628 

and these descriptions included color descriptions. Unlike curated class labels, color in 629 

these descriptions is addressed naturally as a visual attribute modifying a specific object 630 

(e.g., “A green highway sign with the words Queens Bronx.”). These natural language 631 

descriptions enable the models to learn representations that link visual features with 632 

color-related linguistic information. 633 

## Training Datasets of the DNN Models 634 

The DNN models were trained on different datasets depending on their learning 635 

paradigm: ImageNet-1k for the MAE and SL models, and substantially larger 636 

image-text datasets for the CLIP models. ImageNet-1k [67] consists of ∼1.28 million 637 

object-centric natural images that were collected from the Internet and labeled by 638 

human annotators. The datasets used to train the CLIP models consist of natural 639 

images paired with text descriptions drawn from the web, with the image-text pairs 640 

selected by automatic filtering. These were YFCC [68] for the CNN-based models, and 641 

LAION-2B [69], MetaCLIP [70], or DFN [71] for the Transformer-based models, 642 

together comprising ∼15 million to ∼5 billion image-text pairs. 643 

Despite these differences in scale and assembly, the datasets are comparable in their 644 

visual and color content, with no curation toward particular colors. Both span a similar 645 

breadth of everyday content, such as objects, animals, people, scenes, and architecture. 646 

To assess their color content directly, we compared the hue distribution of ImageNet-1k 647 

with that of DFN-2B (the dataset used to train the CLIP models in the main results) 648 

following the method of [37], and found the two distributions to be very similar 649 

(Fig. S12). 650

To create model-based dissimilarity matrices that were comparable to the human 652

2. Each image was individually fed into a model, from which we extracted the 661

• Transformer-based models: The CLS token at each layer and the output of 663 

its image encoder was extracted as the embedding. For object classification 664
