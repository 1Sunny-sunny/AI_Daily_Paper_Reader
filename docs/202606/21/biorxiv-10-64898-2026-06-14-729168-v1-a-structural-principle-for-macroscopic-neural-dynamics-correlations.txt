Abstract 

A central question in neuroscience is how the brain’s structural connectivity gives rise to its emer-gent, correlated dynamics. These large-scale dynamical correlations underlie functional networks that support cognitive functions. Here, we identify coupling correlation—the similarity between the input connectivity profiles of brain regions—as a key structural determinant of macroscopic neural dynam-ical correlation. Using dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) and numerical simulations of random neural network models, we demonstrate that coupling correlation quantitatively governs dynamical correlation. The functional form of this structure–function mapping is dictated by the eigenvalue spectrum of the coupling correlation matrix: networks with bulk eigenspectra exhibit an exact linear relationship, whereas biologically plausible long-tailed spectra yield an approximately linear map-ping except when the magnitude of coupling correlation approaches unity. Particularly, a long-tailed spectrum is necessary to reproduce the appropriate magnitude and size-invariance of coupling corre-lations observed in empirical data, thereby sustaining non-vanishing dynamical correlations that may support brain function in large systems. The theoretical prediction of approximate linearity is consis-tently validated using empirical datasets that include both structural coupling and neural dynamics in humans, mice, and Drosophila . Together, these results provide a mechanistic and quantitative frame-work linking macroscopic brain network structure to emergent neural dynamics—an essential step toward a theory of structure–function relationship in the brain. 

Keywords: neural dynamics; neural correlation; dynamical mean-field theory; random neural network; structure-function relationship

How the brain’s wiring gives rise to its coordinated activity is a fundamental unsolved problem in neuroscience. Prior work has identified correlations between structural and functional connectivity, but these relationships lacked a mechanistic, first-principles explanation. Here, we derive an analytical framework using Dynamical Mean-Field Theory and random neural network models to show that a single structural statistic— coupling correlation , the similarity between the input connectivity profiles of brain regions—linearly and causally determines the magnitude of correlated neural dynamics. We further show that a long-tailed eigenvalue spectrum in biological structural connectivity is necessary to sustain the strong, size-invariant functional correlations observed across species. Validated in humans, mice, and Drosophila using multiple imaging and connectome modalities, this principle may provide a quantitative bridge between structural connectomics and emergent brain dynamics, with implications extending to a broad class of complex networked systems. 1 Main 1

tion approaches unity, the corresponding dynami-498 

cal correlation increases superlinearly; conversely, 499 

when coupling correlations are not particularly 500 

large, the relationship remains approximately lin-501

that dynamical correlations vary more slowly with 503 

coupling correlations than previously predicted. 504 

Meanwhile, the deviation from linearity is further 505 

suppressed by external inputs ( Fig.4c-d ). This 506

correlations rarely reach extreme values, exter-508

be shown in the next section. More importantly, 512 

the approximate linearity ensures that the scale 513 

of dynamical correlation matches that of cou-514 

pling correlation, so that long-tailed spectra confer 515 

scale invariance not only to structural coupling 516 

correlations but also to the emergent dynamical 517 

correlations in large systems. This robust corre-518 

lation scale enables the coordinated activation of 519 

different units—a feature that is likely critical 520 

for supporting coherent brain function in large, 521 

biological networks. 522

The numerical simulations in the previous section 525 

revealed that deviations from linearity are con-526

the presence of external inputs. This observa-529

# 7 Discussion 716 

Our findings reveal that the coupling correla-717 

tion provides an analytically tractable structural 718 

predictor of inter-unit dynamical correlations in 719 

large-scale networks. Behind this relationship lies 720 

a simple yet powerful principle: behavioral simi-721 

larity between two units arises from the similarity 722 

in their received inputs, which in turn reflects 723 

their structural similarity. In large-scale neural 724 

networks, this structural similarity is captured 725 

by the correlation between rows of the coupling 726 

matrix—quantifying how similar the input con-727 

nectivity patterns of different units are. 728 

Supporting evidence for this principle appears 729 

across biological systems of different scales. In 730 

C. elegans , for example, dynamical correlations 731 

between single-neuron activities are shaped by the 732 

fraction of shared neighbors between neurons—a 733 

non-local structural feature analogous to coupling 734 

correlation in our framework, though defined in 735 

a discrete setting [34, 35]. Similarly, in the tran-736 

scriptional regulatory network of E. coli , genes that 737 

share isomorphic input trees—the set of regula-738 

tory pathways terminating at each gene—exhibit 739 

synchronized expression patterns that sustain cel-740 

lular functions [36]. There is, in fact, mounting evi-741 

dence that both synaptic and gene-regulatory net-742 

works exhibit systematic deviations from random-743 

ness, as revealed by motif analyses. In binarized or 744 

otherwise discretized representations of these net-745 

works, divergent motifs—triplets in which a single 746 

unit sends outputs to two others—are consistently 747 

over-represented [37–43]. The prevalence of such 748 

motifs potentially provides a unifying structural 749 

description across systems: in discrete networks, 750 

they reflect shared input or output patterns, while 751 

in weighted or continuous networks they may 752 

manifest as correlated structural couplings, as in 753 

our formulation. Together, these findings suggest 754 

that non-random, motif-based structure—and its 755 

continuous analogue, coupling correlation—is a 756 

ubiquitous organizational principle of biological 757 

networks that shapes their emergent dynamics. 758

of coupling correlation and the resulting struc-767

The magnitude of coupling correlation and its 770 

size-invariance is not a trivial observation and is 771 

closely related to the spectral property. Wang et 772 

al. [44] found a brain-wide scale-invariant spec-773 

trum in the covariance matrix of neural dynamics, 774 

characterized by a long-tail eigenvalue distribu-775 

tion. Our analysis identified a dual long-tailed 776 

spectrum via structural correlations. Coherent 777 

neural activity has been modeled by embed-778 

ding low-rank connectivity in complex networks 779 

[45–54]. Unlike typical low-rank systems with a 780 

few dominant eigenmodes, our long-tailed spec-781 

trum maintains a broad hierarchy of eigenval-782 

ues, leading to highly correlated networks. This 783 

structure may offer functional benefits similar to 784 

low-rank organization, such as efficient control 785 

and resilience, and may explain the prevalence 786 

of structured organization in natural and artifi-787 

cial networks, suggesting a fruitful area for further 788 

study. 789 

Finally, several open questions remain for 790 

future work. First, in this study, for coupling cor-791 

relations with long-tailed spectra, the dynamical 792 

properties—including chaos and dynamical corre-793 

lations—were examined purely through numerical 794 

simulations. While chaos can indeed be confirmed 795 

numerically, developing an analytical framework 796 

would offer deeper insight into the dynamics, 797 

uncovering distinct dynamical regimes and pos-798 

sible phase transitions as functions of network 799 

parameters. Moreover, there is also a need for a 800 

theoretical framework to describe the collective 801 

interaction term γi(t) when it does not converge 802 

to a single mean-field term γ∗(t) in networks with 803 

long-tailed spectra, which could provide deeper 804 

insight into the nature of correlated network sys-805 

tems. Second, in the theoretical analysis of dynam-806 

ics, we considered the case of a single steady 807 

state in which dynamical correlations are time-808 

translation invariant. However, empirical studies 809 

have shown that animal brains exhibit distinct 810 

correlation patterns across behavioral states and 811 

tasks, whereas the assumption of single steady 812 

state results in a unique correlation pattern as 813 

long as the coupling and external drive remain 814 

unchanged. A plausible explanation is that inputs 815 

from subcortical nuclei or physiological signals 816 

vary over time, leading to shifts in dynamical cor-817 

relation patterns. Further investigation is required 818 

to quantitatively characterize such external drives, 819 

and to explore whether random neural networks 820 

is possible to intrinsically transition between mul-821 

tiple steady states. Lastly, although the coupling 822 

strengths in our theory and simulations follow 823 

Gaussian distributions, biological neural networks 824 

typically display long-tailed coupling strengths 825 

[33, 55, 56]. Theoretical frameworks for analyz-826 

ing long-tailed systems remain scarce [57, 58]. 827 

Moreover, defining coupling correlation becomes 828 

mathematically challenging, as long-tailed vari-829 

ables often exhibit divergent means or variances. 830 

Addressing these challenges will not only deepen 831 

our understanding of brain dynamics but also shed 832 

light on the behavior of complex network systems 833 

more broadly. 834
