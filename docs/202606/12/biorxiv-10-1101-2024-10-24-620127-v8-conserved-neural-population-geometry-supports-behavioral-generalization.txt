Abstract 18 

Learned behaviors often generalize across contexts even though neural representations 19 

can vary substantially between environments. How the brain preserves task-relevant 20 

information across these changing neural representations remains unclear. This 21 

problem is particularly evident in the hippocampus because hippocampal spatial 22 

representations reorganize across environments even when learned behaviors 23 

generalize successfully. Here, we recorded hippocampal neuronal activity using calcium 24 

imaging while rats performed a conditioning task in two distinct environments. Despite 25 

remapping of spatial representations, the low-dimensional organization of task related 26 

population activity was conserved across contexts, and the temporal relationships 27 

among neural population states during task execution were preserved. Strikingly, this 28 

relational organization was conserved not only across contexts for individual animals, 29 

but also across animals, despite differences in the underlying neural populations and 30 

individual experiences. Moreover, this conserved organization was sufficient to support 31 

transfer of task decoding across animals, demonstrating that task information could 32 

generalize between independently learned neural representations without shared 33 

neuron identities. These findings identify a conserved population level neural 34 

organization through which the hippocampus can support behavioral generalization 35 

despite contextual remapping and suggest that shared organizational neural 36 

mechanisms and structures may underlie behavioral generalization across individuals. 37

Introduction 42 

Learned behaviors often generalize across contexts even though neural representations 43 

of different contexts can vary substantially. How the brain preserves task-relevant 44 

information across these changing neural representations remains a central question in 45 

neuroscience. 46 

This problem is particularly evident in the hippocampus (HPC) because hippocampal 47 

spatial representations 1-5 reorganize across environments, a phenomenon known as 48 

remapping 6-10 ; surprisingly, learned behaviors generalize successfully despite this 49 

remapping 6-8 . Because hippocampal activity also encodes nonspatial task related 50 

variables 11-16 , a major and unresolved question arises: what aspects of task related 51 

population activity are preserved when contextual representations reorganize? A further 52 

open question is whether different animals solve this problem using shared population 53 

level strategies, or through idiosyncratic and varied neural solutions. 54 

To address these questions, we used in vivo calcium imaging to record hippocampal 55 

CA1 activity while rats performed an HPC-dependent conditioning task, trace eyeblink 56 

conditioning (tEBC), which is rapidly generalized between spatial contexts 17,18 . Animals 57 

learned the task in one environment and were then tested in a different environment, 58 

one that triggered the remapping of spatial representations. We compared 59 

representations of spatial location and task structure across environments and across 60 

animals. 61 

Although spatial representations remapped across environments, task related 62 

population activity preserved a stable low-dimensional geometry during behavioral 63 

generalization, as represented by the relationships among task states. Strikingly, this 64 

relational organization was conserved not only across contexts for individual animals, 65 

but also across animals despite differences in neural populations and experiences. 66 

Moreover, this conserved organization was sufficient to support transfer of task 67 

decoding across animals without shared neuron identities. 68

Results 78 

We trained five freely moving rats in a conditioning task across two distinct 79 

environments, labeled A and B; animals were familiarized only with environment A 80 

before conditioning training began (Fig. 1a, Fig. S1). Environment A was an unscented 81 

rectangular enclosure with white lighting, whereas environment B was a scented ovoidal 82 

enclosure with red lighting (Fig. 1b, Fig. S1). Both environments were placed in the 83 

same position relative to the external room cues (see Methods). During daily training 84 

and testing sessions, we recorded hippocampal CA1 neuronal activity using miniature 85 

microscopes 19 (Miniscopes) and the calcium indicator GCaMP8m. Analyses used either 86 

deconvolved calcium events or calcium traces, as specified (see Methods). 87 

88 

Animals generalize a conditioning task across environments despite 89 

hippocampal remapping 90 

Freely moving rats (Fig. 1b) were trained on trace eyeblink conditioning (tEBC), a 91 

hippocampus-dependent associative learning task 20-23 . In this paradigm, a 250ms 92 

auditory conditioned stimulus (CS; tone) is followed by a 500ms trace interval and then 93 

a 100ms unconditioned stimulus (US; eyelid shock) (Fig. 1c). As training progressed, 94 

animals developed conditioned blink responses (CRs) to the tone and were considered 95 

to have learned the task upon reaching behavioral criterion (70% correct for three days, 96 

occurred in a mean of 20 ± 4.2 training sessions; see Methods, Fig. 1d). After reaching 97 

criterion in environment A, rats were transferred to environment B for one day of 98 

exploration followed by two days of testing on the tEBC task without additional training. 99 

Comparative analysis revealed no significant difference in behavioral performance 100 

between the final three training sessions in environment A and the two test sessions in 101 

environment B (mean %CRs: 74.75 ± 6.49 in environment A versus 77.70 ± 11.68 in 102 

environment B; two-sided t-test, t(24) = −0.83, p > 0.05; Fig. 1d), indicating successful 103 

transfer of the learned behavior to a new environment. 104

Consistent with hippocampal remapping 1,8,24,25 , individual CA1 neurons exhibited 105 

distinct spatial representations in environments A and B (Fig. 1e). For each neuron, we 106 

identified the spatial location of peak calcium event rate (‘rate centers’) during non-107 

conditioning periods 16 and quantified the stability of spatial representations by 108 

measuring the distance between rate centers across sessions and across 109 

environments. Rate centers shifted significantly more between environments than 110 

between sessions in the same environment (two-sided t-test, t(1430) = −2.5, p = 0.01; 111 

Fig. 1f), indicating a reorganization of spatial representations across contexts. In 112 

contrast, shifts of rate centers within environments were significantly smaller than 113 

expected by chance; in shuffled controls, rate centers were randomly reassigned across 114 

neurons within each session (Fig. S2). Spatial decoding analyses yielded similar results 115 

(Fig. S3). These findings indicate that animals generalized the learned behavior despite 116 

robust hippocampal remapping across environments. 117

Task related latent representations generalize across environments 159 

We next asked whether hippocampal population activity contained a low-dimensional 160 

representation of the conditioning task whose organization generalized across 161 

environments. Calcium imaging data from session A(n) were divided into two labeled 162 

epochs corresponding to the CS/trace and US/post-US periods; we refer to this 163 

representation as CSUS2 to emphasize the segmentation of the task into two time bins 164 

(Fig. 2a). We used a nonlinear method for dimensionality reduction, the CEBRA 26 

165 

algorithm, to obtain low-dimensional latent embeddings of the population activity during 166 

task trials. 167 

The activity during each CSUS2 task trial resulted in two clusters of points in the latent 168 

space, one for activity during the CS epoch and one for activity during the US epoch. 169 

The CEBRA algorithm provides an embedding of these points using two latent 170 

dimensions, displayed on the surface of a three-dimensional sphere. The epoch labels 171 

identified structure in the latent space: the embeddings of data from environment A 172 

formed distinct and separable latent trajectories corresponding to the two task epochs. 173

Decoders trained to separate CS points from US points using data from session A(n) 174 

reliably generalized to session A(n−1) (Fig. 2b,c). For all animals, decoding 175 

performance significantly exceeded that of shuffled controls generated by permuting the 176 

task labels corresponding to data points in latent space (all two-sided t-tests, p < 0.001), 177 

indicating that the latent representation of the task was stable across sessions within 178 

the same environment. 179 

We next asked whether this latent organization generalized across contextual 180 

remapping. Activity from session B(1), the first tEBC testing in the novel environment, 181 

exhibited a similar structure to that of A(n) when projected into the latent space learned 182 

from environment A (Fig. 2d). Additionally, decoders trained using only data from 183 

session A(n) successfully distinguished the two task epochs in B(1), significantly 184 

outperforming shuffled controls generated by permuting the correspondence between 185 

latent space points and epoch labels for each animal (Fig. 2e; all two-sided t-tests, p < 186 

0.001). These results demonstrate that hippocampal population activity contains a 187 

temporally structured latent representation of the conditioning task that generalizes 188 

across environments. 189 

To determine whether finer temporal structure within the conditioning period was 190 

similarly preserved, we next used a representation of the task divided into five epochs 191 

(CSUS5): one CS epoch, two trace epochs, and two epochs spanning the US and post-192 

US periods (Fig. 2f). At this finer time resolution, the CEBRA embeddings of data from 193 

environment A showed smooth progressions through latent space, with neighboring 194 

task epochs occupying nearby positions and more distant epochs occupying positions 195 

further apart (Fig. 2g). Decoders trained to separate the five time bins in CSUS5 using 196 

data from session A(n) reliably generalized to session A(n−1) (Fig. 2g), significantly 197 

outperforming shuffled controls generated by permuting the correspondence between 198 

latent space points and epoch labels for each animal (Fig. 2h; all two-sided t-tests, p < 199 

0.001). Confusion matrices showed strong decoding along the diagonal, indicating 200 

preservation of identity of neural states corresponding to each task epoch across 201 

sessions within the same environment (Fig. 2k). 202

We next asked whether this finer latent structure generalized across contextual 203 

remapping. Activity from session B(1) exhibited a similar progression of temporal 204 

epochs when embedded using the model learned from session A(n) (Fig. 2i). Decoders 205 

trained using data from session A(n) accurately decoded activity from session B(1), 206 

again significantly outperforming shuffled controls for each animal (Fig. 2j; all two-sided 207 

t-tests, p < 0.001). Confusion matrices showed strong decoding along the diagonal, 208 

indicating preservation of the identity of neural states corresponding to each task epoch 209 

across environments (Fig. 2k). 210 

Remarkably, for both CSUS2 and CSUS5, decoding accuracy in B(1) did not differ from 211 

decoding accuracy in A(n−1) (two-sided t-tests, both p > 0.05; Fig. 2l), indicating that 212 

cross-context transfer was comparable to within-context transfer. The transfer of task 213 

information was not affected by the remapping associated with the change of context. 214 

Although animals overall showed behavioral transfer to environment B, transfer strength 215 

varied somewhat across individuals. One animal (Rat 2) showed weaker behavioral 216 

transfer during session B(1), the first testing session in the new environment; this was 217 

accompanied by reduced transfer of the latent representation. However, both behavioral 218 

performance and decoding accuracy improved substantially by session B(2), where 219 

CSUS2 decoding accuracy reached almost 70%. This significant improvement over day 220 

B(1) (two-sided t-test, p<0.001, Fig. S4) was in line with the stabilization of the 221 

transferred task representation with increased exposure to the novel environment. 222 

In contrast to the observed transfer of task representation across environments, models 223 

trained to decode spatial position in environment A did not generalize reliably to 224 

environment B, as expected given the remapping of spatial representations (Fig. S3). 225 

Thus, generalization across environments was selective for task related temporal 226 

structure and did not imply a general preservation of hippocampal population activity 227 

across environments. 228

Task related latent geometry is preserved across environments 332 

While the decoding analyses shown above demonstrate that task related information 333 

generalizes across environments, successful decoding alone does not establish 334 

whether the underlying organization of neural states is preserved. Therefore, we next 335 

examined the structure of latent task representations using several complementary 336 

analyses, including consistency scores, geometry-preservation metrics, and trajectory-337 

alignment measures. Together, these analyses assess whether task representations 338 

retain a common relational organization across environments despite hippocampal 339 

remapping. 340 

To directly test whether tEBC representations share a conserved population geometry 341 

across environments, we compared the structure of task related activity in the CEBRA 342 

embedding space. Task representations were highly similar across environments A and 343 

B, with CEBRA consistency scores (a measure of similarity between independently 344 

trained latent embeddings, see Methods) significantly exceeding controls generated by 345 

randomly shuffling time bin labels prior to model training across multiple embedding 346 

dimensions. This consistency was robust for both coarse (CSUS2) and fine (CSUS5) 347 

temporal segmentations of the task (Fig. 3a). All five rats showed significantly greater 348 

correlations in task structure across environments than shuffled controls; this effect 349 

remained significant across embeddings based on up to 10 latent dimensions (Fig. 3a-350 

b). 351

We next asked whether the relational organization of intermediate task related neural 352 

population states was preserved across environments. Latent trajectories during tEBC 353 

followed similar paths in sessions A(n) and B(1), preserving both the temporal ordering 354 

and the relative arrangement of task states across successive time bins (Fig. 3c). 355 

Geometry preservation, quantified from the pairwise distances among task states in 356 

latent space, significantly exceeded shuffled controls generated by permuting time bin 357 

labels in environment B (Fig. 3c; in all rats, no shuffled value exceeded the observed 358 

value, p = 0.002, 500 permutations; see Methods). Notably, although one animal 359 

exhibited comparatively weaker decoding transfer across environments, its latent 360 

geometry remained largely preserved, indicating that geometry preservation and 361 

decoder transfer are distinct metrics for quantifying the generalization of task 362 

representations across environments. 363 

To visualize this shared geometric structure directly, latent trajectories for each 364 

environment were aligned using the orthogonal Procrustes algorithm 27,28 , which finds 365 

the rotation (and possible reflection) that best matches corresponding task states while 366 

preserving the geometry of the latent space (Fig. 3d). After alignment, corresponding 367 

task states occupied similar relative positions across environments. Consistent with this 368 

observation, for all rats, orthogonal Procrustes error was significantly lower than 369 

expected from controls obtained by training models on shuffled time bin labels (all p < 370 

0.002; Fig. S5). This demonstrates that task trajectories were substantially more 371 

alignable across contexts than expected by chance. These results indicate that 372

Discussion 559 

Our results show that hippocampal population activity associated with a conditioning 560 

task exhibits a conserved organization across spatial contexts and across individuals. 561 

Although spatial representations in CA1 remap between environments, the relationships 562 

among task related population states were preserved across environments for each 563 

animal, and across animals. Notably, conserved geometry was observed not only 564 

across animals within the same environment, but also across animals experiencing 565 

different environments, indicating that the shared relational structure of task related 566 

population activity was maintained despite both contextual remapping and individual 567 

variability. These findings suggest that task relevant information is preserved through a 568 

stable organization of population level activity, potentially enabling learned behaviors to 569 

generalize despite changes in contextual representations. 570 

571 

Task representations are conserved across environments 572 

573 

A central finding of this study is that the relational structure of task related hippocampal 574 

population activity was conserved across distinct spatial environments even as spatial 575 

representations reorganized. This dissociation between spatial remapping and 576 

preserved task structure suggests that task related information is not reducible to spatial 577 

coding but instead emerges from stable population level organization across contexts. 578 

Our findings support frameworks proposing that hippocampal representations encode 579 

relational task structure in addition to physical space 29 . In this view, spatial and task 580 

related information coexist within a shared representational neural space, allowing task 581 

structure to remain stable even as contextual features change. Consistent with this idea, 582 

hippocampal population activity preserved the relationships among task related neural 583 

states across environments despite remapping of spatial representations. 584 

Importantly, the conditioning task used here was explicitly designed to remain identical 585 

across contexts, allowing us to isolate generalization of task structure from contextual 586 

discrimination. Under these conditions, hippocampal population activity preserved task 587 

related organization across environments, suggesting that the hippocampus can 588

Task representations are conserved across animals 592 

Unexpectedly, our results showed that the relational organization of task related 593 

hippocampal population activity was conserved across animals. This conservation 594 

occurred despite variability in hippocampal activity across individuals, including 595 

differences in spatial tuning, remapping, and the absence of correspondence at the 596 

single neuron level. This conservation extended beyond overall similarity in activity 597 

patterns and instead involved the preservation of latent task geometry across individual 598 

animals. 599 

Notably, the similarity across animals was comparable to the similarity observed across 600 

sessions within individual animals. This unexpected result suggests that hippocampal 601 

task representations follow shared organizational principles across animals rather than 602 

being entirely idiosyncratic to individual experience. Consistent with this interpretation, 603 

task decoders trained in one animal successfully generalized to another animal after 604 

latent space alignment despite the absence of shared neuron identities. Notably, 605 

successful transfer was observed even between animals experiencing different 606 

environments, indicating that the conserved relational structure was maintained across 607 

both contextual remapping and inter-animal variability. 608 

Conservation across animals is particularly notable given the hippocampus’s 609 

established role in episodic memory and its sensitivity to individual experience 33-37 . Prior 610 

demonstrations of conserved neural population dynamics across animals have largely 611 

involved stereotyped motor or motivational behaviors 38-48 . In contrast, hippocampally 612 

dependent tasks such as the conditioning task implemented and studied here involve 613 

flexible cognitive processes shaped by learning and context 49-52 . The observation that 614 

task related hippocampal population structure was conserved across animals therefore 615

Conclusion 618 

619 

Together, our findings indicate that the hippocampus supports behavioral generalization 620 

through a conserved low-dimensional organization of task related population activity 621 

across contexts and individuals. The ability to transfer task decoding across animals 622 

further suggests that shared relational structure, rather than specific neural identities, 623 

may provide a common representational framework for learned behavior. More broadly, 624 

these findings suggest that flexible cognition can emerge from conserved population 625 

level organization despite substantial variability in underlying neural representations and 626 

individual experience. 627

Video processing was done using CIATAH software 9. Videos were downsampled in 873

filtered with a Gaussian kernel with    2 pixels; neuron diameter was set at a pixel size 880 

of 8. The threshold for merging neurons was set at a calcium trace correlation of 0.65; 881 

neurons were merged if their distances were smaller than 4 pixels and they had both 882 

highly correlated spatial shapes (correlation > 0.8) and small temporal correlations 883 

(correlation < 0.4). 884 

In vivo calcium imaging involves detecting changes in intracellular calcium levels, 885 

which serve as proxies for neuronal activity. Calcium events refer to transient increases 886 

in calcium concentration above a threshold level; these crossings putatively correspond 887 

to spikes in neuronal firing. These events typically appear as peaks in the data and 888 

indicate an active response from the neuron. Calcium traces are continuous recordings 889 

of calcium levels over time. Thus, calcium events highlight specific spike-like neuronal 890 

activations, while calcium traces provide a full temporal picture of these activations 891 

together with baseline activity. 892 

All cells identified using CNMF-E were then scored as neurons or not neurons by 893 

a human scorer. Scoring was also done within CIATAH software in a MATLAB GUI. 894

Use of CEBRA versus alternative methods 931 

We explored several methods for the dimensionality reduction of Ca events before 932 

settling on the use of CEBRA for this study. All methods used the calcium data 933 

processed as described above. A short summary of each tested method can be found 934 

below: 935 

936 

• Principal Component Analysis (PCA) 13 : Principal component analysis (PCA) is 937 

a statistical method used to reduce the dimensionality of data while retaining as 938 

much variability as possible. This linear technique identifies the axes (principal 939 

components) in the dataset that maximize variance. The first principal component 940 

explains the most variance, the second explains the second most, and so on. 941 

Each principal component is a combination of the original features, in this case 942 

the activity of individual neurons; these combinations may not always have clear 943 

or intuitive meanings. In agreement with previous hippocampal data 14 , PCA 944 

required upwards of 15-25 components to capture 95% of the variance of the 945 

data. In addition, across trials within a session, across sessions and 946 

environments, and across behavioral representations (spatial and task related 947 

activity), the manifolds spanned by the largest PCs remained highly similar, with 948 

small principal angles in pairwise comparisons. This similarity in the orientation of 949 

the leading subspaces suggested that PCA did not distinguish between spatial 950 

and behavioral components of the task (Fig. S7). 951 

• Independent Component Analysis (ICA ) 15 : Independent Component Analysis 952 

(ICA) is a computational technique used to separate a multivariate signal into 953 

additive independent components. ICA operates under the assumption that 954 

observed data are linear mixtures of underlying independent sources. It aims to 955 

find a linear transformation that maximizes the statistical independence of the 956 

new components, each a linear combination of the original features. We found 957 

> .CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 10, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.10.24.620127 doi: bioRxiv preprint

that ICA embeddings were unstable throughout the length of a recording session. 958 

In addition, the embeddings did not clearly map onto behavioral states (Fig. S8). 959 

• Isomap 16 : Isomap is a nonlinear manifold learning technique that seeks to 960 

capture the intrinsic geometric structure of data. Isomap is useful when linear 961 

methods like PCA cannot capture the intrinsic structure of the data, as it 962 

preserves the pairwise geodesic (curved) distances between points when 963 

embedding them in a Euclidean space of reduced dimensionality. Unlike linear 964 

methods such as PCA, Isomap can capture nonlinear relationships in the data. 965 

Interestingly, when using Isomap only about 5 neural modes were required to 966 

account for a 90% to 95% cumulative variance. However, the embedding shape 967 

did not relate to any discernible property of either neural data or behavior (Fig. 968 

S9). Dimensionality reduction was achieved, but the resulting representations 969 

were not interpretable (Fig. S9). 970 

• MIND 14,17 : MIND is a decoding method designed for integrating multiple data 971 

modalities to predict various features, particularly sensory and motor functions. 972 

MIND uses recurrent neural networks whose hidden variables provide a 973 

mechanism for remembering previous inputs; this approach is particularly apt for 974 

the analysis of time series data such as neural recordings. While MIND was very 975 

robust at distinguishing the different environments, it was not equipped to handle 976 

relatively short signals separated in time, such as conditioning trials separated by 977 

intertrial intervals. Our analyses using MIND resulted in poor and unstable 978 

embeddings that could not be analyzed (Fig. S10). 979 

CEBRA 18 was chosen for this project for its unique ability to capture nonlinear 980 

relationships in the data and to create stable embeddings over short and long time 981 

periods. Additionally, the components that were separated in the projected latent 982 

representations were well isolated from each other and well correlated with observed 983 

behaviors. If the CEBRA model uses d latent dimensions, the data is displayed on the 984 

surface of a hypersphere embedded in a Euclidean space of dimension (d+1). 985

Model consistency 1053 

Model consistency was computed using the built-in CEBRA consistency function, 1054 

which quantifies the similarity between independently trained embeddings. Consistency 1055 

is assessed by comparing local neighborhood structure across embeddings: for each 1056 

data point in latent space, the method evaluates whether points that are its nearest 1057 

neighbors in one embedding remain nearest neighbors in the other embedding. High 1058 

consistency scores indicate that independently trained models recover similar latent 1059 

local structure despite differences in initialization. 1060 

In the present study, consistency scores were used to compare latent 1061 

embeddings across environments and across animals. Unlike the geometry-1062 

preservation analyses described below, consistency is computed directly from the 1063 

positions of individual data points within the embeddings and does not require defining 1064 

task state centroids or computing pairwise distances between centroids. In contrast, 1065 

geometry-preservation analyses instead quantified whether the relational structure 1066 

among task state representations is preserved across embeddings. 1067 

To quantify consistency between environments and across animals, CEBRA was 1068 

run 20 times independently for each session; the model achieving the lowest training 1069 

loss was selected as the representative embedding. Consistency scores were then 1070 

computed between representative embeddings from different environments and/or 1071 

different animals using the CEBRA consistency metric. Models for each animal were 1072 

trained using that animal’s optimal hyperparameters (see above). 1073

Geometry Preservation Analysis 1078 

To determine whether task state geometry was preserved when neural activity 1079 

was remapped across environments, we independently trained CEBRA on session A(n) 1080 

and session B(1) twenty times for each rat, yielding 20 latent embeddings for A(n) and 1081 

20 latent embeddings for B(1). Geometry-preservation scores were then computed for 1082 

20 arbitrarily paired A(n)–B(1) embedding comparisons, producing 20 geometry-1083 

preservation scores per rat. Because the goal of this analysis was to compare the 1084 

geometry learned by independently trained latent spaces rather than to evaluate 1085 

decoder generalization, all available data from each session were used for both CEBRA 1086 

training and embedding. 1087 

Within each embedding, latent coordinates were averaged across all data points 1088 

corresponding to each of the five CSUS5 time bins, yielding five time bin centroids per 1089 

embedding. Pairwise Euclidean distances among the five centroids were then 1090 

computed, producing a 5 × 5 distance matrix containing 10 unique pairwise distances 1091 

for each embedding. Geometry preservation was quantified as the Spearman 1092 

correlation between the distance arrays from the paired A(n) and B(1) embeddings. The 1093 

geometry-preservation value for each rat was defined as the mean of the 20 resulting 1094 

scores. 1095 

To assess significance, we generated a null distribution separately for each rat. 1096 

For each rat, the observed statistic was a single value: the mean geometry-preservation 1097 

score across the 20 independently trained CEBRA model runs. To generate the null 1098 

distribution, we repeated the following procedure 500 times. For each one of the 20 1099 

paired CEBRA runs, the labels for the five time bins were randomly permuted for the 1100 

B(1) session and the resulting geometry-preservation score was computed. The 20 1101 

shuffled scores were then averaged, yielding one shuffled mean for that null iteration for 1102 

that rat. Repeating this procedure 500 times produced a null distribution of 500 shuffled 1103 

means for that rat. One-sided permutation p-values were calculated as the proportion of 1104

It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 10, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.10.24.620127 doi: bioRxiv preprint

rat-specific shuffled means greater than or equal to the observed rat-specific mean; 1105 

higher correlation values indicate stronger preservation of representational geometry. 1106 

The same method was used to compare geometry-preservation scores across 1107 

rats, except that the distance matrix across rats was computed after averaging all task 1108 

trajectories for each rat. 1109

Embedding Visualization Using Orthogonal Procrustes Alignment 1111 

To visualize shared task structure across rats, we generated one representative 1112 

task trajectory per rat from the CEBRA embeddings of CSUS5 activity. For each rat, 1113 

embeddings for sessions A(n) and B(1) were first averaged across 20 CEBRA runs to 1114 

obtain one embedding centroid per time bin for each of these two sessions. Before 1115 

Procrustes alignment, the resulting centroid coordinates were then z-scored across time 1116 

bins within each latent dimension, separately for A(n) and B(1). Because the geometry-1117 

preservation analysis for individual rats showed that task geometry was conserved 1118 

between A(n) and B(1), the data representing environment B was orthogonal Procrustes 1119 

aligned 19,20 to the data representing environment A for each rat, using corresponding 1120 

time bins. The aligned B(1) embedding was then averaged with the A(n) embedding to 1121 

obtain a single representative A/B task trajectory for that rat. 1122 

To compare trajectories across rats, each rat’s representative A/B trajectory was 1123 

orthogonal Procrustes aligned to a common reference rat trajectory. (A single reference 1124 

rat trajectory was randomly selected only to place all rat trajectories in a common 1125 

coordinate frame; since Procrustes alignment preserves relative task bin geometry, the 1126 

choice of reference rat therefore does not define the task structure itself.) Procrustes 1127 

alignment allowed for rotations, reflections, translations, and uniform scaling while 1128 

preserving the relative geometry. The aligned trajectories were plotted in a shared 1129 

coordinate system, with points corresponding to time bins and lines connecting time 1130 

bins within each trajectory in the appropriate task order. 1131 

This visualization was used as a qualitative illustration of the conserved geometry 1132 

of the task manifold across rats; it complements the quantitative analysis of distance 1133 

correlations across rats. 1134

We performed task state decoding in CEBRA latent space to test whether the 1137 

task temporal latent structure learned from one animal could support decoding in 1138 

another animal. As opposed to matching across sessions for a given animal, matching 1139 

across animals cannot rely on the matching of individual neurons. To circumvent this 1140 

limitation, CEBRA was trained independently for each rat using that rat’s neural activity. 1141 

Transfer between animals was evaluated only after the rat specific latent spaces had 1142 

been aligned; decoder training and testing did not require shared neuron identities. 1143 

To account for variability across CEBRA initializations, we independently trained 1144 

CEBRA 20 times on session A(n) and 20 times on session B(1), using all available 1145 

neurons for each CEBRA run. We evaluated three train/test session comparisons: 1146 

A→A, B →B, and A →B. For each comparison, and for each ordered train-rat/test-rat 1147 

pair, a decoder was trained on the train rat’s CEBRA latent coordinates and evaluated 1148 

on the test rat’s latent coordinates after alignment into the train rat’s latent coordinate 1149 

frame. 1150 

The CEBRA models used to generate these latent representations were trained 1151 

on the full available activity for each session; held out partitioning was applied during 1152 

the downstream alignment and decoder evaluation procedure. For each ordered 1153 

train/test rat pair, data points in latent space were divided into two non-overlapping 1154 

subsets (50% of trials in each subset). One subset was used only to estimate the cross-1155 

rat alignment, and the other subset was reserved for decoder training and evaluation. 1156 

Before alignment, latent coordinates were z-scored independently within each 1157 

rat. This normalization removed differences in latent coordinate scale and variance 1158 

across rats, allowing alignment to focus on the relative organization of time bins rather 1159 

than the absolute magnitude of the embeddings. Within the alignment subset, centroids 1160 

were computed as mean latent coordinates for each time bin, separately for each train 1161 

and test rat. Latent spaces were then aligned using orthogonal Procrustes alignment. 1162 

The goal of the Procrustes transform was to match these time bin means, with the test 1163 

rat means treated as the source points and the train rat means treated as the target 1164 

points. The resulting transform was then applied to the reserved subset of test rat latent 1165 

coordinates. 1166 

Unlike the geometry-preservation analyses, which compare time bin centroids, 1167 

cross-animal decoding was performed on held out latent data points after Procrustes 1168

alignment. Time bin centroids were used solely to estimate the alignment, whereas 1169 

decoder training and testing used the full subset of reserved latent data points. 1170 

For these comparisons across animals, we used CEBRA to obtain low-1171 

dimensional latent embeddings. However, CEBRA was not used for decoding as 1172 

CEBRA cannot decode across different neural populations. Instead, decoding was 1173 

performed using multinomial logistic regression with balanced class weights. A decoder 1174 

was trained on the reserved latent data points for the train rat, and evaluated on the 1175 

aligned, reserved latent data points for the test rat. The entire alignment and decoding 1176 

procedure was repeated across 20 random data partitions, and the resulting decoding 1177 

accuracies were averaged. 1178 

Shuffle controls were computed by randomly permuting the time bin labels for the 1179 

train rat prior to training the decoder. This preserved the latent data structure and label 1180 

distribution for the test rat, while destroying the relationship latent between coordinates 1181 

and time bin identity for the train rat. Shuffle decoding was implemented independently 1182
