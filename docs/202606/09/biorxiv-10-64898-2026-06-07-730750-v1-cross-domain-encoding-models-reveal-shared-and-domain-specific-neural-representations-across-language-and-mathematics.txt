Abstract 17 

Whether language and mathematics rely on shared or distinct neural representations remains 18 

an unresolved question in cognitive neuroscience. Here we combine latent features from a large 19 

language model (LLM) with vertex-wise encoding models to examine cross-domain 20 

generalization between language and mathematics. Thirty-two participants performed sentence 21 

comprehension and calculation tasks during fMRI, and encoding models were trained using 22 

features embedded in a common latent space. Cross-domain prediction identified cortical 23 

regions associated with partially shared representations, most prominently the left 55b, while 24 

control analyses suggested that these effects could not be fully explained by low-level visual 25 

processing or simple task-general factors. Task-specificity contrasts revealed stronger 26 

language-related prediction in the left anterior superior temporal and angular gyri and math-27 

related prediction in the left precentral and intraparietal sulci. Model-weight analyses further 28 

showed that shared and domain-specific prediction patterns were reflected in distinct weight 29 

profiles across cortical regions. Connectivity analyses showed task-dependent functional 30 

coupling between cross-domain regions and language- or math-related networks. Together, 31 

these findings suggest that language and mathematics involve partially shared neural 32 

representations alongside domain-specific cortical organization, helping reconcile previous 33 

contrasting views on their neural basis. 34

Introduction 37 

A prevailing view in cognitive neuroscience posits that language and mathematics are 38 

supported by distinct neural circuits (Fedorenko, Piantadosi, & Gibson, 2024; Fedorenko & 39 

Varley, 2016; Mahowald et al., 2024). Neuroimaging studies on language have shown the 40 

involvement of the left inferior frontal and superior temporal cortices (Fedorenko et al., 2024; 41 

Friederici, Chomsky, Berwick, Moro, & Bolhuis, 2017; Price, 2012), whereas mathematical 42 

processing is more closely associated with the dorsolateral prefrontal and parietal cortices 43 

(Arsalidou, Pawliw-Levac, Sadeghi, & Pascual-Leone, 2018; Nieder, 2025; Nieder & Dehaene, 44 

2009). This distinction has been further supported by studies that directly contrasted brain 45 

activity between language and mathematical tasks, consistently demonstrating their 46 

dissociation (Amalric & Dehaene, 2019; Fedorenko, Behr, & Kanwisher, 2011; Monti, Parsons, 47 

& Osherson, 2012). In addition, neuropsychological evidence has shown that some patients 48 

with aphasia retain relatively preserved mathematical abilities despite severe language 49 

impairments (Benn et al., 2013; Klessinger, Szczerbinski, & Varley, 2007; Varley, Klessinger, 50 

Romanowski, & Siegal, 2005). Collectively, these findings support the view that language and 51 

mathematics rely, at least in part, on distinct neural systems. 52 

In contrast, other studies have suggested that shared neural mechanisms exist between 53 

language and mathematics. For example, some neuroimaging studies have reported 54 

overlapping activations between these two domains in the left frontal cortex (Makuuchi, 55 

Bahlmann, & Friederici, 2012; Nakai & Okanoya, 2018, 2020). Brain lesions around this 56 

region have been shown to affect both language and mathematical abilities (Baldo & Dronkers, 57 

2007; Proios, Tsakpounidou, Karapanayiotides, Priftis, & Semenza, 2021; Semenza et al., 58 

2006). The shared neural substrates may reflect structural properties common to both domains 59 

(Friedrich & Friederici, 2009; Hung et al., 2015; Nakai & Okanoya, 2018; Nakai & Sakai, 60 

2014), as mathematical expressions contain structured relationships among numbers and 61 

operators analogous to those observed in natural language (Matsumoto & Nakai, 2023; 62 

Scheepers & Sturt, 2014; Scheepers et al., 2011; Schneider, Maruyama, Dehaene, & Sigman, 63 

2012; Van de Cavey & Hartsuiker, 2016). Moreover, activity in the left frontal cortex has also 64 

been shown to reflect structural properties in geometric shapes (Al Roumi, Planton, Wang, & 65 

Dehaene, 2023; L. Wang et al., 2019), suggesting that partially overlapping cortical systems 66 

may contribute to multiple symbolic domains (Dehaene, Al Roumi, Lakretz, Planton, & Sablé-67 

Meyer, 2022; Dehaene, Sablé-Meyer, & Ciccione, 2025). Together, these findings contrast 68 

with previously reported dissociations between language and mathematics, showing a gap 69

between perspectives emphasizing neural independence and those supporting shared 70

Cross-domain encoding models could help bridge this gap by quantitatively evaluating 72 

feature-based brain representations and their generalizability (Dupré la Tour, Visconti di 73 

Oleggio Castello, & Gallant, 2025; Naselaris, Kay, Nishimoto, & Gallant, 2011). Encoding 74 

models, often leveraging latent features from large language models (LLMs), have been widely 75 

used to predict brain activity and to characterize neural representations of language 76 

(Caucheteux, Gramfort, & King, 2023; Deniz, Nunez-Elizalde, Huth, & Gallant, 2019; 77 

Goldstein et al., 2025; Huth, de Heer, Griffiths, Theunissen, & Gallant, 2016; Nakai, 78 

Yamaguchi, & Nishimoto, 2021; Tuckute et al., 2024) and mathematics (Nakai & Nishimoto, 79 

2023b, 2023a). The success of these models may partly stem from the ability of LLMs to embed 80 

both linguistic and mathematical symbol sequences within a shared latent feature space ( Figure 81 

1A ). Furthermore, by evaluating whether model predictions generalize across domains, 82 

encoding models may provide a quantitative framework for characterizing partially shared 83 

neural representations. For example, previous studies have demonstrated generalization of 84 

neural prediction across different modalities, formats, and stimulus domains, including 85 

between story listening and text reading (Nakai et al., 2021), between story listening and movie 86 

perception (Tang, Du, Vo, Lal, & Huth, 2023), and between mathematical expressions and 87 

math-word stimuli (Nakai & Nishimoto, 2023b). 88 

Here we extend this framework to characterize shared and domain-specific neural 89 

representations across language and mathematics using latent LLM features and cross-domain 90 

modeling. In this framework, the LLM served as a common feature extractor for embedding 91 

linguistic and mathematical stimuli into a shared latent space, rather than as a model of the 92 

neural computations underlying these domains. Based on previous literature (Arsalidou et al., 93 

2018; Fedorenko et al., 2024; Friederici et al., 2017; Mahowald et al., 2024; Makuuchi et al., 94 

2012; Nakai & Okanoya, 2018, 2020; Nieder, 2025; Nieder & Dehaene, 2009; Price, 2012), 95 

we hypothesized that cross-domain prediction would identify cortical regions showing shared 96 

representational components across language and mathematics, particularly within the left 97 

frontal cortex, while temporal and parietal regions would exhibit stronger domain specificity. 98 

To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) 99 

experiments with thirty-two participants who performed both sentence comprehension and 100 

calculation tasks. For each participant, we constructed vertex-wise encoding models using 100-101 

dimensional latent features extracted from sentences and mathematical expressions by the same 102 

LLM (Llama3) ( Figure 1 ). The language and mathematical stimuli were designed to have 103

Results 127 

Language and math encoding models predicted activity in the left precentral and 128 

bilateral occipital cortices 129 

We first examined whether the encoding models could predict brain activity when the training 130 

and test stimuli belonged to the same cognitive domain (i.e., intra-domain analysis ). For each 131 

participant, we trained encoding models using 100-dimensional latent features extracted from 132 

the intermediate (16 th ) layer of the LLM (Llama3). The dimensionality-reduction step was 133 

fitted only on the training stimuli and then applied to the independent test stimuli and 134 

unstructured control stimuli, ensuring that no test information was used in feature-space 135 

construction. These models were trained either on the language stimuli ( language encoding 136 

models ) or on the math stimuli ( math encoding models ). 137 

The language encoding models significantly predicted brain activity during the 138 

language test condition across large portions of the bilateral temporal, occipital, and frontal 139 

cortices (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p < 0.05; Figure 2A ,140 

shown in blue). Similarly, the math encoding models significantly predicted brain activity 141 

during the math test condition across extensive regions of the bilateral parietal, occipital, and 142 

frontal cortices ( Figure 2A , shown in red). Notably, both language and math encoding models 143 

significantly predicted activity in the dorsal portion of the left precentral gyrus, corresponding 144 

to the area 55b (Glasser et al., 2016) (see Supplementary Figure S1 for its anatomical 145 

location), as well as in the bilateral occipital cortices ( Figure 2A , shown in white). 146 

The intra-domain analysis can shed light on brain regions that contribute to both 147 

language and mathematics, confirming that our encoding models successfully predict well-148 

established brain networks associated with these two cognitive domains. However, this analysis 149 

still leaves open the possibility that activations induced by language and mathematical tasks 150 

merely overlap in these regions, without necessarily sharing similar neural representations. To 151 

address this issue, we applied the encoding models to the test stimuli that were not used for 152 

model training (i.e., cross-domain analysis ). When the math encoding models were tested with 153 

the language test dataset, we found significant predictions (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, FDR-154 

corrected p < 0.05) in somewhat reduced yet distributed regions, including the left precentral 155 

gyrus, left superior temporal gyrus, right angular gyrus, and bilateral occipital cortices ( Figure 156 

2B , shown in blue). Conversely, when the language encoding models were tested with the math 157 

test dataset, we again observed significant predictions in the left precentral gyrus, right angular 158 

gyrus, and bilateral occipital cortices ( Figure 2B , shown in red). Notably, both the language 159

To identify brain regions showing cross-domain generalization across the two domains, 162 

we performed a conjunction analysis between intra- and cross-domain predictions. Based on 163 

the minimum-statistic approach (Nichols, Brett, Andersson, Wager, & Poline, 2005), each 164

lang, math-to-math) and two cross-domain (lang-to-math, math-to-lang) analyses. The results 166 

revealed significant predictions in the left 55b, left middle cingulate cortex (SCEF), right 167 

inferior parietal cortex (PF), and bilateral lateral occipital cortices including the LO2 ( Figure 168 

2C, Table S1 ). Similar regions were identified using a block-permutation test ( Supplementary 169 

Figure S2 ). These regions were hereafter referred to as cross-domain regions .170

Cross-domain prediction extends beyond simple visual or memory factors and is 213 

reproducible across different LLMs 214 

Sentence comprehension and calculation tasks may involve various cognitive factors, including 215 

visual processing, memory retrieval, and spatial attention. The significant predictions observed 216 

in the left 55b, SCEF, right PF, and bilateral lateral occipital cortices by the encoding models 217 

might be explained by these general cognitive factors, or by methodological factors such as 218 

feature dimensionality, model capacity, or properties of LLM-derived latent features unrelated 219 

to shared representations. To address these possibilities, we performed three control analyses 220 

testing the effect of (i) stimulus datasets and task demands, (ii) low-level visual features and 221 

model dimensionality, and (iii) feature-space construction, respectively. 222 

First, to evaluate the influence of stimulus datasets and task demands, we applied the 223 

same language and math encoding models to different test datasets (i.e., the unstructured 224 

stimuli in the control condition). In the control run, the elements within each linguistic or 225 

mathematical stimulus could not be integrated into a coherent sentence or calculable expression 226 

(e.g., five unrelated verbs or five digits), thereby minimizing structural relationships among 227 

stimulus elements ( Supplementary Figure S3A ). Importantly, however, the control stimuli 228 

matched the test stimuli in the number of presented items, and participants were required to 229 

memorize these items. Thus, the demands on low-level visual processing, short-term memory, 230 

and attention were comparable between the control and test stimuli. The whole-brain 231 

conjunction analysis showed limited prediction accuracy in the left 55b with the control stimuli, 232 

while prediction patterns were relatively preserved in the bilateral lateral occipital cortices 233 

(Figure 2E top). 234 

To examine the differences in predictive performance between conditions in greater 235 

detail, we extracted mean prediction accuracy in the predetermined anatomical ROIs. These 236

A key contribution of the present study is that language and mathematical stimuli were 537 

embedded into a shared latent feature space derived from LLMs, enabling quantitative 538 

evaluation of cross-domain prediction and shared representations. This approach made it 539 

possible to predict brain activity across language and mathematics using encoding models 540 

trained in a different domain. Previous studies have investigated the overlap of brain activity 541 

between language and mathematical tasks (Makuuchi et al., 2012; Nakai & Okanoya, 2020), 542 

examined lesions in patients with aphasia and acalculia (Baldo & Dronkers, 2007), or explored 543 

cross-domain interactions through structural priming (Nakai & Okanoya, 2018). However, 544 

these studies did not incorporate computational models capable of quantitatively evaluating 545 

neural representations, and therefore could not determine whether specific brain regions exhibit 546 

partially shared representations across language and mathematics, nor could they quantitatively 547 

assess the generalization across the two domains. The present approach helps address these 548 

limitations and provides a quantitative framework for evaluating partially shared 549 

representations across language and mathematics. 550 

It should be noted, however, that our primary aim was to quantitatively characterize the 551 

commonalities and distinctions between language and mathematics in the brain, rather than to 552 

claim that these domains are represented identically within the LLM. While our results suggest 553 

a partial alignment between neural activity and LLM-derived representations, consistent with 554 

recent studies reporting parallels between biological and artificial neural networks (Caucheteux 555 

et al., 2023; Goldstein et al., 2022; Schrimpf et al., 2021), the internal representations within 556 

the LLM do not necessarily align completely with the neural representations underlying 557 

language and mathematics. In the current study, we used the LLM purely as a feature extractor, 558 

and the correspondence between the brain and the model was not the primary focus of our 559 

investigation. Although the LLMs used in the current study (Llama3, Gemma2, Qwen2.5, 560 

GPT-NeoX, RakutenAI2, and LLM-jp) sufficiently captured latent structure shared across 561 

language and mathematics, they are not necessarily the optimal models. Therefore, a recent 562

The left 55b, in which cross-domain prediction was observed for both language and 567 

mathematics in the present study, was first reported in the 1950s in relation to language 568 

processing but was largely overlooked until it was revisited by the Human Connectome Project 569 

(Glasser et al., 2016; Hopf, 1956). The left 55b is located in the posterior portion of the middle 570 

frontal gyrus, inferior to the frontal eye field and superior to the ventral premotor cortex. 571 

Anatomically, it is characterized by a lower myelin content compared to the surrounding 572 

cortical regions. It has been reported to show higher activation than adjacent areas during 573 

sentence comprehension tasks and to be functionally connected to other language-related 574 

regions such as the IFG (Glasser et al., 2016). Subsequent studies have shown the involvement 575 

of this region in speech production (Chang et al., 2020; Hazem et al., 2021) and in sensorimotor 576 

integration during music perception (Siman-Tov et al., 2022; Zalta, Large, Schön, & Morillon, 577 

2024), suggesting that this region contributes to multiple cognitive functions across domains. 578 

Regarding mathematical cognition, previous meta-analyses have demonstrated involvement of 579 

the left middle frontal gyrus across multiple types of mathematical operations (Arsalidou & 580 

Taylor, 2011; Istomina & Arsalidou, 2024). Although these studies were based on volume-581 

based analyses, their findings are consistent with the possibility that the left 55b contributes to 582 

neural representations shared across language and mathematics. In contrast, previous studies 583 

investigating shared processing between language and mathematics have reported the 584 

involvement of the left IFG (Area 44) (Makuuchi et al., 2012; Nakai & Okanoya, 2018, 2020). 585 

This discrepancy in the reported cortical loci may stem from the fact that most previous studies 586 

employed volume-based analyses, which overlook sulcal folding patterns. In the present study, 587 

we constructed vertex-wise (i.e., surface-based) encoding models to examine how language-588 

and math-related representations are organized across gyri and sulci. Moreover, surface-based 589 

analysis allowed us to avoid the spatial smoothing that can lead to signal contamination, 590 

thereby making our encoding models more sensitive to local activity patterns (Oosterhof, 591 

Wiestler, Downing, & Diedrichsen, 2011; Tucholka, Fritsch, Poline, & Thirion, 2012). 592 

In addition to the left 55b, the left SCEF, bilateral LO2, and right PF were identified as 593 

cross-domain regions. According to previous literature, the left SCEF contributes to the control 594 

of both oculomotor sequences and action sequences, including the selection, ordering, and 595 

monitoring of sequential behaviors (Abzug & Sommer, 2018; Cona & Semenza, 2017; Nachev, 596

Kennard, & Husain, 2008). The SCEF has also been identified as part of the core multiple-597 

demand (MD) network (Assem, Shashidhara, Glasser, & Duncan, 2022; Camilleri et al., 2018), 598 

suggesting a role in cognitive processes shared across multiple task domains. Consistent with 599 

this, recent fMRI studies show that core MD regions such as the SCEF and adjacent sensory-600 

biased regions including area 55b form interleaved functional zones rather than isolated 601 

modules (Assem et al., 2022). Although LO2 is classically defined as part of the visual cortex, 602 

converging evidence indicates that it supports higher-level visual representations such as shape, 603 

configuration, and object structure (Cichy, Chen, & Haynes, 2011; MacEvoy & Epstein, 2011). 604 

Recent encoding model studies further indicate that such high-level visual representations are 605 

aligned with representations in large language models and can generalize across different 606 

stimulus modalities (Doerig et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2023). However, the LO2 did not show a 607 

significant difference between structured test stimuli and unstructured control stimuli ( Figure 608 

2), suggesting that its contribution may not be specific to structured symbolic processing. 609 

Instead, LO2 may reflect higher-level visual or stimulus-format representations that are shared 610 

across the language and math tasks. In contrast, the right PF should be interpreted with caution. 611 

Although this region survived the conjunction analysis and therefore met our operational 612 

definition of a cross-domain region, its representational and connectivity profiles differed from 613 

those of the left 55b, SCEF, and LO2. Specifically, the right PF showed weight distributions 614 

and connectivity patterns distinct from those of the other cross-domain regions. This suggests 615 

that the right PF may not represent a core region shared between language and mathematics. 616 

Instead, it may reflect a parietal interface supporting symbolic manipulation, quantity-related 617 

processing, or attentional control that is recruited by both domains to different degrees. 618 

The language specificity and math specificity analyses revealed involvement of the 619 

temporal and parietal areas, respectively. These results are consistent with previous studies 620 

reporting that language and mathematics are supported by distinct neural networks (Fedorenko 621 

et al., 2024; Fedorenko & Varley, 2016; Mahowald et al., 2024): language primarily involves 622 

fronto-temporal areas, whereas mathematics engages the fronto-parietal areas. Within the 623 

frontal cortex, the middle frontal gyrus has been associated more strongly with mathematics 624 

(Fedorenko et al., 2024; Friederici et al., 2017; Price, 2012), while the inferior frontal gyrus 625 

has been linked with language (Arsalidou et al., 2018; Nieder, 2025; Nieder & Dehaene, 2009). 626 

It should be noted, however, that these findings are not contradictory to our encoding model 627 

results suggesting partially shared neural representations for language and mathematics. The 628 

encoding model likely captures both shared and domain-specific information, and our analyses 629 

captured distinct contributions from these components. Indeed, the weight-correlation analysis 630

Several limitations should be noted regarding the present approach. First, the structural 656 

variety of sentences and mathematical expressions was limited. We included only 657 

mathematical expressions containing two operations, and no stimuli with a single operation 658 

(e.g., “2 + 3”). This design was based on the observation of limited prediction performance in 659 

the single-operator problems in our previous study (Nakai & Nishimoto, 2023a). Therefore, it 660 

remains unclear whether the left 55b and other regions also play roles when participants 661 

perform single-operator problems. It is also unclear how activations in this region, if they 662 

reflect processing of structured symbol sequences, are modulated by the complexity of 663 

structural relationships. Previous studies have reported increased brain activity in the left IFG, 664

Stimuli and procedures 702 

The current experiment comprised four conditions: a language target condition, a language 703 

control condition, a math target condition, and a math control condition. Stimuli in the language 704 

target condition consisted of 200 sentences, 160 of which were used for model training 705 

(referred to as language training stimuli ) and 40 for model testing (referred to as language test 706 

stimuli ). Each sentence contained five Japanese words and expressed a complex noun phrase 707 

with a relative clause (e.g., “risu-wo otta neko-ga hei-kara ochita” [squirrel-ACC chase-PAST 708 

cat-NOM fence-ABL fall-PAST]: “The cat that chased the squirrel fell off the fence”). The 709 

sentences followed a consistent syntactic structure, with variations in case particles (e.g., wo ,710 

de , ni , kara ) depending on the semantic roles required by the verbs. 711 

Stimuli in the language control condition consisted of 40 word lists, all of which were 712 

used only for model testing (referred to as language control stimuli ). Each word list comprised 713 

five unrelated words, consisting either only of nouns or only of verbs, that did not form a 714 

grammatical sentence. In the noun lists, each noun was accompanied by a case particle, whereas 715 

the verb lists consisted of inflected verbs (e.g., “ kiita, oeta, naoshita, kaita, ushinatta ”716 

[hear.PAST finish.PAST cure.PAST write.PAST lose.PAST]). Thus, the language control 717 

condition was designed to be comparable to the language target condition at the word level, 718 

while minimizing sentence-level grammatical structure. 719 

Stimuli in the math target condition consisted of 200 mathematical expressions, 160 of 720 

which were used for model training (referred to as math training stimuli ) and 40 for model 721 

testing (referred to as math test stimuli ). Each expression contained five symbols (three single-722 

digit numbers and two operators) and represented a two-operator arithmetic problem (e.g., “7 723 

× 6 + 3”). The expressions followed a consistent structure, with variations in operators (“+”, 724 

“−”, “×”, and “/”). 725 

Stimuli in the math control condition consisted of 40 digit lists, all of which were used 726 

only for model testing (referred to as math control stimuli ). Each list contained five isolated 727 

single digits that did not form a calculable mathematical expression (e.g., “1, 8, 2, 7, 5”). 728 

Operators were not included in this condition because the number of operator types was limited. 729

Vertex-wise encoding model fitting 854 

In the encoding model, each vertex’s cortical activity was modeled using a finite impulse 855 

response model. This model captured the slow hemodynamic response and its association with 856 

neural activity (Kay, David, Prenger, Hansen, & Gallant, 2008; Nishimoto et al., 2011). A 857 

feature matrix of dimensions [T × 4N] was constructed by concatenating sets of [T × N] feature 858 

matrices, with four temporal delays ranging from 3 to 6 s (where T = number of samples; N = 859 

number of features). The cortical response of dimensions [T × V] was then modeled by 860 

multiplying the feature matrix by the weight matrix of dimensions [4N × V] (where V = number 861 

of vertices). The weight matrix was obtained using L2-regularized linear regression with the 862 

training samples. The optimal regularization parameter was determined using 5-fold cross-863 

validation, where the regularization parameters varied from 1 to 10 6 across seven different 864 

values. As a result, two encoding models were obtained for each subject. These models were 865

Functional connectivity analyses 903 

To elucidate how the brain regions associated with mathematics and language, identified 904 

through the basic prediction accuracy analysis of the encoding model, are functionally related 905 

to language-specific and math-specific regions, we conducted two types of analyses. First, in 906 

the functional connectivity analysis, the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated between 907 

the activity time course extracted from a seed anatomical ROI (averaged across vertices within 908 

the ROI), and that of each vertex and each of the remaining anatomical ROIs across the cortex 909 

for each training run of the math target and the language target conditions. Functional 910 

connectivity was then defined as the mean correlation coefficient across training runs; one 911 

functional connectivity map was then obtained for each of the language and math conditions. 912 

Second, to test whether the functional connectivity is indeed related to the latent 913 

representations captured by encoding models, we performed a similar analysis using predicted 914 

brain responses by the intra-domain encoding models. Pearson correlation coefficient was 915 

calculated between the predicted responses extracted from a seed anatomical ROI (averaged 916 

across vertices within the ROI) and those of each cortical vertex and each of the remaining 917 

anatomical ROIs for both the language and math encoding models. 918

Behavioral data analysis 1189 

To confirm that participants were sufficiently engaged with the experimental tasks, we 1190 

analyzed accuracy for trials in which a probe stimulus was presented (20% of all trials). 1191 

Participants performed both the language and math target conditions with high accuracy 1192 

(>90%). In the language domain, accuracy was significantly higher in the target condition than 1193 

in the control condition (Target: M = 0.912, SD = 0.046; Control: M = 0.863, SD = 0.118; 1194 

Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.039). In contrast, no significant difference was observed 1195 

between the target and control conditions in the math domain (Target: M = 0.958, SD = 0.049; 1196 

Control: M = 0.968, SD = 0.064; p = 0.252). Thus, the control conditions were not easier than 1197 

the corresponding target conditions; if anything, the language control condition was more 1198 

difficult than the language target condition. 1199 

1200 

Block-permutation test 1201 

To further assess whether the cross-domain prediction results were statistically reliable while 1202 

accounting for the temporal structure of the fMRI responses, we performed a block-1203 

permutation test. For each participant and prediction type, the test-run time series was divided 1204 

into consecutive blocks of 10 samples. The order of these blocks was then randomly permuted, 1205 

thereby disrupting the temporal correspondence between the predicted and observed responses 1206 

while preserving the local temporal structure within each block. Prediction accuracy was 1207 

recalculated after each block permutation. 1208 

This procedure was repeated 5,000 times. For each permutation, prediction accuracies 1209 

were averaged across participants to generate a null distribution of group-mean prediction 1210 

accuracy at each cortical vertex. The observed group-mean prediction accuracy was then 1211 

compared with this permutation-based null distribution. A one-sided p value was computed as 1212 

the proportion of permutations in which the group-mean prediction accuracy was equal to or 1213 

greater than the observed value. The resulting significance maps were FDR-corrected for 1214 

multiple comparisons across cortical vertices using the same procedure as in the main analysis, 1215 

and the conjunction analysis across intra-domain and cross-domain predictions was repeated 1216 

using these block-permutation-based significance maps. 1217

Validation of joint PCA as a common feature space 1219 

A potential concern with applying joint PCA to language and math features is that a common 1220 

projection space may fail to preserve the dominant variance structure of one or both domains. 1221

To assess whether joint PCA provided an appropriate common feature space for language and 1222 

math representations, we examined how much variance in each domain was retained after 1223 

projection onto the joint PCA components. Because joint PCA is fit to the concatenated 1224 

language and math features, it does not necessarily maximize variance within either domain 1225 

alone. In contrast, separate PCA provides an upper-bound estimate of how much variance can 1226 

be captured when dimensionality reduction is optimized independently for each domain. We 1227 

therefore compared the variance explained by the first 100 components of the joint PCA with 1228 

that explained by the first 100 components of separate PCAs fit to the language and math 1229 

features independently. 1230 

The first 100 components of the joint PCA explained 63.1% of the variance in language 1231 

features and 99.4% in math features. In contrast, the first 100 components of separate PCAs 1232 

explained 70.1% and 99.8%, respectively. As expected, separate PCA preserved more within-1233 

domain variance than joint PCA, but the reduction associated with using a common projection 1234 

space was modest. These results indicate that joint PCA retained substantial variance in both 1235 

domains while allowing language and math features to be represented in a shared feature space. 1236 

The particularly high variance explained for math features likely reflects the restricted 1237 

structure of the math stimuli. In the present experiment, the math stimuli consisted of relatively 1238 

simple expressions, such as “7 × 6 + 3,” composed of single-digit numbers and two arithmetic 1239 

operators. Thus, compared with the language stimuli, the math stimuli contained a more limited 1240 

set of constituent elements, which may have resulted in a lower-dimensional feature structure 1241 

and consequently higher variance explained by the first 100 components. 1242

Residual-response encoding analysis 1258 

To examine whether the encoding results were driven by mean task-evoked response 1259

separate nuisance regressors. Run intercepts, linear trends, and quadratic trends were also 1264 

included as nuisance regressors. The residual time series were then used as response variables 1265 

in the same LLM-based ridge regression pipeline as in the main analysis. Time points 1266

as in the main analysis. The same residualization procedure was applied to the original test runs, 1268
