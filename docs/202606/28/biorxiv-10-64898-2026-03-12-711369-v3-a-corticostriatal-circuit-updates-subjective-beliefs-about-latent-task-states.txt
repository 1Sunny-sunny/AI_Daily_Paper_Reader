Beliefs about states of the world profoundly impact decision-making and learn-4

ing, but little is known about how neural circuits represent and update beliefs. 5

We performed projection-specific recordings and perturbations from neurons 6

in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) projecting to the intermediate or rostral cau-7

date putamen (CPi/CPr) in rats performing a task with hidden reward states. 8

Stimulating OFC →CPi neurons biased rats’ beliefs towards high reward states. 9

Recordings from optogenetically-tagged OFC →CPi neurons showed that they 10 

encoded categorical evidence for high reward states, shaped by a saturating non-11 

linearity in neural responses. Downstream neurons could, in principle, decode 12 

the full belief distribution over reward states as rats deliberated about deci-13 

sions. Finally, projection-specific perturbations disrupted encoding of hidden 14 

states within OFC. These findings reveal the circuit implementation of a core 15 

cognitive computation, updating subjective beliefs about abstract latent states 16

# Main Text 18 

Inference is a core computation that is important for many cognitive functions, including 19 

perception, motor control, and decision-making ( 1). In partially observable environments, state 20 

inference is required for forward mental simulations, planning, or goal-directed decision-making 21 

(2–4). Uncertainty about the current state can powerfully impact behavior, including by dynam-22 

ically adjusting the rate at which animals learn from new experiences ( 5–9). Moreover, aber-23 

rant belief updating is a hallmark of schizophrenia and other neuropsychiatric disorders ( 10 –12 ). 24 

While the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) has been implicated in inferring values or partially observ-25 

able task contingencies ( 13 –18 ), how the connectivity patterns and dynamics of synaptic inputs in 26 

downstream circuits mediate computations for inference is unclear. Here, we uncover the circuit 27 

mechanisms by which OFC neurons that target the intermediate caudate putamen (CPi) represent 28 

and causally update beliefs about latent task states in rats performing a rich decision-making task. 29

OFC →CPi neurons bias beliefs 78 

We bilaterally injected a retrograde Cre virus in the CPi or CPr and a Cre-inducible excitatory 79 

opsin (ChR2.0) in the OFC, and implanted a tapered fiber optic among OFC →CP axon terminals 80 

in the striatum (Fig. 1M). In optogenetic sessions, we delivered blue light (465nm) for 500 ms 81 

aligned to the time of reward delivery on 40 % of trials. This manipulation caused rats to wait less 82

light was delivered at the time of reward, but the behavioral effect was apparent on trials in which 84 

rats opted out and received no reward, which are by definition different trials from the stimulation 85 

trials. Notably, the optogenetic effect size depended on the block, with the largest effect in low 86 

blocks, an intermediate effect in mixed blocks, and the weakest effect in high blocks (Fig. 1P). 87

We sought to use our behavioral model to generate a hypothesis about the cognitive pro-97 

cess that was disrupted by optogenetic stimulation. The model estimates the most likely block 98 

(P (Bt|Rt)) by combining the likelihood of observing the current reward in that block ( P (Rt|Bt))99 

with the prior belief about the block ( P (Bt)), where the prior is recursively updated over trials. 100 

We found that if we implemented a biased prior, such that the model is biased to believe it is in 101 

a high block and against believing it is in a mixed or low block, this reduces how long the model 102 

is willing to wait, with the largest change in low blocks, intermediate change in mixed blocks, 103 

and weakest change in high blocks (Fig. 1T,U). Intuitively, the biased prior induces mistaken in-104 

ferences in the model, increasing the probability that it will use the opportunity cost or wait time 105 

threshold associated with a high block. Because the opportunity cost of the low block is the most 106 

different from a high block, the strongest effect on wait times is in low blocks, and intermediate 107 

in mixed blocks. Even an ideal Bayesian observer would make mistaken inferences on about 10 %108 

of trials ( 8); the biased prior eliminates mistaken inferences in high blocks, thereby also modestly 109 

reducing wait times in those blocks. 110

Decodable belief distributions 216 

Based on the responses of the OFC →CPi neurons, and our optogenetic results, belief updating 217 

appears to occur at the time of reward. We next wondered if the OFC →CPi neurons might 218 

represent subjective beliefs about the blocks, perhaps at other times in the trial. The posterior 219 

distribution over blocks is a discrete probability distribution with three values on each trial, one 220 

for the posterior probability of each block. Since the probabilities must sum to one, being able 221 

to decode any two of those probabilities allows one to estimate the third, i.e., know the full 222 

probability distribution (Fig. 4A). 223 

Because the block probabilities are highly correlated with the reward offers in this task, we 224 

computed the average firing rate of OFC →CPi neurons for trials offering 20 μL, with similar 225 

maximum a posteriori estimates (i.e., probability for the most likely block) but different full 226 

posterior distributions. Specifically, we selected trials where the most likely block was a mixed 227 

block, with a high probability ( >= 0.8), and the second most likely block was either a high or a 228 

low block with a low probability ( <0.05). Given that the reward (20 μL) and most likely block 229 

(mixed) are all identical on these trials, any differences in firing rates would be consistent with 230 

reflecting the full posterior distribution. Trials meeting these strict criteria were rare, and are 231 

represented by a subset of red and blue dots in the mixed block sector (black shaded region) in 232 

Figure 4A. Despite the limited trials, there was a significant difference in the average firing rates 233 

of OFC →CPi neurons across these conditions, prior to reward delivery while the animals waited 234

Uncertainty in sensory and motor domains has been studied for continuous variables, and ex-340 

perimental evidence has supported various neural coding schemes in these domains, including 341 

probabilistic population codes ( 46 –49 ), distributed distributional codes ( 50 ), and neural sampling 342 

codes ( 51 –54 ). However, none of these traditional coding schemes are applicable to dynamic 343 

inference of categorical (as opposed to continuous) variables. While previous studies have shown 344 

that dopamine neurons can reflect uncertainty about binary categorical variables ( 55 , 56 ), the 345 

simple nature of this problem makes it impossible to dissociate representation of the mean from 346 

that of the full distribution. Our work is consistent with studies showing that uncertainty is rep-347 

resented in higher order cortical regions ( 39 , 57 –59 ), but provides novel insight into how specific 348 

projection classes explicitly represent probability distributions over discrete, categorical variables 349 

for dynamic inference of latent states. These representations were dynamic, as OFC →CPi neu-350 

rons reflected the belief distribution at trial start and as rats waited for rewards, but not at the 351 

time of reward, when they encoded high block evidence. In contrast, the CPr may not be behav-352

OFC →CPr neurons, or specific task variables encoded by that population. 354

The recent proliferation of large-scale recording methods in neuroscience has led to a 368 

paradigm shift that emphasizes the analysis of neural populations over the activity of single neu-369 

rons ( 62 , 63 ). We highlight two points raised by our findings. First, most cognitive tasks are 370 

complex and involve multiple algorithmic computations (e.g., working memory, state inference, 371 

deliberation between alternatives). In the absence of perturbations, which computation is actually 372 

supported by the piece of tissue under study is often unclear. In our recent study, persistent inac-373 

tivation of OFC with muscimol impaired rats’ ability to update their beliefs about the blocks ( 18 ). 374 

This is consistent with our present findings, in which transiently stimulating OFC →CPi neurons 375 

did update rats’ beliefs, but in a biased, directional way (towards high blocks), due to the tuning 376 

of the OFC →CPi projection neurons, which on average have higher firing rates for high block 377 

evidence. Perturbations were critical for generating the hypothesis that OFC →CPi neurons in-378 

stantiate the belief updating computation. It is not clear that we would have inferred this function 379 

from recordings alone. 380

# Materials and Methods 555 

Rats 556 

593 Long-evans rats ( Rattus norvegicus ) between the ages of 6 and 24 months were used in 557 

this study (367 males, 226 females). Rats were water restricted to motivate them to perform the 558 

task. At the end of the day, rats were given 20 minutes of access to ad libitum water. Following 559 

behavioral sessions on Friday until mid-day Sunday, rats received ad libitum water. Animal 560 

use procedures were approved by the New York University Animal Welfare Committee (UAWC 561 

#2021-1120) and carried out in accordance with National Institute of Health standards. 562 

Behavior training and task logic 563 

Rats were trained in incremental stages of task complexity in a high-throughput behavioral 564 

facility using a computerized training protocol as described previously ( 8). 565 

LED illumination from the central nose port indicated that the animal could initiate a trial 566 

by poking its nose in that port. Upon trial initiation the center LED turned off. While in the 567 

central nose port, rats needed to maintain center fixation for a duration drawn uniformly from 568 

[0.8, 1.2] seconds. During the fixation period (“Offer cue”), a tone played from both speakers, 569 

the frequency of which indicated the volume of the offered water reward for that trial [1, 2, 4, 8, 570 

16 kHz, indicating 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 μL rewards for male rats and 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 μL for female 571 

rats]. Female rats consumed less water due to their spaller body size and performed substantially 572 

fewer trials than the males when offered the same rewards as the males. For analysis, the reward 573 

volumes were treated as equivalent to the corresponding values for the male rats. Following 574 

the fixation period, one of the two side LEDs was illuminated, indicating that the reward might 575 

be delivered at that port (“Reward port on”); the side was randomly chosen on each trial. This 576
