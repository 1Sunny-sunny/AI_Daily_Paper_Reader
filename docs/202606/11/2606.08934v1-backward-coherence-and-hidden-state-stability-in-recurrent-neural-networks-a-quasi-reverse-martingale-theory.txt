# Backward Coherence and Hidden-State Stability in Recurrent Neural Networks: A Quasi-Reverse-Martingale Theory 

## Yuan-chin Ivan Chang

Abstract 

Recurrent neural networks maintain a hidden state ht whose probabilistic meaning has remained largely uncharacterised. We develop a theory of hidden-state stability via backward coherence : the degree to which ht can be recovered from its successor ht+1 through a learned backward projector gϕ. Under a contraction condition and summable backward drift, the hidden-state sequence is shown to be a quasi-reverse-martingale , implying almost-sure convergence with a rate under mixing, an interpretable limit representation, and pathwise stopping rules with finite stopping times. A framework for time-uniform confidence sequences is also established theoretically. Simulation experiments confirm the core predictions: backward-coherence regularisation reduces the empirical quasi-martingale total ˆQ by 43–58%, reaches the stability threshold 28–44% earlier than the unregularised baseline, and produces tracking-error recovery consistent with the geometric bounds. Two further experiments probe additional predictions: a proxy-free echo-state forgetting test confirms empirical rates are bounded above by ρ, and a direct computation of the increment-sum tube Rt confirms the pathwise enclosure of Proposition 3.18 (ii) at 100% simultaneous coverage; Rt is valid but loose in practice (median infla-tion 17–30), making the defect-tail proxy ˆQt the practically useful monitoring instrument. Scope and limitations are discussed in Section 5. These guarantees apply when the stated conditions hold; universality is not claimed. Minimising the backward-coherence loss is equivalent to minimising a Kullback–Leibler divergence in a Gaussian backward model, connecting to variational infer-ence. Extensions cover ϕ-mixing inputs, geometric tracking-error bounds at change points, and finite-sample concentration inequalities. Three real-data studies validate the theory: on the PhysioNet 2012 ICU Challenge, the Re-verse Martingale RNN (RMRNN) matches RNN mortality-prediction AUC whilst reaching a stable representation 13 hours earlier in a 48-hour stay; on FRED-MD macroeconomic data, it reduces one-month-ahead forecast error approximately fourfold relative to the unregularised RNN under concept drift; and on UCI Human Activity Recognition, post-transition tracking error decays geometrically and RMRNN maintains a consistently lower tracking error than the unregularised baseline, consistent with the theoretical bound.

# 1 Introduction 

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are the canonical tool for sequential learning. Given an input sequence x1, x 2, . . . ∈ Rd, an RNN maintains a hidden state ht ∈ Rp

updated by ht = fθ(ht−1, x t), accumulating information about the past and providing a running representation used for prediction or control. Gated variants—the Long Short-Term Memory (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) and the Gated Recurrent Unit (Cho et al., 2014)—use multiplicative gates to selectively retain or discard information, enabling practical learning over long sequences. Despite widespread empirical success, a fundamental probabilistic question has remained unanswered: 

does the hidden state ht converge, and if so, to what? Without an answer, the practitioner cannot know when to trust the network’s representation, how to set stopping rules, or what statistical meaning to attach to the accumulated summary. The question arises in clinical sequential prediction, where ht encodes a patient’s evolving physiological profile and an early stable representation enables earlier clinical decisions (Rajpurkar et al., 2022; Reyna et al., 2020; Wiens et al., 2019); in macroeconomic forecasting, where structural breaks and slow concept drift require the hidden state to track a shifting target distribution; and in activity and regime recognition, where abrupt discrete switches demand fast recovery of the hidden state to its new steady-state mean. A complete theory of hidden-state dynamics must simultaneously guarantee almost-sure pathwise convergence of ht, provide a statistically interpretable limit identifiable as a conditional expectation, automatically control the first moment without auxiliary assumptions, and yield anytime-valid sequential confidence sets without a pre-specified sample size. Standard analytical tools each address part of this problem but leave at least one requirement unmet. Lyapunov stability (Khalil, 2002) certifies boundedness of the deterministic map but provides no statistical interpretation of the limit. Ergodic theory and Markov chain methods (Meyn and Tweedie, 2009) guarantee convergence of time-averages but not of individual sample paths, and do not identify the limit as a conditional expectation. Forward martingale convergence requires uniform integrability for both L1 convergence and conditional-expectation identification of the limit, a condition that cannot in general be verified without prior knowledge of the limit itself. Bayesian state-space models (S¨ arkk¨ a, 2013; Shumway and Stoffer, 2000) satisfy all four requirements but only under correct parametric specification—a strong and often unverifiable assumption for modern deep networks. We argue that reverse martingales (Doob, 1953; Neveu, 1975) provide the correct framework. A reverse martingale is adapted to a decreasing filtration F1 ⊇ F 2 ⊇ · · · 

and satisfies E[Mn | F n+1 ] = Mn+1 almost surely. As the conditioning set contracts, the sequence converges almost surely and in L1 to a limit identified as a conditional expectation by L´ evy’s backward theorem—with no uniform integrability assumption required. Rather than imposing a Gaussian or linear model on backward dynamics, as in the Kalman smoother (S¨ arkk¨ a, 2013) or Bayesian state-space methods (Shumway and Stoffer, 2000), we train a backward projector gϕ as an empirical risk minimiser for the conditional mean E[ht | ht+1 ] . The resulting loss LRM is a distribution-free quasi-likelihood objective that requires only first-moment structure of the backward dynamics, replacing brittle parametric assumptions with an approach valid under model misspecification. Remark 3.7 (Gated architectures: when contraction holds and when it does not) .

Theorem 3.2 extends to GRU and LSTM architectures provided Assumption 3.1 holds for the effective hidden-to-hidden Jacobian. For an LSTM, the cell-state Jacobian is ∂c t/∂c t−1 = diag (ft), so the effective contraction factor is ρ∗ = sup t ∥ft∥∞. Since

In such settings the theorem does not apply and the framework should not be used without modification. There are, however, important and commonly encountered settings where ρ∗ < 1is plausible, verifiable, and empirically beneficial: (i) short- to medium-horizon sequential prediction tasks—clinical early warning, activity recognition, short-term macro forecasting—where deliberate recency bias in forget-gate initialisation or regularisation prevents saturation near 1 and is already standard practice; (ii)

These three settings collectively cover a substantial share of applied sequential modelling practice. In each, ρ∗ is estimable from the empirical distribution of ∥ft∥∞

## 4.2 Real-data experiments 

Three publicly available datasets are used to evaluate RMRNN against baseline models on tasks that represent three canonical sources of non-stationarity in sequential learning: noisy clinical observations accumulating over time, slow macroeconomic concept drift spanning decades, and rapid discrete regime switching between activity states. Together they test whether backward-coherence regularisation preserves predictive performance under very different temporal structures, and whether the theoretical quantities ˆQ and τδ carry interpretable meaning on real data. Each study compares four models: the Elman RNN baseline ( λ = 0), RMRNN (λ = 0 .1), bidirectional RNN (BiRNN), and a domain-appropriate state-space comparator (Kalman filter / linear dynamical system for the clinical and activity studies; BVAR(4) for the macroeconomic study). All neural models share the same architecture (hidden dimension p = 32, Adam optimiser, learning rate 10 −3) and differ only in whether the backward-coherence penalty LRM is active. Evaluation follows a 20-seed repeated cross-validation protocol (20 × 3 = 60 fold observations per model) with all random seeds derived deterministically from a single base seed (2026) for reproducibility. Replication scripts are included in the supplementary archive. The state-space comparators serve two roles: as practical benchmarks showing where classical models stand relative to neural approaches, and as theoretical reference points , since the Kalman smoother backward pass is the exact backward projector gϕ∗Backward Coherence and Hidden-State Stability 24 

in the linear-Gaussian case (Section 2.3). It is therefore expected to attain small ˆQ

by construction; the neural models must earn comparable stability through learning. 

Domain 1: Clinical risk prediction under irregular observation (PhysioNet 2012 ICU Challenge). Why this domain. Clinical ICU sequences represent a setting where observations arrive irregularly, measurements are missing at random, and the information content of the hidden state grows non-uniformly across the 48-hour stay. Backward-coherence regularisation is expected to suppress hidden-state drift during periods of low information arrival and to yield an identifiable stabilisation point τδ that is clinically meaningful—ideally well before hour 48 so that predictions can be trusted earlier in the stay.

The PhysioNet 2012 Challenge dataset (Silva et al., 2012) comprises 8,000 ICU patient records (sets A and B combined), each a 48-hour multivariate time series of 12 vital signs and laboratory measurements (heart rate, MAP, temperature, respiratory rate, etc .) resampled to hourly bins with forward-fill imputation. In-hospital mortality is 14.0%. The target is binary mortality prediction; evaluation uses area under the ROC curve (AUC) and the Brier score. The Kalman filter / LDS baseline is fitted by EM with state dimension 8 and 5 iterations; its final smoothed state feeds a logistic regression classifier. The present framework subsumes echo state networks (Jaeger, 2001): any network satisfying ρ(Wh) < 1 and D∞ + P

The computable diagnostic ˆQ captures this probabilistic structure; stochastic approximation theory (Kushner and Yin, 2003; Robbins and Monro, 1951) characterises trajectory dynamics but provides no such scalar summary. The backward projector gϕ is the discriminatively trained counterpart of the Kalman smoother backward pass (S¨ arkk¨ a, 2013; Shumway and Stoffer, 2000): both approximate E[ht | ht+1 ], but gϕ requires no Gaussian linear model assumption and is trained end-to-end. Proposition 3.17 establishes that minimising LRM is equivalent to minimising a KL divergence in a Gaussian backward model (Blei et al., 2017). Recurrent latent-variable models (Chung et al., 2015; Fraccaro et al., 2016) are Backward Coherence and Hidden-State Stability 29 

generative and augment the forward pass with explicit stochastic latent variables; RMRNN is discriminative and requires none. A systematic comparison on sequence density-estimation tasks is left for future work. Structural constraint approaches—Lipschitz recurrent networks (Erichson et al., 2021; Miller and Hardt, 2019) and spectral normalisation (Miyato et al., 2018)—all satisfy Assumption 3.1; Theorem 3.2 provides the probabilistic convergence charac-terisation that these architectural choices enable but do not themselves establish. The quasi-martingale decomposition via Rao (1969) and Krickeberg (1956) is here applied to the vector-valued hidden-state process and the backward filtration, a combination we believe is new. The two auxiliary experiments in Section 4 probe specific predictions without claiming exhaustive confirmation. Experiment 6 confirms Corollary 3.4 as an upper bound: empirical forgetting rates do not exceed ρ, with tanh saturation producing faster-than-predicted decay. Isolating the ϕ-mixing component requires averaging over input realisations rather than initial conditions; a linear RNN, where the two contributions are analytically separable, or a non-equilibrium design with a known input-mixing time, would be needed. Experiment 7 shows that the increment-sum tube Rt is valid (100% simultaneous coverage by the triangle inequality) but loose in practice (median inflation 17–30, reflecting non-monotone hidden-state paths), and that the defect-tail proxy ˆQt is the practically useful tube ( C∗ = 1 .15 for 95% coverage). Proposition 3.10 bounds the deviation of h∞ from the Krickeberg limit M∞ by the computable budget D∞ + P 

> t

εt in L1, providing an operational characterisation of h∞ as a near-conditional-expectation. Several open problems arise from the present work. (i) Backward Markov sufficiency from first principles. Assumption 3.3 is verified empirically; deriving it for specific activations or gate structures would make the framework fully self-contained. (ii) Growing hidden dimension. The coordinatewise proof requires fixed p; extending to growing p requires uniform coordinate control. (iii) Non-expansive case. Convergence at ∥Wh∥ = 1, arising in residual networks (He et al., 2016), requires Opial’s lemma (Opial, 1967) or asymptotic regularity. (iv) Non-asymptotic rate bounds. Explicit bounds E[∥ht − h∞∥] ≤ f (p, ρ, t ) would support theoretically grounded architecture selection. (v) Adaptive rolling window. Online adaptation of the window width when ∆ k is unknown (Gama et al., 2014) would remove the last free tuning parameter from the change-point extension. (vi) L2 perturbation bound. A bound of the form ∥h∞ − M∞∥L2 ≤ g(D∞) would complement the L1 result of Proposition 3.10. (vii) Broader architecture ablation. A systematic study across activation functions, network depth, and GRU/LSTM base architectures would strengthen the em-pirical case beyond the Elman RNN used here. (viii) Transformer analogue. Defining a backward filtration for attention-based models (Vaswani et al., 2017) and extending the quasi-reverse-martingale theory to this setting is an important direction given the practical dominance of transformer-based sequential models.
