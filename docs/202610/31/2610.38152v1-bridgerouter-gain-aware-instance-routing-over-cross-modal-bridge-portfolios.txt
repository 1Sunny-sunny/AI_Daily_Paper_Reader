# ABSTRACT 

Lightweight representation bridges make pretrained, task-adapted EEG founda-tion models executable on other biosignal modalities without retraining their back-bones. However, selecting a single bridge pathway globally assigns every instance to the same transfer route and ignores complementary errors across routes. We therefore formulate multi-bridge transfer as an instance-level algorithm-selection problem and introduce B RIDGE ROUTER , a gain-aware router over a portfolio of frozen cross-modal pathways. A validation-selected Single Best Solver (SBS) serves as a protected default route. During training, task labels are used to com-pute the reduction in negative log-likelihood provided by each alternative route relative to the SBS, and a lightweight MLP predicts these route-specific gains from a target-modality representation extracted by a frozen encoder. At infer-ence, B RIDGE ROUTER observes only the target modality, requires neither EEG, task labels, nor predictions from all candidate routes, and executes exactly one bridge pathway: the route with the largest predicted gain when that gain ex-ceeds a validation-calibrated threshold, and the SBS otherwise. We evaluate seven EEG foundation-model bridge pathways for EMG-based freezing-of-gait detec-tion on FOG and ECG-based sleep classification on ISRUC. Across five seeds, BRIDGE ROUTER achieves 81 .38 ± 1.11% balanced accuracy on FOG, improving over the BIOT SBS by 11 .49 percentage points, and 62 .92 ± 2.17% on ISRUC, improving over the CBraMod SBS by 3.83 points. Per-instance bridge oracles reach 99 .96% and 78 .61% , respectively, revealing substantial remaining portfo-lio headroom. These results show that anchor-relative gain prediction can convert complementary cross-modal bridge behavior into deployable instance-level rout-ing improvements while retaining single-route inference. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

What if the best model for transfer is not the best model for every instance? Cross-modal transfer provides a natural way to reuse knowledge from pretrained biosignal foun-dation models when the source modality is unavailable at deployment. For example, a pretrained EEG model can be connected to EMG or ECG through a lightweight representation bridge learned from paired recordings (Li et al., 2026). When multiple pretrained EEG models are available, a natural strategy is to build a bridge for each model and simply choose the one with the best average performance. However, average performance can hide an important property of a transfer portfolio: different pathways can fail on different instances . A pathway that is weaker overall may still correctly predict examples that a stronger pathway misses. In such a case, its value does not come from being the best standalone model, but from the errors it can correct. This suggests a different question: 

Given the prediction we already have, can another transfer pathway do better on this instance? 

Answering this question is challenging because the information needed to determine the correct pathway is unavailable at inference. During training, paired EEG–target recordings and task labels 1arXiv:2610.38152v1 [cs.AI] 28 Oct 2026 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 reveal which pathways succeed or fail on each instance. At deployment, however, only the target modality is observed. A useful router must therefore learn pathway complementarity from training-time transfer outcomes while making its decisions entirely from target-modality information. We introduce BridgeRouter , a gain-aware framework for instance-level selection among comple-mentary cross-modal pathways. We construct a portfolio of target-side executable pathways, each combining a frozen pretrained EEG foundation model with a lightweight bridge. One pathway is se-lected on the validation set as the Single Best Solver and serves as the default route. During training, the router learns the task-label-derived reduction in negative log-likelihood provided by each alter-native pathway relative to this anchor. At inference, the router predicts these anchor-relative gains using only the target-modality representation. It selects the most promising alternative when its pre-dicted gain exceeds a validation-calibrated protection threshold and selects the anchor otherwise. The routing decision is made before pathway execution, so exactly one selected bridge pathway is executed for each instance. BridgeRouter therefore treats pathway selection as a conditional decision rather than a global model-selection problem. We evaluate BridgeRouter on two cross-modal biosignal transfer tasks: adapting pretrained EEG foundation models to EMG inputs for freezing-of-gait detection and to ECG inputs for sleep clas-sification. We examine whether different bridge pathways exhibit complementary errors, whether their anchor-relative gains can be predicted without access to EEG at inference, and whether adaptive routing improves over the validation-selected SBS. Fixed-route performance, per-instance bridge or-acles, and rescue-harm diagnostics are used to characterize the available portfolio headroom and the behavior of the learned routing policy. Our contributions are: • We formulate cross-modal transfer with multiple pretrained source models as an instance-level pathway selection problem, emphasizing the complementary errors that distinguish portfolio value from standalone performance. • We propose BridgeRouter , which learns task-label-derived, anchor-relative negative-log-likelihood gains from target-modality representations and performs target-modality-only, single-route selection at inference. • We evaluate gain-aware routing across EEG-to-EMG and EEG-to-ECG transfer tasks and quan-tify bridge complementarity through fixed-route performance, per-instance oracle headroom, and rescue-harm analyses. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 EEG F OUNDATION MODELS AND CROSS -M ODAL REPRESENTATION TRANSFER 

Recent EEG foundation models (FMs) leverage self-supervised pretraining on large-scale unlabeled EEG recordings to learn transferable representations for downstream neural decoding tasks. Repre-sentative models, including BENDR Kostas et al. (2021), BIOT Yang et al. (2023), EEGPT Wang et al. (2024), LaBraM Jiang et al. (2024), and CBraMod Wang et al. (2025), adopt different archi-tectures, pretraining objectives, and data sources. Recent benchmark studies have further shown substantial variation in their downstream performance across datasets and tasks, suggesting that the choice of a pretrained EEG representation can depend strongly on the target setting Xiong et al. (2026). Beyond within-modality adaptation, recent work has explored transferring pretrained biosignal rep-resentations across modalities. Lightweight representation bridges provide a practical way to con-nect a frozen source foundation model to a target biosignal modality without retraining the source model. Such approaches make pretrained representations reusable when the target modality has lim-ited labeled data or when a model pretrained on another modality provides useful prior knowledge. However, when multiple source foundation models are available, each can induce a different cross-modal transfer pathway through its corresponding bridge. Existing cross-modal transfer methods typically evaluate these pathways independently and select a fixed model or bridge based on ag-gregate performance. They therefore do not explicitly consider whether different pathways make complementary predictions on individual target instances. 2Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 2.2 MODEL ROUTING AND PER -I NSTANCE MODEL SELECTION 

A complementary line of work studies conditional model selection, where different inputs can be processed by different models or computational pathways. Mixture-of-experts methods dynamically route inputs to specialized experts, while model cascades and conditional computation adapt the amount or type of computation according to the input Shazeer et al. (2017); Wang et al. (2018). More generally, per-instance model selection aims to exploit heterogeneity in model behavior rather than relying on a single model for all inputs. These approaches motivate adaptive selection in a transfer portfolio, but cross-modal transfer in-troduces an additional constraint. At training time, paired source–target recordings and task la-bels can reveal how different pathways behave on the same instance. At deployment, however, the source modality may be unavailable, and executing every source pathway may defeat the purpose of lightweight transfer. Thus, a routing policy must infer the usefulness of alternative pathways from information available on the target side alone. BridgeRouter addresses this setting by predicting anchor-relative pathway gains solely from the target-side representation and selecting a single route before bridge execution, without requiring access to the source modality or executing the entire pathway portfolio at inference. 2.3 ALGORITHM SELECTION AND COMPLEMENTARY MODEL PORTFOLIOS 

Automated Algorithm Selection (AS) studies how to select an algorithm from a portfolio according to the characteristics of an individual problem instance Rice (1976); Smith-Miles (2009). AS has been applied to domains including Boolean satisfiability, combinatorial optimization, and machine learning. A central motivation is that algorithms with different strengths can perform differently across instances, making instance-specific selection potentially more effective than committing to a single globally strongest algorithm. Traditional AS methods often learn performance predictors from handcrafted instance features, while more recent approaches employ learned representations, including convolutional and graph-based encoders, to automatically characterize instances Loreggia et al. (2016); Cao et al. (2025). Recent work has extended this perspective to foundation-model selection. EEG-AS Zhang et al. (2026) formulates EEG foundation model selection as an instance-level AS problem and learns to estimate foundation-model behavior from inference-available EEG representations and an anchor model. Its formulation demonstrates that foundation-model portfolios can benefit from instance-dependent selection rather than relying exclusively on a Single Best Solver. However, EEG-AS con-siders selection among pretrained EEG models within the same modality. In contrast, BridgeRouter considers a portfolio of cross-modal transfer pathways , where each candidate pathway combines a pretrained source model with a target-side bridge. This distinction also changes the selection objective. Rather than predicting which pathway is glob-ally best or reconstructing the absolute performance of every candidate, BridgeRouter focuses on the relative benefit of switching from an anchor pathway to an alternative pathway. This formulation explicitly exploits complementary errors: an alternative pathway is valuable when it succeeds on instances where the anchor fails, even if its overall performance is lower. BridgeRouter therefore connects automated algorithm selection with cross-modal transfer by treating pathway complemen-tarity as a source of instance-level adaptive performance.

# 4 EXPERIMENTS AND DISCUSSION 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We evaluate on two multimodal physiological-signal datasets: the freezing-of-gait (FOG ) dataset Zhang et al. (2022) and the ISRUC sleep dataset Khalighi et al. (2016). FOG contains synchronized EEG and EMG recordings from 12 participants. We use three-second windows and obtain 37,200 paired EEG–EMG windows after preprocessing for binary FOG classification. For IS-RUC, we use Subgroup I with synchronized EEG and ECG recordings. Following BioX-Bridge Li et al. (2026), the original five sleep stages are grouped into Wake versus Sleep, with each 30-second epoch treated as one sample. We use six EEG derivations (F3–A2, C3–A2, O1–A2, F4–A1, C4–A1, and O2–A1). Thus, EEG is the old modality, while EMG and ECG are the new modalities for FOG and ISRUC, respectively. Further preprocessing details are given in Appendix A.1. 

Data Partitioning. We follow the partitioning protocol of BioX-Bridge Li et al. (2026). Each dataset is divided into Dold , Dpair , Dval , and Dnew , with approximate proportions of 33%, 33%, 11%, and 22%. Dold is used to train task-specific heads for the frozen EEG models, Dpair is used for bridge and routing training, Dval is used for model selection, and Dnew is used only for final evaluation. ISRUC is split by subject, so no subject appears in more than one subset. FOG follows the sample-wise BioX protocol and therefore does not evaluate unseen-subject generalization. The detailed FOG split is reported in Appendix A.1. 6Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 

Foundation Models and Bridge Routes. We evaluate seven pretrained EEG foundation models: LaBraM Jiang et al. (2024), EEGPT Wang et al. (2024), REVE El Ouahidi et al. (2026), BIOT Yang et al. (2023), CBraMod Wang et al. (2025), CSBrain Zhou et al. (2026), and BENDR Kostas et al. (2021). We freeze all EEG backbones and train a linear task head on Dold . For the new modalities, we use pretrained NormWear for EMG Luo et al. (2025) and HuBERT-ECG for ECG Coppola et al. (2024), which are also frozen. Each EEG model defines one cross-modal bridge, giving seven candidate routes per dataset. Bridge layer positions are selected following BioX-Bridge. 

Baselines and Comparators. We first evaluate each bridge pathway independently. The single best solver (SBS) is selected exclusively by validation balanced accuracy and frozen before test evaluation. This procedure selects BIOT on FOG and CBraMod on ISRUC. The SBS is never reselected according to test performance. Our primary method is B RIDGE ROUTER , a gain-aware instance-level routing framework over cross-modal bridge pathways. It predicts the instance-specific reduction in negative log-likelihood of each candidate route relative to the validation-selected SBS. The router retains the SBS unless the largest predicted gain exceeds a threshold selected using validation data. It uses task-label-derived gain targets during training but requires neither labels nor bridge predictions at inference. It observes only the target-modality representation and executes one selected bridge pathway. We additionally report fixed-route performance and per-instance bridge oracles. The fixed routes establish the performance of each available pathway without routing. The oracles quantify portfolio complementarity by selecting a correct pathway using the unknown ground-truth label. They are non-deployable and are not used for training, model selection, or calibration. 

Implementation Details. BRIDGE ROUTER uses the pooled NormWear block-6 representation on FOG and the 512-dimensional HuBERT-ECG block-4 representation on ISRUC. Its MLP contains hidden dimensions 256 and 128, GELU activations, and dropout of 0.2. The model predicts the route-specific negative log-likelihood gain relative to the validation-selected SBS and is trained using class-balanced supervision. For each instance, the route with the largest predicted gain is identified. The SBS is retained unless this gain exceeds a protection threshold selected on the validation split. At inference, the router ob-serves only the target-modality representation and executes one bridge pathway. It does not require the test label or the predictions of all candidate bridges. We evaluate seeds 42-46 while keeping all bridge checkpoints and data partitions fixed. 

Evaluation Metrics. Balanced accuracy is the primary metric because both tasks are class-imbalanced. We additionally report accuracy and macro F1. For routing diagnostics, a rescue occurs when the SBS is incorrect and the selected alternative is correct; a harm occurs when the SBS is cor-rect and the selected alternative is incorrect. All stochastic routing results report mean and sample standard deviation over five seeds. 4.2 FROZEN BRIDGE PERFORMANCE

Every ISRUC seed improves over CBraMod in balanced accuracy. The ISRUC improvement is smaller and more variable than the FOG improvement. This is consis-tent with its subject-disjoint protocol and the pronounced validation-to-test pathway-ranking shift. The result shows that relative-gain supervision transfers across the two tasks, but it does not imply dominance over every fixed test pathway. In particular, EEGPT and BENDR obtain higher ISRUC test balanced accuracy than the five-seed router mean, although neither was selected as the SBS using validation balanced accuracy. Relative to the corresponding SBS-to-oracle gaps, the router recovers approximately 38.2% of the available FOG headroom and 19.6% of the ISRUC headroom. At inference, the router observes only the target-modality representation, requires no test label, and executes one bridge pathway. Thus, its computational cost is substantially lower than evaluating all seven routes. The ISRUC result must nevertheless be interpreted conservatively. Its five-seed switch rate is highly variable, largely because one seed switches substantially more often than the remaining four. More-over, the router mean remains below the strongest fixed test pathways. The consistent improvement over the validation-selected SBS demonstrates useful instance-level gain prediction, while the re-maining oracle gap shows that reliable cross-subject route selection remains unresolved. 4.4 CROSS -D ATASET FINDINGS

# 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented B RIDGE ROUTER , an instance-level routing framework for cross-modal transfer from pretrained EEG foundation models. Rather than relying on a single globally selected bridge, our approach learns to predict the relative task-loss gain of candidate bridges and selects an alterna-tive route only when the predicted gain is sufficiently large. This enables instance-adaptive bridge selection while executing only one bridge pathway at inference time. Across the FOG and ISRUC datasets, we found substantial complementarity among frozen EEG-to-target-modality bridges. The proposed router improves over the validation-selected single best solver by 11.49 and 3.83 percentage points in balanced accuracy on FOG and ISRUC, respectively. At the same time, the remaining gap to the oracle and the smaller and more variable routing gains under the subject-disjoint ISRUC setting highlight the difficulty of reliable instance-level routing under distribution shift. These results suggest that cross-modal transfer can benefit from treating pretrained foundation mod-els as a portfolio of complementary bridge routes rather than committing to a single global choice. Future work will investigate more robust routing under cross-subject and cross-domain shifts, as well as richer signals for estimating when a bridge is likely to provide a meaningful transfer gain. A.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We evaluate our method on two multimodal physiological-signal benchmarks: the freezing-of-gait ( FOG ) dataset Zhang et al. (2022) and the ISRUC sleep dataset Khalighi et al. (2016). These datasets represent two distinct clinical prediction settings and provide synchronized recordings from multiple physiological modalities. The FOG dataset contains synchronized EEG and EMG recordings from 12 participants performing gait-related tasks. We segment each recording into three-second windows. The EEG signals are resampled to 200 Hz, producing six-channel windows with 600 temporal samples. The synchro-nized EMG windows are processed using the preprocessing pipeline associated with the pretrained NormWear encoder. After preprocessing and quality filtering, the FOG dataset contains 37,200 paired EEG–EMG windows for binary FOG classification. ISRUC is a polysomnographic sleep-staging dataset containing synchronized EEG and ECG record-ings from multiple participants. We use Subgroup I of the ISRUC-Sleep dataset, which contains overnight polysomnographic recordings with synchronized EEG and ECG signals. Sleep stages are annotated at 30-second resolution. We use the six EEG derivations F3–A2, C3–A2, O1–A2, F4–A1, C4–A1, and O2–A1, together with the synchronized ECG signal. Each annotated 30-second epoch is treated as one sample for the downstream binary classification task. Following BioX-Bridge Li et al. (2026), we collapse the original five sleep stages into a binary Wake-versus-Sleep task, where Wake is retained as one class and all sleep stages are grouped into the other. In both datasets, we consider EEG as the old modality, and EMG and ECG as the new modalities for FOG and ISRUC, respectively. 11 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 Table 4: FOG12 data partitioning. Data Partitioning Protocol. For direct comparison with BioX-Bridge Li et al. (2026), we follow its dataset-specific partitioning protocol. For each dataset, the available recordings are divided into four subsets: Dold , Dpair , Dval , and Dnew , with approximate proportions of 33%, 33%, 11%, and 22%, respectively. Here, Dold provides labeled observations from the old modality for training the task-specific heads of the frozen foundation models; Dpair contains synchronized old- and new-modality observations for learning the cross-modal bridges and transfer signals; Dval is used for model selection and calibration; and Dnew is reserved for final evaluation. For ISRUC, partitioning is performed at the subject level, such that all epochs from a given participant are assigned to a single subset and no participant is shared across Dold , Dpair , Dval , and Dnew .The resulting FOG split is summarized in Table 4. The FOG evaluation follows the BioX-style sample-wise protocol and is therefore not an unseen-subject evaluation. 

EEG Foundation Models and Bridge Routes. We evaluate seven EEG foundation models: LaBraM Jiang et al. (2024), EEGPT Wang et al. (2024), REVE El Ouahidi et al. (2026), BIOT Yang et al. (2023), CBraMod Wang et al. (2025), CSBrain Zhou et al. (2026), and BENDR Kostas et al. (2021). Each model is initialized using its publicly released pretrained weights. For both datasets, we freeze the EEG backbone and train only a task-specific linear classification head on Dold . Both FOG and ISRUC are formulated as binary classification tasks. For the new-modality encoders, we use pretrained NormWear Luo et al. (2025) to encode EMG in FOG and pretrained HuBERT-ECG Coppola et al. (2024) to encode ECG in ISRUC. Both encoders remain frozen throughout bridge training and evaluation. We construct one bridge for each EEG foundation model, yielding seven candidate routes per dataset. For every route, we follow the layer-position selection procedure of BioX-Bridge to determine the source layer in the new-modality encoder and the destination layer in the corresponding EEG foundation model.
