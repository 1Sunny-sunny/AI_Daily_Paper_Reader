ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as computational models of human language processing, yet it remains unclear which components of their representations drive alignment with neural activity. Here, we use ridge reduced-rank regression to identify the brain-predictive subspace of LLM representations: the representational directions that support linear prediction of neural responses. Across three naturalistic language-listening datasets spanning intracranial electro-physiology and fMRI, and six model conditions spanning autoregressive, bidi-rectional masked, and randomly initialized language models, we find that full linear encoding performance can be recovered using only 1–3% of the original embedding dimensionality. These low-dimensional subspaces generalize across held-out participants and, to a lesser extent, across datasets, suggesting that neu-ral predictivity is concentrated in a compact and partially shared representational space. We next compare these brain-predictive directions with the principal axes of variation in the embeddings. Principal component analysis requires substan-tially more dimensions to recover neural prediction, demonstrating that the direc-tions most useful for predicting neural activity are distinct from those capturing the most overall variance within LLM representations themselves. Finally, char-acterizing the learned subspaces allows us to surface interpretable features that are overrepresented in neural activity above and beyond the LLM representations. Together, these results show that LLM–brain alignment is supported by a small, structured subset of model representations and provides a framework for better understanding exactly why these representations align. Code can be found at: https://github.com/thefinnlab/low-rank-alignment 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are strong predictors of human neural activity during language pro-cessing and are increasingly used as computational models of language comprehension (Schrimpf et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2022; Mischler et al., 2024; Zada et al., 2024). Yet it remains unclear which components of their high-dimensional representations drive this alignment. Prior work has related neural predictivity to properties such as training objective, model scale, layer depth, intrinsic dimensionality, and representational generality (Antonello et al., 2023; Antonello & Huth, 2024; Antonello & Cheng, 2024). However, these model-level relationships do not identify the specific representational directions that support neural prediction, and apparent differences in alignment can also be influenced by confounds in the evaluation process (Hadidi et al., 2026). Here, we take a complementary approach. Rather than asking which global properties of a model correlate with its neural predictivity, we ask which part of the model representation actually sup-ports that prediction. Conceptually, we view LLM–brain alignment as arising from information shared between features represented by an LLM and measured neural responses (figure 1a). Under 1arXiv:2610.40511v1 [cs.CL] 29 Oct 2026 Preprint. a linear encoding model, we operationalize this shared information as a brain-predictive subspace of the LLM representation: the set of representational directions that are useful for predicting neural activity. This framing turns a single encoding score into a representational object that can itself be studied. Across three naturalistic language-listening datasets spanning intracranial electrophysiology and fMRI, and six different language models we use reduced rank regression (RRR) (Izenman, 1975) to discover this brain-predictive subspace and find that full linear encoding performance can be recovered from only 1–3% of the original embedding dimensions. These brain-predictive sub-spaces generalize across held-out participants and, partially, across datasets. We then compare each learned subspace against one learned by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) applied only to the LLM embeddings. We find that there is a mismatch between the directions of principal variance in the embeddings as learned from an unsupervised PCA and the directions most useful for neu-ral prediction; suggesting differences in the representational geometry of the two systems. Finally, feature-decoding analyses reveal interpretable differences in the linguistic information emphasized by these subspaces across datasets and recording modalities. Together, these results show that LLM– brain alignment is supported by a compact, structured subset of model representations and provide a framework for identifying which components of those representations drive neural predictivity. 

2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION 

2.1 LANGUAGE ENCODING MODELS 

Encoding models test whether a candidate representational space captures information present in neural responses. In language neuroscience, this approach has progressed from investigator-defined semantic features to contextual representations learned by neural language models, which predict ac-tivity across fMRI, MEG, and intracranial recordings (Huth et al., 2016; Jain & Huth, 2018; Toneva & Wehbe, 2019; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2022; Caucheteux & King, 2022). LLM representations are effective encoding spaces because they capture many aspects of language simultaneously, but this also complicates their interpretation. A single layer may contain thousands of distributed features reflecting lexical, syntactic, semantic, contextual, and predictive information, any combination of which may support neural prediction. Thus, successful encoding establishes that neurally relevant information is linearly accessible from the representation but does not reveal which components carry that signal. Prior work has related neural predictivity to properties including next-word prediction performance, model scale, layer depth, representational generality, intrinsic dimensionality, and particular linguis-tic or computational features (Schrimpf et al., 2021; Antonello & Huth, 2024; Antonello & Cheng, 2024; Kumar et al., 2024; Mischler et al., 2024). These results constrain possible explanations of LLM–brain alignment but primarily characterize representations globally rather than identifying the specific directions responsible for prediction. 2.2 BRAIN -P REDICTIVE SUBSPACES 

Prior work suggests that both language representations and their relationship to neural activity ex-hibit low-dimensional structure. Antonello et al. (2021) identified low-dimensional organization in linguistic representations related to fMRI predictivity, while Lewis et al. (2025) used low-rank constraints to factorize LLM-to-MEG encoding models into interpretable semantic, temporal, and spatial components. These approaches motivate a more direct question: how much of an individual LLM representation is actually required to recover its neural predictivity? Reduced-rank regression provides a super-vised way to answer this question by identifying low-dimensional directions chosen specifically for their ability to predict neural responses. This distinguishes the brain-predictive subspace from un-supervised low-dimensional structure, such as the principal variance directions identified by PCA, and provides a representation whose dimensionality, generalization, and linguistic content can be studied directly. 2Preprint.

3 METHODS 

3.1 DATASETS 

We analyzed three datasets of neural responses to naturalistic spoken language. The Podcast ECoG dataset contains high-gamma (70–200 Hz) recordings from nine participants and 1,330 intracra-nial electrodes collected during a 30-minute narrative podcast (Zada et al., 2025). Brain Treebank contains stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG) recordings from participants viewing movies (Wang et al., 2024); we analyzed Cars 2 , which contained the most heard words ( ∼8,000) viewed by at least three participants. The Deep fMRI dataset contains responses to spoken stories collected across five recording sessions (LeBel et al., 2023); we analyzed eight participants and evaluated encoding mod-els on the repeated held-out test story. 3.2 PREPROCESSING 

We used the released high-gamma responses for Podcast and preprocessed Brain Treebank record-ings to extract 70–200 Hz high-gamma activity. Both intracranial datasets were aligned to word onset and sampled at 32 Hz. We used the released preprocessed fMRI responses. Full preprocessing details are provided in Appendix A.1. 3.3 LANGUAGE MODELS 

We extracted word representations from four autoregressive models—GPT-2, GPT-2 XL, GPT-J 6B, and Llama 3 8B—and the bidirectional masked language model RoBERTa Large (Radford et al., 3Preprint.

For each contextual model, we sampled five layers at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% depth. Additional embedding-generation details are provided in Appendix A.1.1. 3.4 SELECTION OF NEURAL TARGETS 

Because our goal was to estimate the rank of the subspace supporting general neural alignment, we used a broad target-selection procedure designed to retain reliable signal linking LLM embeddings to neural activity while excluding clearly noisy targets. We selected recording sites using held-out predictions from full-embedding ridge encoding models. Significance was assessed against null distributions generated by circularly shifting neural responses, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across recording sites. For iEEG, selected electrodes contributed all sampled lags as RRR targets; for fMRI, selected voxels were used directly. Additional selection details and target counts are provided in Appendix A.5. 3.5 ENCODING MODELS 

We first fit ridge encoding models using the full embedding space to establish a reference perfor-mance for each model and layer. For iEEG, word embeddings predicted electrode responses at sam-pled lags relative to word onset, with performance evaluated using five contiguous held-out word folds. For fMRI, embeddings were aligned to acquisition times and used to construct a four-delay finite impulse response design; models were evaluated on held-out stories. Ridge penalties were selected by cross-validation. 3.6 REDUCED -RANK REGRESSION 

We used ridge reduced-rank regression (RRR) (Izenman, 1975) to identify the optimal rank k sub-space of embeddings for neural prediction (Figure 1b). Within each training partition, we fit a multivariate ridge model from embeddings ( X) to neural responses ( Y ), producing coefficients ( W )and fitted responses ( ˆY = XW ). We decomposed ( ˆY ) and used its leading ( k) right singular vec-tors ( Vk) to construct an input factor ( Ak = W V k), whose columns span embedding directions associated with the leading components of predicted neural activity. 4Preprint. At each rank ( k), we projected embeddings into an orthonormalized version of this subspace and fit a new ridge encoding model using the projected data. We swept ranks from one to the avail-able embedding-dimensionality endpoint to determine how quickly neural prediction was recovered as the bottleneck increased (figure 1c). Performance was evaluated on held-out neural responses, with the full-embedding ridge model serving as the reference. For fMRI, we combined the delay-specific RRR factors into a single orthonormal basis in the original embedding coordinates; details are provided in Appendix A.2.2. We compared RRR with principal component analysis (PCA) of the training embeddings (figure 1b). At each rank, embeddings were projected onto the leading principal components and evaluated using the same ridge refitting and held-out data. 3.7 SATURATION RANK 

To summarize how quickly predictive performance was recovered as rank increased (figure 1c), we defined a saturation rank using the distribution of performance across neural targets. At each rank, we computed the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of held-out target performance and tracked each quantile across the rank sweep. Saturation rank was defined as the minimum rank at which all informative quantile curves reached at least 99% of their respective maximum perfor-mance. Quantiles whose maximum performance did not exceed 0.005 were excluded. This criterion captures recovery across the target-performance distribution rather than relying on mean perfor-mance alone. Additional motivation and threshold-sensitivity analyses are provided in Appendix A.3. 3.8 GENERALIZATION ANALYSES 

To assess cross-participant generalization, we held out one participant at a time and learned an RRR subspace from the remaining participants (figure 1d). We transferred this subspace to the held-out participant and fit a new encoding model using only that participant’s neural responses. We then compared transferred RRR with RRR learned from the held-out participant and with PCA across ranks (figure 1e). For cross-dataset transfer, we learned an RRR or PCA subspace from a source dataset and projected the target dataset’s embeddings into that space. A new ridge model was then fit and evaluated using only target-dataset neural responses. Thus, in both generalization analyses, only the embedding subspace was transferred; the mapping from that subspace to neural activity was estimated separately in the target participant or dataset. 3.9 LANGUAGE -FEATURE DECODING 

To characterize the information preferentially represented in brain-predictive subspaces, we anno-tated stimulus words with lexical, syntactic, semantic, discourse, timing, and pause features. At each rank, we projected embeddings into the RRR and PCA spaces and trained held-out decoders for each available feature. Continuous features were evaluated using Pearson correlation and cate-gorical features using balanced accuracy. The complete feature set and decoding details are provided in Appendix A.7. 

4 RESULTS 

4.1 ALIGNMENT RELIES ON A LOW -R ANK SUBSPACE 

To estimate the dimensionality of the LLM representation required to recover its full neural pre-dictivity, we fit ridge reduced-rank regression (RRR) in each of our three datasets and measured encoding performance as we varied the rank of the predictive bottleneck (Figure 2; see Appendix for full encoding-model results). Across datasets, models, and layers, performance typically peaked at ranks substantially below the full embedding dimensionality, with low-rank RRR models often outperforming the corresponding full-space ridge baseline (Figure 2a). This improvement was not observed in PCA-based dimensionality reduction, indicating that it is not simply a consequence 5Preprint.

Instead, the result is consistent with RRR preferentially re-taining directions that are informative for predicting neural responses while discarding dimensions that contribute little useful predictive signal. Moreover, RRR saturates at only tens of dimensions across all three datasets and across model layers—for example, a mean rank of 20 in the Podcast dataset—whereas PCA generally requires hundreds of dimensions to achieve comparable perfor-mance (Figure 2c). Thus, the directions that support neural prediction constitute a much lower-dimensional subspace than would be inferred from the dominant axes of variance in the embeddings themselves. This low-rank finding is consistent across models, layers, and recording modalities. Whether the same brain-predictive subspaces generalize across participants, stimuli, and datasets, however, remains an open question addressed in the following analyses. 4.2 THE LEARNED LOW -R ANK SUBSPACE GENERALIZES ACROSS HELD -O UT SUBJECTS 

We next asked whether the brain-predictive subspace learned from one group of participants gen-eralizes to an unseen participant (Figure 1d). If the same low-dimensional embedding directions support neural prediction in a held-out participant, this would indicate that the learned subspace reflects structure shared across individuals rather than idiosyncratic properties of a particular partic-ipant’s neural responses. Across all three datasets, transferred RRR subspaces recovered near-maximal neural prediction us-ing only tens of dimensions (Figure 3). The rank required to reach 99% of peak performance was substantially lower for transferred RRR subspaces than for PCA across datasets, indicating that the cross-participant result cannot be explained simply by retaining the dominant dimensions of variation in the embeddings. We additionally compared transferred subspaces with RRR sub-spaces learned using the held-out participant’s responses. In the Podcast and fMRI datasets, the transferred subspaces required a comparable number of dimensions—and in some cases fewer—to reach saturation, whereas the Cars 2 dataset showed a larger difference between transferred and participant-inclusive RRR. These results indicate that much of the low-dimensional structure sup-porting LLM–brain alignment is shared across participants and can be identified without observing neural responses from the participant on whom it is subsequently evaluated. More broadly, the ability to transfer these subspaces across participants motivates a stronger test of their generality: whether the same predictive directions also transfer across datasets, stimuli, and neural recording modalities. 6Preprint.

We next asked whether brain-predictive subspaces generalize across datasets differing in partic-ipants, stimuli, recording modality, and preprocessing. For each directed source–target pair, we learned an RRR subspace from the source dataset, transferred it to the target embeddings, and fit a new ridge model using only target neural responses such that only the embedding subspace was transferred. Reduced transfer performance could therefore reflect either differences in neurally pre-dictive directions across datasets or differences in the stimulus distributions used to estimate those directions. Transferred RRR remained predictive but recovered performance less efficiently than RRR learned within the target dataset (Figure 4a). Because these transferred curves saturated more gradually, we summarized normalized rank–performance curves using area under the curve (AUC), with larger AUC indicating more efficient recovery of predictive performance. Across directed dataset pairs and model layers, transferred RRR had lower AUC than within-dataset RRR but significantly higher AUC than transferred PCA for every dataset pair (Figure 4b). Thus, brain-predictive subspaces contain structure that generalizes across datasets, although this structure is not fully invariant to changes in stimulus and recording setting. We next asked whether some of this RRR transfer loss could be explained by differences in the stim-uli themselves. To obtain a neural-response-independent measure of cross-dataset representational shift, we performed the same transfer analysis using PCA, which is estimated solely from the stim-ulus embeddings. The difference between within-dataset and transferred PCA therefore quantifies how much the dominant embedding geometry changes across stimulus sets. Transferred PCA per-formed significantly worse than within-dataset PCA across all directed dataset comparisons (Figure 4b), indicating substantial stimulus-dependent variation in embedding geometry. If stimulus distribution shift contributes to the loss of RRR transfer performance, then dataset pairs with a larger PCA transfer gap should also tend to have a larger RRR transfer gap. We therefore compared these two quantities across models, layers, and directed dataset pairs. The RRR transfer gap positively covaried with the corresponding PCA transfer gap (Figure 5; Pearson r = 0 .58 ,

n = 184 ), indicating that poorer transfer of the stimulus-defined embedding space was associated with poorer transfer of the brain-predictive subspace. 7Preprint. This relationship does not establish that stimulus shift fully explains the RRR transfer gap, since recording modality, preprocessing, or dataset-specific neural representations may also contribute. However, it indicates that stimulus distribution shift is an important source of cross-dataset vari-ability. LINGUISTIC INFORMATION 

Having shown that RRR recovers neural predictivity in far fewer dimensions than PCA, we next asked what information distinguishes these two low-dimensional spaces. PCA preserves directions of greatest variance in the model embeddings, whereas RRR selects directions specifically for their ability to predict neural responses. Comparing feature decodability between equally sized RRR and PCA subspaces therefore asks which linguistic information is preferentially preserved when the embedding space is constrained by neural prediction rather than by variance alone. Greater decodability from RRR does not imply that a feature itself drives neural prediction, but indicates enrichment of that feature, or correlated information, in the brain-predictive subspace. Across datasets, several features showed preferential decoding from the RRR subspace. For ex-ample, sentence position and GPT-2 next-word prediction entropy were more accessible from RRR than PCA at low ranks across all three datasets (Figure 6a). Word concreteness, however, was preferentially represented in the fMRI-derived RRR subspace at low ranks, but showed no corre-sponding enrichment in either iEEG dataset. Thus, the learned subspaces preserve both shared and dataset-specific linguistic information. We next asked whether these dataset differences showed systematic structure. If RRR is capturing meaningful properties of the neural measurements rather than an arbitrary predictive compression, datasets sharing a recording modality should prioritize similar features. Consistent with this predic-tion, enrichment profiles were strongly correlated between the two iEEG datasets (Podcast versus Cars 2: ( r = 0 .70 ); Figure 6b), but showed little correspondence between either iEEG dataset and fMRI (Podcast versus fMRI: ( r = −0.11 ); Cars 2 versus fMRI: ( r = −0.12 )). This pattern provides convergent support for the interpretation that RRR is isolating meaningful brain-predictive structure. Despite differences in participants and stimuli, the two iEEG datasets emphasize similar linguistic information, while both differ from fMRI. It also complements the cross-dataset transfer results: stimulus shift contributes to transfer loss, but datasets may additionally differ in which components of the embedding space are selected by neural supervision. 8Preprint.

5 DISCUSSION 

Our results show that LLM–brain alignment during naturalistic language processing is supported by a surprisingly low-dimensional subset of the embedding space. Across datasets, models, and layers, neural prediction saturated using only tens of dimensions, and these brain-predictive directions dif-fered from the principal axes of embedding variance. The learned subspaces generalized strongly across held-out participants and partially across datasets, with cross-dataset differences associated with both stimulus-dependent shifts in embedding geometry and differences in the linguistic infor-mation emphasized across recording modalities. Beyond establishing the existence and generality of this low-rank structure, the framework provides a way to probe which components of large model representations contribute most strongly to neural prediction. Because RRR identifies directions that are selected specifically for their ability to predict neural responses, comparing the information available in the RRR and PCA subspaces can reveal representational mismatches between the two spaces. For example, we find that some stimulus-level properties, including volume (Figure 6a), can be decoded more accurately from the RRR space than from a comparable PCA space. This should not be interpreted as evidence that the language model represents acoustic volume: the model receives only the transcript. Instead, the RRR subspace is likely preserving linguistic features that covary with acoustic properties of the stimulus which is useful for predicting responses to heard speech. This example highlights a broader interpretational challenge for neural encoding models built from high-dimensional language representations. Lin-guistic, semantic, syntactic, discourse, and acoustic properties are highly correlated in naturalistic stimuli, such that successful neural prediction does not by itself identify which underlying feature is causally responsible for alignment. An open question remains as to what is limiting the dimensionality of this aligned subspace. One possibility is that coarse neural recordings which we typically have access to in human neuroscience contain little signal and we are in fact recovering it well with tens of dimensions. Another possibility though is that the model embedding representations are missing some portion of the true represen-tational signal contained in this neural data. While our analysis cannot answer this question, it does provide evidence that the primary features of LLM embeddings which align with the neural data are not equivalent to the principal axes of the embeddings, demonstrating a divide in the representational geometry of the two systems. 9Preprint. A APPENDIX 

A.1 DATA PREPROCESSING 

A.1.1 LLM E MBEDDINGS 

We generated contextual representations from the stimulus transcripts using GPT-2, GPT-2 XL, GPT-J 6B, Llama 3 8B, and RoBERTa Large. Each transcript word was tokenized independently with a leading space, and the resulting subword tokens were concatenated in transcript order. For the causal models, we processed overlapping windows of up to 1,023 tokens, advancing by 512 tokens per window. We retained all token representations from the first window and only newly introduced token representations from subsequent windows. Native causal attention restricted each represen-tation to the target token and preceding tokens within its window. For RoBERTa, we constructed a separate window of up to 511 tokens ending at each target token and retained its final-position representation. Thus, although RoBERTa uses bidirectional attention within a window, its extracted representations had no access to subsequent transcript tokens. We extracted hidden states at five depths spanning each model: layers 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 for GPT-2; 0, 12, 24, 36, and 48 for GPT-2 XL; 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 for GPT-J; 0, 8, 16, 24, and 32 for Llama; and 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 for RoBERTa. Layer 0 denotes the embedding-stage hidden state. Word representations were obtained by averaging the hidden states of all subword tokens belonging to that word. Contextual representations were generated before applying the vocabulary restrictions used in the neural analyses. Controls comprised a randomly initialized GPT-2 model with initialization seed 42 and static 300-dimensional GloVe embeddings. A.1.2 CARS 2 P REPROCESSING 

We analyzed Brain Treebank intracranial recordings from subjects 3, 7, and 10 during viewing of Cars 2. Contacts identified as corrupted in the dataset annotations were removed, and the continuous voltage recordings were resampled from 2,048 Hz to 512 Hz. We then despiked each contact by identifying samples whose absolute deviation from its median exceeded four times its interquartile range. The artifact mask was expanded using a 100-ms filter applied forward and backward, and masked samples were replaced using piecewise cubic Hermite interpolation. 13 Preprint. We applied shaft-Laplacian rereferencing, subtracting the mean voltage of the two immediately adjacent contacts on the same shaft from each contact: 

V ref n (t) = Vn(t) − Vn−1(t) + Vn+1 (t)2

Only contacts with both consecutively numbered neighbors available after corrupted-contact re-moval were retained. We applied notch filters at 60, 120, and 180 Hz with 2-Hz notch widths, followed by a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter from 70 to 200 Hz. High-gamma amplitude was defined as the magnitude of the analytic signal obtained through the Hilbert transform. A.1.3 WORD ALIGNMENT AND EPOCH CONSTRUCTION .We reconstructed transcript words by matching the dataset’s transcript entries to the corresponding sentence text, recovering punctuation and contractions where applicable. When multiple separately timed entries mapped to a single sentence word, we retained the separately timed entries. Each resulting word received a persistent transcript index linking its embedding to its neural epoch. Movie timestamps were mapped separately to each subject’s neural recording using the supplied synchronization triggers. Words lacking valid onset or offset times, or beginning after the final synchronization trigger, were excluded from neural alignment. For each remaining word, we rounded its recording-relative onset to the nearest sample on the 512-Hz preprocessing grid and extracted a high-gamma epoch covering [-2,2) seconds relative to onset. When multiple words mapped to the same onset sample, we retained the first; epochs extending beyond the recording boundaries were discarded. Each extracted epoch was then independently resampled to 32 Hz using FFT resampling with a Hamming window, yielding 128 samples per contact at 31.25-ms intervals. Consequently, every epoch used a sampling grid anchored to its own word onset. We stored the retained transcript indices alongside the epochs and used these indices to select the corresponding word embeddings. Analyses combining subjects used the intersection of their re-tained word indices. The iEEG analyses additionally restricted words to those covered by the 300-dimensional GloVe vocabulary, ensuring a common vocabulary across model comparisons. A.2 METHODS DETAILS 

A.2.1 RRR FITTING AND RANK EVALUATION 

Within each training partition, we fit a multivariate ridge model from ( X) to ( Y ), yielding coefficient matrix ( W ) and fitted responses ( ˆY = XW ). After decomposing ( ˆY ), we used its first ( k) right singular vectors ( Vk) to construct ( Ak = W V k). Before projection, the truncated input factor was orthonormalized using QR decomposition so that differences in column magnitude did not influence the refitted model or comparisons with PCA. Ranks generally doubled in powers of 2 to the available embedding-dimensionality endpoint. At each rank, a new ridge encoding model was fit after projection. Ridge penalties were selected by inner cross-validation from a 25-value scale-aware grid. Performance was measured as Pearson correlation between predicted and observed held-out responses. For iEEG, correlations across lags were averaged in Fisher-(z) space to obtain per-electrode performance. A.2.2 CONSTRUCTION OF THE F MRI RRR SUBSPACE 

For fMRI, the initial RRR fit operated on the complete four-delay finite impulse response design, yielding a separate input-factor block for each delay. We combined these blocks to obtain a (k)-dimensional subspace in the original embedding coordinates. For each of the first (k) RRR compo-nents, coefficients were normalized across the four delays and scaled by the corresponding singular value of the ridge model’s fitted training responses. This weighting gives components with greater fitted prediction energy greater influence while preserving their distribution across delays. 14 Preprint. The four weighted blocks were concatenated, and their leading (k) left singular vectors were used as an orthonormal embedding basis. At each rank, embeddings were projected into this basis, the four-delay design was reconstructed, and a new ridge encoding model was fit for held-out evaluation. A.2.3 LANGUAGE -FEATURE DECODING DETAILS 

Stimulus words were annotated with the available lexical, syntactic, semantic, discourse, timing, and pause features. At each rank, embeddings were projected separately into the RRR and PCA spaces. Continuous features were decoded using ridge regression and evaluated by Pearson correlation; categorical features were decoded using class-balanced logistic regression and evaluated by balanced accuracy. Features unavailable for a given dataset were omitted from that dataset’s analysis. Acomplete list of decoded features and their sources is provided below. A.3 SATURATION RANK DEFINITION 

Our goal was to estimate the minimum rank required to recover near-maximal neural predictive per-formance. In principle, this could be defined separately for every neural target (voxel or electrode). Individual target-level rank–performance curves are noisy, however, making target-specific satura-tion estimates unstable. Conversely, defining saturation from only the mean performance across targets can obscure heterogeneity across the neural-target distribution. We therefore characterized saturation at the level of the target-performance distribution. At each evaluated rank, we computed the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of held-out target performance and tracked each quantile across the rank sweep. For each quantile, we defined its maximum as the highest performance attained across evaluated ranks. The overall saturation rank was then the minimum rank at which every informative quantile reached at least 99% of its respective maximum performance. Quantile curves whose maximum performance did not exceed 0.005 were excluded because they contained almost no positive predictive performance for which a recovery rank could be meaning-fully defined. Requiring all remaining quantiles to reach threshold prevents saturation from being determined solely by high-performing targets or by changes in the mean and instead requires near-maximal recovery across a broad portion of the neural-target distribution. Because the estimated saturation rank depends on the chosen recovery threshold, we additionally evaluated the sensitivity of our conclusions to alternative thresholds. See figures 9, 10, 12, 11 for example quantiles and sensitivity analyses of these measures. A.3.1 RELATIVE DECODING PERFORMANCE 

After clipping each decoding score below at zero, we defined relative advantage as 

D(k) = sRRR (k) − sP CA (k)[sRRR (k) + sP CA (k)] /2 + 0 .05 

The value is greater than 0 if a feature can be more readily decoded from the RRR space than the PCA space and less than 0 otherwise. It is normalized by the mean of the two values and 0.05 is added to prevent unstable values at low performance. We repeat this computation across many ranks to study how it changes with the size of the subspaces. A.4 SIGNIFICANCE TESTS

A.4.2 SATURATION -RANK COMPARISONS .For each dataset, model–layer combination, and method, we summarized dimensionality by the rank required to reach 99% of peak performance. We first averaged target-level correlations across evaluation folds in Fisher-z space and computed the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles across neural targets. Each percentile curve was made monotonic by taking its cumulative maximum and normalized by its own peak. Curves with peak correlations at or below 0.005 were excluded. Saturation rank was the largest of the first ranks at which the eligible percentile curves reached the threshold, with linear interpolation between tested ranks. We tested whether RRR required fewer dimensions than PCA using paired differences in log 2 sat-uration rank. For leave-one-subject-out analyses, we computed saturation ranks separately for each subject and averaged the finite ranks across subjects within each model–layer combination before applying the logarithmic transformation. We tested whether transferred RRR required fewer dimen-sions than the within-subject PCA baseline and whether the RRR baseline required fewer dimensions than transferred RRR. Comparisons used model–layer combinations with finite saturation estimates for every method shown in the panel. Sensitivity analyses repeated these comparisons across alternative saturation thresholds and, for internal analyses, alternative minimum peak correlations. A.4.3 CROSS -DATASET TRANSFER COMPARISONS 

We summarized each performance curve by its area under the curve (AUC). Correlations were clipped below at zero, made monotonic using a cumulative maximum, and divided by the curve’s own peak. We then integrated the normalized curve using the trapezoidal rule over normalized log rank, log (k)/log (kmax ), from rank 1 to the maximum tested rank. For each of the six directed dataset pairs, we tested whether the mean paired AUC difference was positive for three comparisons: within-dataset RRR versus transferred RRR, transferred RRR versus transferred PCA, and within-dataset PCA versus transferred PCA. Each test used matched model–layer combinations with valid AUCs. A.4.4 CORRELATION ANALYSES 

We used Pearson correlations with two-sided p-values to assess associations between the within-dataset-minus-transfer AUC gaps for PCA and RRR, pooling matched observations across all six transfer directions. We also assessed cross-dataset consistency of the relative decoding advantage of RRR over PCA. We integrated the relative decoding performance quantity over normalized log rank and correlated the resulting AUCs between datasets, matching observations by model, layer, and decoding task and retaining combinations available in all three datasets. Statistical significance was indicated at p < 0.05 . Cross-dataset AUC tests and Pearson correla-tion tests used unadjusted p-values; Holm correction was confined to the comparisons within each saturation panel. A.5 SIGNIFICANCE SELECTION
