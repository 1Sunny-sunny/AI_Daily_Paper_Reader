# SpecBraM: What Should an EEG Foundation Model Predict? Masked Band-Power Prediction versus Waveform Reconstruction 

## Peng Xie 1, Yequan Bie 1, Jianda Mao 1, Kani Chen 1

> 1

The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology 

Abstract 

Self-supervised EEG models often reconstruct masked wave-forms or predict discrete codes. We study a task-aligned alter-native: masked band-power prediction (MBP), which predicts fixed narrow-band log spectral energy for masked channel-time patches. This target retains rhythm power relevant to sleep staging while avoiding phase-sensitive waveform reconstruc-tion and a learned codebook. Across three pretraining seeds, we compare band-power and waveform targets with matched backbones, pretraining data (2,388 hours), and training steps, including a 2x2 tokenizer-by-target design. On ISRUC and HMC sleep staging, MBP exceeds raw- and band-waveform reconstruction by 1.6–2.8 balanced-accuracy points with all labels and 4.7–7.3 points with 1% of labels under a strict linear probe; the target effect exceeds the tokenizer effect. Its frozen features reach 0.7916/0.7425 balanced accuracy, versus 0.7636/0.7227 for a matched rich handcrafted spec-tral baseline, although the gap is about one point with 1% of labels. Full fine-tuning reaches 0.8107/0.7669. The gains do not extend to every task with spectral cues, including mo-tor imagery, depression screening, and vigilance regression. These results support choosing pretraining targets to match the physical quantities and spatial and temporal scales relevant to downstream labels. 

## 1 Introduction 

EEG is central to clinical neuroscience—sleep staging, screening for abnormal background activity, studies of de-pression and cognitive load—but expert labels are scarce, which motivates foundation models pretrained once on large unlabeled corpora such as the TUH EEG Corpus (TUEG) (Obeid and Picone 2016) and transferred to many tasks (Jiang, Zhao, and Lu 2024; Wang et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025; El Ouahidi et al. 2025). The dominant designs bor-row their pretext task from other modalities. LaBraM (Jiang, Zhao, and Lu 2024) trains a vector-quantized tokenizer and predicts its discrete codes, as BEiT does for images (Bao et al. 2022). CBraMod (Wang et al. 2025), CSBrain (Zhou et al. 2025) and REVE (El Ouahidi et al. 2025) reconstruct the raw waveform of masked patches, as masked autoencoders reconstruct pixels (He et al. 2022). We ask what an EEG pretext target should be. Our answer is a principle rather than a new architecture: predict the phys-ical quantity that the downstream readouts depend on, not a proxy of the signal. In EEG, a large class of readouts is de-fined by spectral energy: AASM sleep stages are specified by the amount of delta activity, spindles and alpha (Berry et al. 2017); clinical reports describe “background slowing”; quan-titative EEG uses band powers and their ratios. Meanwhile the phase of each rhythm within a sub-second patch car-ries little information about the state and cannot be inferred from neighbouring patches, so a waveform-reconstruction loss spends much of its gradient on an irreducible compo-nent. Sec. 4 makes this precise under an idealized stationary-Gaussian model. We test the principle with its simplest instance, masked band-power prediction : on a standard masked-patch trans-former, replace the waveform target by the log energy of each masked patch in five fixed bands. Our contributions are: 

• A controlled target study. Holding backbone, data, step budget and launcher fixed, we compare six targets on the filterbank tokenizer and a 2×2 tokenizer × target design, each over three pretraining seeds (Tables 1 and 2). Every band-power variant beats both waveform-reconstruction variants on ISRUC and HMC under a strict linear probe, on every seed. A band-waveform reconstruction control also loses; this is consistent with discarding phase (and the patch’s innovation) being the useful ingredient, though that control also changes the output dimension and loss scale and so does not isolate the cause on its own. 

• Few labels and short training. The gap widens to 5–7 points with 1% of the labels, and band power at pretrain-ing epoch 4 already exceeds reconstruction at epoch 16 (Tables 2 and 3). 

• Comparison with direct spectral features. Probed iden-tically, SpecBraM exceeds a rich handcrafted spectral fea-ture set by 2.0–2.8 points (about one point with 1% of labels), whereas the waveform-reconstruction model of the same architecture does not exceed it on HMC or at 1% of labels (Tables 4 and 5). 

• Comparison with existing models. Under the same strict probe, SpecBraM’s frozen features beat the offi-cial CBraMod and LaBraM weights on all seven tasks (Tables 4, 7–9); with fine-tuning it reaches the best pub-lished sleep-staging results on ISRUC and HMC (Ta-ble 6). ## 2 Related Work 

Pretext targets of EEG foundation models. Most models pretrain on TUEG and differ mainly in the masked-prediction target. Raw-waveform reconstruction : CBraMod (Wang et al. 2025) with a criss-cross transformer, CSBrain (Zhou et al. 2025) with cross-scale modeling, REVE (El Ouahidi et al. 2025) with an additional global-token reconstruction; ear-lier, BENDR (Kostas, Aroca-Ouellette, and Rudzicz 2021) and Neuro-GPT (Cui et al. 2024) used contrastive or autore-gressive objectives, EEGPT (Wang et al. 2024) combines reconstruction with latent alignment, and NeuroLM (Jiang et al. 2025) bridges EEG to language tokens. Discrete codes :LaBraM (Jiang, Zhao, and Lu 2024) predicts codes of a VQ tokenizer trained to reconstruct the Fourier amplitude 

and phase of each patch; BioSerenity-E1 (Bettinardi, Rah-mouni, and Gimenez 2025) quantizes log-multitaper spectra; BandVQ (Sukhbaatar, Imamura, and Tanaka 2026) trains one VQ tokenizer per frequency band and predicts quantized log-power codes. In intracranial recordings BrainBERT (Wang et al. 2023) reconstructs masked spectrograms and Brant (Zhang et al. 2023) reconstructs raw patches with spectral input features. BIOT (Yang, Westover, and Sun 2023) tok-enizes biosignals with STFT magnitudes. 

Concurrent work. FAME (Yu et al. 2026) observes that the 1/f spectrum of EEG and the ℓ2 loss bias masked au-toencoders towards low frequencies, and corrects this by decoding the band-averaged log time–frequency power of the whole sequence (masked and unmasked positions) with per-band standardization and equal band weights. MANAS-2 (Kulkarni et al. 2026) jointly reconstructs raw waveform patches and six-band log-power targets and adds a constraint on the RMS energy of the reconstructed waveform. Our tar-get is close to FAME’s, but the motivation and evidence dif-fer: we predict only masked patches from context, frame the choice as discarding unpredictable phase (Sec. 4), and isolate the effect of the target with matched controls including band-waveform reconstruction. FAME’s per-band standardization is a natural refinement of our target (Sec. 7). 

Hand-crafted targets in other modalities. MaskFeat (Wei et al. 2022) showed that predicting HOG features of masked regions outperforms predicting pixels; the first iteration of HuBERT (Hsu et al. 2021) predicts clusters of MFCC fea-tures. Band power plays the same role for EEG: a fixed feature that keeps semantics and discards low-level detail. Latent-predictive methods (I-JEPA (Assran et al. 2023), data2vec (Baevski et al. 2022), EEG2Rep (Foumani et al. 2024)) pre-dict learned representations instead and need explicit anti-collapse mechanisms such as SIGReg (Balestriero and Le-Cun 2025), which we use as a light regularizer. 

## 3 Method 

Input and tokens. A 10-s window at 256 Hz over the 19 channels of the 10–20 montage, normalized per recording (median and interquartile range), is cut per channel into non-overlapping patches of P =200 samples ( 0.78 s), giving a token grid with C=19 channels and Tp=⌊2560 /200 ⌋=12 

time patches; the last 160 samples ( 0.625 s) of each window are not used, during pretraining and in every downstream window (including each of the three 10-s windows of a 30-s sleep epoch). The main model embeds each patch with a single linear map (as CBraMod does). A variant decom-poses each patch with a learnable 5-band filterbank before embedding (“filterbank tokenizer”); we report both because the target study crosses tokenizer and target. 

Encoder and masking. A 12-layer criss-cross transformer (D=512 , 8 heads; 51.3M parameters) alternates attention along the channel and time axes, with learned channel and time position embeddings. Each (channel, time) patch is masked independently with probability 0.3; the embeddings of masked patches are replaced by a learned mask token. 

Target. For each patch xc,t ∈ RP we compute five energies with fixed (non-learnable) band-pass filters hb applied to the uncorrupted signal:

## 4 Why Band Power: an Idealized Analysis 

We formalize the motivation under an idealized model; the full statements and proofs are in Appendix A. The analysis is a motivation and a source of testable predictions, not an exact description of the implementation (which uses log(1 + P )

and overlapping narrow-band filters). 

Model. Given a brain state θ, a patch is a segment of a zero-mean stationary Gaussian process with spectral density Sθ ;the state acts on the data only through Sθ . Asymptotically, the discrete Fourier coefficients at distinct frequencies are independent circular complex Gaussians, so the periodogram ordinates are Ik ≈ Sθ (fk)Ek with Ek ∼ Exp(1) , and the phases are uniform and independent of θ (Brillinger 1981). The Whittle likelihood (Whittle 1953) depends on the data only through {Ik}.

Consequences. (i) The periodogram is sufficient for θ and the phases are ancillary (Prop. 1). (ii) If the spectrum is ap-proximately constant within each of a few bands and the ob-servation is restricted to those bands, the band-averaged pe-riodogram is the sufficient statistic and maximum-likelihood estimate of the band levels (Prop. 2). (iii) Var[log Pb] = 

ψ′(mb) ≈ 1/m b does not depend on the state, so the log is variance-stabilizing and squared error is an appropriate loss (Prop. 3); for the implemented log(1 + P ) the noise variance is about m−1

(iv) For any target, the Bayes risk of predicting a masked patch from its context splits into the posterior uncertainty about the state plus a term that no model can reduce (Prop. 5). For band power the irreducible term is small and the reducible term is exactly uncertainty about the state; for the waveform the irreducible term is the patch’s own innovation (random amplitude and phase), which scales with the signal power and, through Parseval’s identity, is dominated by low frequencies under a 1/f spectrum. 

Predictions. When labels describe spectral states, a band-power target should give representations that are easier to read out linearly than a waveform target, especially with few labels; reconstructing band-filtered waveforms should not help, because it still requires predicting phase; and the advantage should shrink for tasks that depend on transient waveform shape or cross-channel phase relations. Sec. 6 tests these. 

## 5 Experimental Setup 

Pretraining. TUEG v2.0.1 at 256 Hz, 10-s windows, per-recording robust normalization, with recordings shorter than 5 min dropped, 60 s trimmed from each end and windows exceeding 150 μV rejected. All models use a random subset of 2,100 patients (859,606 windows, 2,388 h; the same sub-set for every run), AdamW (learning rate 1.7×10 −3, weight decay 0.05 ), 7 warm-up epochs and a cosine schedule de-fined over 105 epochs, stopped after epoch 16 for every run (matched steps), effective batch 512 on four RTX A6000 GPUs. Pretraining seeds 42/43/44 change initialization and data order on the same subset. Downstream patients are not excluded from pretraining (as in prior work). 

Strict linear probe (primary metric) . The encoder is frozen; the tokens of a window are mean-pooled; features are standardized on the training split; a class-balanced lo-gistic regression is fitted with C ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1, 1} selected on the validation split by balanced accuracy, and the test split is scored once. For sleep, a 30-s epoch is the mean of its three 10-s window features. The probe is determinis-tic, so baselines with a single released checkpoint need no repetitions; our models are reported over three pretraining seeds. Label efficiency reuses the selected C on 1–50% of the training epochs (five fixed random subsets, identical for all models). Each model receives the same samples in its own documented input convention: ours at 256 Hz with per-recording robust scaling on the 19 standard leads; CBraMod and LaBraM at 200 Hz, raw μV/100 , with the recording’s own channels (LaBraM by its channel names); BIOT with its 95th-percentile scaling on C3/C4 fed to its C3–A2/C4–A1 channel tokens. This mean-pool protocol does not use the baselines’ downstream heads, which we discuss in Sec. 7. 

Fine-tuning (sleep). Following CBraMod/CSBrain, 30-s epochs are grouped in sequences of 20; each epoch is en-coded (mean of its three 10-s windows), and a shared one-layer transformer predicts one stage per epoch. Encoder and head are trained with AdamW (learning rate 10 −4, weight decay 0.05 ) for up to 50 epochs with early stopping on vali-dation balanced accuracy (patience 10). 

Downstream data. Sleep: ISRUC subgroup I (Khalighi et al. 2016) (subjects 1–80/81–90/91–100) and HMC (Alvarez-Estevez and Rijsman 2021) (100/25/26 by sorted subject ID), the splits of CBraMod and CSBrain. 6.2 Frozen features versus EEG foundation models 

Under the same strict linear probe (Table 4), SpecBraM reaches 0.7916 ±0.0081 on ISRUC and 0.7425 ±0.0045 on HMC, against 0.7523 /0.6323 for LaBraM, 0.7278 /0.6190 for CBraMod and at most 0.7430 /0.6578 for BIOT; REVE-Large reports 0.758 /0.710 under its own probing protocol. All base-lines are above the random-initialization floor of our archi-tecture ( 0.6884 /0.6256 ), which is a basic sanity check of our adapters; it does not exclude that each model’s input adapta-tion and mean-pooled readout affect the comparison (Sec. 7). Label efficiency (Table 5, Fig. 3) is the clearest view: with 1% 

of the training labels SpecBraM reaches 0.760 /0.685 , above the full-label probes of all external pretrained models we test (best 0.752 on ISRUC, 0.658 on HMC). It does not ex-ceed every model: the same-architecture raw-reconstruction model reaches 0.775 /0.714 with all labels, and needs about 10–25% of the labels to match what SpecBraM achieves with 1%. 

Direct spectral features. A natural question is what self-supervision adds over simply computing spectral features. We probe two handcrafted feature sets on the same epochs with the same probe, averaging over the three 10-s win-dows as for every encoder (this was better than whole-epoch features): five-band log and relative power, and a rich set per channel (1-Hz log power spectrum from 0.5 to 45 Hz, relative power in six bands, Hjorth parameters, spectral en-tropy, 95% spectral edge, line length and kurtosis). The rich set reaches 0.7636 /0.7227 (Table 4)—above every external foundation model under this probe, and above the same-architecture raw-reconstruction model on HMC ( 0.7141 ). SpecBraM exceeds it by 2.8/2.0 points with all labels, but only by about one point with 1% of the labels ( 0.760 /0.685 

vs. 0.747 /0.674 ; Table 5), and with all labels the rich features exceed SpecBraM’s 1%-label result. Self-supervision with a spectral target thus adds a modest but consistent gain over direct spectral features for sleep staging, whereas waveform reconstruction does not reliably reach them. All probes share the same grid C ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1, 1}; the handcrafted probes se-lect its lower edge, so a wider grid might raise them slightly.

6.5 Where the benefit stops: vigilance 

SEED-VIG (Table 9) regresses PERCLOS, an eye-closure measure. With the standard 0.5–45 Hz input the band-power and raw targets tie in Pearson correlation ( 0.565 vs. 0.548 ), raw reconstruction is better in R2 (0.209 vs. 0.166 ), and after fine-tuning raw reconstruction is clearly better ( 0.626 /0.357 

vs. 0.587 /0.260 ). A simple explanation—that waveform tar-gets help because they preserve blink shapes—is not sup-ported by a high-pass experiment: removing activity below 4 Hz improves the band-power models by 5–7 points of cor-relation ( 0.565 → 0.619 linear, 0.540 → 0.611 filterbank) while leaving the raw-reconstruction models unchanged or worse ( 0.548 → 0.572 , 0.564 → 0.549 ); the validation split independently selects the 4 Hz cutoff for both linear-tokenizer models. The high-pass changes the input to the frozen en-coder and the spectral content of each patch at the same time, so it does not identify a mechanism. One hypothesis is that ocular artifacts, one to two orders of magnitude larger than cortical activity, dominate the lowest target bands and the corresponding dimensions of the representation, mask-ing drowsiness-related alpha and theta changes; we have not tested it directly (e.g. by high-passing only the target dur-ing pretraining). The case shows that a spectral-energy target helps only when the energy it measures matches the label, which is not guaranteed in the presence of large artifacts. 

## 7 Limitations 

The implemented target. The main target uses five overlap-ping narrow-band filters and log(1+ P ). The former does not represent activity below 2 Hz; the latter is nearly linear for the small energies of the high bands, so the variance-stabilizing argument of Sec. 4 holds only partially there. The standard-band FFT variant keeps the advantage over reconstruction but is slightly lower on HMC; per-band standardized log power (as in FAME (Yu et al. 2026)) is a natural next step.

Scope. The advantage of the target is clearest for sleep stag-ing; it does not extend to every task with spectral cues (motor imagery, depression screening, vigilance). Several cohorts are small (MA has four test subjects). Downstream record-ings may overlap TUEG for all models; an abnormality-detection (TUAB) evaluation is in progress. Pretraining uses a single 2,388-hour budget and one model size; comparisons with external models that differ in data, duration and archi-tecture do not establish data efficiency in general. 

Theory. Sec. 4 assumes stationarity, Gaussianity and a state that acts only through the spectrum. Real EEG contains tran-sients (K-complexes, spikes, blinks), heavy tails and cross-channel phase relations; the analysis motivates the target and yields predictions but is not a proof that band power is opti-mal. 

## 8 Conclusion 

With the same backbone, data and training steps, predicting fixed narrow-band spectral energies of masked patches yields sleep representations that are easier to read out linearly than those learned by waveform reconstruction, on every seed and with either tokenizer. The advantage is largest with few labels and appears early in pretraining, but not every task with spectral information benefits: the target also has to match the spatial scale, time scale and artifact exposure of the label. We hope the controlled comparisons and the failure cases help choose pretext targets for physiological signals more deliberately.

## B Downstream Data Details 

Beyond-sleep tasks. For MA, Mumtaz and PhysioNet-MI we reproduce CBraMod’s preprocessing_*.py scripts line by line (file ordering, split slices, channel selection, fil-tering, resampling to 200 Hz and window length) and store the samples once; every model is probed on these samples. FACED’s official processed release was not reachable from our region; we rebuilt it from the public raw BIDS record-ings following the FACED paper (last 30 s of each clip, 0.05– 47 Hz, common average reference, 250 Hz, bad-channel in-terpolation; no ICA) and then applied CBraMod’s FACED preprocessing. Our input convention adds, for our model only, 0.5–45 Hz band-pass, resampling to 256 Hz and per-recording robust scaling of the same samples, restricted to the 19 standard leads, which all four tasks contain. 

SEED-VIG. CBraMod’s released preprocessing script sorts the 23 session files lexicographically (’10_. . . ’ before ’1_. . . ’), whereas its paper states the split 1–15/16–19/20– 23 by subject. The R2 and RMSE reported in the CBraMod paper imply a test-label standard deviation of 0.318 (for both its CBraMod and BIOT rows); the subject-ordered split gives 0.318 and the lexicographic split 0.175 , so we use the subject-ordered split. SEED-VIG has 17 temporal and posterior leads; for our encoder, whose channel embeddings correspond to 19 fixed 10–20 positions, each SEED-VIG lead receives an inverse-distance-weighted mix of the embeddings of its two nearest standard positions (exact positional matches take that slot). Ridge regularization for the probe is selected on validation R2 from 10 −2 to 10 8.
