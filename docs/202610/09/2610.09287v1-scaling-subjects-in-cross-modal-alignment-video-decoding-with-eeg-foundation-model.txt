# Scaling subjects in cross-modal alignment: video decoding with EEG foundation model 

Dung Truong 1 Kuntal Kokate 1 Arnaud Delorme 1,21UC San Diego, USA 2CNRS, France 

{dutruong, kkokate, adelorme}@ucsd.edu 

## Abstract 

Naturalistic visual decoding from EEG has long been constrained by cohort size: existing scaling literature caps out at a few dozen subjects, leading prior work to conclude that scaling subject cohorts yields minimal performance gains. Training a cross-modal EEG–video contrastive encoder on a cohort larger by more than an order of magnitude, we find the axis is productive but has an onset. Below S ≈ 50 

— the entirety of the range prior work occupies — no model improves meaningfully over an untrained encoder; above it, decoding rises log-linearly in subject count with no saturation at the top of our ladder, on a fitted stimulus-feature probe and on fit-free movie-moment retrieval alike, and the gain transfers to a second, unseen film. How far the axis carries then depends on initialisation more than on capacity: an encoder initialised from an EEG foundation model scales ∼1.6× faster per doubling of the cohort than randomly initialised encoders at two depths, is the only one still converting subjects into retrieval accuracy at the top of the ladder, and converges on a fraction of the alignment compute. Subject scaling on shared naturalistic stimuli is thus an effective and currently unsaturated frontier for scaling EEG foundation models. 

## 1 Introduction 

Naturalistic visual decoding is a growing area of interest in brain–computer interfacing research (Song et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Ergezer, 2026). While large-scale pretraining has been shown to help naturalistic EEG decoding (Banville et al., 2026), no work has investigated the effect of data scaling for movie decoding. Prior scaling work has found that the axis that plays best to EEG’s accessibility advantage — increasing the number of subjects — is precisely the unproductive one (Banville et al., 2025). The classical stimulus-response literature says the signal that subject scaling should sharpen is demonstrably there: correlated-component analysis and inter-subject correlation isolate a component of movie-watching EEG that is reliably shared across viewers of the same film (Dmochowski et al., 2012; Camenzind et al., 2026). The naturalistic-video EEG literature has simply not been in a position to test that axis, running on cohorts well below n ≈ 50 : 20 subjects in SEED-DV (Liu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025), 6 in the only long-form film corpus (Gao et al., 2025), 48 in the largest single cohort prior scaling work uses (Grootswagers et al., 2022). The one corpus large enough to try — HBN, 2634 participants, most of whom watched the same four short films (Alexander et al., 2017; Shirazi et al., 2024) — has been used only for clip and task classification (Ergezer, 2026), never for stimulus decoding. Using it, we report the largest subject-scaling study for EEG naturalistic video decoding to date. We annotate every frame with 12 continuous features and a shot/scene segmentation, and score every checkpoint on both a fitted feature probe and fit-free cross-modal retrieval, the latter unreported on continuous film. We run the subject ladder three times: once on an encoder initialised from a pretrained EEG model and twice on randomly initialised encoders at

Initialisation moves the exponent more than capacity does (§3.2): the pretrained arm scales 1.5–1.7× faster per doubling of the cohort on the probe than either random-init arm, and is the only arm still converting subjects into retrieval accuracy past 700, where both randomly initialised depths flatten. Depth shifts the same slopes too, by less, and what it does to the level flips sign between within- and cross-task. 3. ## 2 Method 

Data and targets. We use 129-channel HBN EEG recordings sampled at 200 Hz (Shirazi et al., 2024), from subjects watching two animated movies: The Present for training and Despicable Me for cross-task evaluation. EEG is per-channel z-scored and cut into aligned 2 s windows (Appendix A.1). Out of the 10 public releases we reserve release R6 (108 subjects’ recordings, zero overlap with other releases) as our held-out test set, and subsample training cohorts from the remaining releases, a pool of 2156 recordings, on the ladder S ∈ { 10 , 20 , 50 , 100 , 200 , 400 , 701 , 1000 , 1400 , 1863 } with three nested draws at each rung. All three arms draw their cohorts with the same seeds, so arms are compared on identical subjects at every rung. As prediction targets we annotate the movie frames with 12 continuous features spanning low-level (luminance, contrast, entropy), mid-level (motion energy, depth, faces) and high-level (scene naturalness, narrative events) properties (Appendix A.2), and segment it into shots and scenes, keeping only those a 2 s window resolves (Appendix A.3), which sets the retrieval pools — and hence chance — at 101 timepoints, 49 shots and 35 scenes. 

Architecture. We adopt REVE (El Ouahidi et al., 2025) as our base architecture ( dmodel = 512 ,depth 22 , 8 heads, 200 -sample patches with 20 -sample overlap), which leads competing foundation models on naturalistic decoding (Banville et al., 2026); we use its publicly released first-stage weights via braindecode (Schirrmeister et al., 2017). To vary model scale among the random-init arms we also train a smaller REVE-like variant, depth 12 at the same width, with non-overlapping 400 -sample patches. 

Objective and training. EEG-movie alignment is trained using soft-target CLIP (Andonian et al., 2022) on mean-centered movie embedding targets (Figure 1, Appendix B.2). The frozen V-JEPA-2 (Assran et al., 2025) supplying the vision targets doubles as teacher, its own window-to-window similarities saying how close those windows should sit in the shared space, so the EEG encoder fits a graded distance rather than a binary match/mismatch — a natural fit for continuous film, where windows seconds apart are genuinely alike. This beat hard-target CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and a scene-mask loss (Appendix B.3); Appendices B.1 and B.4 give the loss, the optimiser and the pretrained initialisation in full. To keep training budget comparable, we fix the number of gradient steps in an epoch and the total steps per training run, choosing that total from a prior study of where each model converges (Appendices C.2 and C.3). Below S = 701 models train for 4400 gradient steps under a cosine schedule annealed over its own budget. Above S = 701 the random-init arms were retrained at 8800 steps, the shorter budget having proved limiting (§3.3); since the schedule anneals over the total, that is a different trajectory rather than a longer one, so convergence is compared only within the 4400-step runs all arms share. Every cell is 3 nested subject draws, and is read at its own checkpoint rather than a shared one, so no arm is held to another’s stopping point. That checkpoint is chosen on the draw’s cohort complement — the 293 subjects of the pool it did not train on — as the argmax of held-out time-pool retrieval over checkpoints saved every 25 epochs, unsmoothed. Selecting instead on the held-out probe moves the chosen epoch but not the outcome (Appendix C.2). Evaluation is on the test release only, which no arm and no draw has seen. 

## 3 Results 

3.1 Subjects scale, and the flat regime is where prior work sits Every readout that carries signal rises with subjects. On the untouched test release the pretrained arm reaches mean Pearson r = 0 .306 on the 12-feature probe and 8.0× chance on 101-way time-pool retrieval (Figure 2, blue). Both fit a log-linear subject law — +0 .0244 r and +0 .0093 top-1 per doubling of the cohort, R2 = 0 .94 and 0.90 — with no sign of saturation. Neither law rests on the top rung: dropping S=1863 , where three draws share 86 % of their subjects and so barely replicate (Limitations), lowers each slope by at most 0.001 and lowers R2, and that rung sits above the line the nine below it predict. Appendices D.1, D.2 and D.3 give every cell and fit. 

The floor is not near zero, and the flat regime is where prior work sits. An encoder whose weights are never updated already reaches r = 0 .105 on the within-task probe — about a third of our best cell — so absolute scores here are uninterpretable without that floor, which every panel therefore carries. Read against it, the ladder below S ≈ 50 is barely off the floor: no arm reaches 1.4× an untrained network on the probe or 2× on retrieval, against 2.0–2.9× and 5.0–8.0× at the top. That range is also the one prior work occupies — the largest cohort of any modality in the scaling study we contend with (Banville et al., 2025) is 48 subjects (shaded column) — so our disagreement with it needs no difference of method: below S ≈ 50 our own curve moves too little for a design at their n

to resolve, and no image-decoding dataset available to them could have reached the regime above it.

3.2 A pretrained initialisation lifts the curve and keeps it rising The lift holds on every readout that carries signal. At matched depth the pretrained arm leads on both probes at every rung, and on within-task retrieval from the scaling onset upward; below that onset the arms are indistinguishable, as §3.1 says of the whole floor region. It is not a head start. What separates the arms is the slope: fitted per doubling of the cohort, the pretrained arm gains 1.5–1.7×

as much as either random-init arm, on the probe and on retrieval alike, so the gap widens with every rung rather than sitting as a constant offset. The distinction matters most at the top of the ladder, where the two readouts part company. Both arms are still converting subjects into probe accuracy there. Retrieval is where the random-init arms stall: over the top of the ladder ( S=701 → 1863 ) the deeper one adds +0 .003 top-1 where the pretrained arm adds +0 .022 , and the shallower one peaks early and turns down. Flattening on the subject axis is therefore real but not a property of the axis: it is confined to one readout of the random-init arms, and the pretrained arm does not share it. 

Depth matters under random initialisation, and the sign flips. Pairing the two random-init arms draw by draw - each pair trained on identical subjects - the shallower arm wins within-task (+0 .008 r on the probe and +0 .017 on retrieval, 9 of 9 pairs) and loses cross-task ( −0.005 r, 1 of 9), averaged over S ≥ 1000 where they separate. However, the advantage peaks at S=1400 and narrows at S=1863 (the shallower arm turns down first) so it should not be extrapolated past the ladder. And with no pretrained depth-12 arm, whether that initialisation would erase the gap is untested. Appendix E gives every cell. 43.3 Convergence behavior and compute budget Where the optimum sits, and what it costs. The random-init arms’ optimum travels the length of the ladder — median epoch 125 at S = 10 to 775 at S = 1863 — while the pretrained optimum barely moves, sitting at 325 of a 375 budget at the top (Figure 2, bottom). Two things follow. First, a fixed epoch count cannot be held across a subject ladder: the budget that suits the small-S cells truncates the large-S ones, and the apparent saturation that produces belongs to the protocol, not the subject axis. Hence each cell is read at its own optimum, and the random-init arms above S = 701 

were retrained at twice the budget — which did not settle it, since at S = 1863 all three depth-22 draws still peak on the last checkpoint of the longer schedule and so report a bound (hollow markers). Second, the optimum’s location is a cost: 63 .3 PFLOPs for the depth-22 random-init arm against 

26 .6 for the pretrained arm, which is also the better model on every readout. The comparison that matters for choosing an architecture, though, is between the two random-init arms: a depth-22 step costs 3.8× a depth-12 one, so the shallower arm reaches its optimum on 15 .6 PFLOPs — a quarter of the deeper arm’s compute — while beating it on every within-task readout (§3.2). At this data scale the extra depth buys nothing worth paying for. The compute that produced the released REVE weights is not counted in any figure here: already paid, and reusable. 

Model selection is reliable for the pretrained arm and unreliable without it. Early stopping with patience — the rule a practitioner would actually deploy — selects the best available checkpoint in 12 of 15 high-S cells for the pretrained arm and 8 of 15 for each random-init arm, with patience set as the same fraction of each arm’s own budget. The failures are not near misses: random-init cells quit at epoch 200 when the best checkpoint lay at 620–680. The arm selects well because it has converged, so without convergence the stopping rule becomes a second uncontrolled variable (Appendix C.3). 

## 4 Discussion 

Subject count is a productive scaling axis for naturalistic EEG decoding: every readout carrying signal rises log-linearly and none saturates at the top of our ladder — but only above an onset near S ≈ 50 ,below which no arm meaningfully beats an untrained encoder. Initialisation matters more than capacity: the pretrained arm scales faster per doubling, alone keeps converting subjects into retrieval accuracy at the top, and reaches a better optimum on a fraction of the compute. Cross-task zero-shot retrieval, our negative control, never leaves chance. We say nothing about the hours-per-subject law, which a single animated clip ( ∼3.4 minutes per subject) cannot test. Our disagreement with Banville et al. (2025) is a range restriction, not a contradiction (§3.1). Two design differences predict who should see an onset: their decoders start from random weights, and our random-init arms scale at roughly half the pretrained exponent; and their per-subject linear layers relieve the shared trunk of subject-invariance, where ours makes subjects the only pressure toward it — the very differences they blame for their flat axis. 

Limitations. All of this rests on a single dataset, montage and protocol, with one training stimulus and one transfer target. Fit and test share a stimulus on the within-task probe, inflating its absolute 

r; subjects stay disjoint and the overlap is constant across S, so it cannot produce a slope — as the cross-task probe, on an unseen film, confirms. Three draws per cell put the smallest attainable per-step p at 0.10 , so we rest on the ladder as a whole rather than any one step, and the top rung barely replicates, each draw there taking 86 % of the pool — as in any scaling study approaching its dataset’s size (Appendix C.1). Our capacity axis is two depths rather than a sweep. No arm received its own hyperparameter sweep, so a residual tuning advantage is possible. 

Outlook. The natural extension is across datasets with heterogeneous montages but shared stimuli, which REVE’s montage-agnostic input supports. Under current data-sharing and standardisation efforts such as NEMAR (Delorme et al., 2022), EEGDash (Aristimunha et al., 2026) and HED (Robbins et al., 2022), subject pooling is an effective and cheap axis to grow. 5References

## A Data and annotation 

A.1 EEG preprocessing 

The EEG side of the pipeline runs in two stages. An offline stage is applied once per release and task and written to disk; a load-time stage runs inside every training and evaluation job and is what determines the windows a model actually sees. Separating them matters because normalisation happens in both. 

Offline preprocessing. Recordings shorter than 10 s are discarded. The remainder are resampled from the native 500 Hz to 200 Hz and bandpass filtered to 0.5–99 .5 Hz – a zero-phase FIR filter with a Hamming window and automatically chosen transition bands, all library defaults at these cutoffs – then cast to float32 . Each channel is then z-scored using a mean and standard deviation pooled over every time sample of every recording within one release and task, computed in a single streaming pass in double precision, and the result is clipped to ±15 standard deviations. Clipping is the only amplitude-based intervention anywhere in the pipeline: extreme samples are clamped, never dropped, and no recording or window is discarded on amplitude grounds. All 129 channels are carried through unchanged. 

Windowing and stimulus alignment. At load time a recording is required to carry both movie-onset and movie-offset event markers, and the duration they bracket must fall short of the nominal film length by no more than 1 s; recordings failing either check are dropped. This screen is what sets the realised cohort sizes: the release opt-in yields 1863 usable The Present recordings, one per subject, but only 1832 for Despicable Me , and the 1832 figure is identical across every cross-task cell, so it is a property of the opt-in rather than per-run measurement noise. The window grid is anchored at the movie-onset sample itself and steps in non-overlapping 400 -sample ( 2 s) strides, with the bracketed span first trimmed to the nominal film length so the grid cannot run past the credits and any trailing partial window dropped. Anchoring on the event rather than on recording start is what makes window k the same movie time in every subject, and is what yields the 101 anchors on The Present and 85 on Despicable Me used throughout. Windows for which no V-JEPA-2 (Assran et al., 2025) target falls inside the 2 s span are dropped rather than zero-filled; because the target grid is uniform, these are always a trailing trim and never an interior hole. No visual-processing delay is applied: EEG window k is paired with the stimulus content of movie seconds [2 k, 2k + 2) .

Normalisation is applied twice. The per-channel z-score quoted in the main text is the load-time one, recomputed on each clip as it is read: per channel, over the windows drawn from that recording, which for the single-window sampling used here is per channel per 2 s window. It is computed from the clip itself, not from stored statistics, and it supersedes the offline global z-score rather than compounding with it. What the offline z-score leaves behind is the frame in which clipping was applied – the ±15 bound is fixed in release-and-task units and is permanent – and unit-scaled files on disk. This is also the reason the stored offline statistics are never reapplied at load time. Note that the claim in Appendix A.2 that nothing is standardised between extraction and the probe is scoped to the stimulus feature chain; it says nothing about the EEG side, where the two normalisations above both apply. 

What is not done. There is no notch or line-noise filtering, no re-referencing of any kind, no bad-channel detection or interpolation, no ICA, ASR or other artifact decomposition, no epoch-level amplitude rejection and no baseline correction. Two optional load-time transforms exist in the implementation – appending a 1–8 Hz analytic envelope as extra channels, and a correlated-component spatial projection – and both are disabled in every run reported in this paper, which therefore all use the 129 raw channels. 8A.2 How the 12 probe features are produced 

The twelve features are the entire feature-encoding readout: every mean(12) figure in the main text and in Appendix D.1 is an average over them. This section specifies each one. Shots and scenes, the other half of the same annotation pipeline, are in Appendix A.3. 

Frame-level extraction. Every frame of the source video is annotated at native resolution and native frame rate; nothing is downsampled and no frames are skipped. The Present is 640 × 360 

at 23.995 fps and Despicable Me 624 × 336 at 25 fps, giving 4877 and 4266 annotated frames respectively. The The Present container declares 4878 frames, of which the last does not decode, so the two figures differ by one and both appear in this appendix: shot detection runs on the container and is reported against 4878 (Appendix A.3), while the feature table has one row per successfully decoded frame and so is 4877 long. Ten of the twelve are per-frame image statistics or pretrained-model outputs; the remaining two, position_in_movie and narrative_event_score , are derived from the resulting frame table. Table A1 summarises all twelve. 

Low-level statistics. All five are computed on the full-resolution frame and lie in [0 , 1] except entropy. luminance_mean is the mean Rec. 601 luma 0.299 R + 0 .587 G + 0 .114 B divided by 255, and contrast_rms the population standard deviation of that same luma, likewise divided by 255. 

saturation_mean is the mean of the 8-bit HSV saturation channel over 255. edge_density is the fraction of pixels flagged by a Canny detector (Canny, 1986) at hysteresis thresholds (100 , 200) on the 0–255 scale, 3 × 3 Sobel aperture and L1 gradient magnitude. entropy is the Shannon entropy, in bits, of the 256-bin grayscale intensity histogram, so it is bounded by [0 , 8] and is a first-order intensity statistic only – it carries no spatial term. 

Mid-level: motion, depth, faces. motion_energy is the spatial mean of the per-pixel L2 mag-nitude of dense Farnebäck optical flow (Farnebäck, 2003) between consecutive grayscale frames (pyramid scale 0.5, 3 levels, window 15, 3 iterations, polynomial order 5, σ = 1 .2); the first frame is 0 by construction. From frames to windows. Each non-overlapping 2 s EEG window takes the feature row of the single frame at its onset , at zero assumed visual-processing delay – the twelve are not averaged within the window. The V-JEPA-2 targets are treated differently, mean-pooled over the window’s full 2 s span, and that asymmetry is deliberate: the targets are the training signal and benefit from averaging, while the probe features are read out and are easier to interpret as an instantaneous frame statistic. Nothing else is done to them. No smoothing, clipping, log transform or standardisation is applied anywhere between extraction and the ridge probe; the internal z-scores inside narrative_event_score are the only standardisation in the chain, and at probe time only the EEG embeddings are standardised while the targets stay raw. 

A.3 Shot and scene segmentation Shots. Shot boundaries are taken from TransNetV2 (Souˇ cek and Lokoˇ c, 2020), which finds 53 boundaries in The Present (4878 container frames at 23.995 fps; see Appendix A.2 for why the feature table has 4877 rows) and 34 in Despicable Me (4266 frames at 25 fps), giving 54 and 35 shots respectively. We validated that choice against two independent detectors rather than deriving the segmentation from them. PySceneDetect finds 53 and 35 boundaries, FFmpeg scene-change detection 56 and 29. At a matching tolerance of 12 frames ( ≈ 0.5 s), pairwise F1 on The Present is 0.962 

(PySceneDetect–TransNetV2), 0.973 (PySceneDetect–FFmpeg) and 0.982 (TransNetV2–FFmpeg), and 51 of TransNetV2’s 53 boundaries are corroborated by both other detectors. On Despicable Me the corresponding F1 values are 0.986 , 0.906 and 0.921 , with 29 three-way-agreed boundaries – FFmpeg is the outlier there, missing six cuts the other two both find. Shot detection on this material is therefore not the uncertain step; we use the single detector with the best agreement profile and keep every boundary it reports.

Why the retrieval pools are smaller than the segmentations. ## B Objective and optimisation 

B.1 The soft-target objective in detail 

This section states exactly what is optimised. The comparison that selected it over the alternatives is Appendix B.3. 

What a batch is. One training example is a single 2 s EEG window from one recording, paired with the V-JEPA-2 embedding of the movie span that window covers – frame embeddings mean-pooled over the 2 s span, then mean-centered by a single global mean vector computed once over the training clips (Appendix B.2). A batch is N = 64 such examples drawn from 64 independently sampled recordings, so two subjects can contribute the same movie moment to one batch; such pairs are left in place rather than deduplicated, and the teacher below assigns them a high target similarity anyway. Shot and scene identities are not used by this objective: all multi-positive structure comes from the teacher. 

The two projections. The EEG encoder’s tokens are mean-pooled over the patch and channel axes to a 512-d vector per window and passed through the EEG projection – Linear(512 →512) , a residual 11 block (GELU, Linear, dropout 0.5), LayerNorm – then ℓ2-normalised to zei . The vision projection is a learnable Linear(1408 →512) on the centered target ˆvi, likewise ℓ2-normalised, giving zvi . Logits are s z ei · zvj with a learnable scalar scale s initialised to 1/0.07 as in CLIP and clamped to s ≤ 100 .

B.2 Why the V-JEPA-2 targets are mean-centered 

Raw V-JEPA-2 clip embeddings carry a dominant shared component, so every pairwise cosine on The Present sits in [0 .97 , 1.0] : same-shot pairs average 0.999 ± 0.002 and different-shot pairs 

0.991 ± 0.013 . The discriminative geometry exists – shot identity explains η2 = 0 .89 of angular embedding variance, F = 54 .6 – but it is compressed into a sliver of the cosine range that a temperature-scaled softmax cannot resolve. Subtracting a single global mean vector, computed once over the training clips, opens the range: same-shot pairs move to 0.793 ± 0.250 , different-shot pairs to −0.008 ± 0.529 , d′ from 0.87 to 1.94 , and same-versus-different ROC-AUC from 0.88 to 0.92 .This is why the teacher term qij is computed on centered targets: on raw targets the teacher softmax is nearly uniform regardless of τT , and the soft-target loss degenerates to the hard-target one. Centering is free on the readout side. It changes the cosine metric but not what a linear model can fit, and empirically shot-averaged ridge R2 under centered and raw embeddings agree to within 0.003 –so none of the probe baselines shift because of it. We should be clear about what is not established. The evidence above is geometric, measured on the target space directly. There is no controlled end-to-end ablation of centering at fixed learning rate and epoch budget: the comparison sometimes quoted for it (a centered run at +0 .0272 mean ∆R2 versus an uncentered one at +0 .0014 ) also changes the learning rate ( 3×10 −4 → 10 −4) and the epoch count (50 → 499 ), so it cannot carry the attribution. The claim we make is the narrow geometric one. 

The 2 s temporal buffer. The same analysis sets the buffer width. Treating a cross-shot pair with centered cosine > 0.5 as a candidate false negative, a 2 s buffer removes 283 of 18,445 candidates while sacrificing 25 legitimate negatives ( 0.04 % of them) – 92 % of what it removes is a real false negative, an 11 × efficiency. Wider buffers stop paying: at 8 s purity falls to 56 % and legitimate 12 negatives are lost roughly one-for-one. The buffer is also not a solution to false negatives in general, only to the shot-cut-boundary case: the median candidate false negative is ∆t ≈ 42 s away (95th percentile ≈ 138 s), i.e. visually similar shots scattered across the film, which is precisely the structure the soft teacher target handles instead. 

B.3 Choosing the training objective 

The objective was selected in the regime this paper trains in – a single movie, one alignment stage – on the depth-12 random-init pipeline, against two alternatives: hard-target CLIP, in which only the paired window is a positive, and a scene-mask multi-positive loss, which treats every window sharing an agglomerative scene ID as a positive. Soft-target sits between them, taking its multi-positive structure from the frozen teacher’s own similarities rather than from a discrete label. The operating point α = 0 .5, τT = 0 .05 (Appendix B.1 defines both) comes from a 160-epoch sensitivity sweep: τT = 0 .05 scored +0 .0316 mean ∆R2 against +0 .0284 at the default τT = 0 .1

and +0 .0306 at α = 0 .25 , τ T = 0 .1. The mechanism is teacher sharpness – at τT = 0 .1 the teacher distributes mass over ≈ 5–6 effective positives per anchor, at τT = 0 .05 over ≈ 2–3, and the sharper setting wins. Because our teacher is frozen and therefore reliable from step 0, Andonian et al.’s progressive schedule collapses to a fixed α, as the main text notes. Table A3 gives the head-to-head against vanilla hard-target CLIP at 400 epochs with three seeds and everything else matched. B.4 Optimisation settings Budget. Every cell trains for 400 epochs, and an epoch is a fixed 703 -window random draw from that cell’s cohort rather than a pass over it: 11 steps at batch 64, so 4400 optimiser steps for every cell on the ladder, from S = 10 to the top rung. The one exception is the pair of random-init arms above 

S = 701 , which were retrained at 800 epochs (8800 steps) once the shorter budget proved binding for them; the pretrained arm needed no such retrain. Since the schedule anneals over the total, those are different trajectories rather than longer ones, and Appendix C.3 measures what the original budget cost. The draw is uniform over the cohort and independent from epoch to epoch, so coverage stays even across epochs while the step count is decoupled from S. Were an epoch instead a pass over the cohort, every added subject would also buy added optimisation, and the ladder could not separate the two. The learning-rate schedule is a function of the epoch count alone, so it is identical step for step across every cell and both arms. 

Optimiser. Adam without weight decay, peak lr 10 −4, five epochs of linear warmup then cosine decay to 10 −6, stepped once per epoch; gradient-norm clipping at 1.0; single precision throughout, one 44 GB GPU per cell. Checkpoints are written every 25 epochs – 15 per cell, 31 for the 800-epoch retrains – which is what makes the per-cell selection of §C.1 a choice made after training rather than during it. Nothing is early-stopped, and no validation quantity gates the loop. 

Initialisation. The pretrained arm copies the released REVE encoder weights and nothing else – the two projections, the optimiser state and the schedule all begin fresh. The random-init arm is the same runs with that copy omitted, so the two arms differ in initialisation and in nothing else, and cells within an arm differ only in which subjects the sampler may draw. 

Batch size. Batch 64 is carried over from the random-init development line, where two comparisons favoured it and none favoured anything larger. At the depth-12 shape and lr 10 −4, batch 128 lost 0.0044 mean ∆R2 over 300 epochs with everything else held fixed. In an earlier, shallower configuration, batch 256 with the learning rate square-root-scaled to 2 × 10 −4 reached +0 .0078 

against +0 .0099 for batch 64 at lr 10 −4 at 99 epochs. Two caveats keep this from being a clean batch-size result. Both comparisons ran under the scene-mask objective rather than the soft-target one, and both were epoch-matched, so the larger-batch arms also took proportionally fewer optimizer steps – exactly the confound the fixed-step budget above removes. We did not re-run the comparison at matched steps, nor with gradient accumulation, and nothing below 64 was tried; memory bounded the top end, with batch 512 exceeding a 141 GB device 14 and batch 128 a 44 GB one at the earlier configuration’s footprint. Read the setting as inherited and never contradicted, not as an optimum we located. 

## C Checkpoint selection and compute 

C.1 How each cell’s checkpoint is chosen 

Every cell is evaluated at its own optimum rather than at an epoch shared across the ladder. The selector is held-out time-pool e →v@1, read at every saved checkpoint of that cell and taken as a plain argmax – no smoothing, and no snapping, since the search ranges over the saved checkpoints themselves. The held-out set is the draw’s cohort complement: with cohorts drawn from a pool of 

2156 , a cell trained on S subjects leaves 2156 − S of them untouched, and those recordings are legal selection data for that cell. Nothing in the selection touches the test release. Selected epochs run 75 –375 for the pretrained arm (median 288 ), 75 –775 for the depth-22 random-init arm (median 350 ) and 100 –775 for the depth-12 one (median 338 ), so no single shared epoch would have been right for any pair of arms, let alone all three. Six of the pretrained arm’s 30 cells select the last checkpoint available to them, against five and six for the two random-init arms; those cells report a bound rather than a located optimum and are marked as such wherever they are plotted. Appendix C.3 gives the per-rung medians and what the budget costs the cells that pin against it. 

The top rung barely replicates, and that is structural. The three draws at S = 1863 are not independent: each takes 1863 of the pool’s 2156 subjects, so two draws share 86 .4 % of their subjects in expectation. The between-draw spread at that rung therefore measures something much narrower than draw variance and should not be read as an error bar comparable to those at smaller S. This is why the top rung is excluded from every fit, and it is not a defect of this study in particular: any scaling ladder whose last rung approaches the size of its own dataset has it, and the cost of ignoring it is concrete. In an earlier sweep, whose top rung was the whole pool and so admitted a single draw, that one cell produced a coherent, five-readout, apparently-real saturation-then-decline – which reversed sign as soon as the pool was enlarged and the same S became drawable three ways. The same step in S measured −0.0347 r with one draw and +0 .0125 with three. 

Collapse at initialisation, and the screen for it. Roughly one cell in ten trained with this recipe fails silently: the contrastive loss sits at chance ( ln 64 = 4 .16 ) from epoch 1 and never moves, but evaluation still produces well-formed artifacts with numbers a reader would not flag. A cell like that is indistinguishable from a genuine low-S result unless the training loss is inspected, which is why we screen rather than eyeball. The test is relative: a cell is flagged when its final training loss exceeds 

1.30 × the median of its own S group. Relative, because loss scales with cohort size – S = 10 sits near 0.70 and S = 1863 near 2.8 – so a fixed cutoff would miss the same failure at low S. The threshold separates cleanly on our data: genuine failures land at 1.67 –3.09 × their group median while the worst healthy cell reaches 1.19 ×. Flagged cells are re-run at a different seed (2025, 7, 2026 in turn), capped at three attempts so that the procedure cannot become a search for a preferred answer, and every superseded run is retained on disk with its artifacts. The observed rate is 10 –11 % across two independent sweeps of 31 and 28 cells run with this recipe. Applied to the random-init arms of §3.2, the same screen flagged nothing.

The random-init optimum travels the length of the ladder under either selector, and the pretrained optimum does not – so no single fixed epoch can serve both arms, and a protocol that imposes one will report the random-init arm as having saturated when it has been truncated. And the random-init arms pin against their ceiling at the top of the ladder even at the doubled budget, so their terminal cells report bounds. Read nothing off the columns at S ≤ 50 : both arms are within ∼10 % of their peak by the first saved checkpoint there, so the curve is a plateau and its argmax is placed by noise rather than by convergence. 16 What the shorter budget cost. An argmax at the last checkpoint cannot distinguish a converged curve from a truncated one, so we measured the difference instead. So the budget was binding above S ≈ 1000 and not at all below S = 400 . Two consequences. The optimum grows with cohort size, which means larger cohorts need more optimisation and not merely more data. And had we kept the shorter budget, the random-init arm’s high-S points would sit about 

0.012 r low, understating its subject slope and overstating the initialisation gap at the top of the ladder by roughly that amount – which is why those cells were retrained rather than reported as they stood. 

Early stopping, in detail. The rule is patience on the same held-out retrieval curve, with patience set to 10 % of each arm’s own checkpoint count so that the unequal budgets are handled by the protocol rather than by a caveat: two checkpoints ( 50 epochs) for the pretrained arm, three ( 75 

epochs) for the random-init arms. Over the 15 cells per arm at S ≥ 400 it reproduces the argmax in 12, 8 and 8 cells respectively. The disagreements are not small perturbations but occasional catastrophic exits, where a temporary plateau exhausts the counter before the curve resumes climbing: the pretrained arm’s three misses cost 0.0028 –0.0249 , while the random-init depth-22 arm quits at epoch 200 on two cells whose optima lie at 625 and 675, costing 0.0401 and 0.0410 . Mean regret over the same cells is 0.0000 for the pretrained arm against 0.0031 for each random-init arm. 

Scope of these claims. These are claims about whether an arm finishes inside its budget, what its optimum costs, and whether a deployable rule can find it. They are not claims that the pretrained arm trains more smoothly – between-draw outcome variance is indistinguishable across arms, and its epoch curve is rougher relative to its own rise – nor about how quickly the optimum arrives, since the learning rate anneals over each arm’s own budget and the arms were not given equal ones. Nor is the compute that produced the released REVE weights counted in any FLOP figure here; that cost is real, but already paid and reusable across every cell of the ladder.

The percent-of-native figures above are the depth-12 arm measured against its own matched anchor and stand; they should not be read onto depth 22, and a depth-22 native cell, which would settle it, does not exist. 

Why the zero-shot null is a real failure and not a baseline artifact. For the depth-12 arm the transferred model scores below a random encoder at every K, while DM-native cells clear the same inflated baseline – so the metric can detect genuine alignment when it is present. The encoder learns something about EEG-during-movie-watching that is not specific to one film; the EEG projection that maps it into V-JEPA-2 space is specific to that film.
