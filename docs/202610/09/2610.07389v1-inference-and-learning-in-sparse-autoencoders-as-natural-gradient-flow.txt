## ABSTRACT 

Sparse autoencoders are widely used to uncover interpretable features in neural networks, yet reliable recovery remains difficult when features overlap or activate infrequently. These challenges involve both inferring which features explain an input and learning the dictionary that represents them. Here, we unify inference and dictionary learning as natural-gradient flows on a shared variational free energy. We instantiate this framework as Be FOND, an encoder-free sparse coding model with closed-form inference and learning dynamics. We show how recurrent explaining away reduces interference between overlapping features, while Fisher precondi-tioning can compensate for the slow learning of rare features. On synthetic data, 

Be FOND improves dictionary recovery and rare-feature detection, with a growing advantage over amortized baselines as superposition increases. On language-model activations, it improves single-feature concept detection and selective intervention, outperforming pretrained reference SAEs with substantially less training data. Its feature quality continues to improve with dictionary width, whereas the evaluated baselines largely plateau. Together, these results show how improving inference and learning within a unified probabilistic framework can make better use of data and dictionary capacity to interpret and intervene on neural representations. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) infer sparse representations of data in terms of a learned, overcomplete dictionary of features. Their roots lie in sparse coding (Olshausen & Field, 1996; Lewicki & Sejnowski, 2000), motivated by the organization of sensory representations in biological systems (Olshausen & Field, 1997; 2004). SAEs have since become central in interpreting neural network representations (reviewed in Klindt et al., 2026), especially those of language models (Yun et al., 2021; Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025). Despite these successes, SAEs face challenges in both inference and dictionary learning. Standard SAEs use a trained neural network to perform one-pass inference—known as amortized inference— which can struggle to disentangle overlapping features in superposition (O’Neill et al., 2025). But improving inference alone is insufficient. In controlled experiments, Barin-Pacela et al. (2026) identified dictionary learning as the bottleneck: replacing an SAE’s encoder with iterative inference on the same learned dictionary failed to close the feature-recovery gap. These problems are coupled during training: the dictionary determines which explanations of the data are available, while inferred feature activations shape the learning signal used to update the dictionary. Therefore, inference errors can compromise dictionary learning, and a poor dictionary can limit what inference can recover. Unlike standard SAEs, classical sparse coding methods typically perform inference through iterative, per-sample optimization (Rozell et al., 2008; Gregor & LeCun, 2010). More recently in this lineage, Vafaii et al. (2025) introduced the Free-energy Online Natural-gradient Dynamics (FOND) frame-work, which formulates inference as natural-gradient flow on variational free energy. FOND’s guiding principle is to respect the intrinsic geometry of the statistical manifold: updates are measured by their effect on the represented probability distribution through the Fisher information metric (Amari, 1998; Martens, 2020; Khan & Rue, 2023). Here, we extend this principle to learning. Just as inference follows natural-gradient flow on free energy with respect to variational parameters, we define learning as natural-gradient flow on the same objective with respect to generative parameters. Both processes thus respect the statistical geometry of the distributions they update. We instantiate this extended FOND framework as Be FOND: an encoder-free iterative SAE with Bernoulli latents, a factorized prior, and a linear Gaussian decoder. We derive closed-form expressions for its inference and learning dynamics, allowing the dictionary to be learned without backpropagating through the inference trajectory. On the inference side, Be FOND implements explaining away through recurrent interactions between overlapping features, enabling lateral competition leading to sparse, disentangled representations. We isolate the contribution of these interactions and show that they become increasingly important as fea-ture superposition increases. On the learning side, we show how infrequent activation slows Euclidean dictionary learning. We prove that Fisher preconditioning removes this frequency dependence, giving rare and common features the same local learning rate. Motivated by this result, we hypothesize that Fisher preconditioning would improve rare-feature recovery in Be FOND. Empirically, Be FOND learns more accurate dictionaries and detects rare features with few misses or false positives, while using substantially fewer training samples than SAE baselines and Euclidean ablations. We evaluate Be FOND on synthetic data with known ground-truth features using SynthSAEBench 

(Chanin & Garriga-Alonso, 2026), and on language-model representations using SAEBench (Kar-vonen et al., 2025). Baselines include amortized SAEs—BatchTopK (Bussmann et al., 2024), Ma-tryoshka BatchTopK (Bussmann et al., 2025), and JumpReLU (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024b)—and an iterative mean-field variational baseline with Adam decoder learning (Kingma & Ba, 2015). 2On Gemma-2-2B layer-12 activations, Be FOND outperforms all evaluated baselines on both sparse probing (Kantamneni et al., 2025) and RAVEL disentanglement (Huang et al., 2024). Be FOND’s performance continues to improve as we increase dictionary width from 16 k to 524 k latents, whereas the baselines plateau (Fig. 1b,d). These gains also emerge with substantially less training data: at a dictionary width of 524 k, Be FOND matches the evaluated Gemma Scope JumpReLU checkpoints (Lieberum et al., 2024) in sparse probing after just ∼6M training tokens, compared with the 8B tokens used to train those checkpoints, achieving over 1,000 × reduction in training tokens (Fig. 1a). Summary of our contributions and paper outline: 

(1) We review necessary background (section 2). We then extend FOND (Vafaii et al., 2025) to unify inference and learning as natural-gradient flows on variational free energy, instantiate it as Bernoulli-distributed Be FOND, and derive its dynamics in closed-form (section 3 and section B). 

(2) We analyze Be FOND’s recurrent “explaining away” (section 4.1) and use ablations to establish its contribution to feature recovery under increasing superposition (section C.3.1). 

(3) We characterize how Fisher preconditioning in Be FOND’s learning rule compensates for feature rarity (section 4.2), and test its consequences for dictionary learning through theoretical analysis (theorem 4.2), synthetic experiments, and optimizer ablations (sections C.3.2, C.4 and C.5). 

(4) We evaluate Be FOND on language-model representations, examining feature quality, sample efficiency, and scaling with dictionary width (section 5). 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Notation. Bold lowercase and uppercase symbols denote vectors and matrices, such as a and A.Inference and learning quantities appear in red and blue, respectively. 

Sparse coding. Classical sparse coding (Olshausen & Field, 1996; 1997; Lewicki & Sejnowski, 2000; Aharon et al., 2006; Rozell et al., 2008; Gregor & LeCun, 2010; Mairal et al., 2010) models each observation x ∈ RM as a linear combination of dictionary atoms with sparse coefficients z:

x = Φz + ϵ , ϵ ∼ N (0, IM ) , (1) The dictionary Φ ∈ RM ×K has atoms Φ·i ≡ ϕ(i) as its columns. We consider overcomplete dictionaries, whose atoms outnumber the input dimensions ( K > M ), and therefore cannot all be mutually orthogonal, making some overlap between atoms unavoidable. Learning estimates the dictionary from data, while inference finds sparse codes that explain each input. These processes are coupled because inference uses the current dictionary, and dictionary updates depend on the inferred codes. In the overcomplete setting, superposition —overlap between atoms—makes sparse code inference particularly challenging (see section A.1). 

Amortized versus iterative inference. Standard SAEs learn an encoder z = fϕ(x) that predicts a sparse code in one forward pass. These include ReLU (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2024), JumpReLU (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024b), gated (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024a), TopK (Makhzani & Frey, 2014; Gao et al., 2025), BatchTopK (Bussmann et al., 2024), and Matryoshka BatchTopK (Bussmann et al., 2025) SAEs. Training this mapping across samples 

amortizes inference, replacing a separate test-time optimization for each input. Iterative sparse coding instead refines the code for each observation (Rozell et al., 2008; Beck & Teboulle, 2009; Fang et al., 2022). In continuous time, this takes the schematic form, ˙z = g(z, Φ, x).Iterative inference has yielded mixed results on language-model representations (Chanin, 2025; Sun et al., 2026). See section A.2 for further discussion.

## 4 THEORETICAL ANALYSES , SYNTHETIC EXPERIMENTS , AND ABLATIONS 

We study inference and learning using theory and synthetic data with known features and activations. Ablations test lateral interactions and compare separately tuned natural and Euclidean recipes. 4.1 INFERENCE : Be FOND’ S RECURRENT TERM ENABLES EXPLAINING AWAY 

Superposition mixes evidence from overlapping features (Elhage et al., 2022; Klindt et al., 2026), making inference of sparse codes difficult (section A.1). Here, we show how Be FOND’s iterative inference mitigates this interference through a mechanism known as explaining away . As an example, wet grass could be explained by rain or a sprinkler. Observing a wet roof supports rain without changing the sprinkler’s direct evidence from the grass. As rain explains more of the wetness, the sprinkler becomes less likely. This reduction in belief in one explanation through evidence for another is known as explaining away (Murphy, 2012). The challenge is that the influence of one piece of evidence depends on the others. An encoder that compresses the input to one fixed projection cannot generally capture the full dependence, as the following proposition establishes.

Features extracted from language models vary widely in activation frequency (Gao et al., 2025), and larger SAE dictionaries recover increasingly rare concepts (Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024). Scaling dictionary width therefore places greater demands on learning from rare examples: additional neurons must capture infrequent features to expand the model’s coverage. 

Learning an infrequently active feature. For an isolated feature active with probability ρ, averag-ing over inputs scales the decoder gradient by ρ. The same decoder error therefore produces a smaller update for a rarer feature. Matched Fisher preconditioning divides out this frequency factor. 

Theorem 4.2 (Exact rarity compensation) . For p(x | z) = N (x; Φz, I), fix a training minibatch and hold its inferred distributions and prior references fixed. Let Φ∗ minimize F. If the averaged second moments satisfy Ep = E ≻ 0, then: Euclidean gradient : ˙Φ = −ηΦ∇ΦF = −ηΦ(Φ − Φ∗)E ,

Natural gradient : ˙Φ = −ηΦG−1Φ ∇ΦF = −ηΦ(Φ − Φ∗) ,

where GΦ = Ep ⊗ I and ηΦ > 0.Natural flow reduces every decoder error by e−ηΦ∆t over an interval ∆t ≥ 0. For the same fractional error reduction, Euclidean flow takes κ(E) ≥ ρmax /ρ min times as long in its slowest eigendirection as in its fastest, where ρi = Eii is the minibatch-averaged posterior activation probability. 

The rate difference becomes more pronounced as the frequency range widens. If ρi = c i −α, with 

α > 0 and i = 1 , . . . , K , the slowest Euclidean direction takes at least Kα times as long as the fastest to achieve the same fractional error reduction. Under the theorem’s matched-moment assumptions, natural flow gives every direction the same rate. For the proof and the extension to unequal prior and posterior second moments, see section C.4. Section C.4.2 shows that, with known codes and frozen Adam normalization, an optimer used by many common SAE baselines (Bussmann et al., 2024; Rajamanoharan et al., 2024b), Zipfian feature frequencies leave a local condition number of at least Kα/ 2, whereas matched Fisher preconditioning reduces it to one. 

Recovering rare features. The full-width SynthSAEBench setting (Chanin & Garriga-Alonso, 2026) tests whether decoder capacity recovers the rare tail, with 16,384 learned latents for 16,384 generating features. This supplies one decoder slot per true feature, so complete recovery requires representing rare features alongside common ones. MCC measures recovery of the feature directions, while Rare F1 measures detection of the 25% least frequent generating features, averaging their activation F1 scores. Be FOND leads all metrics, exceeding the evaluated baselines (Fig. 4; Table 5). 

Ablating inference and learning geometry. The natural-inference/natural-learning recipe reaches stronger recovery with fewer decoder updates than the separately tuned Euclidean variants (Fig. 7; section C.3.2). Section C.5.2 reports learning curves against the other baselines, and Table 5 reports dead-latent counts alongside recovery. 

## 5 EVALUATING Be FOND ON LANGUAGE -MODEL ACTIVATIONS 

We evaluate whether Be FOND’s inference and learning benefits studied in section 4 translate to improved feature quality in language-model representations. We focus on three questions: (1) whether 

Be FOND outperforms widely used SAE baselines on Gemma 2B scale activations, (2) whether these benefits increase with dictionary width, and (3) how early they emerge during training.

Evaluation metrics. Reconstruction quality alone does not establish whether an SAE has learned useful features (Gao et al., 2025; Karvonen et al., 2025). We therefore evaluate two complementary capabilities using SAEBench (Karvonen et al., 2025). 

Sparse probing (Kantamneni et al., 2025) tests concept detection using a logistic probe restricted to 

k = 1 or k = 5 selected SAE features. We average task-level accuracy, ROC-AUC, and F1 across 

113 classification tasks. Accuracy measures correct predictions, ROC-AUC measures the ranking of positive versus negative examples, and F1 summarizes the balance between precision and recall. 

RAVEL (Resolving Attribute–Value Entanglements in Language Models; Huang et al., 2024) tests selective intervention on represented attributes. Its cause score measures successful changes to a target attribute, while isolation measures preservation of other attributes, and disentanglement is the mean of these two scores. Higher scores are better for all reported metrics. We report full numerical results in Table 6. 

Scaling with dictionary width. Be FOND achieves the highest mean RAVEL disentanglement at every tested width, including comparisons with the evaluated Gemma Scope checkpoints (Fig. 1d). At 

524 k latents, its score is 0.827 ± 0.001 , compared with 0.748 ± 0.004 for BatchTopK, the strongest amortized baseline trained on 50 M tokens (Table 6). This 7.9 percentage-point advantage reflects improvements in both cause and isolation: Be FOND scores 0.800 and 0.854 , respectively, versus 

0.719 and 0.776 for BatchTopK. Single-feature probing also favors Be FOND at large widths. At 524 k, its accuracy is 0.789 ± 0.002 ,versus 0.774 ± 0.002 for Matryoshka BatchTopK, an improvement of 1.5 percentage points. Across widths from 16 k to 524 k, Be FOND’s single-feature accuracy increases from 0.771 to 0.789 , and its RAVEL score increases from 0.786 to 0.827 (Fig. 1b,d). Over the same range, BatchTopK and Matryoshka each improve by approximately 0.6 percentage points in single-feature accuracy. Thus, increasing dictionary width produces larger gains for Be FOND than for either amortized baseline. Differences between methods are smaller in five-feature probing accuracy, and the AUC and F1 rankings vary across widths (Table 9). 9High feature quality emerges early in training. At 524 k latents, Be FOND reaches the evaluated Gemma Scope single-feature probing score by ∼6M training tokens (Fig. 1a). The corresponding Gemma Scope checkpoints were trained on 8B tokens, a training-budget ratio of over 1,000 ×. By 50M tokens, Be FOND exceeds their single-feature accuracy ( 0.789 versus 0.7735 ) and RAVEL disentanglement ( 0.827 versus 0.7555 ), using 160 × fewer training tokens. 

Comparison with iterative mean-field inference. The mean-field baseline performs iterative variational inference and learns its decoder using Adam. At 524 k, it reaches single-feature accuracy of 0.751 ± 0.007 and RAVEL disentanglement of 0.685 ± 0.029 , significantly underperforming 

Be FOND on both metrics (Table 6). 

## 6 CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION 

We formulate inference and dictionary learning as natural-gradient flows on a shared variational free energy (the FOND framework). Our theory establishes limitations of single-projection encoders and shows how Fisher preconditioning compensates for infrequent activation under frozen, matched moments. We introduce Be FOND, which improves synthetic feature recovery and supports increasing feature quality at larger dictionary widths on Gemma. 

Limitations. Repeated inference steps and numerical decoder updates increase Be FOND’s compu-tational cost (sections B.8 and D.3). Future work will focus on improving computational efficiency, speeding up inference convergence by adding momentum, and extending the factorized variational family with pairwise interactions to represent dependencies between latent features. On Gemma-2-2B layer-12 activations, Be FOND improves concept detection and selective interven-tion. Its gains increase with dictionary width while the evaluated baselines largely plateau. Together with recurrent explaining away and improved rare-feature recovery, these results show the practical value of taking the statistical geometry of inference and learning seriously. The broader lesson is how adaptation dynamics should be derived: 

Physics of Adaptation : deriving covariant adaptation dynamics Inference and learning dynamics should respect the intrinsic geometry and symmetries of the underlying model (section A.5).

A.2 ITERATIVE SPARSE ENCODERS .Iterative encoders refine sparse representations through successive updates rather than a single feed-forward prediction. ISTA, FISTA, and soft-thresholding LCA optimize ℓ1-regularized reconstruction (Daubechies et al., 2004; Beck & Teboulle, 2009; Rozell et al., 2008), with LCA implementing lateral competition. LISTA learns a finite-step approximation (Gregor & LeCun, 2010), while matching pursuit (MP) greedily selects atoms to explain the remaining residual (Mallat & Zhang, 1993). With a fixed pretrained dictionary, better inference remains limited by the learned feature directions. Additional ISTA refinement improves reconstruction with little change in BatchTopK probe scores; non-amortized ISTA worsens splitting for Gemma TopK and increases dead latents for Pythia Standard SAEs (Sun et al., 2026). FISTA applied to fixed SAE dictionaries also fails to close the feature-recovery gap in controlled experiments, implicating the learned dictionary as a bottleneck (Barin-Pacela et al., 2026). On Pythia-160M, however, Sun et al. report improved targeted-probe selectivity and generally higher generative intervention scores with semi- and non-amortized inference (Sun et al., 2026, Appendix C). Approaches that also learn the dictionary have shown promising results: MP-SAE captures hier-archical features through residual-guided selection and supports different inference-time sparsity levels (Costa et al., 2025), while LocA-SAE seeks similar feature-quality benefits through grouped amortized encoders rather than explicit iterative refinement (Sun et al., 2026). But better reconstruc-tion has not consistently translated into better interpretability tools. On Gemma-2-2B, MP-SAEs improve reconstruction but underperform the tested feed-forward baselines on sparse probing, with substantially longer training. The evaluator therefore recommends against them as a first choice for practical interpretability (Chanin, 2025). LocA-SAE eliminates dead latents on Gemma but scores below the Standard SAE on the same proxy probes, so improved utilization need not yield more predictive features (Sun et al., 2026). The principle of general covariance. With the potential energy fixed by the loss, let us turn to the kinetic part of the Lagrangian. How should we choose it? Should we just do a bunch of trial and error? Or is there a principled way to approach this? Physics gives us a basic requirement: The principle of general covariance “The general laws of nature are to be expressed by equations which hold good for all systems of co-ordinates, that is, are co-variant with respect to any substitutions whatever (generally co-variant). ” — Einstein (1916) Imagine tossing a ball across a room. If two people track its motion—one standing upright and one tilted sideways—they will record different numbers for its horizontal and vertical motion. Yet, both of them watch the ball land in the exact same spot. The ball has no idea about the observers watching it; it follows a physical path that does not change simply because different observers choose to describe it in different ways. More concretely, consider Newton’s second law, F = ma. When you change your coordinate system, the actual numerical values of force and acceleration change, but the mathematical form of the law does not: F = ma becomes F′ = ma′ because both sides “co-vary” in lockstep under the transformation. In this sense, Newton’s law describes a “ co-variant ” dynamical rule under Galilean transformations. If rotating or shifting our reference frame altered where the ball landed, the equation wouldn’t be describing the ball at all—it would merely be describing our choice of coordinates. 

Descartes, geometry, and algebra. This idea is not new. It goes back to Descartes’ link between geometry and algebra: choose a coordinate system, and a point becomes a tuple of numbers. We have done this so often that it is easy to forget what comes with it: redundancy in the description. The origin, the orientation of the axes, and the units are all choices of the modeler. The same physical trajectory admits many different numerical descriptions. Covariance requires the equations to transform consistently between them, so that they all predict the same physical motion. 

The principle of general covariance for inference and learning. Once again, we argue that the dynamics of adaptation are no different. A Bernoulli belief can be described by its activation probability or by its logit. These describe exactly the same distribution. Why should the learner’s response to evidence change just because we switched between these two descriptions? We require the continuous dynamics of inference and learning to describe the same evolution of distributions under smooth, invertible reparameterizations (Amari, 1998; Martens, 2020). There is also a concrete computational reason for this requirement. Take a scalar belief parameter 

u and write it instead as v = 10 u. If we apply ordinary Euclidean gradient descent in v, the gradient becomes ten times smaller, and converting the step back to u introduces another factor of ten. 24 Therefore, with the same learning rate, the update in u becomes 100 × smaller. Same belief, same evidence, same objective, but a different pace of adaptation, just because we changed the units. We can fix this scalar example by re-tuning the learning rate. But when different coordinates are rescaled differently, a single learning rate cannot generally restore all their relative update speeds. Such artificial conditioning can force small steps for stability in some directions while leaving others barely moving (Martens, 2020). With a limited inference or training budget, this can determine which beliefs or features we have time to learn. We may then spend extra computation, and extra stochastic training samples, compensating for arbitrary scales chosen by the engineer. A coordinate choice should not get to decide how well we can adapt. Therefore, we draw inspiration from Einstein (1916) and postulate the same thing for physics of adaptation: The principle of general covariance, applied to inference and learning The physics of adaptation must not depend on your choice of coordinate system. To implement this principle, we need a measure of motion that remain invariant under coordinate transformations. This is what motivates the kinetic energy of beliefs below. 

The kinetic energy of beliefs. We can arrive at a suitable kinetic term from two directions. The first is geometric. The second route starts with a more physical question: what does it cost to change one’s mind ?Zellner (1988) derived Bayes’ rule as an optimal information processor, with no information lost between its inputs and outputs. Later, Zellner (2002) showed that introducing costs of changing beliefs can alter the optimal update, producing dynamic partial adjustment rather than an immediate revision. This is the distinction we need: specifying the posterior we ought to reach does not tell us how a physical learner gets there, or what the journey costs. We take the next step by giving beliefs a kinetic term, representing their resistance to change as inertia. For a brain, this cost has a concrete physical basis. Brains are made of matter, and changing their state takes energy. Fast changes in neural activity involve ion flows across cell membranes, and restoring the ion gradients costs ATP (Attwell & Laughlin, 2001). Slower changes in synaptic strength can involve protein synthesis and physical remodeling of dendritic spines, supported by local metabolic resources (Rangaraju et al., 2019). Inference and learning take place through these physical processes. Why should the beliefs they implement be free to move? To quantify this motion, let qλ(t) represent the learner’s current belief. It should also obey a composition rule: describing an outcome in stages should give the same information as describing it all at once. For finite outcome spaces, these requirements uniquely select DKL (Q ∥ P ), up to a positive constant. Summary. Let us take stock of what we have done. We started from a physics-centered view of “dynamics is everything,” and demonstrated how dynamics can be derived from applying the principle of stationary action plus dissipation. We then identified the loss as a potential, required adaptation to be independent of arbitrary coordinates (principle of general covariance), and used local information gain to construct the kinetic energy of beliefs. A standard choice of friction then gave us the full dissipative equations of motion, including inertia and geometric corrections. Natural-gradient inference and learning followed in the overdamped (massless) limit, where that inertia is negligible. 

Physics of adaptation as a broad framework for deriving inference and learning dynamics. 

We could have motivated the same practical update through a KL trust region (section A.4), or appealed directly to Amari’s natural gradient (Amari, 1998). Those arguments are enough to justify the rule. We hope this route also reveals the richer framework underneath it: beliefs moving through a statistical manifold, driven by a task and constrained by the physical cost of adaptation. Future work should consider deriving other dynamics within that same framework by keeping inertia, changing the dissipation law, or considering more generalized belief states with different, perhaps adaptive, geometries that evolve in time in response to experience. 28 B EXTENDED FOND MODEL AND DYNAMICS

B.6 NUMERICAL INTEGRATION AND MATRIX -FREE UPDATES 

Stiffness and exponential integration. Natural gradients specify the continuous-time evolution of beliefs and model parameters, but implementing these dynamics requires a numerical update. A diffi-culty with explicit Euler is that stable continuous dynamics can become unstable after discretization. For the simple relaxation ˙v = −βv , Euler multiplies v by 1−βh , whereas exact integration multiplies it by e−βh . Euler therefore requires 0 < βh < 2 for decay. When fast relaxation accompanies slower dynamics, this stability restriction can force small steps even when the solution changes slowly. Exponential integrators address this source of stiffness by integrating the linear component exactly (Cox & Matthews, 2002; Hochbruck & Ostermann, 2010). 

Inference integration. Write eq. (72) as ˙u = a(r) − β(u − uref ), where a contains the recon-struction and Bernoulli uncertainty terms.
