## ABSTRACT 

The goal of the partial information decomposition (PID) is to quantify the re-dundant and synergistic information that multiple sources provide about a target. PID has many applications in machine learning, neuroscience, and other fields, but defining and computing it for high-dimensional continuous systems remains challenging. Here, we define a PID for multivariate Gaussian systems based on the Blackwell order, which formalizes when one channel is more informative than another. We prove that Gaussian channels are optimal for extracting both redun-dant and union information, yielding an intuitive geometric interpretation and an efficient numerical algorithm for the PID. Our union information and synergy co-incide with the well-known BROJA measures, and we derive closed-form expres-sions for both in the case of two sources. We also argue that Blackwell redundancy (which differs from BROJA) is the only existing redundancy measure that satis-fies a set of natural desiderata. We demonstrate the scalability of our method on systems with up to a thousand dimensions or sources. Our approach is illustrated on an optimal control problem, where it identifies redundant and synergistic inter-actions between sensor and memory. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

In many scientific domains, one seeks to understand how information about a target variable Y is distributed among a set of source variables X1, . . . , X n. For example, in neuroscience, one may ask how information about a stimulus is encoded across different brain regions (Luppi et al., 2024); in machine learning, one may ask how different features, layers, or modalities combine to produce the model’s output (Liang et al., 2023); and so on. To answer such questions, the partial information decomposition (PID) (Williams & Beer, 2010) aims to decompose the information that the sources have about the target into redundancy (present in each individual source), union information (present in at least one source), unique information (non-redundant information in each source), and synergy 

(present only in the joint outcome of the sources). In general, the PID is inspired by an analogy be-tween information theory and set theory. For instance, if the information in each source is imagined as a set, then redundancy is the size of the intersection of these sets, while synergy is the difference between the size of the joint set and that of the union (Williams, 2011; Kolchinsky, 2022). The PID requires an extension of standard information theory because existing quantities such as mutual information conflate synergy and redundancy (Williams & Beer, 2010). However, develop-ment of this extension faces conceptual and practical challenges. Conceptually, it has proven difficult to define the PID in a way that is operationally meaningful and applicable to any number of sources. Nonetheless, recent work has shown that these difficulties can be overcome by making the information-set analogy formal; the only requirement is a mathematical condition that specifies when one channel is less informative about the target than another (Kolchin-sky, 2022). The Blackwell order provides a principled answer: one channel is less informative if it can be produced by stochastic processing of the other or, equivalently, if it is no better on any decision problem (Blackwell, 1953). The resulting “Blackwell PID” leads to nonnegative measures of redundant, union, and synergistic information for any number of sources (Kolchinsky, 2022). 1arXiv:2610.07360v1 [cs.IT] 5 Oct 2026 The practical challenge is that PID quantities, such as redundancy and synergy, may be difficult to compute, especially in continuous systems with many sources or high-dimensional variables.

As an important first step, we address these challenges in the setting of multivariate Gaus-sians, which play a fundamental role in time-series modeling, filtering, control theory, ma-chine learning, and many other settings. Our formulation also has practical advantages. In particular, for any number of sources, we show that redundancy and union information can be efficiently computed by solving a con-vex optimization problem, which easily scales to a thousand dimensions or sources. In the special case of two sources, we uncover closed-form expressions for union information and synergy, allowing for very fast evaluation. We illustrate our approach on an optimal control problem, where we quantify redundant and syn-ergistic interactions between sensor and memory. Throughout, we measure information in units of nats. Proofs and additional material are provided in the Supplementary Material.

A channel PB|Y is Blackwell less in-formative than PC|Y , written PB|Y ⊑Y PC|Y , if there is a Markov kernel κ from C to B such that 

PB|Y (E|y) = R κ(E|c) PC|Y (dc |y) for every measurable set E and PY -almost every y. In other words, B can be simulated by garbling the output of C, without further access to Y . The Black-well order has a fundamental operational characterization in decision theory (Blackwell, 1953): 

PB|Y ⊑Y PC|Y iff an agent with C can do at least as well as an agent with B on every decision problem about Y . Recently, the Blackwell order has attracted growing attention in machine learning applications (Feng et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026; Halder et al., 2025; Kaissis et al., 2024). Combining the Blackwell order with Eq. (2) gives the Blackwell PID . Blackwell redundancy I∩ is the most information one can have about Y without being able to outperform any single source on any decision problem; Blackwell union information I∪ is the least information needed in order to perform at least as well as every source. These measures are defined for any number of sources, uniquely satisfy a natural set of axioms, and behave sensibly on various examples. Blackwell redun-dancy can also be formulated as an information-bottleneck-style objective, where one maximizes predictive information while hiding source identity (Kolchinsky, 2024). Blackwell redundancy and union information are formal duals, but their numerical values are not re-lated by inclusion–exclusion (Möbius inversion). In fact, it has been argued that inclusion–exclusion should not apply to the PID, since it leads to inconsistencies (Kolchinsky, 2022).

## 9 DISCUSSION 

We have shown that the Blackwell PID admits an exact and tractable characterization for arbitrarily many Gaussian sources and target dimensions, leading to a simple geometric interpretation and efficient numerical methods. This provides a principled PID framework for characterizing how information is encoded across distributed representations. We are also able to identify optimal channels that extract the content of redundant and union informa-tion. Although we have not explored this here, such analyses could be useful in various data-driven applications. For example, in high-dimensional neuroimaging data (Luppi et al., 2024), one may consider redundant modes shared across brain regions or timepoints. Another direction is to study how the organization of information changes during learning. Deep linear networks (Saxe et al., 2014) provide a controlled setting in which to follow redundancy and synergy across training trajectories, and the optimal channels could reveal whether learning causes different units to represent the same target features or to specialize in different ones. Finally, it is interesting to explore the use of our PID quantities as components of learning objectives. Penalizing redundancy between groups of units or modalities would push them to encode different target features, favoring disentangled or complementary representations, whereas rewarding redun-dancy would favor robustness to the loss of individual units. Similarly, penalizing synergy would favor representations whose information can be read out from individual components. We also note that PID-based objectives have been proposed as local goal functions for individual neurons (Wibral et al., 2017; Makkeh et al., 2025; Schneider et al., 2025); our approach could be used to extend such objectives to continuous, multivariate representations. 9REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT

## S3 NEWTON –PCG OPTIMIZATION ALGORITHM 

Here we describe our optimization algorithm for computing redundancy and union information. Both use the same method: Newton’s method with matrix-free steps computed by preconditioned conjugate gradients (PCG), constraints imposed by log-barriers, and primal–dual stopping bounds used to certify accuracy. After a common preprocessing step, we describe the reduced problem, its bounds, and its barrier formulas separately for redundancy (Sec. S3.2) and union information (Sec. Certified accuracy. Our formulation returns explicit bounds ILB ≤ I ≤ IUB on the globally optimal information value and reports success only when IUB − ILB ≤ ϵabs . The original gradient-based BROJA implementations (Venkatesh et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025) instead stop when the objective stagnates, which does not certify optimality. Thus, for each matched instance, we first obtain our certified union interval [ILB , I UB ]. We then run each competing method until its feasible objective reaches or improves on IUB , or until the time limit is reached. The competitors do not compute lower bounds or certify an optimality gap themselves. This protocol deliberately favors them, because our timing includes certification.
