# Conditional Flow Matching for Transport Between Markov Processes 

Syamantak Kumar † Dheeraj Nagaraj * Saptarshi Roy ‡ Purnamrita Sarkar §

Abstract 

Motivated by sequence-to-sequence transport in the context time-series domain adaptation, we study the problem of transportation between trajectories of Markov processes. Given a limited number of trajectories from source distribution and the target distribution, we formulate a flow matching based algorithm which learns a transport map from the source to target trajectory distribution, while preserving the Markov structure. We show that this is consistent in the population limit and derive finite-sample error bounds under mixing time assumptions, following the analysis of classical statistical problems including regression (Nagaraj et al., 2020), principal component analysis (Kumar and Sarkar, 2023), and matrix concentration (Neeman et al., 2024) in the Markov setting. We complement that with a lower-bound construction showing that a mixing-time dependent sample complexity is unavoidable even with regular Gaussian conditional transitions. We evaluate on synthetic and real-world data. For image retrieval from electroencephalography (EEG) on THINGS-EEG2 (Gifford et al., 2022), the task is to identify the viewed image from EEG signals captured from human subjects, which suffers from high inter subject variability. We augment the ENIGMA decoder (Kneeland et al., 2026) with a conditional flow before its subject-specific temporal map. This improves mean top-5 retrieval accuracy from 

43 .87% to 49 .05% , an 11 .82% relative improvement.

Sequence-to-sequence tasks seek to learn a map from an input sequence to an output sequence. In time-series adaptation, such a map can transform observations from a source process to match the statistical behavior of a target process. Related transport and alignment problems arise in sensor calibration (Kim et al., 2020), climate-model bias correction (Robin et al., 2019), and time-series domain adaptation (Painblanc et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2026). In many of these cases, the sequences themselves are long, but the number of sequences available from the source and target distributions is limited. A practical concern is how much data must be collected to learn this map for a subject or device. Sensor calibration can require repeated, costly data collection (Kim et al., 2020), while neural decoding motivates adapting pretrained models using limited recordings from each new subject. ENIGMA and MindEye2 pursue this objective for EEG and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), respectively (Kneeland et al., 2026; Scotti et al., 2024). These constraints motivate studying adaptation from one or a few observed trajectories. Although each trajectory contains many observations, those observations are temporally dependent. This raises the question: 

Can we learn, from one source and one target trajectory, a map that transforms a fresh source trajectory into one that follows the target dynamics? 

In this paper, we study this problem for Markovian trajectories. When both the source and target follow Markov processes, we want the learned mapping to preserve the target’s transition law and hence its Markov structure in the generated trajectory. At first glance, this task seems daunting: treating an entire trajectory as one high-dimensional datapoint leaves us with only one sample from each trajectory distribution. However, the Markov property and time homogeneity let us learn a common transition rule from successive observations. Under stationarity, these observations share a common transition-pair distribution, although they remain dependent. We exploit this structure to give a principled answer to the question above. 

In particular, we propose an algorithm based on conditional flow matching for transporting a source trajectory to a target trajectory, accounting for the source history through the current source state. We prove exact population-level transport under suitable assumptions, provide finite-sample error bounds, and evaluate the algorithm on real and simulated data. 

Transport between Markov chains has also been studied through cost-minimizing joint distributions over transitions and trajectories (O’Connor et al., 2022; Moulos, 2021). Empirical evaluation. Section 5 evaluates Gaussian and nonlinear transition recovery and supervised EEG-to-image retrieval. This raises mean top-5 retrieval accuracy from 43 .87% to 49 .05% , an 11 .82% relative improvement. 

1.2 Related work 

Kerrigan et al. (2024a) and Chemseddine et al. (2025) construct flows between conditional distri-butions. For Markov chains, Moulos (2021) and O’Connor et al. (2022) optimize trajectory and transition couplings through dynamic programming, while O’Connor et al. (2024) estimate stationary transport plans and their costs from observations. We use conditional flow matching to learn a continuous transition map from one source and one target trajectory. Conditioning on current source and target states lets us reuse this map to generate from a fresh source trajectory. Zhou and Liu (2025) and Gaur et al. (2025) give finite-sample error bounds for neural flow matching trained on independent samples. For sequential data, Lim et al. (2026) express the empirical flow-matching solution as a similarity-weighted average of observed transition velocities and prove convergence as the trajectory grows. We give finite-sample bounds for learning from dependent transitions: for a finite candidate class, we bound the number of transitions needed for a prescribed velocity error in terms of the transition mixing time, and prove that a mixing-dependent sample threshold is necessary. Appendix F surveys further related work.

# 6 Conclusion 

We prove that conditional flow matching transforms a fresh source trajectory into the exact target trajectory distribution at the population level. For a finite candidate class, we prove a sample-complexity upper bound proportional to mixing time up to logarithmic factors, a lower bound showing that this dependence is unavoidable, and a finite-sample rollout bound. Our synthetic experiments show the value of conditioning on the previous source state, and our EEG study shows improved adaptation between subjects. Extending the analysis beyond finite classes, identifying when accuracy on observed transitions carries over to generated trajectories, and handling longer memory remain open directions. Generation can be slow because it requires sequential ODE solves along the trajectory. Reducing this latency is another interesting direction, potentially through parallel Picard iteration (Shih et al., 2023) or training flows to generate several future states jointly.

A.1 Conditional transport and trajectory generation

# E Ten-subject EEG study 

E.1 Protocol 

We evaluate all ten THINGS-EEG2 subjects using the released ENIGMA implementation (Kneeland et al., 2026; Alljoined, 2025) under the matched subject-adaptation protocol in Section 5. ENIGMA uses subject-specific alignment and fine-tuning on calibration data to adapt to differences between subjects (Kneeland et al., 2026, Sections 3.3 and 4.3). Other EEG retrieval methods address these differences through label-free calibration or coordinate alignment (Huang and Zhu, 2026; Cui et al., 2026). Each recording contains 250 time samples from 63 channels over one second following image presentation. ENIGMA maps recordings to image embeddings and ranks candidate images by embedding similarity. Split seed 20260904 selects 4,096 calibration and 1,000 validation images. Evaluation averages 80 EEG repetitions per image in the official 200-image gallery. Baseline and flow use identical splits and seeds. This 63-channel adaptation protocol differs from ENIGMA’s published 32-channel joint-training experiment (Kneeland et al., 2026, Sections 3.3–4.1). For adapted subject s, ENIGMA is pretrained on reference subjects Ss: subjects 5–10 for s ∈{1, 2, 3, 4}; subjects {1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10 } for s ∈ { 5, 6}; and subjects 1–6 for s ∈ { 7, 8, 9, 10 }. The 35 baseline and both flow widths share this pretrained model, whose encoder and projection network remain frozen. We average seeds 0, 1, and 42 within each subject. Confidence intervals are percentiles 2.5 and 97.5 over 100,000 paired bootstrap resamples of ten subjects. For Figure 2c, each resample recomputes the mean CFM and baseline accuracies ¯a and ¯b and their relative lift 100(¯ a/ ¯b − 1)% ;for the absolute gains below, it computes their difference. All configurations use the final training update; no test value selects a checkpoint, width, or context length.

F.1 Sequential transport and generation 

Time-series adaptation methods align observations, time axes, or representations across domains (Painblanc et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2026). Flow models generate entire functions or correlated sequences (Kerrigan et al., 2024b; Wei and Ma, 2025); Schrödinger-bridge methods learn dynamics between endpoint laws or complete time-series laws (Tong et al., 2024; Hamdouche et al., 2026). COT-GAN uses causal transport as a training objective for sequential data (Xu et al., 2020). We instead transform a prescribed fresh source trajectory by recursively applying a learned conditional transition map. Causal transport requires Law( y0: i | x0: n) = Law( y0: i | x0: i); bicausal transport imposes the reverse identity as well (Backhoff et al., 2017; Moulos, 2021). For Markov chains, Moulos (2021) compute bicausal transport costs by value iteration, and O’Connor et al. (2022) optimize stationary transition couplings for long-run average cost. O’Connor et al. (2024) give finite-sample cost bounds for estimated stationary couplings of finite-valued processes under mixing assumptions. Our statistical bound controls the squared prediction error of a continuous conditional velocity; (49) converts it into accuracy of generated states. 

F.2 Statistical error and dependence 

Benton et al. (2024, Theorem 1) and Gao et al. (2024, Proposition 6.2) convert population velocity error into Wasserstein error. For stochastic diffusion flow matching, Silveri et al. (2024, Theorems 2–3) bound Kullback–Leibler error; Bansal et al. (2026, Theorems 1–2) and Roy et al. (2026) analyze rectified-flow discretization. We combine velocity estimation from dependent transitions with conditional Wasserstein bounds and recursive generation. Our estimation proof uses the coupling and fourth-moment approach to dependent square-loss regression, as in Roy et al. (2021, Theorem 3.1). For convex or realizable predictor classes, Ziemann et al. (2024, Theorem 3.1 and discussion following (5)) show that, under suitable moment assumptions, the leading square-loss error term need not contain a mixing-time factor. Dependence enters the minimum sample size and higher-order terms. Our lower bound is consistent with this distinction: it establishes a mixing-dependent sample threshold.
