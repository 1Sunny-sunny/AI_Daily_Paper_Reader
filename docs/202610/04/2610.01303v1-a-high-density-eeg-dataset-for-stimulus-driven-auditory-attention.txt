## ABSTRACT 

Elucidating how the human auditory system automatically prioritizes competing sound streams in complex environments is a central goal in both cognitive neuroscience and the development of auditory brain-computer interfaces (BCIs). To track these dynamic processes, electroencephalography (EEG)-based auditory attention decoding (AAD) has emerged as a crucial approach. However, most open-access EEG benchmarks focus on top-down, instructed target selection, falling short of capturing bottom-up, stimulus-driven orienting during instruction-free dichotic competition. To bridge this gap, we present the SAAD-EEG dataset, comprising 128-channel EEG recordings from 23 participants exposed to competing naturalistic sounds drawn from environmental, speech, and music domains. The experimental paradigm was deliberately designed without explicit target instructions, enabling the investigation of stimulus-driven prioritization based on acoustic and semantic salience. Alongside raw and preprocessed EEG data, the release includes trial-aligned orienting labels, reaction times, and subjective sound ratings. Comprehensive technical validation confirms the dataset’s high neurophysiological quality, behavioral consistency, and robust decodability. This open-access resource opens new avenues for studying the neural mechanisms of bottom-up auditory attention and provides a critical foundation for developing attention-aware hearing technologies. 

## Background & Summary 

Recent progress in auditory BCIs and assistive listening devices has advocated the use of neural decoding to determine which auditory stream is being prioritized by the user 1–7 . EEG-based AAD has emerged as a promising, non-invasive, and temporally precise approach for tracking these attention-related neural dynamics 8–14 . However, most existing open-access EEG datasets have focused almost exclusively on goal-directed (top-down) selection, utilizing instructed paradigms wherein participants are explicitly directed to attend to a designated target audio stream 15–22 . While these resources have facilitated benchmarking of top-down AAD algorithms 23–27 , real-world communication frequently takes place in complex environments, where listeners are often interrupted by urgent or salient events, such as an infant crying or a fire alarm, that capture attention irrespective of current goals. Auditory BCIs incapable of tracking this stimulus-driven (bottom-up) orienting risk missing critical environmental cues. Despite the significance of this challenge, available EEG resources for stimulus-driven auditory attention remain scarce and are largely limited to detecting temporal salience within a single stream 14, 28–34 , rather than resolving instruction-free competition between multiple streams. Understanding how the brain manages these demands is crucial for developing comprehensive models of natural auditory attention, underscoring an urgent need for datasets and paradigms that reflect the true dynamics of unprompted, stimulus-driven auditory orienting in competing scenes. Here, we introduce the SAAD-EEG (Stimulus-Driven Auditory Attention EEG) dataset, which comprises 128-channel EEG recordings from 23 participants engaged in an instruction-free dichotic listening paradigm. The stimuli consist of naturalistic sound events categorized into eight subcategories and 24 cross-category pairing types, encompassing environmental sounds with both high and low ecological validity, speech, and music. In each trial, participants were presented with a dichotic pair of sounds without any explicit attentional cues. To minimize goal-directed selection and isolate genuine bottom-up orienting, they continuously watched a silent movie during sound presentation. After a brief post-stimulus delay, participants indicated which side was more attention-capturing. This design offers a unique opportunity to examine how competing audio streams draw attention based on their acoustic prominence, semantic meaning, and ecological relevance. Validation analyses confirm the dataset’s high quality, behavioral consistency, and robust decodability across multiple cross-validation schemes. The dataset also includes comprehensive behavioral annotations for the stimuli, including trial-level orienting-side labels, precise reaction times, and participant-level subjective ratings of each sound’s familiarity, urgency, and likability. These detailed annotations enable fine-grained analyses of how specific acoustic, semantic, and ecological properties arXiv:2610.01303v1 [q-bio.NC] 1 Oct 2026 influence neural processing and attentional capture in complex auditory environments. All data, including raw and preprocessed EEG files, stimulus manifests, and preprocessing scripts, are provided in standardized formats to ensure accessibility and reproducibility (see Figure 1 for an overview of the dataset generation and validation pipeline). The SAAD-EEG dataset offers several advantages for advancing research in auditory neuroscience and neuroengineering. It facilitates investigation of the neural mechanisms underlying bottom-up auditory attention, illuminating how the brain automatically evaluates and prioritizes competing sound streams in the absence of explicit instruction. The inclusion of category-structured, naturalistic stimuli enables exploration of how ecological validity and semantic urgency influence stimulus-driven orienting. Ultimately, this reproducible and open-access resource provides a foundation for developing next-generation, attention-aware hearing devices that can seamlessly adapt to the complexities of real-world acoustic environments. ## Methods 

Participants 

The dataset comprises EEG recordings from 23 adult participants (14 males and 9 females), with a mean age of 27.0 ± 2.7 years. All participants were native Chinese speakers, fluent in English, and enrolled as students at The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. 22 participants were right-handed and one participant was left-handed. All participants had normal hearing and reported no history of neurological disorders. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (Reference No. HSEARS20230628001). Before the experiment, participants received a detailed explanation of the experimental procedure, potential risks, and task-related requirements. Each participant provided written informed consent and agreed to the voluntary collection and public release of their data. They were compensated for their time and contribution.

Stimuli 

Stimuli were constructed as dichotic audio pairs, simultaneously presented to the left and right ears. Source clips were selected from AudioSet 35 , a manually annotated corpus of audio events sourced from YouTube, to ensure broad coverage of naturalistic, real-world sounds. The stimulus pool was organized according to a hierarchical taxonomy comprising four main categories: high-ecological (HE) and low-ecological (LE) environmental sounds, speech, and music, each subdivided into two subcategories. Ecological validity was defined in terms of everyday occurrence and attentional relevance, facilitating comparisons across distinct sound categories. Complete subcategory definitions and codes are provided in Table 1. Clips were manually screened, and 2-s excerpts were carefully selected to maximize label consistency while preserving perceptual clarity. The amplitude of each clip was normalized based on the root mean square (RMS) amplitude calculated from the loudest 1% of samples, reducing potential loudness-related confounds across stimulus pairs. Pair construction was restricted to cross-category combinations, with within-category and self-pairs excluded. Under these constraints, 24 valid subcategory-level pairing types were generated, as enumerated in Table 2. Each pairing type was repeated 10 times and expanded with mirrored left–right versions to balance ear assignment and limit side confounds, resulting in a total of 480 dichotic stimulus pairs. 

Experimental Procedure 

All recordings were conducted in a sound-attenuated and electromagnetically shielded room. As illustrated in Figures 2a and 2b, participants wore an EEG cap and wired headphones, viewed a monitor positioned approximately 50 cm in front, and responded using a response pad. Saline was applied to ensure stable conductivity prior to recording. A five-trial practice block preceded the main acquisition phase. During the main phase, the 480 dichotic stimulus pairs were randomly divided into three sessions (160 trials per session). To minimize fatigue and maintain data quality, adequate rest was provided between sessions, during which saline was replenished as needed to preserve electrode contact. At the beginning of each session, a central fixation cross was presented to prompt participants to maintain attention. Participants initiated the run by pressing the response pad. As shown in Figure 2c, each trial began with a 3-second silent period, ensuring that each dichotic stimulus pair started from silence. The two sound clips were then simultaneously presented to the left and right ears for 2 seconds without ear-specific attentional instruction. A 1-second post-stimulus delay preceded response collection to reduce potential contamination of stimulus-related EEG activity by motor responses associated with button presses 36 . After this delay, a response prompt instructed participants to report which side was more attention-capturing by pressing the corresponding left or right key. Participants were allowed up to five seconds to respond, and trials exceeding this response window were excluded from subsequent analysis. Trial labels therefore reflected participants’ post-stimulus self-reports of the more attention-capturing side, rather than externally instructed or objectively verified attended ears. Data Acquisition 

EEG signals were recorded using a 128-channel HydroCel GSN system (Electrical Geodesics, Inc.) at a sampling rate of 250 Hz. Electrode positions followed the international 10–10 system, and impedance was maintained below 10 k Ω before recording. During acquisition, signals were referenced online to the vertex (Cz), with the COM sensor (posterior to Cz) used as ground. Stimulus presentation, event-marker delivery, and response logging were implemented in E-Prime 2.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA). Behavioral responses were collected via the EGI Chronos response pad, which recorded left/right choices and reaction time (RT) with millisecond precision. Through the E-Prime–Chronos interface, trial events, behavioral responses, and EEG recordings were temporally aligned at the trial level. In addition, participants completed post-experiment subjective ratings using an online questionnaire. For each subcategory, one representative audio clip was presented, and participants rated familiarity, liking, and urgency on a 0–4 scale. Each item was rated after listening to its corresponding clip. At the end of the experiment, raw EEG data together with event markers were saved in .mff format for offline analysis, and behavioral logs were exported as .txt files. 

Data preprocessing 

EEG preprocessing was performed offline in Python (v3.12.7) using MNE-Python (v1.10.2 37 ), PyPREP (v0.4.3 38 ) and mne-icalabel 39 . Raw recordings were configured with the GSN-HydroCel-128 montage, with VREF treated as a miscellaneous channel. Noisy channels were automatically identified using PyPREP based on local correlation criteria and then interpolated using spherical spline interpolation. The data were subsequently re-referenced to the common average reference. A 0.1-40 Hz FIR band-pass filter was applied to remove DC offset and suppress slow baseline drifts, while retaining frequency components relevant to auditory attention. A 50 Hz notch filter was then applied to attenuate line noise. For artifact reduction, independent component analysis (ICA) with the Infomax algorithm 40 was used to decompose the EEG into independent components. The ICLabel classifier (mne-icalabel) was then applied to assign each component to categories such as brain, eye, and muscle; non-brain components were removed, and the cleaned EEG was reconstructed from the remaining components. ## Data Records 

The dataset is publicly available on the Zenodo platform 41 . The release follows a hierarchical organization designed for transparent reuse. As shown in Figure 3, the dataset includes root-level metadata files and four main subdirectories: raw_eeg/ (raw EEG recordings), processed/ (preprocessed EEG data), stimuli/ (stimulus audio files), and ratings/ (participant-level subjective ratings). For clarity, the figure shows the full tree for sub-01 only; all other participants and the remaining sessions follow the same file naming and folder structure. 

Root-level metadata 

The README.md file describes the Zenodo folder layout and refers readers to preprocessing code on GitHub (Code availability). The dataset_description.json file records technical parameters of the dataset, including sampling rate, channel count, trial count, and epoch timing. Participant ID, sex, age, and handedness are listed in participants.tsv . The data contains no personally identifiable information beyond these demographics. The CHANGES.txt file logs changes between dataset releases. 

Raw EEG folder 

The raw_eeg/ directory stores continuous session-level EEG recordings in EGI .mff format, organized by participant (sub-XX/ ) and session ( ses-01/ , ses-02/ , ses-03/ ). Each session folder contains one recording, sub-XX_ses-YY_ task-SAAD_run-01_eeg.mff/ , as acquired during data collection. 

Processed folder 

The processed/ directory contains trial-epoched EEG and behavioral data per participant. The three sessions are merged: each sub-XX/ folder contains 480 trials in four files. The file sub-XX_preprocessed_eeg.npy stores epoched EEG as a NumPy array of shape (480, 128, 1500) at 250 Hz, corresponding to 6 s per trial spanning the pre-stimulus, dichotic-stimulation, and post-stimulus intervals of each trial. Stimuli folder 

The stimuli/ directory contains the auditory materials used in the experiment. The wav/ subfolder contains single-ear reference clips organized by four main categories, each with two subcategory folders. The pairs/ subfolder holds 480 dichotic pair waveforms ( .wav ), one file per experimental pair. Baseline Decoding Results 

To evaluate the decodability of the released EEG dataset, we employed EEGNet 44 , a compact deep learning model designed for EEG-based decoding tasks, as a baseline classifier. Using depthwise separable convolutions, EEGNet learns spatial and temporal features efficiently with fewer parameters. This model has been successfully applied to auditory attention decoding tasks 12, 45 .To ensure a fair and reproducible model evaluation, we adopted a within-subject, five-fold cross-validation strategy. For each participant, trials were randomly shuffled and partitioned into five approximately equal-sized folds. Each fold was used once as the test set, while the remaining four folds were used for training. The same partitioning procedure was applied to all participants, and fold assignments were generated using a fixed random seed to enable reproducibility. Because the labels represent participants’ self-reported orienting choices, class distributions may be imbalanced and vary across individuals. Therefore, balanced accuracy (BACC) and standard deviation (SD) were reported to provide equal weighting across classes. For each participant, BACC was averaged across the five test folds. As shown in Table 3, EEGNet achieved above-chance trial-wise prediction of the response labels under the random split cross-validation scheme. At the group level, balanced accuracy averaged 68.38% (SD = 9.5% across participants), indicating reliable but moderate decodability on this stimulus-driven listening task. Individual performance varied across participants, with mean BACC values ranging from approximately 57% to 88%. However, within-participant variability across cross-validation folds was relatively small for most individuals, indicating stable evaluation across different test splits. Overall, these results demonstrate the presence of discriminative neural information in the released EEG data while highlighting inter-individual variability commonly observed in EEG decoding studies. 

Robustness across partitioning strategies 

Beyond the trial-level random split, we designed two additional cross-validation schemes to assess whether decoding perfor-mance was driven by trial-level neural information rather than shortcut learning from ear-of-presentation cues or memorized stimulus pairings. All analyses were conducted within-subject, with only the rule for grouping trials into folds differing across schemes. 

Mirror-constrained split. Random splitting may introduce shared acoustic information across training and testing folds, allowing decoders to exploit ear-of-presentation or stimulus-specific features. To reduce this potential leakage, mirrored trials from the same pair were assigned to the same fold, ensuring that the model never encountered the opposite ear assignment of a given stimulus pair during training. Five-fold cross-validation was performed under this grouping strategy. 

Pairing-type held-out split. Models may learn specific subcategory pairing patterns rather than generalize to unseen stimulus combinations. We therefore adopted a six-fold cross-validation scheme based on the 24 subcategory-level pairing types defined in the stimulus taxonomy. In each split, models were trained on 20 pairing types and evaluated on 4 held-out pairing types. This scheme evaluates generalization to unseen stimulus pairings rather than leave-one-category-out generalization to entirely novel sound categories. For each scheme, we trained EEGNet with the same preprocessing and hyperparameters, summarized per-participant BACC, and then reported group-level means across participants. As shown in Figure 4g, the three cross-validation schemes yielded comparable decoding performance: random split (68.4%), mirror-constrained split (68.2%), and pairing-type held-out split (68.3%). The small performance differences across schemes suggest that decoding was not primarily driven by ear-of-

Speech was relatively familiar, and LE sounds generally received lower liking ratings. These subjective profiles were broadly consistent with the expected perceptual characteristics of the predefined stimulus categories. 

Usage Notes 

The SAAD-EEG dataset provides a structured and reproducible resource for studies of stimulus-driven auditory attention, combining high-density EEG recordings with rich behavioral, stimulus, and subjective annotations. Researchers interested in customized preprocessing or validating alternative preprocessing strategies may use the raw .mff recordings, whereas the released preprocessed EEG epochs are intended for direct statistical analysis and machine learning. The released epochs preserve the complete trial structure, enabling flexible analyses of pre-stimulus, stimulus-evoked, and post-stimulus neural activity. We recommend MNE-Python (validated with version 1.10.2) to ensure compatibility with the released preprocessing pipeline. Unlike conventional auditory attention datasets based on explicit listening instructions, the released labels represent participants’ self-reported orienting outcomes during instruction-free dichotic competition. They should therefore be interpreted as behavioral measures of stimulus-driven attentional orienting rather than objective measures of attentional state. Beyond serving as a reference benchmark for stimulus-driven auditory attention decoding, the released dataset supports several additional research directions. First, the high-density EEG recordings collected under an instruction-free paradigm provide a valuable resource for investigating the neural mechanisms of stimulus-driven auditory attention, complementing the extensive literature based on instructed attention paradigms. Second, the combination of behavioral annotations, stimulus metadata, and subjective ratings enables investigations of how acoustic, semantic, and ecological sound properties influence attentional orienting and its neural correlates. Finally, the dataset supports the development and evaluation of multimodal computational models of stimulus-driven auditory attention.
