# ABSTRACT 

Electroencephalography (EEG) based brain-computer interfaces enable direct brain-to-device communication for applications such as rehabilitation and com-munication. However, their practical utility is often limited as the non-stationary nature of the EEG data introduces distribution shifts across domains (e.g., sessions and subjects). Adapting machine learning models to be invariant to these shifts in an unsupervised way, without using costly labeled calibration data, would drasti-cally improve the utility of EEG data. In this work, we use a classic generative model of EEG to study distribution shifts introduced by the domain-specific for-ward process, which is associated with factors such as head geometry. We theo-retically show that such distribution shifts can be recovered solely through linear transformations on the Symmetric Positive Definite manifold. Building on this in-sight, we propose SPDAlign, an interpretable framework for promoting domain-invariant EEG learning. SPDAlign first aligns the domain-specific means and cor-rects global rotations across domains using a recent optimal transport technique called Wasserstein Procrustes. We systematically study the proposed approach through simulations and demonstrate its competitive performance on extensive public EEG datasets. Additionally, SPDAlign is a globally linear framework and is intrinsically interpretable, so that the framework can identify frequency ranges of interest, determine the spatial patterns reflecting source–sensor relationships, and address cross-subject variability. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Electroencephalography (EEG) records multi-channel brain activity from the scalp, providing non-invasive access to neuronal dynamics with millisecond resolution (Niedermeyer & da Silva, 2005). EEG-based brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) enable direct brain-to-device communication for ap-plications such as rehabilitation (Ang & Guan, 2016), emotion recognition (Lazarou et al., 2018), and assistive technology (Wolpaw et al., 2002). Despite these promising applications, their practi-cal utility is limited by poor generalization due to distribution shifts across subjects (Fairclough & Lotte, 2020). Conventional strategies for mitigating distribution shifts in EEG applications involve collecting la-beled calibration data and training domain-specific models (Lotte et al., 2018). While effective in controlled settings, this approach poses significant challenges to the scalability of the technology (Wei et al., 2022). As an alternative, domain adaptation aims to learn a model from source domains that generalizes effectively to unseen but related target domains, offering a principled alternative to training domain-specific models (Ben-David et al., 2010). Within the BCI field, domain adaptation mainly addresses the cross-subject transfer learning problem (Wu et al., 2020), which is one of the grand challenges in EEG research (Fairclough & Lotte, 2020). Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) (Wilson & Cook, 2020) without requiring target domain labels is considered the key to tackling this challenge (Wei et al., 2022). Current literature casts it as a multi-source and multi-target UDA problem, attempting to address it with Riemannian alignment frameworks (Ju et al., 2025). Once aligned, a model trained on source domains typically generalizes to related target domains (Rodrigues et al., 2019). However, despite achieving state-of-the-art (SotA) performance (Li et al., 2025; Mellot et al., 2024), these heuristic alignment methods lack theoretical foundations and thus offer limited explanatory power. In this work, we theoretically demonstrate that Riemannian alignment frameworks can compensate for distribution shifts introduced by the EEG forward modeling process in Eq. (1), which character-izes the observed EEG data as the linear mixing of latent source signals (Sabbagh et al., 2019; Haufe et al., 2014). This scenario represents a form of latent model shift, where discrepancies are driven by the domain-specific forward matrices associated with subject-specific variations such as head geometry (Mellot et al., 2023; Sabbagh et al., 2020). By accounting for domain-specific forward matrices, we show that robust latent representations can be recovered solely through linear trans-formations on the SPD manifold. Specifically, this involves mean matching followed by a global rotation transformation. The resulting representations encode the latent source covariance, aligning with neuroscience studies that utilize source power as a robust biomarker (Blankertz et al., 2007; Grosse-Wentrup & Buss, 2008). Building on these theoretical insights and recent advances in end-to-end SPD learning (Kobler et al., 2022a), we propose SPDAlign (Fig. 1 (a)), an end-to-end Riemannian learning framework designed to facilitate EEG domain-invariant representation learning. SPDAlign aligns the domain-specific means (Fig. 1 (a)) and subsequently applies a global rotation transformation across domains (Fig. 1 (b)). These rotations are estimated using orthogonal Procrustes analysis for labeled source do-mains and Wasserstein Procrustes for unlabeled target domains (Grave et al., 2019). Unlike prior semi-supervised frameworks limited to single-source, single-target settings (Rodrigues et al., 2019; Bleuz´ e et al., 2022), SPDAlign generalizes to unsupervised multi-source, multi-target adaptation without requiring target domain labels. By addressing forward modeling shifts (Fig. 1 (c)), it aligns representations across subjects and steps toward domain-invariant representations. SPDAlign is a globally linear framework, comprising a linear feature extractor, linear transfor-mations, and a linear classifier, and therefore is intrinsically interpretable. Classifier weights can be mapped back to spatial patterns and source contributions, supporting transparent and clinically meaningful decision-making (Haufe et al., 2014). We systematically evaluate SPDAlign through simulations under the assumed generative model and show its competitive performance in EEG cross-subject decoding via extensive experiments on public datasets spanning motor imagery, work-load, mental stress, and driving fatigue. Moreover, using the mental workload task as a case study, we show that SPDAlign successfully identifies task-discriminative source activities peaking in the theta band within the frontal and parietal regions in Fig. 4, which aligns with neurophysiologi-cal markers of mental workload (Borghini et al., 2014; Dehais et al., 2020). # 3 METHODS 

3.1 EEG FORWARD MODELING SHIFTS 

Following Li et al. (2025); Sabbagh et al. (2020), we focus on EEG forward modeling shifts where the marginal distribution of the observed data varies across domains while assuming the conditional source-to-label relationship remains invariant. Under this assumption, a shared regression model in Eq. (3) can generalize effectively across domains once these distributional discrepancies are ap-propriately mitigated. Specifically, we examine shifts introduced by the domain-specific forward matrix Aj (Eq. (1)). This is a case of latent model shifting, where variations in the forward model-ing process (head geometry, sensor placement, and cognitive states) induce distribution shifts in the observed sensor data (Mellot et al., 2023). Changes in neural dynamics, such as impedance drift, 4Preprint may also occur but are typically modeled as noise (Akalin Acar & Makeig, 2013). By leveraging the uniqueness of the polar decomposition for square invertible matrices, we decompose Aj as: 

Aj = Qj exp( Pj ) (10) where the transformation Aj is expressed as a domain-specific transition exp( Pj ) and orthogonal matrix Qj ∈ { Q ∈ RP ×P : QT Q = IP }. We select the polar decomposition to characterize these shifts because it allows decomposing the possible shifts to translations and rotations on the SPD manifold, therefore naturally linking EEG forward modeling to common geometric operations on SPD matrices (Bhatia & Congedo, 2019). Specifically, mean shifts correspond to global power changes that occur similarly across all channels (Congedo et al., 2017), and rotation shifts corre-spond to scale shifts that differ across classes. As demonstrated by Eq. (12), robust latent representations log( Ei) can be recovered by compen-sating for distribution shifts introduced by the domain-specific forward matrix Aj (Eq. (1)) via alignment of domain-specific first-order moments followed by a global rotation. Building on these theoretical insights, we propose SPDAlign: an end-to-end Riemannian deep learning framework on the SPD manifold designed for multi-source/multi-target unsupervised domain adaptation. SP-DAlign addresses forward modeling shifts by leveraging source domain labels when available, while operating entirely without labels during target domain inference.

To tackle forward modeling shifts, mϕ tracks the running domain-specific Fr´ echet mean during training and recenters these first-order moments to the identity matrix; and employs orthogonal Procrustes analysis in Eq. (6) to estimate the rotation between each domain data point and a reference domain, with labels providing known correspondences. Finally, gψ serves as the shared linear classifier. Model training proceeds in two stages ( algorithm 1 ). In the first stage (70% of the training itera-tions), we train the model without applying the rotation alignment. This stage learns a good feature extractor that can extract common representations across domains. In the second stage, we freeze the parameters θ of the feature extractor, estimate and apply the domain-specific rotation alignment, and subsequently fine-tune the classifier parameters ψ on the aligned representations. To estimate the rotation transformation, the Orthogonal Procrustes (Eq. (6)) is applied during source training using labels to construct class-constrained sample correspondences. We select the reference domain randomly. For each remaining domain, we compare its class-wise sample counts with those of the reference domain and, for any class with unequal sample counts, downsample the larger subset to match the smaller one. 5Preprint

By maintaining global linearity, SPDAlign is equivalent to the Tangent Space Mapping framework, providing interpretability. SPDAlign builds on standard simplifying assumptions regarding the linear generative process of EEG (Sabbagh et al., 2019; Kobler et al., 2021). In practice, the method is applied to covariance representations obtained after feature extraction. Because convolutions act as linear operators, this extraction step inherently preserves the structural form of the spatial generative model, so our theo-retical analysis remains applicable (Haufe et al., 2014). 

Target inference. During inference, SPDAlign freezes the learned feature extractor fθ and the shared linear classifier gψ . When samples from previously unseen target domains arrive, it lever-ages the entire target domain data to estimate the domain-specific mean (Eq. (14)) and the rotation transformation between the reference source domain (set in the training stage) and the target domain data via Wasserstein Procrustes (Eq. (9)). In cases of label shifts, the Wasserstein component can be replaced with Unbalanced Optimal Transport (UOT) (Peyr´ e & Cuturi, 2019; Koc ¸ et al., 2026), which relaxes marginal constraints. The target rotations are estimated totally without target labels, and the resulting optimization procedure is detailed in algorithm 2 . At each iteration t, we compute the optimal transport coupling between the entire reference domain and the target domain data. The resulting transport plan is then used to compute the gradient with respect to the orthogonal matrix 

ˆQ, which is updated via projected gradient descent on the orthogonal group. We initialize ˆQ to the identity matrix for simplicity and iterate for 50 epochs with a learning rate of 0.01. 

Interpretability. Kobler et al. (2021) demonstrated that the coupling of latent sources can be re-covered by solving a generalized eigenvalue problem between the Fr´ echet mean and classifier pat-terns (Haufe et al., 2014). With an additional linear orthogonal transformation, SPDAlign maintains global linearity, equivalent to the typical TSM learning framework. This global linearity ensures the model preserves a direct correspondence between classifier weights and latent source contributions: the learned representations can be mapped back to the sensor space, and the resulting spatial pat-terns reflect source–sensor relationships and source contributions. Such interpretability is crucial for determining the specific brain regions (measurement channels) and frequencies that reflect certain cognitive processes and for supporting trustworthy clinical BCI systems (Haufe et al., 2014).

Model Validation. The high performance of the Oracle in Tab. 1 supports that forward modeling shifts are the primary cause of distribution shifts in EEG BCI datasets. In this setting, the classifier is fixed and shared across domains, while target labels are used only to estimate the optimal rotation for alignment. The resulting improvements across datasets suggest that a substantial portion of the cross-subject distribution shift can be addressed through conditional rotation alignment. These results suggest that the main driver of the domain shift can be explained by transformations in our model, and the remaining gap in the unsupervised setting is primarily due to suboptimal rotation estimation (visualized in Fig. 3 b and c), not model mismatch. Correcting for these shifts nearly closes the cross-subject decoding gap, which provides strong empirical validation for the generative model in Eq. (1). Furthermore, the high performance implies that the underlying latent source covariance is stable across individuals. These results emphasize the necessity of tackling forward modeling shifts, provide empirical support for our theoretical result, and confirm that the forward model Eq. (1) effectively characterizes the physical processes of EEG. 

Room for improvement. While Wasserstein Procrustes estimation allows the unsupervised SP-DAlign to achieve state-of-the-art performance, a gap remains relative to the ERM version. This discrepancy arises because the Wasserstein Procrustes in algorithm 2 relies on distribution-level optimal transport to infer point correspondences, which can be ambiguous in the absence of label supervision. When class-conditional structures overlap or are weakly separated, the estimated trans-port plan may associate semantically mismatched points, leading to a biased estimation of the global rotation (Courty et al., 2016). Such erroneous correspondences can accumulate during the iterative optimization and prevent the recovered rotation from reaching the empirical optimum. Our sim-ulation results (Fig. 5) further show that unsupervised rotation estimation degrades under extreme rotation shifts or limited class separability. These results highlight the potential to develop more robust rotation estimation techniques to bridge the remaining gap toward the oracle upper bound. 

Ablation. Tab. 2 summarizes the combination of moment and rotation alignment within SPDAlign. We highlight the three important observations: (i) the full configuration (both components enabled) achieves the best performance, confirming that both alignment steps are necessary; (ii) rotation alignment appears to be particularly critical, as its removal leads to a statistically significant perfor-mance drop ( p = 0 .02 ); and (iii) the omission of both components results in the largest decrease in accuracy, underscoring their effect in mitigating distribution shifts. A global reference mean is used across domains in our implementation; otherwise, the performance will degrade to chance level, which in turn makes it difficult to analyze other parts. # 5 DISCUSSIONS 

In this work, we presented SPDAlign, a global linear Riemannian learning framework for multi-source/multi-target UDA. By bridging the physical generative model of EEG with Riemannian ge-ometry, we demonstrated that distribution shifts induced by domain-specific forward modeling can be effectively mitigated through unsupervised alignment on the SPD manifold. Our results highlight the critical role of rotational alignment; as shown in our ablation study (Tab. 2), omitting this step significantly degrades performance. By achieving SotA results on three of four benchmark datasets (Tab. 1), we show that our approach effectively recovers robust latent representations without requir-ing target labels. Furthermore, we demonstrated the interpretability of SPDAlign; the framework can identify frequency ranges of interest and determine the specific brain regions. Altogether, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of our framework, SPDAlign, which achieves highly compet-itive performance while retaining inherent interpretability. The limitation of our framework, which applies to any method involving eigendecompositions, is the computational cost, which limits its ap-plication to high-dimensional SPD features. While we limited this study to offline evaluations, our 9Preprint framework could be readily extended to online UDA by tracking the running statistics (Kobler et al., 2022a) and replacing the mini-batch Wasserstein distance estimation with online variants (Mensch & Peyr´ e, 2020). Future work could explore more robust rotation estimation methods and extend the framework to online BCI applications. 10 Preprint

To construct the covariance matrices Ci ∈ S + 

> P

with P = 2 , we initiated the generation process in the log-space. Specifically, log-space features, denoted as upper log( Ei), were synthesized using the 

scikit-learn function make classification , with one dimension configured to encode label-relevant information. These features were subsequently standardized to achieve zero mean and unit variance. To map these points onto the SPD manifold S+ 

> P

, we applied the inverse vector-ization operator upper −1 and the exponential map Exp IP as defined in (17). The resulting data were partitioned across source and target domains, with each domain receiving 500 observations. Finally, the data were projected into the channel space via domain-specific forward matrices Aj ,following (10). To introduce rotation shifts, we either artificially varied the rotation degree using angle-parameterized rotation matrices or generated random orthogonal matrices. Class separability Fig. 5: in the absence of rotation, moment alignment alone effectively com-pensates for the domain-specific transition effects introduced by EEG forward modeling shifts (Eq. (10)). As expected, classification performance declines as the magnitude of rotational shifts increases across all scenarios, though higher class separability confers greater robustness. No-tably, while the RCT method (green) is highly sensitive to even mild rotations, SPDAlign (red) demonstrates superior resilience, maintaining stable accuracy across a wider range of shifts. The source-domain performance (blue line) represents the empirical upper bound for these unsupervised adaptation tasks. Sample size Fig. 6: Performance degrades gradually as the number of samples decreases, primarily affecting the estimation of covariance matrices and Wasserstein Procrustes. However, SPDAlign maintains a clear advantage once moderate sample sizes are available (e.g., typical EEG epoch counts), even when the rotation is severe. Informative sources (Fig. 7): we vary the number of label-informative latent sources to evaluate the effect of distributed task-relevant information. Across different settings, classification perfor-mance decreases as rotational shifts become more severe, while SPDAlign generally remains more robust than RCT. The advantage is particularly evident when sufficient informative source structure is available, supporting the robustness of SPDAlign under typical EEG regimes. Label shifts (Fig. 8): the ratio represents the degree of class imbalance in the simulation. We com-pared the optimal transport (OT) method used in our paper with its unbalanced variant, UOT. Perfor-mance was tested under four different ratios, ranging from mild to severe label shifts. As expected, label shifts degraded performance, but UOT, which relaxes the marginal constraints, still provided performance gains, indicating that UOT is a suitable choice when class imbalance is present. Data availability (Fig. 9): to examine the effect of data availability in an online learning setting, we progressively vary the proportion of target-domain samples available for alignment. With lim-ited incoming data, the estimation is less reliable, resulting in a smaller advantage over RCT. As more target samples become available, SPDAlign consistently shows advantages over RCT. This result suggests that SPDAlign can be easily extended to online settings, where its alignment quality progressively improves as data accumulate. SNR (Fig. 10): As SNR decreases (i.e., increasing proportion of noise sources in the simulation), all methods’ performance declines. # D EXPERIMENT DETAILS 

D.1 DATASET DETAILS 

Motor imagery : (Tangermann et al., 2012) is a motor imagery dataset containing EEG from 9 subjects recorded with 22 channels at 250 Hz. The paradigm involves two motor imagery tasks: left hand and right hand, with 144 trials per class per subject. Data were collected across two sessions on separate days, each comprising six runs of 48 trials. Each trial lasted 4 seconds. 

Workload : (Roy et al., 2022) containing EEG from 15 subjects recorded with 64 channels at 500 Hz. The paradigm involves three distinct mental workload levels: low, medium, and high, induced by the Multi-Attribute Task Battery II (MATB-II) task. Each participant completed three sessions on different days, with each workload condition lasting 5 minutes per session. 

Mental Stress : (Zyma et al., 2019) containing EEG from 36 subjects recorded with 19 channels at 500 Hz using the Neurocom system. The paradigm involves two cognitive states: a resting state and a mental stress state induced by an intensive serial subtraction arithmetic task. Each participant completed a single session consisting of a 3-minute resting period followed by 4 minutes of mental arithmetic. 

Driving Fatigue : (Cao et al., 2019) containing EEG from 11 subjects recorded with 32 channels at 500 Hz. The paradigm involves a prolonged 90-minute dynamic driving simulation designed to induce varying levels of fatigue. Continuous EEG recordings were collected, with lane departure events and reaction times monitored to assess alertness and fatigue throughout the session. 

Preprocessing. For motor imagery, signals were resampled to 256 Hz, band-pass filtered between 4–36 Hz, and segmented into 3-second epochs corresponding to the two most discriminative labels. For workload, signals were band-pass filtered at 4–36 Hz, resampled to 200 Hz, and segmented into 2-second epochs. For driving fatigue, signals were band-pass filtered at 1–50 Hz, resampled to 128 Hz, and segmented into 3-second epochs. For mental stress, signals were processed with a 30 Hz low-pass filter and a 50 Hz notch filter, resampled to 500 Hz, and segmented into 4-second epochs. D.2 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

All models were implemented using official repositories, maintaining the default hyperparameters. Each session is treated as a domain. We evaluate using either leave-one-subject-out (subject count 

≤ 10 ) or 10-fold cross-validation, with subjects serving as the grouping variable for cross-subject adaptation. Euclidean frameworks use the Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba, 2014), while geometric frameworks use Riemannian Adam (B´ ecigneul & Ganea, 2018), both with default PyTorch hyper-parameters. Source-domain data are split into 80% training and 20% validation sets, stratified by domain and label, and trained for 50 epochs with cross-entropy loss. Baseline methods were implemented using publicly available Python code; custom approaches were developed using PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019), scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), and Geoopt (Kochurov et al., 2020). All experiments were performed on standard computation PCs equipped with 32-core CPUs, 128 GB of RAM, and a single GPU. 21 Preprint
