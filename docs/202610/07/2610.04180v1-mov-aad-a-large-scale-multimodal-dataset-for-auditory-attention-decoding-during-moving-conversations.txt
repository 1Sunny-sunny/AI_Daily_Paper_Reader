# Abstract 

Auditory attention decoding (AAD) is often evaluated on static, simplified speech scenes that poorly match everyday listening. We introduce MOV-AAD, a large-scale dataset for studying au-ditory attention under moving, naturalistic conversations. MOV-AAD combines 64-channel EEG with synchronized physiolog-ical recordings, including eye tracking, respiration, galvanic skin response, heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, body temperature, body motion, and photoplethysmography, enabling analysis of cross-modal neural and physiological markers of attention and listening effort. This dataset uses a more eco-logically valid listening paradigm with dynamically moving conversational speech sources and behavioral measures of at-tentional engagement. MOV-AAD supports research on robust AAD in realistic spatial dynamics, multimodal attention model-ing, listening effort, and intersubject neural responses, providing a resource for benchmarking selective auditory attention in natu-ralistic listening. 

Index Terms : auditory attention decoding, multimodal dataset, naturalistic speech, EEG, physiological signals 

# 1. Introduction 

Selective auditory attention enables listeners to focus on a tar-get speaker in noisy environments, commonly known as the “cocktail party effect” [1]. Neural studies using EEG and MEG have demonstrated that cortical activity preferentially tracks the speech envelope of the attended speaker [2, 3, 4], forming the basis of auditory attention decoding (AAD) in multi-speaker scenarios. These findings have motivated research on neural decoding algorithms and neuro-steered hearing technologies. Attentive listening, however, is not solely a cortical phe-nomenon. Selective attention and listening effort engage dis-tributed neural and autonomic systems. Peripheral physiological markers such as pupillometry, heart rate variability, respiration dynamics, and electrodermal activity (e.g. galvanic skin re-sponse) have been linked to cognitive load, arousal regulation, and sustained attention during speech processing [5, 6, 7, 8]. Eye-tracking measures including pupil dilation and gaze sta-bility similarly reflect task engagement and top-down control processes [9, 10, 11]. Intersubject correlation (ISC) analyses further reveal shared stimulus-driven neural dynamics during attentive engagement [12, 13, 14]. These findings suggest that auditory attention involves coordinated interactions across neural and peripheral physiological systems.

However, most publicly available AAD datasets focus pri-marily on EEG and lack synchronized autonomic and behavioral measurements. Moreover, many existing datasets rely on spa-tially stationary speakers, synthetic mixtures, or constrained acoustic paradigms that only partially approximate real-world listening environments [15, 16]. The lack of naturalistic multi-modal recordings limits investigations of how neural and physio-logical processes jointly support attention and effort in dynamic acoustic scenes. Here we introduce MOV-AAD 1, a large-scale multimodal dataset designed to support research on auditory attention decod-ing and cognitive state assessment under naturalistic listening conditions. The dataset integrates 64-channel EEG with syn-chronized physiological and behavioral modalities, including heart rate, respiration flow and effort, galvanic skin response (GSR), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO 2), body temperature, body motion (accelerometry), photoplethysmography (PPG), and binocular eye tracking (pupil dilation and gaze location), recorded from 50 participants. By combining neural and auto-nomic measures under dynamic listening conditions, MOV-AAD provides a foundation for investigating how top-down attention is coordinated across cortical and peripheral systems. To position MOV-AAD within the existing literature, Table 1 summarizes representative publicly available non-invasive AAD datasets. While datasets such as DTU [16, 17] and KULeuven [18, 19] provide high-density EEG recordings in two-speaker paradigms, most employ spatially stationary speech mixtures and lack synchronized peripheral physiological measurements. The PhyAAt dataset [20] incorporates selected physiological signals but primarily focuses on single-speaker listening with noise rather than competitive multi-talker attention. Compared to these resources, MOV-AAD uniquely combines (i) dynamic spatial movement of conversational speech sources with turn-taking, (ii) a substantial participant cohort (N=50), and (iii) comprehensive multimodal recordings integrating EEG with autonomic and behavioral measures. Each participant completed both single-conversation and multi-conversation tasks. In the single-conversation condition (40 trials), participants listened to conversational narratives pre-sented from a spatially moving source. In the multi-conversation condition (56 trials), they attended to one speaker among dy-namically moving competing conversational streams spanning

# 2. Dataset Description 

2.1. Participants 

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the authors’ university. Fifty participants (28 male at birth; age 18-38 years, mean = 24 , SD = 4 .5) with self-reported normal hearing and no history of neurological or attention dis-orders were recruited between October 2022 and June 2025. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation, and all participants were compensated. 

2.2. Speech Materials and Spatialization 

Speech stimuli were constructed as naturalistic conversational streams spoken by native American English talkers. Each con-versation consisted of temporally continuous speech segments with occasional embedded repeated words used for attention monitoring. The mean interval between repeated-word onsets within a stream was 7.0 s (SD = 1.0 s). All speech streams were spatialized using head-related trans-fer functions (HRTFs) and presented through earphones. Dur-ing moving-speaker conditions, conversations moved continu-ously within the frontal-horizontal plane (azimuth range: -90 ◦ to +90 ◦). The perceived distance of each stream was held constant across trials. In multi-conversation trials, streams were matched in root-mean-square (RMS) power. Diotic background noise (pedestrian noise or speech bab-ble) was added at levels 9 or 12 dB below the power of each conversation stream.

2.3.3. Single Moving Speaker Conversation (SC) 

Participants completed 40 trials in which a single conversational stream moved continuously within the frontal horizontal plane (-90 ◦ to +90 ◦). Speaker identity varied within trials, and par-ticipants responded to embedded repeated words in a 1-back detection task. 

2.3.4. Multiple Moving Speakers Conversation (MC) 

This task extended the SC framework by presenting two indepen-dent conversational streams simultaneously. Participants com-pleted 56 trials in which two spatially separated conversational streams moving within the same azimuth range. Participants were instructed to attend to the conversation that began first and to press a button upon detecting repeated words in the attended stream. The unattended stream began 3 s after the onset of the attended stream. In the attended conversation, one talker switch occurred at approximately 50% of the trial duration, whereas in the unattended stream, two switches occurred at approximately 25% and 75%. Assignment of male and female talkers was coun-terbalanced across trials. Repeated-word events did not overlap across streams. 

2.4. Data Acquisition and Preprocessing 

EEG (64 channels) and peripheral physiological signals were acquired using g.tec sensors and a g.HIamp amplifier (g.tec medical engineering GmbH, Austria) at 1200 Hz with a 60 Hz notch filter. The peripheral modalities included GSR, triaxial accelerometry, respiratory flow (nasal airflow), respiratory effort (thoracic belt), SpO 2, PPG, heart rate, and skin temperature. Eye-tracking signals, including pupil diameter and instantaneous horizontal and vertical gaze coordinates, were acquired using a Tobii Pro Nano eye tracker (Tobii AB, Sweden). All neural, physiological, and eye-tracking streams were synchronized and recorded through Simulink. Behavioral responses (repeated-word detections and local-ization reports) were collected through a customized MATLAB-based interface and synchronized to physiological recordings. Audio playback was simultaneously recorded, and temporal alignment was established by cross-correlating the recorded au-dio with the original stimulus waveform. The released dataset includes recordings from the repeated-sentence task ( ∼12 min per participant), SC tasks ( ∼30 min per participant), and MC tasks ( ∼45 min per participant); and behavioral reports from the localization task, SC and MC tasks. Specifically, for SC and MC trials, we provide EEG and periph-eral signals at 1200 Hz together with aligned stimulus audio and repeated-word detection performance metrics at both the subject and trial levels. EEG channels with abnormal variance were automatically detected and replaced via spherical interpola-tion [28, 29, 30]. A 3 s pre-onset baseline segment is included for each trial. Peripheral modalities are released in minimally processed form, with 1200 Hz acquisition and hardware notch filtering only, without additional modality-specific preprocess-ing. For the localization task, trial- and subject-level localization metrics are provided. 

# 3. Method 

3.1. Behavioral Validation Metrics 

3.1.1. Localization Perception 

The localization task included nine discrete azimuth locations spanning -90° to +90°. For each participant, localization per-formance was quantified by accuracy and error. Localization accuracy was computed as the proportion of correctly identified source locations across trials, with chance performance defined as 1/9. Localization performance was further quantified using the mean absolute error (MAE) between the reported and the ground-truth stimulus location. For each participant, MAE was computed as

3.1.2. Repeated-word Detection Metrics 

Repeated-word performance was used to assess sustained atten-tion during the conversation tasks. A repeated word was con-sidered correctly detected if a space-bar press occurred within 2 s of the onset of the repeated word in the attended stream. A space-bar press was counted as a false alarm if no attended repeated-word onset occurred in the preceding 2 s window. For each participant, we computed hit rate, precision, and F1 score separately for the SC and MC conditions. Statistical comparisons were performed using two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. F1 scores were compared between MC and SC conditions as well as two streams within the MC condition (Attended vs. Unattended). For all tests, we report 

p-values and rank-based effect sizes ( r = Z/ √N ), where Z

denotes the standardized statistic from the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and N is the number of non-zero paired differences. 

3.2. Physiological Data Quality Assessment 

We perform a data quality sanity check by extracting a set of basic physiological features from the MC session, which in-clude heart rate, skin temperature, skin conductance level (SCL, extracted from GSR), non-specific skin conductance response rate (NS-SCR, extracted from GSR), respiration frequency, ac-celerometer magnitude, SpO 2, heart rate variability (HRV, ex-tracted from PPG) features, and pupil dilation. GSR was characterized using both tonic and phasic mea-sures. The slow-changing tonic component (SCL) was computed by low-pass filtering and averaging the raw GSR signal to cap-ture baseline level. The phasic component (NS-SCR rate) was computed as the number of spontaneous SCR events per minute. Accelerometer signals were summarized using the magni-tude of tri-axial acceleration, expressed in units of gravity (g), as a proxy for body movement during the task. Since this measure includes the static gravitational component, a baseline around 1 g is expected, and deviations from this value reflect changes in movement and/or orientation. HRV metrics were derived from PPG signals by detecting pulse peaks and computing inter-beat intervals. From these in-tervals, standard HRV metrics were calculated: SDNN (standard deviation of NN intervals), reflecting overall variability; and RMSSD (root mean square of successive differences), reflecting parasympathetic activity. All features were computed within fixed-length analysis windows (40 s) and averaged across trials and participants. The resulting distributions were compared against expected physio-logical ranges based on prior literature[31, 32, 33, 34] and device specifications. 

3.3. Baseline Analyses 

3.3.1. Envelope Reconstruction 

For each trial, the speech envelope was extracted using the mag-nitude of the analytic signal (Hilbert transform), followed by 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filtering at 8 Hz. This approach follows common practice in cortical speech tracking studies targeting slow amplitude modulations [14, 35]. To reconstruct the speech envelope from EEG, data were resampled from 1200 Hz to 100 Hz, with an embedded anti-aliasing filter. Signals were normalized and nonlinearly com-pressed using 5 tanh( x/ 5) to mitigate extreme outliers. EEG was then band-pass filtered to 1-8 Hz using a zero-phase FIR filter (order 256). This frequency band captures low-frequency neural tracking of the speech envelope widely reported in EEG and MEG literature [4, 36]. Envelope reconstruction was performed using a backward model implemented in the mTRF Toolbox v2.3 [37]. Decoders were trained with time lags from 0 to 400 ms. Ridge parame-ters were logarithmically spaced from 10 −2 to 10 4 and selected via leave-one-trial-out cross-validation. Reconstruction perfor-mance was quantified as the Pearson correlation between the reconstructed and true envelopes on the held-out trial. Single-speaker and competing-speaker sessions were modeled and eval-uated separately. 

3.3.2. Trajectory Reconstruction 

Spatial trajectories were defined as the time-varying azimuth positions of each conversational stream. Trajectories were gener-ated during stimulus design following the procedure described in [26]. Briefly, speaker positions were constrained to the frontal half of the horizontal plane (-90° to +90°) and were generated using a first-order Markov chain with 1° spatial resolution. The resulting sequences were smoothed and upsampled to 10 Hz prior to spatial rendering via HRTF-based processing. The final trajectories were further interpolated to 100 Hz to match the neural sampling rate. Trajectory reconstruction was performed following the de-coding framework described in [25]. EEG signals were resam-pled to 100 Hz and robustly normalized prior to band-specific filtering. 3.3.3. Auditory Attention Decoding (AAD) 

For multi-conversation trials, AAD was performed separately for each participant using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) following [25]. For each trial, rA and rU denote the Pearson correlations between the reconstructed signal and the attended and unattended stimuli, respectively. Trial-wise feature vectors were constructed as [rA, r U]. To form a balanced binary classification problem during training, swapped feature vectors [rU, r A] were included as an alternative class. Decoding was evaluated using leave-one-trial-out cross-validation across trials. In each fold, the held-out trial was tested using the true-order feature vector, while training was performed on both true-order and swapped-order samples from the remain-ing trials. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correctly classified held-out true-order trials. 3.3.4. Intersubject Correlation (ISC) 

We quantified stimulus-locked response similarity across partici-pants using ISC computed in correlated components of the mul-tichannel signals [13, 12]. All modalities were notched-filtered at 60Hz and resampled to 100 Hz prior to analysis. Signal prepa-ration was modality-specific: • EEG: Mean-centered across time (per channel) and band-pass filtered using zero-phase FIR filters to either 1-8 Hz (low-frequency band that manifests the envelope tracking) or 8-12 Hz (alpha band). • Eye tracking: Pupil size and horizontal or vertical gaze instan-taneous position were averaged across eyes after excluding samples marked invalid by the eye tracker (Tobii Pro Nano, Tobii AB, Sweden). • Respiration flow, effort, and PPG: Detrended or high-pass filtered at 0.05 Hz to remove slow drifts. • Heart rate and SpO 2: High-pass filtered at 0.05 Hz and subsequently low-pass filtered at 1 Hz. • GSR: Median filtered to reduce impulse artifacts. For each trial, data were arranged as X(t) ∈ RD×N (chan-nels × subjects). Spatial filters were obtained by maximizing between-subject covariance relative to within-subject covariance using generalized eigenvalue decomposition. Within-subject co-variance was shrinkage-regularized [13]. We retained three EEG components and one component for single-channel modalities. Trial-wise ISC scores were computed by correlating each participant’s projected component time series with the average of the remaining participants. Trial-level ISC values were averaged across trials to yield one ISC value per subject. However, for slowly varying physiological measures (e.g., temperature and SpO 2), trial-wise ISC scores were unstable due to limited within-trial temporal variability. Therefore, trials were aggregated in non-overlapping blocks of 28 trials (half of the experiment) to compute ISC at a longer time scale. Chance-level ISC was estimated using surrogate data gener-ated by phase randomization, which preserves temporal autocor-relation while disrupting cross-subject alignment [13]. For each subject, significance p-values were computed by comparing the observed ISC against the permutation-derived null distribution (100 permutations). Group-level significance was assessed using paired right-tailed tests comparing observed ISC to chance across subjects (paired t-test when ISC–chance differences passed a Lilliefors normality test; otherwise Wilcoxon signed-rank test). To quantify prevalence, the number of subjects with permutation test p < 0.05 was evaluated using a binomial test against the expected false-positive rate. 

# 4. Results and Discussion 

To demonstrate the validity and analytical potential of the dataset, we report behavioral performance, stimulus reconstruction ac-curacy, auditory attention decoding accuracy, and intersubject correlation using the standard evaluation metrics described in the Method section. 

4.1. Behavioral Validation 

4.1.1. Localization Performance 

We used the localization task to assess the feasibility of our moving-conversation tracking paradigm. As shown in Fig. 2, the polar visualization illustrates the mapping between true and per-ceived angles. Although localization responses showed reduced discriminability at extreme lateral positions, the overall angular structure was preserved, and perceived angles remained largely aligned with the correct hemifield across both higher-accuracy (top 33%) and lower-accuracy (bottom 33%) groups. The greater variability observed at lateral angles is consis-tent with established characteristics of human spatial hearing, where localization precision typically decreases toward extreme azimuths due to cue saturation of interaural time and level differ-ences and increased ambiguity [38]. Such effects may be further amplified when non-individualized HRTFs are employed [39]. The observed performance quantifies the magnitude of these lim-itations: The spatial response patterns remained systematically structured, indicating that spatial cues were preserved at a level sufficient to support moving-speaker tracking at the group level. At the subject level, localization performance showed moder-ate variability across participants (Fig. 2, bottom). Localization accuracy ranged broadly but remained above chance level (ap-proximately 11.1%) for most participants, while mean absolute localization error was substantially below the level expected from random guessing chance level (approximately 66 .7◦). These re-sults indicate that although spatial perception accuracy varied across individuals, the majority of participants maintained reli-able spatial discrimination under the current experimental setup. 

4.1.2. Repeated-word Detection Performance 

Repeated-word detection was conventionally used to evaluate participants’ attentional engagement during tasks. As shown in Fig. 3, repeated-word detection performance confirmed effective attentional engagement during the conversation tasks. In the behavioral map, SC and MC-A clustered in the high-precision, high-recall region for all participants, whereas MC-U showed markedly reduced detection performance. Consistently, F1-scores were significantly higher for MC-A compared to MC-U (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 10 −4, r = 0 .87 ), confirming successful attentional separation between attended and unat-tended streams. Although the difference between SC and MC-A was modest, F1-scores were also significantly higher in SC than in MC-A ( p < 10 −4, r = 0 .52 ), indicating slightly reduced detection performance when involving interfering conversations. 

4.2. Physiological Signal Sanity Check 

Basic physiological features, including heart rate, skin temper-ature, GSR (quantified using SCL and NS-SCR), respiration frequency, accelerometer magnitude, SpO 2, HRV (quantified using SDNN and RMSSD), and pupil dilation, were extracted during the MC task (see Method for details), as shown in Fig. 4. These features exhibited distributions consistent with expected physiological ranges defined based on prior literature and device specifications. Most measurements fell within or close to these ranges, indicating overall good signal quality. A small number of outliers were observed, likely reflecting natural variability or minor recording artifacts.

4.4. Inter-subject Correlation 

ISC measures the similarity of time-varying responses across individuals exposed to the same stimulus and is widely used to characterize shared neural and physiological dynamics during naturalistic stimulation [12]. Here, we quantified ISC across all recorded modalities to assess signal reliability beyond EEG and to evaluate reproducible stimulus-locked temporal structure. As shown in Fig. 8, several modalities demonstrated above-chance ISC at the group level and in a substantial fraction of participants. In particular, EEG, gaze location, pupil dilation, and PPG exhibited consistent cross-subject temporal alignment, in-dicating reliable stimulus-related dynamics. In contrast, slower-varying measures such as temperature displayed minimal evi-dence of shared temporal structure at the trial level. Some tonic physiological signals yielded significant group-level effects but limited subject-level prevalence, such as heart rate and SpO 2,suggesting heterogeneous responsiveness across participants. Overall, these findings indicate that multiple recorded modal-ities contain reproducible stimulus-locked structure under natu-ralistic listening conditions in the present dataset. The observed ISC profiles support the suitability of these signals for down-stream multimodal modeling and integration. 

# 5. Conclusion 

We introduced MOV-AAD, a large-scale multimodal dataset for auditory attention decoding and cognitive state assessment under realistic listening conditions. The dataset integrates EEG with synchronized physiological and behavioral recordings from 50 participants during spatially moving listening tasks. We conducted validation analyses including stimulus recon-struction, auditory attention decoding, and intersubject correla-tion across modalities. The results demonstrate reliable EEG attentional modulation, cross-subject temporal alignment, and consistent multimodal physiological responses, indicating re-producible stimulus- and attention-related structure suitable for downstream modeling and benchmarking. Combining ecological validity, multimodal coverage, and a substantial sample size, MOV-AAD supports investigations of neural and autonomic dynamics of selective attention and the development of decoding approaches in complex acoustic environments. The dataset is publicly available to facilitate reproducibility and cross-laboratory comparison.
