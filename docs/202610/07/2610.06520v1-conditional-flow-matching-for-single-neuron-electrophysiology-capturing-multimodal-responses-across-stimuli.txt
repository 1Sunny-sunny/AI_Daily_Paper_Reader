# Conditional Flow Matching for Single-Neuron Electrophysiology: Capturing Multimodal Responses Across Stimuli 

Cameron Schofield 1,* , Luca Ghafourpour 1,*,† , Philip H. Wong 2, Costas A. Anastassiou 2,3,4 and Richard E. Turner 1   

> 1University of Cambridge, 2Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, 3California Institute of Technology, 4Archimedes AI, Athena Research Center
> *These authors contributed equally to this work
> †Corresponding author: ldg34@cam.ac.uk

Neurons of the brain exhibit a rich repertoire of electrophysiology dynamics with the same repeated stimulus eliciting very different voltage responses from the same cell. One common approach in biophys-ically detailed models is to capture this variability through ensembles of deterministic parametrizations, at a cost of hundreds of thousands of CPU hours. Existing machine learning surrogates inherit the same limitation, where a stimulus is mapped to a single voltage response. We address this challenge by learning a conditional generative model for single-neuron electrophysiology, using flow matching with a velocity field conditioned on the input current. On biophysically detailed models of two human cortical in-terneuron types, the generated responses closely reproduce the electrophysiological feature distributions, spike-time structure, and excitability profiles, even matching the experimental recordings from the corre-sponding human cortical neurons. Near the firing threshold, firing and non-firing responses coexist at the same stimulus amplitude, and at high amplitudes, ensembles may split into low- and high-firing modes near depolarization block. We show that our model recovers both modes in each case, while a neural oper-ator baseline suppresses spiking near threshold and blurs the gap between modes at depolarization block. 

## 1. Introduction 

Neurons in the mammalian brain exhibit a wide range of electrophysiological responses that are area-, type-, state-, and time-specific. This is also reflected in trial-to-trial variability where the same input to the same cell typically results in very different responses in terms of spike timing, firing rate, and burstiness [1–4]. Capturing such diverse dynamics faithfully is essential for any single-neuron model intended to support bio-realistic circuit-level modeling [5–7], and especially so for the human brain, where cell types differ in transcriptomic, morphological, and electrophysiological identity [8–11]. Biophysically detailed, multi-compartment PDE models grounded in cable theory provide an avenue to study these dynamics under perturbations that are experimentally infeasible in humans [12, 13], with ion channel conductances tuned by evolutionary optimization to match multimodal experimental recordings [14–17]. Because many parametrizations perform comparably along the Pareto front [18], an ensemble of “hall-of-fame” (HoF) parametrizations is retained for each neuron and is often treated as a representation of the neuron’s variability. This substitution is imperfect: each HoF model is itself deterministic, so the ensemble reflects uncertainty in the model parametrization rather than genuine trial-to-trial variability. Obtaining it also requires on the order of hundreds of thousands of CPU hours [16, 17]. An alternative introduces stochasticity directly into the mechanistic model [19–21], but this requires making assumptions on the noise source and form and relies on repeated numerical integration of the underlying dynamical system. These limitations have motivated machine learning surrogates for single-neuron electrophysiology. Neural networks, physics-informed neural networks [22–24], and neural operators [25–27] have been applied to canonical point-neuron models such as FitzHugh-Nagumo [28, 29] and Hodgkin-arXiv:2610.06520v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 Conditional Flow Matching for Single-Neuron Electrophysiology: Capturing Multimodal Responses Across Stimuli 

Huxley [30], with promising results for predicting spiking membrane potentials [31–38]. These approaches are largely deterministic, producing a single voltage response per stimulus, and are evaluated predominantly within the regular firing regime, with performance in the highly nonlinear transition around firing threshold less clear. NOBLE [39] addresses both limitations, learning a neural operator conditioned on an embedding of interpretable electrophysiological descriptors that identifies each member of a HoF ensemble. Interpolating this embedding yields responses for models beyond the finite set obtained through optimization, something the underlying biophysical models do not support in parameter space. The operator nevertheless remains deterministic given a stimulus and embedding: variability arises from sampling the embedding space rather than from learning a conditional distribution over membrane potentials. Generative modeling provides a natural alternative in which the voltage response distribution itself becomes the target. Rather than introducing variability through an intermediate model representation, single-neuron electrophysiology can be formulated as the conditional generation of a membrane poten-tial 𝑉 given an applied current 𝐼 , directly targeting 𝑝 (𝑉 | 𝐼 ). Learning this distribution requires a model to represent multimodality in spike timing and spike count, preserve translation equivariance to shifts in the applied stimulus, resolve both sharp action potentials and smooth subthreshold dynamics across multiple temporal scales, and generate a coherent full voltage trace. Denoising generative models such as diffusion and flow matching are well suited to these requirements as they generate complete high-dimensional samples from noise while conditioning on auxiliary inputs [40–42]. In particular, flow matching [42] learns a continuous velocity field that transports samples from a simple source distribution to the target data distribution. Conditioning this velocity field on the time-aligned applied current yields a direct model of 𝑝 (𝑉 | 𝐼 ), where different samples from the source noise produce differ-ent membrane potential trajectories for the same stimulus. Variability is therefore represented directly within the response distribution, without requiring an explicit neuron-model embedding. This distinc-tion matters most where the conditioning input places the underlying deterministic dynamics near a bifurcation. There, the response distribution becomes genuinely multimodal, and a surrogate trained with a pointwise objective moves towards an intermediate response that belongs to neither mode.

## 2. Bio-Realistic Neuron Modeling 

The biophysical single neuron PDE models are built using high resolution morphological reconstruc-tions of human cortical neurons from Patch-seq datasets [8–10]. For simulation, the models are instantiated via the BioNet interface [43] in the NEURON environment [44]. In each PDE model, active ion channels are placed in an “all-active” configuration [16, 17], distributing conductances across both somatic and dendritic compartments throughout the full morphology. The multi-objective evolutionary optimization is organized in two stages [14–16]. In the first stage, passive subthreshold membrane properties are optimized against non-spiking electrophysiological responses. In the second stage, active ion channel conductances are included in the optimization to account for spiking dynamics and the frequency-current (F-I) curve. In each generation of optimization, candidate HoF PDE models are ranked by mean z-score error across a biologically relevant set of electrophysiological features. After 250 generations, the best-performing parameter sets across all generations are retained as the HoF ensemble. To evaluate the benefits of generative modeling, we focus on one parvalbumin-positive (PVALB) neuron and one vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) neuron from an inhibitory interneuron Patch-seq dataset of the human middle temporal gyrus [10]. PVALB neurons are specialized fast-spiking interneurons that orchestrate high-frequency gamma oscillations (30–80 Hz) [45]. VIP neurons are often found in disinhibitory circuit motifs where they target other inhibitory interneurons and release excitatory cells from dendritic inhibition [46–48]. These two cell types exhibit qualitatively distinct firing regimes. While PVALB neurons coordinate high-frequency rhythms, VIP neurons operate on a slower timescale [9, 49], allowing us to test the generalization of our framework across electrophysiological phenotypes.

## 3. Method 

3.1. Problem Formulation 

We aim to learn a conditional generative model 𝑝 𝜃 (𝑉 | 𝐼 ) that approximates the distribution of the membrane potential 𝑉 given an applied current 𝐼 , where both are discretized traces in R𝑇 . To capture variability, we treat the responses from an ensemble of HoF models as samples from 𝑝 (𝑉 | 𝐼 ).We construct the dataset D = {( 𝐼 𝑖 , 𝑉 𝑖 )} 𝑁 𝑖 =1 by simulating the voltage responses of randomly paired input currents and HoF models. Our model captures ensemble variability through source noise and conditions on the full current waveform to produce time-aligned voltage responses. Since the full stimulus waveform is available at inference time, we generate the voltage response jointly rather than autoregressively, allowing temporal dependencies to be represented across the complete response.

3.3. Training Details 

We parameterize the velocity field 𝑢 𝜃 𝑡 with a five-resolution one-dimensional U-Net [53], using dilated convolutions at the bottleneck to capture long-range temporal dependencies. The input current is encoded and injected at each resolution, while flow time is embedded sinusoidally. Models are trained with AdamW [54] for 200 epochs and sampled using 20 midpoint steps. Full architectural, optimization, and sampling details, together with associated ablations, are provided in Appendix A.2. As reported in [39], the numerical solver takes 36,200 seconds to generate 10,000 voltage responses. We record the time necessary to generate 10,000 samples on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU using our trained generative model. Each sample requires 40 network evaluations. When doing one inference at a time, we generate 10,000 samples in 6,600 seconds, a speedup of approximately 5 .5×.Using a batch size of 1000, the model generates 10,000 samples in approximately 865 seconds, a speedup of approximately 42 ×. Inference cost can be reduced further, either by taking fewer network evaluations per sample or by distilling the learned velocity field into a few-step generator [55–57], in both cases at a fixed cost in fidelity.

4.1. Recovering Response Distributions at Unseen Currents 

We first assess the generated ensemble of full voltage traces at unseen current amplitudes. Figure 1 qualitatively showcases that for both the PVALB and VIP neurons, the generated ensembles closely resemble those produced by the HoF models, capturing both the response dynamics and their variability. To compare spike shape more directly, we compute spike-triggered averages, where the median and interquartile range of the generated responses closely match those of the HoFs, while the experimental curves lie within the same range. We observe similar agreement in the F-I curves, where the generated firing-rate distributions track the HoFs across stimulation amplitudes and the experimental measurements align closely with the median response. These results indicate that our model recovers the underlying conditional response distribution at unseen currents. To assess whether the model preserves electrophysiological structure beyond individual voltage traces, we compute a set of electrophysiological features for each response (see Appendix A.1 for definitions). 4.3. Capturing Multimodal Dynamics 

At sufficiently high current amplitudes, the VIP HoF ensemble approaches depolarization block. Differences across HoF parametrizations place models on either side of this transition, with some continuing to fire while others enter a low-firing state. This produces a multimodal conditional response distribution. Figure 5 shows this clearly at 0.28nA, where the HoF spike-count distribution separates into distinct low- and high-firing modes. Our generative model recovers both modes and their approximate probability mass, whereas NOBLE spreads mass across the range and resolves neither mode. Importantly, this difference would be obscured by the mean response alone. 4.4. Generalization to Time-Varying Stimulation Protocols 

While the previous sections consider DC-step inputs, conditioning on the full time-aligned stimulus waveform allows the model to handle more general stimulation protocols. We therefore generated a new dataset of time-varying current waveforms (Appendix A.3) and trained a separate model on this broader stimulus distribution, using the same model, optimizer, and training hyperparameters as the model trained on DC step waveforms. Table 2 repeats the dynamical-regime evaluation of Table 1, but using the model trained on more complex time-varying stimuli. Performance remains comparable across threshold, low-firing, and high-firing regimes, with no new regime-specific failure mode. Hence, expanding the training distribution to time-varying inputs does not substantially degrade the model’s ability to reproduce the response distributions elicited by DC steps. We next evaluate both models on unseen time-varying stimuli. Figure 6 compares their generated response ensembles on representative held-out waveforms. The DC-trained model generalizes to some non-DC stimuli by producing spikes in the correct regions, but blur in the generated ensembles indicates poor ISI consistency. ## 5. Conclusion 

We introduced a conditional flow matching model of voltage dynamics that learns 𝑝 (𝑉 | 𝐼 ) directly, representing variability directly without an ad hoc distribution over latent feature embeddings. Trained on ensembles of PDE simulations for PVALB and VIP neurons, it recovers the response distribution at unseen currents, captures temporal dependencies and multimodal dynamics, and transfers across stimulus families. These properties make conditional generative surrogates a promising route toward large-scale circuit modeling, where biophysically detailed simulations become prohibitively expensive. Extending the framework from single neurons to interacting populations, while using distillation or few-step generation to approach the speed of deterministic surrogates such as NOBLE , are natural directions for future work.

A.2.2. Training Objective 

The L2 loss is minimized by the conditional mean that recovers the marginal velocity field required to transport probability mass from 𝑝 0 to 𝑞 . The L1 loss is minimized by the conditional median; for multimodal distributions, the minimizer is pulled toward whichever mode carries more mass, rather than averaging across divergent paths. This yields a biased field that may fail to transport the distribution to 𝑞 .

Despite this theoretical limitation, we tested both loss functions to see if mode selection would sharpen individual traces that averaging would blur under spike-timing jitter. Empirically, L1 reduces subthreshold noise and improves spike amplitude accuracy (Table A.2). However, L2 better preserves the firing distribution while L1 has a greater tendency to have mode collapse. We used the L2 loss to train our models. We performed a hyperparameter sweep on the U-Net to determine an optimal configuration. ## A.3. Dataset Generation 

The voltage response datasets for the PVALB (with DC step inputs and time-varying inputs) and VIP (with DC step inputs) neurons are generated from a single neuron morphology and a set of 60 HoF conductance parametrizations obtained by multi-objective evolutionary optimization, of which 50 are used for training. For each model we draw 1,680 stimuli, giving 100,800 current–voltage pairs; every stimulus is paired with a single model, so a conditioner is almost never observed more than once. Responses are simulated in NEURON [44] at a 0 .02ms timestep over a 400 ms activation window and cropped to 45 ms before onset, the activation window, and 115 ms after it. Traces are then low-pass filtered and decimated by a factor of three, which preserves the fidelity of the extracted features while reducing computational expense [39], giving 9,334 samples at Δ𝑡 = 0.06ms. Current and voltage traces are min-max scaled to [− 1, 1], fit on the training split. The 50 training models contribute 84,000 traces, split 90/10 into 75,600 training and 8,400 validation examples.
