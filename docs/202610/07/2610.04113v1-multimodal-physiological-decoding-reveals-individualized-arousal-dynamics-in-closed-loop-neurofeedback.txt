Abstract Objective. Arousal-based brain-computer interfaces have predominantly relied on EEG to decode cognitive state, yet arousal is fundamentally an autonomic phenomenon. We investigated whether peripheral physiological signals provide superior decoding of task-demand-related arousal, and whether the resulting signal recovers theoretically predicted arousal-performance relationships with sufficient fidelity to support individualized closed-loop neuromodulation. 

Approach. We re-analyzed a publicly available dataset from a demanding boundary-avoidance flight task in which participants received BCI neurofeedback, sham feedback, or silence. A multimodal deep learning decoder was trained on peripheral physiological signals (heart rate, heart rate variability, respira-tion, electrodermal activity, and pupillometry) and benchmarked against a retrained EEG-only decoder and the original filter-bank common spatial pattern (FBCSP) pipeline. Decoded arousal trajectories were validated post hoc against task performance and behavioral outcomes rather than against a direct arousal measure. Individual differences in baseline HRV and gamma power were used to stratify subjects into arousal-sensitive and arousal-tolerant groups, from which personalized control bands were derived. 

Main results. The peripheral decoder achieved 93 .0% ± 1.1% within-subject AUC for task-demand classification, outperforming the EEG-only decoder ( 85 .2% ) and the FBCSP benchmark ( 79 .8% ). Only the peripheral decoder recovered the Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U relationship between decoded arousal and performance—to our knowledge among the first demonstrations of continuous Yerkes-Dodson recovery from a physiological decoder. Arousal trajectories did not differ across feedback conditions, suggesting that BCI-mediated performance benefits reflect targeted regulation at critical task moments rather than sustained trajectory shifts. Per-trial deviations from a time-varying optimal arousal trajectory significantly predicted performance ( r = −0.28 to −0.24 , all p < 0.01 ). Group-personalized control bands improved trial-separation Cohen’s d from 1.21 to 1.32 (arousal-sensitive) and 0.85 to 1.10 (arousal-tolerant), and improved the deviation-performance correlation from r = 0 .308 to r = 0 .359 overall. 

Significance. These findings establish that peripheral physiological signals, rather than EEG, are the appropriate modality for continuous arousal decoding, and provide a technical specification for individualized closed-loop arousal regulation. The results motivate a prospective trial of personalized transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation (tVNS) timed to the decoded arousal signal, with subject-specific control thresholds derived from baseline neurophysiological phenotype.

1arXiv:2610.04113v1 [q-bio.NC] 2 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

The Yerkes-Dodson law [1, 2] posits an inverted-U relationship between arousal and cognitive performance: performance is maximized within an optimal arousal band and degrades on either side. Maintaining an operator within that band is a compelling target for closed-loop brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), with direct relevance to high-stakes domains where arousal escalation leads to catastrophic failure, such as pilot-induced oscillations (PIOs) in aviation [3, 4]. Faller et al [5] demonstrated a proof of concept using a virtual-reality boundary-avoidance task (BAT). An EEG decoder based on filter-bank common spatial patterns (FBCSP) [6] and linear discriminant analysis decoded task-dependent arousal with an AUC of 79 .8% and drove loudness-modulated auditory neurofeed-back. Subjects receiving veridical feedback flew approximately 7 s longer on the hard course than in silence, with corroborating increases in HRV and reductions in pupil diameter suggesting genuine down-regulation of arousal. This work remains a primary demonstration of how EEG-based neurofeedback can improve performance in a demanding real-time task. Because that study delivered EEG-based feedback rather than the peripheral signal we evaluate here, the closed-loop framework we develop below is a prospective specification: every analysis in this paper is offline, and the peripheral decoder is never placed in the feedback loop. We revisit this dataset to address three gaps. First, decoding accuracy was modest and, critically, the continuous output of the FBCSP decoder was never validated against the Yerkes-Dodson law. High classification accuracy does not guarantee that the decoded signal faithfully represents graded arousal state: a decoder trained to separate large from small rings may capture any feature correlated with task difficulty without tracking the graded arousal that governs performance. Second, although feedback improved hard-course flight time, the mechanism by which feedback acts on the arousal signal itself was not characterized: does it shift overall arousal level, or does it act locally at performance-critical moments? Third, feedback was delivered reactively against a uniform threshold, ignoring well-documented individual differences in arousal sensitivity. A broader question motivates all three gaps: why use EEG at all for arousal decoding? Arousal is mediated by the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system [7], which simultaneously drives cortical excitability and peripheral autonomic output. Heart rate, HRV, skin conductance, and pupil diameter are direct readouts of this system [18]. Despite extensive use in affective computing [8, 9], peripheral physiological signals have been largely ignored in BCIs, even though they are mechanistically closer to the target variable and far more practical for real-world deployment. The contributions of this paper are cumulative by design: each builds on the one before. We first show that a multimodal deep learning decoder using peripheral signals substantially outperforms both an EEG-only variant and the original FBCSP benchmark. That accuracy advantage is meaningful only if the decoded signal represents genuine arousal, so we next validate it against the Yerkes-Dodson law, providing among the first demonstrations that a continuously decoded physiological arousal signal recovers the theoretically predicted inverted-U relationship with performance. We then examine how feedback affects this signal, finding no sustained trajectory shifts across feedback conditions, which reframes how neurofeedback should be interpreted. We derive a time-varying optimal arousal trajectory from control trials and show that per-trial deviations from it significantly predict performance. Finally, we show that individual differences in baseline HRV and gamma power significantly shape each subject’s arousal-performance curve, motivating a classification into arousal-sensitive and arousal-tolerant groups whose group-specific control bands improve trial-quality discrimination over a one-size-fits-all policy. 22 Data and experimental setup 

We analyzed our previously collected data, specifically that we had made publicly available in Faller et al. [5] (IEEE DataPort: https://doi.org/10.21227/rn3e-bp31 ). Full experimental details are given in the original Faller paper; we describe here only what is necessary to interpret our analyses. 

2.1 Participants and task 

N = 16 healthy adults performed a virtual-reality boundary-avoidance task (BAT) in which they navigated a simulated aircraft through sequences of red rectangular boundary rings arranged along a sinusoidal trajectory. Ring sizes decreased every 30 s (large, medium, small), increasing task difficulty across each maximum 90 s trial. Performance was measured as time in flight before missing a ring. Two courses were used: an easy course (larger rings throughout) and a hard course (uniformly smaller rings). The hard course is the primary focus of the analysis, as its difficulty range drives arousal into the region where Yerkes-Dodson dynamics are most pronounced. The closed-loop experiment comprised alternating easy and hard flights. Each flight was randomly assigned to one of three feedback conditions: BCI (an EEG-decoded arousal index modulating the loudness of auditory heartbeat feedback); Sham (a 50/50 blend of the true decoder output and an autoregressive surrogate); or Silence (no feedback). Conditions rotated so that each appeared twice per six consecutive flights. 

2.2 Physiological recordings 

All signals were recorded at 2048 Hz (BioSemi ActiveTwo) and downsampled to 256 Hz prior to analysis. Recordings comprised 64-channel EEG, ECG from two thoracic electrodes, EDA from two left-hand elec-trodes, respiration from a chest belt, and pupillometry from the VR headset eye tracker (60 Hz), synchronized via Lab Streaming Layer [14]. From these raw signals, we derived seven peripheral physiological channels: heart rate (HR), beat-to-beat HRV (pNN35) [19], respiratory amplitude (RESP), phasic EDA, tonic EDA, and left and right pupil diameters (PUP-L, PUP-R). No preprocessing beyond downsampling was applied to peripheral channels prior to decoder training. 

2.3 Epoch extraction and labels 

For each ring crossing event, a 2 s (512-sample) epoch of physiological signal immediately preceding the crossing was extracted. Following Faller et al [5], labels were assigned by ring size: large rings (low task demand) were class 0; medium and small rings (high task demand) were class 1. Training data comprised epochs from a 10-minute calibration session (easy course, silence condition); held-out test data comprised all epochs from the closed-loop phase. 

# 3 Methods 

3.1 Multimodal peripheral decoder 

3.1.1 Architecture. 

The seven peripheral channels were grouped into four functional modalities: cardiac (HR, HRV-pNN35), respiratory (RESP), electrodermal (EDA-phasic, EDA-tonic), and pupillometric (PUP-L, PUP-R). Each modality was independently processed by a SimpleTemporalEncoder consisting of a two-layer 1D convolu-tional stack (kernel size 3, same padding, batch normalization, ReLU) followed by global average pooling 3into a 64-dimensional embedding. The four embeddings were concatenated into a 256-dimensional vector and classified by a two-layer MLP (hidden dimension 128, ReLU, dropout p = 0 .1, output dimension 2). All weights used Kaiming normal initialization. The full architecture is illustrated in figure 1A. 

3.1.2 Training. 

Per-channel z-score normalization was computed from training data only and applied to held-out test data. Data augmentation applied two strategies independently with p = 0 .5: temporal masking (approximately 15% of the epoch zeroed at a random contiguous position) and additive Gaussian noise ( σ = 0 .1). Cross-entropy loss with label smoothing ( ε = 0 .05 ) was minimized with Adam (lr = 10 −3), using a step learning rate scheduler ( γ = 0 .1, every 10 epochs) for up to 25 epochs with early stopping (patience 10, activated after epoch 15). Each subject’s decoder was trained 10 times with independent random seeds; softmax probabilities were averaged across repetitions to produce a stable arousal estimate. 

3.1.3 Continuous arousal estimation. 

For trial-level analysis, the trained model was applied to a sliding window advancing every 16 samples (62.5 ms), using the most recent 512-sample epoch. Raw probabilities were normalized to a 0–100 arousal index using the 5th and 95th percentiles of the training-set distribution, then filtered with an asymmetric first-order infinite impulse response filter ( τrise = 0 .75 s, τfall = 1 .5 s) to reflect the faster escalation than recovery of autonomic arousal. The time constants were chosen to match established sympathetic activation latency (<1 s for HR acceleration) versus parasympathetic recovery (1–3 s for HR deceleration) [15]; sensitivity analyses with symmetric filters ( τ = 1 .0 s and τ = 1 .5 s) produced qualitatively identical trajectory shapes and deviation metric correlations, confirming that the downstream results are robust to this modelling choice. 

3.1.4 Comparison conditions. 

The peripheral decoder was compared against (a) a retrained EEG-only variant [20] with identical architecture applied to 64-channel EEG decomposed into four standard frequency bands (theta 4–8 Hz, alpha 8–13 Hz, beta 13–30 Hz, gamma 30–55 Hz via Butterworth bandpass filters; 256 input channels total), (b) the original Faller et al FBCSP benchmark, and (c) an autonomic+EEG variant that included all five input modalities. Feature attribution was performed using Integrated Gradients [16] with 50 integration steps, averaged across held-out validation epochs. 

3.2 Yerkes-Dodson validation 

To test whether the decoded arousal signal captures the inverted-U arousal-performance relationship, we fitted linear mixed-effects models of the form: Perf ∼ Ar + Ar 2 + Diff + Cond + (1 | Subj ) (1) where Ar is the average decoded arousal for the trial, Diff is the ring difficulty, and Cond is the feedback condition. A significant negative coefficient on Ar 2 confirms the inverted-U. Separate models were fitted for each decoder, and the fits were compared using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). 

3.3 Effect of feedback condition on arousal trajectory 

To test whether the neurofeedback condition modifies the overall arousal trajectory, we computed mean arousal traces separately for BCI, sham, and silence trials and compared them via cluster-based permutation tests over trial time. We additionally extracted per-trial summaries (mean arousal, peak arousal, area under 4the trajectory, % time above the optimal trajectory) and tested for between-condition differences with mixed-effects ANOVAs including subject as a random effect. 

3.4 Individual differences and sensitivity groups 

From the 10-minute calibration session, two baseline physiological features were extracted per subject: resting HRV-pNN35 and gamma power in the 30–55 Hz band. We compute the average value of these two features across the session and then z-score across subjects. An arousal sensitivity score was computed as 

s = −HRV z + Gamma z , combining low parasympathetic tone with high cortical excitability as indicators of arousal sensitivity [10, 11]. Subjects above the median score were classified as arousal-sensitive ; those at or below as arousal-tolerant . Effects of baseline physiology on the arousal-performance curve were tested by adding interaction terms (HRV z × Ar k, Gamma z × Ar k, k ∈ { 1, 2}) to equation (1). 

3.5 Optimal arousal trajectory and control algorithm 

3.5.1 Trajectory derivation. 

Using hard-course trials under silence and sham conditions (approximately 240 trials), mean performance was computed for each combination of 1-second time bin and 5-unit arousal bin. Bins were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel ( σ = 1 bin). The optimal arousal ˆa(t) at each time point was taken as the arousal value at peak smoothed performance in that bin; the confidence band was defined as ˆa(t) ± ˆσ(t), where ˆσ(t) is the SD of performance across the neighboring arousal bins near the peak. A polynomial was fitted to the ˆa(t)

series to produce a smooth optimal trajectory across the full 90-second trial. 

3.5.2 Deviation metrics. 

For each trial, four deviation metrics were computed: (1) percentage of time above the upper confidence bound; (2) percentage of time within the confidence band; (3) mean excursion magnitude (above-band deviation averaged over above-band intervals); and (4) excursion rate (band crossings per minute). Each metric was correlated with trial flight time using Pearson r, and differences between good (top performance quartile) and bad (bottom quartile) trials were quantified by Cohen’s d. A fifth characterization — mean dwell time above the upper confidence bound before band exit — was computed to assess the feasibility of reactive closed-loop intervention; excursions sustained for fewer than 2 s were excluded as transient fluctuations inconsistent with a state change in LC-NE dynamics. 

3.5.3 Personalized threshold optimization. 

Band width multipliers m were optimized separately for the sensitive and tolerant groups by grid search over 

m ∈ { 0.50 , 0.75 , . . . , 3.00 }, where the personalized control band was [ˆ a(t) − mˆσ(t), ˆa(t) + mˆσ(t)] . The grid search jointly maximized Cohen’s d between good and bad trials on the % time above band metric, and Pearson r between the % time above band metric and performance. All hypothesis tests used α = 0 .05 with Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. # 4 Results 

4.1 Peripheral signals substantially outperform EEG for arousal decoding 

The multimodal peripheral decoder ( autonomic-only ) achieved within-subject AUC of 93 .0% ± 1.1% , and the combined autonomic+EEG variant achieved 94 .2% ± 0.71% , both significantly exceeding the retrained EEG-only decoder ( 85 .2% ) and the original FBCSP benchmark ( 79 .8% ± 7.2% ), with all pairwise differences significant ( p < 0.001 , paired t-tests; figure 1B). All 16 subjects exceeded 80% AUC under the autonomic decoder; 14 of 16 exceeded 90%. The small additional gain from adding EEG to the autonomic decoder (+1.2 percentage points) suggests that EEG contributes minimal unique information beyond what is already captured by peripheral signals; subsequent analyses use the autonomic-only decoder as the primary signal, with the autonomic+EEG variant retained for sensitivity checks. Feature attribution (Integrated Gradients) confirmed that cardiac signals (HR and HRV-pNN35) domi-nated predictions, accounting for the majority of total attribution mass (figure 1C). Tonic EDA contributed substantially, with respiration and pupillometry showing modest but stable contributions, and a late-epoch rise in respiratory attribution in the final 500 ms before ring crossing, consistent with phasic LC-NE activity at task-demand peaks [17]. EEG attribution was essentially zero throughout the epoch, even in the combined model, again indicating that the autonomic channels carry the bulk of the task-relevant information. 64.2 The peripheral decoder recovers the Yerkes-Dodson relationship; the EEG decoder does not 

For the multimodal autonomic decoder, the mixed-effects model (equation 1) produced significant main effects of both decoded arousal ( p < 0.01 ) and the quadratic term ( p < 0.01 ), confirming the theoretically predicted inverted-U (figure 2A). The estimated optimal arousal level was approximately 51 (on the 0–100 normalized scale) for both difficulty levels, with the easy and hard courses producing essentially overlapping optima that differ only in peak performance height. Applying the same mixed-effects analysis to the outputs of the Faller et al FBCSP EEG decoder, a re-analysis we perform here rather than a result reported in their original study, the fitted curve was much shallower and the estimated optima diverged substantially between easy ( ∼ 62 ) and hard ( ∼ 76 ) courses (figure 2B), inconsistent with a stable arousal-performance relationship that should not depend on task difficulty alignment. AIC comparison confirmed that the autonomic quadratic model provided the best overall fit (figure 2C). The autonomic quadratic model dominated all alternatives, with ∆AIC > 18 over the next-best autonomic linear variant, and ∆AIC > 44 over both EEG-based models. As external validation, the peripheral decoder correlated strongly with raw physiological arousal markers: HR ( r = 0 .71 , p < 0.001 ) and HRV-pNN35 inversely ( r = −0.58 , p < 0.001 ), whereas the EEG decoder showed substantially weaker correlations with both.

4.4 Feedback condition does not shift the arousal trajectory 

We next asked how closed-loop feedback affects the decoded arousal signal itself. Comparing mean arousal trajectories across BCI, sham, and silence conditions revealed no significant differences in either the time-resolved trajectory (cluster-based permutation, all p > 0.10 ) or trial-level summaries (mean arousal, peak arousal, area under the trajectory, all p > 0.10 ). Subjects produced indistinguishable overall arousal trajectories regardless of whether they received veridical, sham, or no feedback. This is a substantive finding rather than a null result. The Faller et al [5] closed-loop study reported a significant performance advantage of BCI feedback over silence (approximately 7 s longer flight time on the hard course). Our observation that this performance gain occurs without an accompanying sustained shift in the arousal trajectory implies that the feedback benefit must arise from localized arousal modulation at performance-critical moments (e.g., approaching small rings under high task demand), rather than from a wholesale change in arousal level across the trial. This reframes the mechanism of arousal-based neuro-feedback as moment-targeted regulation, and supports an intervention strategy that acts at specific critical moments rather than attempting to maintain a globally lower or higher arousal state. 84.5 A time-varying optimal arousal trajectory predicts per-trial performance 

4.5.1 Time-varying optimal arousal. 

The optimal arousal level derived from the joint arousal-performance surface was not constant across a trial (figure 4A). It rose monotonically from low values at trial onset to higher levels by approximately the 45-second mark, reflecting the escalating attentional demands as ring sizes decrease over the course of a trial: a higher arousal level is adaptive for the final, most demanding segment. A fixed threshold applied uniformly across trial time would therefore be miscalibrated for a substantial portion of each trial. This is the mechanistic basis for the control algorithm in section 4.7: the band against which trial arousal is compared must itself be a function of trial time. 

4.5.2 Deviation metrics predict performance. 

All four deviation metrics significantly predicted trial flight time (table 1). Trials in the top performance quartile spent approximately 80% of their duration within the optimal confidence band, whereas bottom-quartile trials spent only approximately 57% within the band (figure 4D; pairwise contrast p < 0.01 ,Holm-corrected). The corresponding arousal trajectories (figure 4C) clearly differentiate good trials (green, tracking the optimal trajectory closely from below) from bad trials (red, rising rapidly above the optimal trajectory and exiting the confidence band). After excluding single-second threshold crossings (15% of naive detections), mean dwell time above the personalized band prior to band exit was 6.1 ± 3.6 s for arousal-sensitive subjects (median 6.0 s, p10 = 2.0

s) and 8.6 ± 7.4 s for tolerant subjects (median 6.0 s, p10 = 2.0 s). No sustained excursion fell below 2 s, indicating that the reactive latency budget is sufficient for both groups under their respective personalized bands.

The sensitive group benefited from a tight band (optimal ms = 1 .5× SD), reflecting their steep arousal-performance curve (figure 6B). The tolerant group benefited from a much wider band (optimal 

mt = 2 .5× SD), reflecting their broader operating range (figure 6C). The two band widths and the underlying time-varying trajectory are shown together in figure 6A. The personalized policy improved trial-separation effect sizes. Applying a uniform multiplier of m = 1 .0

# 5 Discussion 

5.1 The case for peripheral signals in arousal-based BCIs 

The 8–14 percentage point AUC advantage of the peripheral decoder over EEG (approximately 8 points over the retrained EEG-only decoder and 13–14 over the FBCSP benchmark) is substantial by BCI standards, but the more important finding is what that advantage means for the physiological validity of the decoded signal. The FBCSP decoder achieves 79.8% AUC but produces a flat arousal-performance curve. The peripheral decoder achieves 93.0% AUC and recovers the Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U with a stable optimum across difficulty levels. These are distinct properties, and it is the second, not the first, that matters for closed-loop arousal control. The theoretical basis for this result is straightforward. The LC-NE system [7] directly drives both cortical excitability and peripheral sympathetic-parasympathetic balance. The cardiac dominance in our feature attribution is expected: HR and HRV are primary LC-NE effector outputs. Peripheral sensors also sidestep a systematic risk inherent to EEG-based arousal decoding — the gamma-band EMG confound identified in Faller et al. [5], where scalp muscle artifact mimics high-frequency cortical activity and inflates apparent arousal sensitivity. Because autonomic signals lack such artifactual coupling, they provide a cleaner readout of the underlying regulatory axis. The negligible contribution of EEG when added to the autonomic decoder (+1 .2 AUC points; near-zero attribution in figure 1C) reinforces this: once the autonomic axis is well represented, scalp EEG carries little additional information about graded arousal state.

The distinction matters: classification accuracy on a task-difficulty label is necessary but not sufficient. A decoder trained to separate large from small rings may score well by detecting any feature correlated with difficulty, while the decoded signal remains physiologically uninformative as an arousal index. The Yerkes-Dodson test directly asks whether the signal has the right structure for closed-loop control. The behavior of the FBCSP decoder in figure 2B — a difficulty-dependent “optimum” that drifts from ∼ 62 on easy to ∼ 76 on hard — is consistent with a decoder that has learned a difficulty-correlated feature rather than a graded arousal state, and would be miscalibrated as the control variable in a closed-loop system. Establishing Yerkes-Dodson compliance at the level of a continuously decoded arousal signal as a benchmark would enable principled comparison across decoder architectures, sensing modalities, and paradigms. 

5.3 Reframing what neurofeedback does 

The finding that arousal trajectories do not differ across BCI, sham, and silence conditions, despite the documented BCI performance advantage [5], has implications for how arousal-based neurofeedback should 12 be conceptualized and evaluated. A natural assumption is that auditory neurofeedback works by shifting the average arousal level downward over the course of a trial; our data do not support this. Instead, the most plausible interpretation is that feedback enables subjects to deploy arousal regulation selectively at critical moments — e.g. maintaining an arousal envelope when approaching small rings under high task demand. Two implications follow. First, evaluating closed-loop arousal interventions by their effect on mean or peak arousal is likely to underestimate them; performance-conditional analyses (e.g., arousal at the time of failure events) may be more sensitive. Second, an intervention with the timing precision to act only at critical moments — rather than a persistent intervention that biases arousal throughout a trial — is the more rational deployment target. A future tVNS system triggered by deviations from the personalized optimal band would fit this profile: a discrete intervention timed to the moment of greatest predicted performance risk. 

5.4 Individualization as a practical requirement 

The improvements in effect size and correlation strength from personalized thresholds reflect a real difference in the control policy each group requires. Sensitive subjects have a steep, narrow Yerkes-Dodson curve: small deviations above optimal arousal incur large performance costs, so a tight control band is warranted. Tolerant subjects have a broad, flat curve: wider deviations are acceptable, and a tight threshold would trigger unnecessary interventions. A threshold calibrated for a sensitive subject will chronically over-trigger for a tolerant one, and vice versa — an interpretation directly supported by figure 5: in the most tolerant subjects, % time within band carries essentially no information about performance, whereas in the most sensitive it carries the strongest signal in the dataset. The baseline measures required for this classification (resting HRV and gamma power) can be extracted from a standard 10-minute calibration session already included in most BCI paradigms, with no additional experimental burden. There is a theoretical reason to expect this classification to be stable within an individual across sessions and tasks. Resting HRV is a trait-level property of cardiac autonomic regulation with high test-retest reliability over weeks [10], and resting gamma power reflects baseline cortical excitability that similarly shows within-subject consistency [11]. Neither measure is expected to fluctuate substantially with task familiarity or acute arousal. A one-time calibration could therefore define sensitivity group membership durably, though this should be confirmed empirically across sessions and paradigms. 

5.5 A complete framework for closed-loop neurofeedback or neurostimulation 

The components developed here form a coherent, technically specified, closed-loop pipeline. During a 10-minute calibration session, resting HRV and gamma power are extracted and used to classify the subject as arousal-sensitive or arousal-tolerant. Continuous peripheral physiological signals then drive the multimodal decoder in real time at 62.5 ms resolution, producing a normalized arousal index validated against the Yerkes-Dodson law. That index is compared against the personalized time-varying optimal trajectory derived during calibration, with bandwidth scaled to the subject’s sensitivity group ( ×1.5 SD for sensitive, ×2.5 SD for tolerant). When the trajectory exits the personalized band into the suboptimal region, feedback can be triggered to modulate the arousal signal at the exact moments our analyses identify as most closely coupled to performance decrement. The neurofeedback condition analysis in section 4.4 also suggests a specific advantage of tVNS over auditory feedback in this paradigm. Auditory neurofeedback appears to work by enabling targeted self-regulation at critical moments rather than producing sustained changes in arousal; this requires the subject to interpret and act on the feedback while simultaneously performing a high-workload task. tVNS bypasses this attentional bottleneck as a brief stimulation pulse produces measurable efferent autonomic effects within 2–5 s [12,13,21,22]. The dwell-time analysis in section 4.5.2 confirms that this timing is achievable: sustained above-band excursions persisted for a median of 6 s before band exit, with no excursions below 2 s after 13 excluding transient single-bin spikes, providing empirical grounding for the reactive architecture rather than relying solely on the tVNS latency literature. The two interventions are conceptually complementary, and a comparison between them within an otherwise identical closed-loop architecture would directly address whether the bottleneck in auditory feedback lies in the precision of self-regulation or in the latency of the physiological response. The prospective study would differ from Faller et al [5] on three dimensions: a more accurate and Yerkes-Dodson-valid arousal signal ( 93 .0% vs 79 .8% AUC), an intervention timed to personalized deviations from the optimal trajectory rather than to a fixed threshold, and a stimulation modality matched to the moment-targeted regulation hypothesis. A preregistered crossover design (active tVNS, sham tVNS, silence) with N ≈ 24 subjects provides conservative 80% power based on the Faller et al effect size and the expected variance reduction from personalization. 

5.6 Limitations 

The individualization analyses rest on a sample size that warrants direct acknowledgment. With N = 16 

subjects and a median split to define sensitivity groups, each group contains n = 8 individuals. The group-specific optimal multipliers ( ms = 1 .5, mt = 2 .5) were selected by grid search within each group, and the improvements in Cohen’s d are computed across the same n = 8 splits. The grid-searched multipliers optimize a noisy performance landscape with limited degrees of freedom and have not been validated on held-out data. The sensitivity classification itself, derived from baseline HRV and gamma power, has not been cross-validated as a predictor of the optimal multiplier in an independent sample. We present these individualization results as proof-of-concept findings motivating a properly powered prospective study with pre-specified sensitivity group cutoffs and multipliers, not as final validated thresholds. Two methodological choices in this pipeline are likewise pragmatic rather than optimized: dichotomizing subjects at the median discards graded information and creates a boundary that is unstable in a small sample, and several signal-processing parameters (the pNN35 cardiac feature, which departs from the more common pNN50, and the asymmetric filter time constants) were fixed a priori rather than tuned, so a continuous sensitivity model and a sensitivity analysis over these parameters are both warranted in future work. Independent replication in a cohort of at least N = 30 –40 subjects is required to confirm the stability of the HRV-gamma sensitivity score and the generalizability of the group-specific multipliers. The condition-equivalence finding (section 4.4) likewise should be interpreted carefully. A null result on cluster-based permutation tests does not rule out small sustained shifts; what it does rule out is the simple “feedback lowers mean arousal” hypothesis that would be expected if neurofeedback acted by global down-regulation. Targeted analyses of arousal dynamics around specific failure events are the natural follow-up, though the current dataset is underpowered to support them. The decoder was trained on a single task paradigm using ring size as an arousal surrogate; whether it generalizes to other arousal sources (sustained fatigue, emotional stressors, pharmacological interventions) is unknown. The Faller et al. dataset used EEG-based neurofeedback rather than the peripheral decoder signal evaluated here; the performance advantage of the peripheral decoder cannot be attributed to improved feedback quality in the present data, and a prospective study is required to establish that claim. 

# 6 Conclusion 

Peripheral physiological signals provide a more accurate and, more importantly, physiologically valid representation of cognitive arousal than EEG for a demanding sensorimotor task. The multimodal peripheral decoder achieves 93.0% within-subject AUC and uniquely recovers the Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U between decoded arousal and performance. Closed-loop auditory neurofeedback does not change the overall arousal 14 trajectory, suggesting that its known performance benefit reflects targeted regulation at critical moments rather than sustained arousal shifts — a reframing that motivates moment-targeted interventions such as tVNS. Deviations from a time-varying optimal arousal trajectory derived from control trials significantly predict per-trial performance, and individual differences in baseline HRV and gamma power modulate the shape of this curve, such that group-personalized control bands meaningfully improve trial-separation effect sizes over a uniform policy. Together, these components provide a complete, technically specified framework for individualized closed-loop arousal regulation, ready for prospective validation with personalized tVNS timed to the decoded arousal signal.
