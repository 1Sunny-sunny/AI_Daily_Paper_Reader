# Decoupling Time and Space: A Temporally Conditioned Refinement for EEG Source Imaging 

Marco Morik, Jesse Palarus, Carmen Vidaurre, Klaus-Robert M ¨ uller, and Shinichi Nakajima 

Abstract — Electroencephalography (EEG) offers millisec-ond temporal resolution, but inferring underlying neural sources is a severely ill-posed spatial inverse problem. While deep learning has advanced spatial reconstruction, current architectures face a critical dilemma: frame-by-frame models discard vital temporal context, whereas full 4D spatiotemporal networks introduce an architectural trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and inference cost. We propose a novel two-stream framework that explicitly decouples global temporal representation learning from per-time-point spatial refinement. A Transformer-based Tempo-ral Condition Encoder processes the entire EEG sequence via factorized spatiotemporal attention, retaining sensor-resolved features. A fixed inverse then maps these features into source-indexed conditioning for a per-timestep Source-Space Transformer or volumetric convolutional refiner. Ex-tensive evaluations on realistic synthetic data demonstrate that this temporal prior dramatically improves spatial local-ization, outperforming classical and spatiotemporal base-lines, particularly in high-noise and multi-source regimes. Training across diverse leadfields and explicit operator mismatches improves transfer to unseen head geometries and brings template-based reconstruction closer to subject-specific inversion. Furthermore, we apply the model trained only on synthetic EEG data to real-world EEG. A logistic regressor fit on source power differences in eyes-open, eyes-closed conditions successfully decodes age groups. 

Index Terms — Electroencephalography, inverse problems, signal reconstruction, deep learning, time series analysis

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Understanding human brain dynamics requires imaging modalities with high temporal resolution and reliable spatial inference [1], [2]. Electroencephalography (EEG) and magne-toencephalography (MEG) provide millisecond-scale measure-ments of neural activity, enabling the study of fast cognitive processes [3]–[9]. However, recovering the underlying neural sources from scalp sensor measurements remains fundamentally ill-posed [10]. In a typical clinical or research setting, the number of sensors 

M is considerably smaller than the number of candidate sources 

S whose activity is to be estimated. The forward model, yt =

Lx t + ϵt, where L is the lead field matrix, is therefore highly underdetermined [11]. The lead field varies substantially across subjects [12]–[14]. Accurate reconstruction depends on forward-model fidelity, noise, and the imposed priors. Classical distributed methods, such as minimum norm estimates (MNE) and eLORETA [15], introduce hand-crafted mathematical regularization (e.g., L2 norms or spatial smooth-ness) [16]. While computationally efficient, these approaches impose rigid assumptions that limit their ability to capture the diverse, sparse, and distributed patterns observed in true neural activity [17]. Deep learning methods provide a powerful data-driven alternative, learning complex priors from simulated data and directly mapping sensor measurements to source estimates [18]– [20]. Although these end-to-end approaches have demonstrated improved performance [21] with generic and personalized head models [22], they typically incorporate the biophysical forward model only implicitly through their training data. Transfer under changed head models and noise therefore requires explicit evaluation. Hybrid approaches combine physics-based initialization with neural refinement, for example by applying an eLORETA pseudo-inverse followed by volumetric convolutional networks [23]. While effective for spatial reconstruction, these methods process each time step independently, neglecting the highly autocorrelated nature of signals from biological neural networks. Spatiotemporal architectures offer different trade-offs between reconstruction accuracy, spatial representation, temporal con-text, and inference cost. Approaches such as recurrent networks [19] are fast, but accuracy is limited, while full spatiotemporal convolutions that explicitly attempt to model time in source space incur severe computational costs. The size of this 4D spatiotemporal volume forces compromises, limiting either arXiv:2610.06726v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 2 SUBMITTED TO IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON MEDICAL IMAGING 

the spatial resolution of the source space or the extent of the temporal context. To address these limitations, we propose a temporally conditioned framework (SoST+TC) that explicitly decouples global temporal modeling from spatial reconstruction. Our proposed method distills a compact representation of the global temporal context by learning to solve the biophysical inverse problem strictly on simulated data. This design enables the efficient integration of long-range temporal priors to steer volumetric source reconstruction, while simultaneously learning a representation of temporal brain dynamics. Furthermore, we show that robust training with varying leadfields improves transfer across subject head geometries. Our operator-mixing protocol explicitly incorporates mismatches between data-generating and inversion leadfields during training, enabling template-based reconstruction to approach the accuracy of subject-specific inversion on held-out head geometries.

II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

A. Classical Approaches to Source Localization 

Traditional approaches to the EEG inverse problem rely on mathematical regularization to constrain the infinite solution space [24]. Early parametric methods, such as equivalent current dipole (ECD) fitting, assume a small, a priori fixed number of active focal regions and optimize their locations and orientations [25]. While effective for simple evoked potentials, these methods struggle with distributed brain networks and often converge to local minima [10]. Distributed inverse solutions bypass the need to specify the number of sources by discretizing the brain volume or cortical surface and jointly estimating amplitudes across all locations. To obtain a unique solution, these methods introduce spatial or energy-based priors. Minimum Norm Estimates (MNE) [16] use an ℓ2-norm penalty to find the lowest-energy configuration and can favor superficial sources without depth weighting. Several extensions have been proposed to mitigate this limitation or improve localization properties, including dSPM [26], LORETA [27], sLORETA [28] and eLORETA [15]. When focal source estimates are desirable, sparsity can be promoted through ℓ1-norm regularization [5], [29]. Many widely used linear methods estimate source activity indepen-dently at each time point, failing to leverage the complex, long-range oscillatory dynamics inherent to biological neural networks [11]. Beamformers use the sensor covariance matrix across multiple observations to construct spatial filters [30], [31]. Some classical methods do include temporal information, e.g., TF-MxNE promotes sparse, non-stationary activity in the time-frequency domain [32], while Markov priors explicitly couple source activity over time [33]. Sparse Bayesian Learning (SBL) instead uses a hierarchical probabilistic model to estimate the source and noise hyperparameters from data [34], [35]. Spatiotemporal Bayesian formulations model temporal dependencies and structured noise [36], [37]. Thus, unlike fixed linear inverse operators, these methods adapt aspects of the inverse solution to the statistics of the measured data. 

B. Deep Learning and Physics-Informed Approaches 

Driven by the availability of realistic large-scale brain simulations [38], deep learning has emerged as a powerful alternative, replacing hand-crafted mathematical priors with data-driven priors. Early architectures focused on mapping instantaneous sensor topographies directly to 3D source spaces. For example, ConvDip [20] utilized convolutional neural networks to estimate distributed activity from single-timepoint EEG data, demonstrating improved robustness over classical methods. More recently, hybrid physics-informed architectures have bridged the gap between classical theory and deep learning. For the sparse setting, recent work augments iterative Bayesian inference by learning structured corrections to the underlying update procedure [39]. For the dense setting, approaches such as 3D-PIUNet [23] compute an initial, physics-based solution via an eLORETA pseudo-inverse and subsequently refine this volumetric estimate using a 3D CNN. These approaches anchor the network in the biophysics of the forward model. However, these spatial refiners operate frame-by-frame, discarding the rich temporal structure of the EEG signal. 

C. Spatiotemporal and Sequence Modeling 

To address the limitations of instantaneous inference, another line of work integrates temporal modeling. Recurrent neural networks support neuroimaging decoding [40] and model brain dynamics over time [19], [41]. Recently, transformer encoders have also been applied to EEG source reconstruction [42]. Closely related, SSINet combines spatial residual features and bidirectional LSTM features through Transformer-based fusion [43]. However, treating high-resolution source localiza-tion as a joint 4D mapping problem (3D space + 1D time) is computationally demanding. Therefore, these architectures offer different trade-offs between temporal context, spatial representation, and inference cost. Concurrently, within self-supervised learning (SSL), EEG foundation models such as ALFEE [44], NeurIPT [45], CEReBrO [46] and CBraMod [47] use factorized attention mechanisms, explicitly separating temporal self-attention from inter-channel spatial attention, to efficiently process long, multichannel recordings. While these models demonstrate that decoupling time and space is highly effective for sequence modeling, they are designed strictly for representation learning, not for inverting the ill-posed biophysical forward model. A. Factorized Temporal Condition Encoder 

The Temporal Condition Encoder ( gθ ) distills the full EEG recording into temporally aware, sensor-resolved features. First the EEG signal is normalized across all sensors and the full sequence. A shared 1D convolution with kernel length 16, stride 2, padding 7, and GELU embeds overlapping windows into hidden width H = 96 . For T = 512 , this yields P = 256 

patches and a token array of shape M × P × H.To preserve the biophysical topology of the recording, we inject 3D Cartesian spatial encodings via independent 1D sinusoidal encodings to the known (x, y, z ) coordinates of each electrode. This encodes electrode positions and permits coordinate-aware inputs [48]. Additionally, we use a 1D sinusoidal encoding depending on the time, providing each token with temporal information. To have uniform token counts within a batch, variable sensor/source counts are padded, masks exclude padded entries from attention, normalization statistics, and source losses. 

Factorized Spatiotemporal Attention: A Transformer [49] operating on the flattened M × P grid requires O(( M P )2)

pairwise interactions. Inspired by efficient video architectures [50] and EEG sequence models [46], we employ factorized attention. The core network consists of N = 8 transformer blocks that alternately perform self-attention over time and sensor dimensions, 4 blocks each, with each block having eight heads, feed-forward width 384 and dropout 0.1: 1) Time-Level Attention: Self-attention across the P tem-poral patches independently for each sensor captures sequence-wide context. 2) Sensor-Level Attention: Self-attention is applied across the M sensors independently for each temporal patch, al-lowing spatial integration of instantaneous topographies. This factorization reduces the attention interactions per block to O(P M 2 + P 2M ). After the final block, the patch-based representation is upsampled via a linear projection to match the original temporal resolution H → 2C, yielding the raw condition matrix c ∈ RM ×T ×C with output width C = 32 ,maintaining both the sensor and temporal dimensions of the input. 4 SUBMITTED TO IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON MEDICAL IMAGING

D. Biophysical Data Generation Pipeline 

Our source models are trained entirely on synthetic EEG. Therefore, the synthetic data-generating process defines the prior learned by the models, with the richness of the simulated training distribution determining its inductive bias. Selected source locations remain fixed within each trial, imposing a prior of temporally stable source support. The generation pipeline explicitly models spatiotemporal neural dynamics and samples a range of source extents, oscillatory time courses, noise levels, and head geometries. Each training sample ( Y ∈ RM ×T ) is generated through a four-stage process: 

1. Spatial Configuration: The number K of active source patches is sampled uniformly from {1, . . . , 20 }. Each Gaus-sian patch is centered at a uniformly sampled location on the discretized brain and has a spatial standard deviation 

σk ∼ U (5 , 40) mm. Its spatial weights have unit ℓ2 norm. A normalized isotropic Gaussian vector supplies one orientation per patch, yielding S(k) ∈ R3S .

2. Temporal Dynamics: To reflect canonical EEG rhythms, each source is assigned a band-limited time course sk ∈ RT

generated by forward-backward bandpass filtering Gaussian white noise and normalizing it to unit ℓ2 norm. The band is sampled uniformly from delta ( 0.5 – 4 Hz ), theta ( 4 – 8 Hz ), alpha ( 8 – 13 Hz ), mu ( 8 – 12 Hz ), and beta ( 12 – 30 Hz ). With probability 0.5, a time course is modulated by smooth Gaussian ERD/ERS-inspired amplitude envelopes [56]. The full source activity is X = PKk=1 S(k) ⊗ sk.

V. E XPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

In the following, we present our experimental results. We first evaluate synthetic reconstruction accuracy and the added cost of TC, then examine robustness, dependence on conditioning, and transfer across head-model pools. Finally, LEMON age-group decoding assesses downstream utility on real EEG. 

A. Qualitative and Quantitative Performance 

Source reconstruction must preserve both the spatial distri-bution of activity and its amplitude over time. The temporal trajectory in Figure 2 illustrates this aspect at one active patch center. SoST+TC follows the displayed ground-truth magnitude trajectory more closely than SoST. This example illustrates the potential of temporal context to stabilize reconstruction in the noisy, ill-posed inverse problem. To assess aggregate reconstruction accuracy and compu-tational cost, we provide quantitative analysis in Table II. SoST+TC outperforms the evaluated learned and classical baselines in reconstruction accuracy, at the cost of greater inference time. However, the cost of temporal conditioning is modest, on the surface source space, it reduces SoST’s NMAE by 21.7% while increasing measured inference latency by only 3.1%. Incorporating sequence context therefore yields a substantial accuracy improvement for a small additional cost over the unconditioned refiner. The volumetric experiments further distinguish the contribution of the reconstruction architecture from that of temporal conditioning. On the volume source space, the transformer architecture SoST performs comparably to the convolutional 3D-PIUNet [23]. Adding temporal conditioning also improves 3D-PIUNet, its benefit is therefore not restricted to the transformer architecture, although the improvement is greater for SoST. Together, these results support temporal conditioning as a useful addition to both evaluated reconstruction backbones, rather than an advantage specific to one architecture. Operator-mismatch training provides a further improvement for SoST+TC. Although introduced primarily to address head-model generalization (see Section V-D), it also benefits the reconstruction results summarized here. 

B. Robustness to Noise and Complex Source Settings 

A primary failure mode of EEG inverse solvers is the high susceptibility to sensor and environmental noise, as well as a rise in complexity depending on the number of simultaneously active sources. In Fig. 3, we evaluated reconstruction quality across Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNR) ranging from −10 dB 

(highly corrupted) to 15 dB (moderate noise), as well as the number of sources ranging from K = 1 to K = 10 .At an SNR of −10 dB , SoST+TC already detects some spatial structure. Across the evaluated noise levels, SoST+TC reduces reconstruction errors relative to its time-agnostic variant SoST. As source count increases, its spatial marginal NEMD rises, but remains below the compared baselines. It learns to disentangle spatially overlapping source contributions by exploiting their distinct temporal and oscillatory signatures. C. Role of conditioning vector 

To examine how the trained model uses conditioning, we intervene on its inputs for one held-out IBP2021 head. Figure 4 shows strong dependence on conditioning: zeroing the condition increases errors more than zeroing the initial source estimation branch. Note that spatial NEMD is invariant to permuting prediction time indices, and temporal NEMD measures total activation over time, not source-specific timing. We observe that time-shuffled conditioning primarily increases temporal NEMD, the little change in spatial NEMD shows that spatial structures are indeed reconstructed, however at the wrong points in time. Overall, this demonstrates that SoST+TC strongly relies on the time-step conditioning vector for source reconstruction. 

D. Head Model Generalization 

We next examine whether reconstruction accuracy is main-tained when the head geometries encountered at inference differ from those used during training. Figure 5 distinguishes two aspects of this problem: the coverage of head geometries in the training pool, and the consistency between the data-generating leadfield and the leadfield used to construct the inverse operator. We compare SoST+TC trained only on LEMON geometries, only on IBP2021 geometries, or on both pools, and evaluate each model using subject-specific and template inverses. Training on one geometry pool leads to worse performance on other unseen head models, while combined training reduces cross-dataset transfer loss. Although pooled-model errors remain higher than those of the corresponding within-pool specialists, they are substantially lower than the errors obtained by applying a specialist to the other dataset’s heads. Pooling therefore reduces the dependence on a single training geometry pool while retaining good reconstruction accuracy on held-out heads from both datasets. E. Real-World Age Decoding on LEMON 

Neuroimaging-based brain-age estimates are being investi-gated as biomarkers of brain health [63]. Age-related EEG differences span multiple frequency bands. Previous work [64] reported age differences across delta, theta, alpha, and beta, together with preserved overall eyes-open/eyes-closed reactivity but more focal topographic changes in older adults. Source-space analyses have also reported age-related shifts in the spatial distribution of resting power [65]. These observations motivate examining spatially resolved reactivity across multiple frequency ranges rather than focusing exclusively on alpha power. Resting-state electrophysiology has also been used for age prediction. In a benchmark that included LEMON, Engemann et al. [66] pooled eyes-open (EO) and eyes-closed (EC) recordings before feature extraction and identified their relationship as a question for further investigation in brain-age modeling. Here, we evaluate whether source-space EO-EC reactivity supports age-group decoding (20-35 versus 55-80 years) in LEMON [53]. Paired recordings and required metadata yielded 115 training participants. For testing, we selected the ten participants (six younger, four older) whose geometries were excluded from synthetic source-model training. Features were unadjusted EO-EC differences in log 10 subject-mean power, with 516 template-source features per band. Thus, reactivity represents the logarithm of the EO-to-EC power ratio rather than absolute power in either condition. Welch power used Hann windows of up to two seconds, 50% overlap, and summed orientation-component powers. L2-regularized logistic regression used class balancing, training-only feature standardization, and a 0.5 older-class probability threshold. Figure 6A shows spatially heterogeneous age contrasts, with both positive and negative effects whose distribution and magnitude depend on the reconstruction method. The maps describe age differences in state-dependent modulation, not simply reduced or increased resting activity. Compared with eLORETA and SoST, SoST+TC displays a different spatial balance of these effects, including a prominent bilateral negative-effect pattern. The frequency profile of age decoding also depends on the representation (Fig. 6B). Scalp-EEG features perform best in beta, whereas eLORETA features perform best in theta. SoST+TC achieved 100% balanced accuracy for theta, alpha, and total 4-35 Hz power, and 75% for beta. Relative to SoST, adding the TC component improves performance in theta, alpha, and total power on this test set, but not in beta, where SoST and scalp EEG perform better. Thus, the result is not a uniform advantage across frequency bands, rather, the source representation changes which spectral features are most useful for this decoding task. These findings demonstrate that source models trained on synthetic EEG can provide discriminative features for real recordings with unseen geometries. The spatial maps offer candidate patterns for follow-up investigation of age-associated reactivity. The ten-participant test set provides limited precision for estimating generalization performance, and the effect-size maps are descriptive. Larger, independently evaluated samples are needed to establish reproducible spatial signatures or broad decoding advantages. Neither these maps nor subject-mean power decoding directly validates anatomical localization or temporal stability. VI. D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

In this work, we decouple global temporal representation learning from spatial refinement and arrive at a spatiotemporal model of EEG. Extensive empirical evaluations reveal that temporal conditioning improves source imaging performance relative to the unconditioned refiners, including noisy and multi-source conditions. This indicates that when instantaneous sensor topographies are ambiguous, global temporal context provides complementary information that helps to stabilize the biophysical inversion. Projecting the temporal condition into source space via L† prior to concatenation retains an anatomically indexed conditioning signal. While the temporal conditioning also improves the convolutional 3D-PIUNet back-bone, our proposed SoST transformer supports both volumetric and surface-based sources, providing extra flexibility. The head-model experiments identify training-operator diver-sity as a second important component of robust reconstruction. Mixed-pool training reduces the loss associated with applying a single-dataset specialist to another geometry pool, but broader geometry coverage alone does not eliminate sensitivity to the assumed inverse. Operator mixing addresses this additional mismatch by exposing the model to differences between the data-generating and inversion leadfields during training. Consequently, template-based reconstruction approaches the accuracy of subject-specific inversion on the evaluated held-out heads, while reconstruction with subject-specific inverses also improves. This suggests that synthetic training should represent not only the expected head geometries, but also uncertainty in the operator available at application time. These findings reduce dependence on a subject-specific inverse within the tested settings, without establishing unrestricted generalization to arbitrary montages or leadfields. The ten-participant LEMON holdout evaluates a comple-mentary aspect of utility. Source-power features from a model trained exclusively on synthetic EEG support age-group dis-crimination using a simple classifier trained on real participants. This provides evidence of downstream usefulness, rather than a general claim of classification superiority. The principal objective of this framework is not to replace task-specific sensor-space classifiers, but to support the analysis of spatially resolved activity over time. Temporal conditioning should reduce spurious reconstruction fluctuations while preserving the emergence, modulation, and disappearance of sources. While our proposed SoST+TC shines in diverse synthetic settings, the gap between simulation and biological reality remains the primary limitation. The simulator uses simplified Gaussian patches, band-limited time courses, and additive noise. Future steps of this framework could incorporate more biolog-ically realistic priors, such as non-linear neural mass models [19], structured physiological noise, and source covariances derived from resting-state fMRI connectivity [67]. Nevertheless, increasing simulation complexity does not by itself establish biological validity. A temporally coherent reconstruction can still be biased when the underlying activity differs from the learned priors. Further evaluation should therefore distinguish noise suppression from attenuation of brief or rapidly changing sources, and assess reproducibility across recordings rather than treating temporal smoothness as evidence of accuracy. In conclusion, decoupled temporal conditioning improves source reconstruction with a modest incremental inference cost over the corresponding unconditioned refiner in our measurements. Together with operator-mismatch training, it provides a framework for combining temporal context with physics-informed spatial estimation. Its contribution extends beyond downstream prediction toward investigating spatially resolved source dynamics within relevant time windows.
