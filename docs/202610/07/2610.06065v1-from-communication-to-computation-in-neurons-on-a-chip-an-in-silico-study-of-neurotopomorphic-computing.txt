# Highlights 

• Neurons-on-a-chip networks produce distinct communication phenotypes. • Networks scoring highest on the new IC 3 framework were among the poorest decoders. • Simple feedforward chains outperformed densely connected neuronal networks. • Results motivate neurotopomorphic computing through engineered network organisation. 

# Summary 

Living neuronal networks transform inputs through recurrent cellular and population dynamics, yet it is unknown which network architecture supports which computation. Neurons-on-a-chip turn this question into a design problem because microchannels guide axonal growth and set the network architecture. We introduce IC 3, an Integrated Characterisation of Communication-Driven Computation, which characterizes network state through neuronal dynamics, functional communication, and structural support. We implemented nine architectures in silico as conductance-based spiking networks and tested each on frequency decoding, temporal-order discrimination, and fading memory. Predominantly feedforward circuits decoded best. Se-quential Chain and Microchannel Diode had the lowest IC 3 and recruited a third of reachable neurons, yet achieved the two highest scores on both classification tasks. Across architectures, higher IC 3 went with lower classification scores. We term this new direction neurotopomorphic computing , in which the physical organisation of neuronal connectivity is engineered as part of the computing substrate. 

# 1 Introduction 

Living neuronal networks can transform structured stimulation into population responses that support task-relevant readouts [2, 3]. Closed-loop interaction can further modify neuronal activ-ity during task performance, and structured input can reorganise their collective dynamics [1, 2]. 1arXiv:2610.06065v1 [q-bio.NC] 5 Oct 2026 With a trained readout attached, cultured networks have separated input patterns and, under feedback control, generated target temporal signals [3, 4]. Two questions remain open [5, 7, 32]: how does neuronal architecture organise the communication state expressed by a neuronal net-work, and how does that state relate to the computational transformations accessible to a readout? In neurons-on-a-chip, the physical layout of the circuit is a design variable for its network. Cell placement, axonal growth, stimulation, and electrophysiological recording can all be con-trolled within the same culture system [5, 6]. Because these platforms can incorporate human-derived cells, they are also used to study disease mechanisms, neurotoxicity and therapeutic response [5, 6]. The question is whether different cultures with the same neuronal layout pro-duce similar responses to the same stimulation [9, 15]. Activity has to follow the intended routes and still carry usable information when it reaches downstream neurons. Microfabrication sets the distances, bottlenecks, directional pathways and recurrent loops along which this may be engineer to happen [7, 8, 15]. Several fabrication strategies control neuron-on-a-chip properties at different spatial scales, and each alters the resulting network dynamics [14, 15]. Adhesive patterning can confine small neuronal populations to isolated microislands, and topographical tracks and valleys constrain neurite growth and organise collective activity [10, 14]. Compartmentalised microfluidic devices separate neuronal somata into defined modules and connect them through axon-guiding mi-crochannels, so that modular circuits can be assembled and recorded on microelectrode arrays [7, 9, 11, 15]. Channel geometry can also impose directionality, e.g. asymmetric microchannels and microtunnels bias axonal growth, and circular and pseudo-feedforward couplings create di-rectional communication between modules [8, 12, 16]. More recently, microscale scaffolds and single-cell printing have enabled reproducible human neuronal circuits with defined cell place-ment [13]. We represent the engineered neuronal circuits using nine candidate network topologies, or ar-chitectures. Network architecture can be controlled experimentally in neurons-on-a-chip [7, 15]. However, comparing different architectures in vitro requires fabricating each design and matur-ing separate cultures. Biological variation between cultures can then be difficult to separate from the effects of topology as well as be resource-intensive. We therefore compared the ar-chitectures in silico , where topology can be changed with the neuronal model and stimulation fixed. Synthetic biology uses the same simulation-informed methodology when candidate cir-cuits are compared computationally before construction [41]. Each of the nine architectures has an experimental or theoretical precedent. All-to-All is the fully connected reference [28]. Sparse and Directed Random are directed Erdős–Rényi ensembles with connection probabilities of 0.10 and 0.12 [40]. Star routes all traffic through one hub, as in hub-centred micropatterns [29]. Sequential Chain links populations one after another, as directed microchannels do in vitro 

[7]. Microchannel Diode adds weak reverse access to the chain, after axonal-diode designs that bias neurite growth in one direction [7]. Clustered Ring couples dense modules in a cycle, fol-lowing engineered modular circuits with controlled intermodule coupling [16, 39]. Small-World and Scale-Free reproduce two organisations reported in cultures: local clustering with a few long-range shortcuts [30], and a heavy-tailed degree distribution with a few hubs [31]. Choosing between them needs measurements that link the imposed design to the neuronal state it pro-duces and to what that state can compute. Structural connectivity specifies where signals could travel but not that neurons after many hops will be activated by the initial input signal [15, 31]. Transfer entropy (TE) usually estimates directed predictive information transfer between pairs of spike trains. However, TE does not represent the population states or structural con-2straints within which that transfer occurs [25]. Task performance is a joint test of the input, the activity it evokes, the features extracted from that activity and the readout. Architecture, activity and task output are therefore three levels of one engineered system, and each needs its own characterization [15, 18, 25]. A useful design framework must connect these levels without treating network characterization as equivalent to computational performance [7, 15, 18, 32]. Our previous work treats neuronal circuits as communication systems that can be engineered to compute. We first studied engineered neuron-astrocyte signalling and synthetic neuronal logic circuits as information-processing systems [33, 34]. We then extended this direction towards reservoir computing and sequential neuronal biocomputation, examining how neuronal dynam-ics and circuit organization shape computational behaviour [35, 37]. Our more recent work found that communication and computation differ with topology in bioengineered networks, and pro-posed neurons-on-a-chip as a platform for engineering biological intelligence by design [36, 38]. Loeffler et al. have since shown in living cultures that microengineered architecture changes both electrophysiological dynamics and decoding performance [32]. Here we introduce IC 3, an Integrated Characterisation of Communication-Driven Computa-tion, to link the state a network enters under stimulation to what it can compute. IC 3 splits that state into three coupled domains. Neuronal dynamics covers the activity states the network gen-erates, functional communication the information exchanged as activity propagates, and struc-tural support the architecture that constrains both. For neurons-on-a-chip we integrate IC 3 with eight network observables: spike entropy, effective dimensionality, inter-spike-interval variability, burst-like and regular-spiking fractions, mutual information, TE and a graph-structure. We apply IC 3 in four steps. We first measure how architecture and stimulation frequency change IC 3 and TE, and how stable the IC 3 ranking is to its weights. We then compare graph reachability with the neurons that actually respond, and path delay with response latency. From response probability, firing rate, latency, timing variability, information transfer and spike expenditure we build each network’s communication phenotype . Finally, we test every network on close-frequency decoding, temporal-order discrimination, and fading memory. Across nine architectures and 20 independently seeded network instances per architecture, topology produced distinct communication states and task-dependent computational profiles. Sequential Chain and Microchannel Diode had the lowest IC 3 yet produced the two highest mean scores for frequency decoding and temporal-order discrimination, and fading-memory per-formance remained near baseline in every architecture. Across architecture means, higher IC 3

and higher TE were associated with lower classification scores ( ρ between −0.73 and −0.81 ). These results motivate what we term neurotopomorphic computing , in which the physical wiring of a neuronal circuit is designed alongside its stimulation, dynamics and readout. A candidate circuit can then be screened in simulation, for the communication state it produces and for whether that state supports the intended computation, before it is fabricated.

Three stochastic trials were performed for each architecture–network instance. A neuron was structurally reachable when a directed path connected it to the stimulated source. 2.3 Neuronal architectures differ in recruitment, timing and reliability 

For each independent network, we characterized its communication phenotype by measuring how strongly and reliably activity propagated through the circuit. We quantified this using the 90th-percentile neuronal firing rate, post-stimulus response probability, downstream latency, latency jitter, inter-spike-interval (ISI) variability, and spike expenditure across the matched input sweep (Figure 4). All six communication measures varied with architecture and stimulation frequency. The multivariate profiles revealed architecture-specific patterns of functional communication (Figure 4a,b,d). Small-World networks had the highest mean 90th-percentile rate (120.3 Hz) and the greatest standardized spike expenditure. Star networks combined high post-stimulus response probability (0.780) with the shortest mean downstream latency (11.55 ms). All-to-All networks reached the highest response probability (0.823) at 13.09 ms. Sequential Chain and Microchannel Diode networks used less activity but had lower response probabilities (0.327 and 0.292). Sequential Chain networks also had the longest mean latency (15.52 ms). No architecture simultaneously maximized firing activity, recruitment, temporal precision, and activity efficiency. We quantified each downstream neuron’s network centrality using out-closeness, which mea-sures how directly that neuron can reach other nodes through outgoing paths. After adjustment for hop distance and architecture, greater centrality was associated with lower post-stimulus response probability (95% CI −0.473 to −0.067 ; Figure 4c). Centrality was not associated with firing rate (95% CI −9.37 to 26.82). Figure 4b,d shows the resulting design constraint. Across the nine architectures, a gain in one communication measure came with a loss in another, so architecture has to be chosen together with input frequency and the phenotype a device needs. A topology that maximizes propagation may be unsuitable when latency or spike expenditure is the limiting device constraint.

Cultured neuronal networks can exhibit transient fading memory on subsecond timescales [26], and repeated stimulation can induce plastic changes that establish stimulus-specific memory traces [27]. The present result characterises untrained fading-memory processing. The relationship between network characterization and computation differed between architecture-level and within-architecture analyses. At the architecture level, IC 3 showed negative rank associations with both supported classification tasks. This relationship was clearest for temporal-order discrimination ( ρ = −0.767 ), while frequency decoding showed a similar negative ordering ( ρ = −0.728 ). TE showed comparable negative associations with frequency decoding ( ρ = −0.812 ) and temporal-order discrimination ( ρ = −0.767 ). Fading memory showed little association with either IC 3 or TE. Within architectures, the relationship between IC 3 and computation remained task-specific. Temporal-order discrimination decreased as IC 3 increased ( βW = −1.567 ), supporting the same negative relationship observed across architecture means. Frequency decoding showed a much weaker within-architecture association ( βW = −0.260 ). Expanding the readout beyond the directly stimulated neurons did not improve decoding in any tested architecture–task combination (Figure 5c). Rewiring effects were also architecture and task-specific. Small-World networks favoured the rewired configuration for frequency decoding (∆P = −0.237 ), whereas Directed Random ( ∆P = 0 .157 ) and Small-World ( ∆P = 0 .363 )favoured their native configurations for temporal-order discrimination. Because rewiring also changes the network instance and, for some architectures, geometry and propagation delays, the contrast is interpreted at the level of the complete network configuration. 

# 3 Discussion 

Widespread network engagement did not coincide with the strongest computational performance in this study. All-to-All, Small-World, and Scale-Free networks occupied the upper end of the IC 3 range, whereas Sequential Chain and Microchannel Diode produced much lower IC 3 values. Yet the latter two architectures achieved the highest mean scores on both supported classification tasks. They also recruited only a fraction of the neurons that were structurally reachable from the stimulated source. This suggests that, in engineered neuronal systems, spreading activity across more of the network does not necessarily improve computation. Preserving input distinctions as signals move through the circuit may be more important. The communication phenotype provides a functional description of how network architecture changes the response expressed under stimulation. Architecture shapes which neurons respond, when they respond, how consistent their timing is, how strongly they fire, and how much activ-ity the response produces. These properties describe the neuronal state available to a readout without assuming that the communication phenotype itself mediates task performance. 11 neurotopomorphic computing, the aim is to relate an engineered network form to the communi-cation state it produces and then ask which computational functions remain accessible under a defined readout. The same view separates IC 3, TE and task performance. TE captures directed predictive information transfer between neuron pairs. By contrast, IC 3 describes a broader network state that also includes neuronal dynamics and structural support. Neither quantity increased with computational performance, and both were negatively rank-associated with the two classification tasks. Temporal-order discrimination decreased with increasing IC 3 at both the architecture and within-architecture levels, while frequency decoding showed a weaker within-architecture relationship. A network can therefore express a high-IC 3 state without making a particular task easier to solve. This distinction is consistent with reservoir-computing studies in which computational performance emerges from the interaction between input encoding, recurrent dynamics, and readout [18–20], and with work on spiking neuromorphic networks, in which capacity peaked at criticality but only complex tasks benefited [43]. IC 3 therefore characterises the state a network generates, and we do not use it to predict task performance. The name refers to the three domains IC 3 integrates. Task performance tests whether that state contains the distinctions a computation requires. The readout analysis shows that extending the feature set from the directly stimulated neu-rons to first-hop neurons or the full network produced no supported improvement in decoding, despite the additional neurons contributing measurable activity. A larger feature set may contain more activity without containing more task-relevant variation, particularly when downstream responses are correlated with one another or with signals already present near the stimulation sites. It may also introduce variability that increases the apparent complexity of the represen-tation without improving the stability of the decision boundary used by the readout. For the feature representation and readout used in our study, additional network activity did not provide additional information that improved held-out prediction. One possibility is that downstream responses become increasingly redundant with information already present close to the stimu-lation sites. Another is that propagation transforms task-relevant distinctions into forms that are less accessible to the chosen decoder. Our simulations suggest that information can remain present in a network while becoming less useful for a particular computation. We did not test the redundancy explanation directly. We hypothesise that restricting how signals spread preserves the input distinctions these two tasks require. Sequential Chain and Microchannel Diode both constrain propagation pre-dominantly in one direction. In living cultures, unidirectional axon routing between modules improved reservoir performance over bidirectional channels [7]. Across the nine architectures, more connectivity, greater recruitment, and stronger overall network activity did not consistently produce better task performance. However, our design does not isolate directionality. The two architectures also differ from the others in four respects that may contribute: i) path redun-dancy, ii) geometry, iii) propagation delay and iv) recurrent coupling. The circular embedding of the Microchannel Diode also placed the second temporal-order stimulation site at a different chain position from the Sequential Chain, so Chain–Diode differences cannot be attributed to directionality alone. In an in vitro circuit, the connectivity that neurotopomorphic computing treats as a design variable is realised through five physical properties, namely directionality, compartmentalisation, path redundancy, path length and spatial routing, and each can alter how activity propagates. These properties can be patterned experimentally with cell source, stimulation and readout protocols. Communication and computation can then be compared across physically distinct 12 circuits with everything else matched. No architecture produced fading memory. Performance stayed close to baseline in all nine over lags of 25–200 ms ( η2 = 0 .029 ), although the same architectures separated close-frequency and temporal-order inputs strongly. Topology changed how current input propagated but had no measurable effect on memory of earlier input. We define input fidelity as the preservation of current-input distinctions at the readout, and persistence as the recovery of past input from the current state (held-out R2 at lags of 25–200 ms). We hypothesise that the two place different demands on recurrence, and none of the tested architectures achieved both. The null result is specific to the present network, synaptic, stimulation and readout configuration. Cultured neuronal networks do hold short-term memory and stimulus-specific traces under other protocols [26, 27]. Structural reachability set where activity could travel but did not determine which neurons were recruited. Response probability declined with hop distance on average, and downstream latency grew faster than physical path delay. After adjustment for hop distance and architecture, neurons with higher out-closeness responded to fewer stimulus onsets, so a structurally favourable position did not make a neuron a stronger functional relay. We argue that the design target for neurons-on-a-chip is the transformation between stimu-lation and readout. Temporal-order discrimination, close-frequency decoding and memory place different demands on the measured neuronal response [15, 32]. On a device, these demands map onto coupled variables that include microfluidic directionality, compartmentalisation, recurrent coupling, path length, stimulation protocol and readout location. Several can be fabricated before culture, so candidate network forms can be compared before a biological architecture is chosen. Neuromorphic and reservoir-computing studies already treat topology as a design variable [19, 20]. Neurotopomorphic computing differs in its substrate, since the topology is physically patterned in living neurons. Circuits with different directional, modular and spatial organisations can be fabricated and then recorded through the same electrophysiological inter-face. The resulting architecture–communication–performance maps could identify topological motifs worth testing in silicon, hybrid or other neuromorphic substrates. We propose that neuronal circuits designed for computation be measured at three levels: architecture, which sets the physical possibilities; the communication phenotype, which records how neuronal activity expresses them; and task performance, which shows which part of that activity a readout can use. Simulation allowed all three levels to be measured under matched stimulation and readout before fabrication, subject to the layout confounds noted in the Lim-itations. Patterned neuronal cultures are the next test, since biological variability, maturation and plasticity could each change how one level maps onto the next. 

Limitations of the study 

Each circuit contained 15 Izhikevich neurons with fixed synaptic weights, and learning was con-fined to the readout. Short-term plasticity was evaluated only as a sensitivity analysis. Neuronal positions were geometric surrogates generated by force-directed and ring layouts, so edge lengths and delays follow from the layout rule, not from a fabricated device. The Microchannel Diode carries a circular embedding although its adjacency is a chain, so its path lengths and delays differ from those of the Sequential Chain. IC 3 theory 

IC 3 defines the Integrated Characterisation of Communication-Driven Computation. Its defini-tion does not assume a particular neuronal model or measurement platform, and here we test one implementation. Let a neuronal system be S = ( G, Θ) , where G is its physical or effective architecture and Θ contains its cellular and coupling parameters. Under input u and stochastic realization ξ, the system produces a spatiotemporal neuronal state trajectory 

X = Φ( G, Θ, u, ξ ). (34) Three coupled domains are extracted from this trajectory and its architecture:

Computational tasks and readouts 

We evaluated three complementary computational properties of each neuronal architecture: dis-crimination of nearby input frequencies, discrimination of temporal order, and retention of recent input history. Frequency decoding tests whether small differences in periodic input generate sep-arable population states. Temporal-order discrimination tests whether the network preserves the sequence of spatially distinct inputs. Fading memory measures how much information about earlier inputs remains in the current neuronal state. Network dynamics and model parameters remained fixed throughout these assays and learning was confined to readouts. Close-frequency decoding and fading-memory stimulation were applied to the architecture-specific source neuron defined in Network architecture. Close-frequency decoding used four stimulation classes at 8.5, 10.0, 11.5, and 13.0 Hz to cre-ate a narrow-band temporal discrimination problem around the operating regime used in the main simulations. Each class contained five trials per network instance. Stimulation strength was fixed at Istim = 6 across the principal simulations, providing a common input operating point for comparisons across architectures and stimulation conditions. Each trajectory lasted 1200 ms. Population activity was represented by dividing each trajectory into eight equal tem-poral windows and concatenating spike counts from all 15 neurons. Temporal-order discrimination tested whether the same network could distinguish the se-quence of two spatially separated inputs. Equal pulse amplitude and duration kept the stim-ulation energy matched between classes, while the 75–125 ms inter-pulse interval allowed the network state to evolve between two inputs. The first pulse occurred between 150 and 250 ms, and five trials per class were generated for each network instance over 2000 ms trajectories. Pop-ulation activity was encoded using the same eight-window spike-count representation used for frequency decoding. For both classification tasks, neuronal features were aligned across independently seeded networks according to functional role. The stimulated source was assigned the first position. In the temporal-order task, stimulation sites A and B were kept in their designated positions, 22 while the remaining neurons were ordered by directed distance and node identity. Classification used L2-regularised multinomial logistic regression. Feature standardisation and selection of C ∈{0.1, 1, 10 } were performed within the training data of each outer leave-one-network-instance-out fold using five-fold grouped cross-validation. For fading memory, each network received a binary telegraph input on a 10 ms grid with switching probability 0.15 and Istim = 6 . Three 2000 ms trajectories were generated per network instance. The neuronal state was represented by per-neuron spike counts over the preceding 20 ms and sampled every 10 ms after a 500 ms washout period. Ridge-regression readouts re-constructed input states 25, 50, 100, and 200 ms in the past. The regularisation parameter 

λ ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1, 1, 10 , 100 } was selected by grouped inner cross-validation. Held-out R2 equals zero when the readout predicts only the mean input. As a reference for the autocorrelation of the telegraph input, we also computed the R2 obtained by predicting each lagged input from the current input state alone. Simulation design and robustness Stimulation and replication. The main dynamics sweep used a 50%-duty unipolar square-wave input with Istim = 6 at 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 and 40 Hz. Each trajectory lasted 2000 ms and three stochastic trials were generated for every condition. The 0.5- and 1-Hz conditions contained approximately one and two stimulation cycles, respectively, and are interpreted as finite-window operating points. Structure–function analysis used the 5 Hz condition, while communication-phenotype analysis used 2, 5, 10 and 20 Hz. These analyses reused the corresponding dynamics-sweep trajectories. 23 Input and readout assignment. The architecture-specific source s defined above was used as the stimulation site for the single-input assays. For Star, Sequential Chain, and Microchannel Diode networks, this corresponded to node 0; for the remaining architectures, the source was selected independently for each network instance using Equation (16). In the temporal-order task, stimulation site A was the architecture-specific source and site B was the physically most distant neuron from A in the embedded circuit. No dedicated output neuron was imposed. Structure–function analyses treated all downstream neurons except the stimulated source as potential targets. For the computational assays, the full-network readout used the activity of all 15 neurons. Restricted H0 and H0+H1 readouts were used only to quantify the contribution of the directly stimulated and first-hop populations.

Quantification and statistical analysis 

Independent network instances were treated as the unit of replication, with stochastic trials averaged within each instance and condition. Effects of architecture, stimulation frequency, and their interaction were assessed using Gaussian linear models with CR1 cluster-robust covariance by network instance. Overall effects were evaluated using cluster-Wald tests, with Benjamini– Hochberg correction applied where multiple related endpoints were tested. Task-specific architecture effects were evaluated across the nine architectures and summa-rized using η2. Comparisons between nested readouts and between native and rewired networks used paired analyses across matched network instances with confidence intervals where applica-ble. Associations between IC 3, TE, and task performance were analysed separately at architec-ture and within-architecture levels. Architecture-level analyses used architecture means with confidence intervals and exact permutation tests. All tests were two-sided unless stated other-wise.
