ABSTRACT 

BACKGROUND Automated neonatal EEG analysis can support continuous monitoring where specialist expertise is limited. Existing event detectors typically classify short EEG segments, restricting temporal context and limiting detection resolution. Methods are also commonly developed separately for individual event types. 

OBJECTIVES To develop a general framework that treats neonatal EEG event detection as a semantic segmentation task, improving temporal localisation, detection accuracy, and computational efficiency across seizures, low-voltage intervals (LVIs), and artefacts. 

METHODS We developed U-NeXt, a convolutional encoder–decoder network that directly segments individual EEG channels, using longer EEG segments to preserve temporal context. A shared architectural design and training framework were developed using LVI, artefact, and seizure datasets from 51, 98, and 278 neonates, respectively, comprising over 11,000 hours of EEG and 82,000 annotated events. Performance was compared with task-specific ConvNeXt classifiers. For seizure detection, we also evaluated a model trained on an expanded dataset, U-NeXt+. Both U-NeXt variants and the ConvNeXt baseline were evaluated for seizure detection on two independent held-out datasets from Cork and Helsinki, comprising 130 neonates, with recordings annotated by three experts. 

RESULTS U-NeXt improved detection across all three event types, with particular gains in sensitivity for short events. The U-NeXt+ variant achieved Matthews correlation coefficients of 0.862 and 0.891 on Helsinki and Cork, respectively, and improved hourly seizure burden correlations by 30–40% relative to ConvNeXt. U-NeXt+ met the criterion for expert-level performance on both datasets ( p< 0.001). In separate computational benchmarks, U-NeXt reduced batch inference time relative to ConvNeXt by 254-fold on CPU and 200-fold on GPU. 

CONCLUSION A common segmentation framework improves detection across diverse neonatal EEG event types while substantially reducing inference time. Accurate seizure burden estimates and performance comparable to expert reviewers support its potential use in continuous EEG monitoring and clinical decision support. arXiv:2610.32811v1 [cs.LG] 23 Oct 2026 U-NeXt: neonatal EEG segmentation Research article 

# 1 Introduction 

Neonatal brain injury in the hours and days after birth remains a serious concern for the estimated 8.5 per 1000 infants born globally with neonatal encephalopathy (NE)[1]. The negative impacts of NE on neurodevel-opmental outcome make it the second leading neurological disorder contributing to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [2]. Early diagnosis and treatment are paramount to improving lifelong neurodevelopmental outcome. Continuous monitoring with electroencephalography (EEG) is the gold standard for the assessment of neonatal brain function. It is a relatively inexpensive and portable technology, but interpretation difficulty has limited widespread adoption. Neonatal EEG review is a highly specialised skill that is absent in many clinical settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where the burden of NE is particularly high [3]. In recent years there has been increasing interest in automated systems to reduce laborious expert EEG review in the NICU [4–7]. To date, most research in this area has focused on models for a single event detection task, often trained and evaluated on a single dataset [10–12]. This leads to highly specialised models that are tuned for that particular task–dataset combination and often use a specific electrode array or montage [13]. Datasets are often small, with performance evaluated through cross-validation, seldom including independent held-out validation [10, 11]. Insufficient evaluation means generalisation performance is unclear at best. At worst, methods overfit to particular task–dataset pairings, obscuring potential downstream utility and hindering clinical translation. In addition, each neonatal EEG event type requires its own study, making it difficult to build robust and generalisable methods. EEG event detection commonly uses short-time classification, assigning one label per segment (e.g. 16 s for seizures). Post-processing then assembles these detections into a probability trace and binary mask. This approach has several limitations. First, the time resolution of the prediction mask is dependent on the length of the segments. Overlap percentage controls the sample rate, but not the resolution, although a high overlap is needed to capture the start and end times. A longer segment may make the event easier to detect but harder to localise in time [14]. Selecting a segment size is therefore a compromise for events with widely varying durations, e.g. artefacts [12, 15]. Second, short-time classification lacks the continuous temporal context experts use to annotate event boundaries. Treating variable-duration event detection as fixed-segment classification rather than segmentation therefore limits both context and temporal localisation.

In this work, we address these limitations by introducing a new general framework for event detection in neonatal EEG. We develop a new model based on a U-Net style architecture which directly learns the semantic segmentation task for 3 distinct applications: detection of seizures, low-voltage intervals (LVIs), and artefacts. Importantly, we do not customise the architecture or training recipe for each case and the design is based on single-channel input. This ensures that the framework is generalisable to other event types, independent of EEG montage. We trained and validated a model for each task using the development datasets, then evaluated seizure detection on the held-out datasets Cork and Helsinki. Training a high-quality detector for this diverse set of tasks with a single unified architecture is a significant challenge but an important step towards more general and scalable approaches to AI for neonatal EEG. The history of AI development has repeatedly shown that detailed customisation of models to each task may yield short-term gains but will ultimately be shown to be more brittle than the general systems that inevitably surpass them. 

# 2 Methods 

We adapted our state-of-the-art 1D ConvNeXt seizure classifier [8] for segmentation using a U-Net encoder, bottleneck, and decoder with encoder–decoder skip connections. The model width and depth can be adjusted with two parameters to suit the dataset size. We apply the same model and optimiser hyperparameters to three event detection tasks, namely seizure, artefact, and LVI detection. Event durations and signal morphologies in all three tasks vary considerably. Seizures annotated in this study are characterised by repetitive EEG patterns that evolve over the event duration [16]. They can be represented as harmonic signals with slowly varying instantaneous frequency, amplitude modulation, and morphology. Event duration can range from 10 s to many minutes, with a median duration of approximately 2 minutes [17]. LVIs are events that are ≥ 2 s with peak-to-peak amplitude <25 

μV. Artefacts are the most heterogeneous group, reflecting their varied sources. They include regular patterns, such as periodic respiration artefact, and stochastic activity, such as muscle artefacts resembling band-limited noise. Artefact durations range from <1 s to hours, complicating short-time classification. Typical segment sizes for classification are from 8 to 32 s for the seizure task [8, 18], 2 s for the LVI task [9], and between 4 and 64 s [12, 15] for artefact. For all three tasks, 256 s input segments accommodate varied event durations and let the model learn from longer temporal context, similar to human review. 

2.1 U-NeXt architecture 

Our proposed U-NeXt architecture is a U-Net model based on the ConvNeXt backbone described in [8]. U-NeXt uses the ConvNeXt encoder without its head (Figure 1) and with downsampling now 4× between all stages, rather than 4× after the stem and 2× thereafter [8]. We then add a lightweight decoder stage for upsampling. Two integers control the architecture: D sets the blocks per stage (depth), and W sets each block’s feature dimension (width). In this work, we use 3 variants (see Table 1): Nano ( D = 1 , W = 1 ), Medium ( D = 3 , W = 4 ), and XL ( D = 6 , W = 10 ). Each decoder stage comprises an upsampling layer, concatenation with an encoder skip connection, and a DeConvNeXt block based on a simplified ConvNeXt block (see Figure 1). Note also that the decoder scales with W but does not scale with D. More complex decoders, such as mirrored ConvNeXt block structures or additional DeConvNeXt blocks, increased parameters and FLOPs without reliable performance gains. Predicting annotation masks at the 64 Hz input resolution exceeds annotation precision and risks overfitting. To address this, we made 3 modifications: 1. increased the downsampling between encoder stages, 2. reduced the number of decoder stages, 3. used deep supervision of the outputs of both decoder stages. These changes limited the maximum output resolution to 4 Hz, stabilised learning, and yielded good generali-sation performance.

2.2 Tasks and datasets 

We test this method on 3 tasks: detection of seizures, LVIs, and artefacts. These events cover a wide range of durations, amplitudes, frequency content, and characteristic morphologies, providing a broad assessment of neonatal EEG event detection. Good performance across these diverse tasks suggests that the framework may also be suitable for other event types. For model development, we used EEG records from 329 term neonates in the fully anonymised NeoBase neonatal EEG database. EEG recordings in NeoBase were acquired during research studies at Cork University Maternity Hospital (CUMH), Ireland and University College London Hospital (UCLH), UK. During the original research studies, ethical approval was obtained from the relevant clinical research ethics committee and informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians for the use of fully anonymised data in future research studies. In addition, ethical approval for secondary use of these datasets was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Cork Teaching Hospitals in Ireland. EEG recordings began as soon as possible after birth and lasted hours or days using several different EEG acquisition machines (see [8] for details). EEGs were recorded from neonates with mixed aetiologies who were at risk of seizures in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) as well as a smaller group of healthy term newborns recorded in the postnatal wards ( ≤ 2 hours in recording duration) [10, 19–21]. This EEG data was used to create 3 task-specific development datasets (see Table 2). Two of the datasets used, seizure and LVI, have been previously described in [8] and [9], respectively; the artefact dataset was created for this work. Multi-channel EEG was reviewed by a neonatal neurophysiologist 1 in each case, and events were annotated on a per-channel basis. For seizures, the entire recording was reviewed. For LVI and artefact, epochs of 1 and 3 hours, respectively, were reviewed. In the case of artefact, only the 2 central channels (C4–CZ, CZ–C3) were annotated with binary labels (artefact/no artefact). Compared to [8], we have expanded the seizure development data with long-duration, fully anonymised EEGs from a further 76 neonates recorded at UCLH, annotated per channel by a single neurophysiologist. We refer to this additional cohort as "Seizure+" (see Table 2). Combining the original Seizure dataset ( n=202) with Seizure+ gives 278 neonates, 10,893 hours of multi-channel EEG, and 24,432 per-channel seizure events. U-NeXt+ was trained on this combined dataset. For direct comparison with our previous ConvNeXt model, we also trained a U-NeXt model using the original 202-neonate dataset. For seizure evaluation, we used EEG recordings from 130 neonates in the same two held-out datasets as in [8], referred to throughout as Helsinki and Cork. Helsinki is the publicly available dataset ( n=79; 112 hours; 18 channels) [18]. Cork is a subset of NeoBase ( n=51; 1,572 hours; 8 channels) recorded at CUMH. Both datasets have global seizure annotations from 3 independent reviewers. Neither the EEG data nor the reviewers overlap with those used for seizure development. 

2.3 Pre-processing and data augmentation 

Pre-processing of the EEG consisted of bandpass filtering within the 0.3–30 Hz passband and downsampling to 64 Hz. EEG was divided into 300 s segments with no overlap. During training, a random crop of 256 s was taken at each iteration. The training data was further dynamically augmented with flipping, magnitude jitter, and cutout [22]. Augmentation hyperparameters were fixed across datasets. We split each development dataset by neonate into training and validation sets. For the seizure dataset we used a single 80:20 split. 2.4 Post-processing 

In [8] we tried to use a minimal approach to post-processing, but recovering a high-resolution prediction from classification of 16 s segments required >98% segment overlap and smoothing with a 32 s window. We argued that this step may improve performance when tuned to a particular benchmark dataset or metric, but will in most cases impair performance on another dataset or metric. With U-NeXt the direct output of a 4 Hz prediction mask (compared to 1/16 Hz in [8]) removes the need for overlap and reduces the importance of smoothing. Keeping a 16 s smoothing window provided limited benefit for seizure detection but we dropped it for the other models. We also removed the filling of short gaps between events. The only remaining post-processing step is to remove predictions shorter than the minimum event durations ( < 10 s for seizures; 

< 2 s for LVIs; no filter for artefacts). The LVI ConvNeXt baseline retained 1 s rectangular smoothing, filling of gaps shorter than 1 s, and removal of events shorter than 1 s [9]. Without the need for inference overlap, each second of EEG passes through the model only once. Combined with a more efficient forward pass (Table 1), this substantially speeds inference (Section 3).

# 3 Results 

3.1 Development set results 

U-NeXt outperforms ConvNeXt on all three tasks (Figure 2). For LVI, U-NeXt achieves a higher pooled MCC (0.821 vs 0.799), indicating better overall detection performance, although ConvNeXt performs better on some individual metrics. Notably, each ConvNeXt baseline was designed with task-specific input lengths, hyperparameters, augmentation, and even minor architectural adaptations such as kernel sizes. U-NeXt used almost the same configuration across datasets to maintain generalisability. The superior performance across all event types and lack of customisation make it likely that the U-NeXt framework will generalise well to other event types of interest. Furthermore, the performance differences are most clear when there is larger headroom above the ConvNeXt models. As described in [9] the ConvNeXt model already does exceptionally well at detecting LVIs so there is relatively little room for improvement. As the task complexity increases, however, the results demonstrate that U-NeXt is the more flexible and capable architecture in these regimes. An example output illustrates the nature of the improvement over ConvNeXt (Figure 3(a)). ConvNeXt detects most seizures but produces a noisier trace and often misses event boundaries due to limited temporal context and output resolution. U-NeXt segments with broader EEG context, producing more coherent probabilities and accurate boundaries that better resemble human annotations. Figure 3(b) shows higher sample sensitivity across event durations, with larger relative gains for shorter events; performance is comparable for the shortest LVIs. U-NeXt improves substantially upon the previous state-of-the-art ConvNeXt model across a diverse set of metrics. Notably, we see large improvements in the estimation of seizure burden, with a 30–40% relative improvement over ConvNeXt in the correlation between the predicted and true seizure burden. We also report seizure burden mean absolute error (MAE), the mean absolute difference between predicted and reference burden. For U-NeXt+ on Cork, this is <10 s/h for neonates with seizures and approximately 3 s/h for those without. The event-aware segmentation of U-NeXt versus the short-segment classification approach of ConvNeXt as discussed in Section 3.1 can give some explanation for this improvement. This improvement is clinically important because seizure burden is associated with neurodevelopmental outcome [25] and initial treatment response [26]. The additional training data for U-NeXt+ led to substantial improvements over U-NeXt on Cork, but smaller gains on Helsinki. We suspect that this reflects Helsinki’s shorter total recording duration (112 hours) and these models approaching its performance ceiling. Further improvements may be difficult to assess with the existing 3 reviewers but there might still be value in comparing to a more robust consensus formed by a larger group of reviewers.

We also tested for expert-level equivalence. There have been several published methods for such a test that are comprehensively described and evaluated in [27]. Here we found U-NeXt+ to be equivalent to experts on 2 variants of the most reliable test which measures the impact of replacing an expert with the AI on the level of inter-rater agreement, ∆κ (see Table 3). In the first test, if ∆κ is statistically consistent with 0, then the model is deemed to be expert-level i.e. failing to reject the null hypothesis that the model is equivalent (U-NeXt+ p = 0 .368 for Helsinki and p = 0 .470 for Cork; expert equivalent). A legitimate criticism is that failing to detect a difference does not establish equivalence. Alternatively, the same ∆κ distribution supports a non-inferiority test with a margin set by the margin of error in inter-rater agreement. In this framing, rejecting the null hypothesis of model inferiority supports expert equivalence (U-NeXt+ p < 0.001 for both Helsinki and Cork; expert equivalent). In practical terms the former test is a constraint on the upper CI of the ∆κ

distribution whereas the latter constrains the lower CI. 

3.3 Clinical significance of false detections 

One metric that is particularly salient to end users is the number of false detections. This is commonly reported as the hourly number of distinct prediction events with no overlap with a true event (FD/h in Table 3). This is clinically relevant to alarm disruption, unnecessary specialist reviews, and treatment decision-support reliability. However, FD/h is sensitive to small model changes and post-processing details. We can illustrate this by considering the case of a prediction that is improved to contain less false-positive time overall but fragments one false detection into several. Figure 4(a) shows this for a real case of a false positive on Cork where the FD/h was tripled despite a reduction in total false-positive time when moving from ConvNeXt to U-NeXt. Clearly the U-NeXt prediction is preferable but selecting for FD/h only would be misleading. The focus on FD/h therefore incentivises post-processing techniques which result in long contiguous events e.g. adding a prediction "collar" [11, 24, 28]. This however will necessarily introduce systematic bias in seizure burden estimation. In [8] we argued for minimal post-processing and we further improved that here (see Sec. 2.4) because we wanted to avoid such biases and keep the models as generalisable as possible. Figure 5(b) shows that longer segments give modest performance improvements, likely because they provide more temporal context, further supporting the move away from short-segment classification. A deployed model should therefore retain some recent EEG context, which will reintroduce some overlap and increase computational cost. 

3.5 Reduced montage detection 

Helsinki and Cork use 18- and 8-channel montages, respectively, whereas long-duration neonatal clinical monitoring commonly uses reduced channel montages such as 2 channels for amplitude-integrated EEG (aEEG) systems. As was the case for ConvNeXt, the per-channel training lets U-NeXt accommodate different montages. In Figure 6 we show the performance of U-NeXt+ on Cork with different reduced channel config-urations. As expected, we see some degradation in sensitivity due to localised seizures occurring outside the retained channels, but even with just two central channels, the model retains more than 80% of the eight-channel event sensitivity. Notably, overall MCC (Figure 6(c)) remains remarkably stable because the increase in PPV offsets the decrease in sensitivity. This analysis suggests that the model can be usefully deployed in reduced channel settings with the important caveat that focal seizures outside the retained channels can be missed.

# 4 Discussion 

We have introduced U-NeXt, a new general-purpose event detector for neonatal EEG. It improves upon our previous ConvNeXt models, which are state of the art in both LVI [9] and seizure detection [8], along several axes. First, it comprehensively outperforms the ConvNeXt model on 3 different tasks: LVI, artefact, and seizure detection. It does this while also being far more general, without the need to tune the architecture, hyperparameters, or post-processing to a particular task. It sets a new state-of-the-art result for neonatal seizure detection on Helsinki, achieves expert-level performance on both Helsinki and Cork, and produces high-fidelity estimates of key clinical markers like seizure burden [25, 26]. Finally, we demonstrate a significantly more computationally efficient model with ∼250 × speedup vs ConvNeXt models for batch inference. Much of the literature has focused on treating event detection as a classification problem through short-time analysis. By contrast, experts perform semantic segmentation by annotating event start and end times in continuous EEG. Rather than discard this detail, we use these sample annotations directly as a rich training signal. The resulting model detection masks are much more human-like than those produced by previous approaches with smooth, coherent detections and accurate start and end times. This also improved detection sensitivity across event types, including short events that are challenging for experts and automated systems alike [31]. As in our previous work, we use per-channel modelling, which, although costly, improves training-signal quality and prediction flexibility. We demonstrate that with just 2 of 8 channels, U-NeXt retained remarkably stable MCC and more than 80% of the eight-channel event sensitivity, although focal seizures outside the retained channels could be missed. We also explored the practical clinical implications of reported false detections. Although important and of great interest to clinicians, FD/h can misrepresent performance and clinical impact. False detections should not be equated with false alarms: FD/h can overestimate the false-alarm rate by a factor of 2 or more. We also highlighted that it should always be reported in the context of inter-rater false detections, which we found to be comparable to U-NeXt on Cork but twice as large on Helsinki. Finally, when used in the context of decision support based on seizure burden, U-NeXt had remarkably good performance with a sensitivity/specificity of 86.7%/99.2% at a threshold of 12 min/h on Cork. This suggests that U-NeXt could be a valuable adjunct decision support tool for cotside caregivers when EEG expertise is unavailable or delayed. This study is not without limitations. Despite its improved generality, the framework still requires a separate model for each task. Disjoint annotations and differences in dataset scale complicate joint training. Training one large model, for example for seizure detection, then fine-tuning for other tasks may be feasible, but was not explored here. Another area for improvement would be testing with additional event types to further

assess the framework’s generality. We selected LVI, artefact, and seizure detection for their clinical utility and diverse durations and morphologies, although many event types remain untested. The artefact dataset itself has events that vary widely in duration and morphology, ranging from brief transients to sustained periodic patterns and irregular, noise-like activity. It is promising, therefore, that this architecture would also work well on other event types. These results demonstrate the value of end-to-end learning, generality, and scale. Removing hand-crafted features, overly customised post-processing, and task-specific model adaptations can produce a high-performance, general-purpose event detector design. This can then adapt quickly to new tasks without architectural redesign.
