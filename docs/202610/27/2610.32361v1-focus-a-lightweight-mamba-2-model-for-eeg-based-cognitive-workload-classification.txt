Abstract —Cognitive workload estimation from physiological signals is increasingly relevant in high-risk environments, where continuous and unobtrusive monitoring can inform adaptive systems and safety interventions. This paper presents FOCUS (Frequency-band Optimized Cognitive-load Ultralight SSM), a compact Mamba-2-based model for EEG-based binary workload classification under constrained data conditions. Rather than learn-ing frequency sensitivity from data, FOCUS encodes preprocessed multi-channel EEG using a fixed FFT decomposition that covers the five canonical EEG bands. The proposed model combines depthwise-separable convolution and a weight-shared encoder-bottleneck Mamba-2 pair, with 0.04 M parameters and 0.11 GFLOPs. Evaluated in a pilot study with 11 participants under leave-one-subject-out cross-validation across three cognitively distinct tasks, FOCUS achieves 74.75% accuracy on the full dataset at 0.08 ms GPU model-forward latency. In the weight-sharing ablation, the shared configuration achieves 4.55 percentage points higher accuracy on the full dataset than the non-shared variant while reducing the SSM parameter budget; however, this difference does not reach statistical significance under a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test ( p = 0 .083 ). Overall, the selected FOCUS configuration provides a favorable trade-off between classification performance and computational cost within this controlled pilot setting. 

Index Terms —cognitive fatigue, mental workload, physiological signals, brain activity, electroencephalogram 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Cognitive workload refers to the mental effort working memory requires to perform a task. In high-risk domains such as aviation, automotive systems, robotics, and automated in-dustries, real-time cognitive workload estimation has important implications for safety and performance. Detecting when a person may need support due to increased cognitive workload is also relevant beyond professional environments, such as health care and education. For instance, early identification of elevated cognitive workload may enable timely assistance [1] [2]. Work-load assessment methods are generally divided into subjective and objective approaches [3]. Subjective tools, such as the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX), are well validated but can only be applied after task completion, preventing real-time monitoring [4]. Objective approaches leverage the relationship between mental effort and autonomic or cortical nervous system activity, allowing continuous estimation through physiological sensors [5]. Common physiological modalities for cognitive workload estimation include electroencephalography (EEG), electrocardiography (ECG), electrodermal activity (EDA), and respiratory signals. Different physiological and neuroimaging signals capture distinct aspects of cognitive demand, reflecting its multidimensional nature [6]. The cognitive workload liter-ature has explored a broad range of modality configurations, from focused single-signal approaches to predefined multimodal combinations, each demonstrating value across different tasks and deployment contexts [5], [7]–[9]. A significant portion of multimodal architectures in this space employs modality-specific feature extraction and explicit fusion mechanisms, such as intra-modality encoders, dual-branch networks, or decision-level modules, which are typically designed around a fixed sensor configuration and optimized for specific input settings [10]–[12]. Among these physiological modalities, EEG has received particular attention because it directly measures cortical ac-tivity and captures rapid changes associated with cognitive engagement [13]. EEG-based workload studies often examine arXiv:2610.32361v1 [cs.AI] 23 Oct 2026 oscillatory activity because alpha and theta rhythms have been associated with cognitive and memory processes [14]. These characteristics make EEG relevant for monitoring workload in applied domains where excessive cognitive demand may affect safety and performance, including aviation, driving, and human-machine interaction [15]. However, EEG-based workload assessment remains challenging because workload-related neural patterns can vary across individuals, tasks, and experimental contexts, making generalization across task conditions an important issue [16]. This pilot study followed a controlled experimental protocol involving 11 participants who completed three cognitive tasks: a fluid intelligence reasoning task, a numerical processing task, and an interactive video game. Each task was administered at two difficulty levels, easy and hard, in order to induce different levels of cognitive workload. Continuous EEG recordings were acquired during task performance to capture workload-related neural activity across both conditions. In addition, this study proposes Frequency-band Optimized Cognitive-load Ultralight SSM (FOCUS), a compact Mamba-2-based sequence model designed for high efficiency and low model-side computational cost in adaptive systems and user-support applications. II. R ELATED WORK 

The EEG modality has been studied extensively for cognitive workload estimation, with CNN and LSTM architectures among the most common for extracting spatial and temporal represen-tations from raw or preprocessed signals [17]. However, more complex network designs have shown improved performance [18], and systematic reviews suggest that, in general, deep learning approaches outperform feature engineering pipelines [19]. Furthermore, Transformer and Conformer architectures have also been applied to EEG-based workload estimation, achieving competitive results at low computational cost [20], [21]. Beyond general engineering and human–machine interaction contexts, EEG-based cognitive workload monitoring has gained particular relevance in health-related settings, where cognitive overload and fatigue may affect clinical performance and patient safety. In the surgical domain, prefrontal EEG beta activity has been shown to differentiate workload levels imposed by surgical procedures of varying complexity, suggesting that EEG-based indices can provide objective, nonintrusive measures of mental overload in surgeons and may support monitoring of surgeons and residents during training [22]. More recent work has extended this line of research to robot-assisted surgery, proposing machine learning frameworks that estimate cognitive workload from EEG epochs recorded across multiple cortical regions, including the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital areas [23]. The relationship between cognitive fatigue and medical error has also been examined in simulation-based medical education, where mobile EEG has been proposed as an objective tool for assessing cognitive fatigue in medical trainees during simulated clinical work [24]. Outside the surgical context, EEG-based passive brain–computer interfaces have been explored for adaptive learning systems, where real-time cognitive load estimation can dynamically adjust the pace of information presentation [25]. In cognitive training contexts, wearable EEG setups have demonstrated feasibility for tracking workload during N-back tasks, suggesting potential relevance for evaluating cognitive training approaches, including future clinical or rehabilitation-oriented applications [26]. Collectively, these studies indicate that EEG-based workload estimation is not merely a performance optimization tool but a potentially useful measurement modality for adaptive health informatics, medical training, and cognitive monitoring systems. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

This section describes the data acquisition and pre-processing pipeline as well as the proposed FOCUS architecture for EEG-based cognitive workload classification. 

A. Data Collection 

This study uses the EEG dataset described in the study [21]. The dataset comprises eleven participants (6 male and 5 female; ages 20–40 years; mean age 25 ±5.5). None reported neurological disorders or use of substances affecting the nervous system on the day of the experiment. All participants provided written informed consent, and the Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol. Participants performed three cognitively diverse tasks: an abstract reasoning task (IQ, Raven’s Progressive Matrices), a numerical problem-solving task (MATH), and an interactive video game task (GAME, Flappy Bird). Each task was presented at two prede-fined difficulty levels (Easy and Hard), providing controlled experimental conditions designed to induce differences in cognitive demand. The validity of these conditions has been previously examined using the same task protocol. Analysis of NASA-TLX responses showed significant differences between the Easy and Hard conditions for the Raven and Numerical tasks [6], while an EEG-based mixed-model ANOVA showed significant differences between the Easy and Hard conditions for the Numerical and Game tasks [27]. These complementary subjective and EEG-based analyses provide prior evidence supporting the experimental difficulty manipulation. Continuous EEG was recorded using the EMOTIV EPOC wireless headset with C = 14 measurement channels (AF3, F7, F3, FC5, T7, P7, O1, O2, P8, T8, FC6, F4, F8, AF4) at a sampling frequency of fs = 128 Hz following the international 10–20 system. Each participant contributed 12 samples per difficulty level per task, yielding N = 264 samples per task (EASY = 132, HARD = 132). Consequently, each held-out LOSO fold is balanced between the EASY and HARD classes, containing 12 samples per class for each individual task and 36 samples per class in the pooled ALL setting. Each task-difficulty condition lasted 2 min and was divided into twelve non-overlapping 10 s samples; thus, one sample corresponds to a complete 10 s, 14-channel EEG input with T = 1280 time points. 

B. Pre-processing 

EEG signals were pre-processed offline using EEGLAB in MATLAB. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was ap-plied to remove eye-blink and muscular artifacts. Components Fig. 1: Overview of the proposed pipeline. associated with non-neural activity were visually inspected and excluded, producing clean 10 s samples with T = 1280 time points. The offline ICA artifact-removal stage is not included in the reported model-forward latency.

D2: Depthwise-separable temporal convolution (substantially fewer parameters than a standard convolution). 

D3: Weight sharing between the encoder and bottleneck Mamba-2 blocks (halves SSM parameters).

D. Training Configuration 

All models are optimized with AdamW ( η = 10 −4, weight decay λ = 0 .05 ) under a cosine annealing learning-rate schedule with 20 warm-up and 10 cool-down epochs over 190 total training epochs. A batch size of 32 and label smoothing of 0.10 

were utilized during training. Data augmentation is applied per channel during training: polarity inversion, additive Gaussian noise, and temporal masking (window size 15 − 30 % of the sequence) are each applied with probability drawn uniformly from [0 .01 , 0.10] . For each LOSO fold, the model is initialized and trained from scratch on 10 participants, while the remaining participant is held out for evaluation. The reported result for each fold corresponds to the maximum accuracy achieved on the held-out participant over the fixed 190-epoch training horizon, and final performance is computed as the mean across all 11 folds. IV. E XPERIMENTAL EVALUATION & R ESULTS 

This section presents experiments evaluating EEG signals for cognitive workload assessment using the LOSO evaluation protocol across 11 participants and three cognitive tasks: IQ, MATH, and GAME. The primary objective is to identify the FOCUS configuration that provides the best overall trade-off between classification performance and computational cost. We systematically varied four design decisions: weight sharing between SSM blocks (D3), convolution type (D2), model width d (D4), and SSM state dimension dstate (D4) to assess their individual contribution to classification performance and computational cost. Binary labels correspond to the predefined easy and hard task-difficulty conditions and do not represent direct subjective workload measurements. Results in Table I are reported separately for IQ, MATH, and GAME, as well as for ALL, which denotes a separate LOSO experiment on the full dataset containing samples from all three tasks. For ALL, a single model is trained in each LOSO fold using the full training-participant data, and task identity is not provided as an input to the model. ALL is therefore not an arithmetic average of the three task-specific results. Results are reported as mean ± std over 11 LOSO folds. Accuracy is computed as the overall proportion of correctly classified samples, while precision and F1-score are macro-averaged across the EASY and HARD classes, assigning equal weight to both classes. For the ALL setting, each architectural variant was compared with A1 using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 11 paired participant-level accuracies ( α = 0 .05 ), with rank-biserial correlation reported as the effect-size measure. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied. 

Weight sharing (D3). The baseline A1 (weight-shared encoder and bottleneck blocks) is compared with A2, which trains two independent Mamba-2 blocks. Removing weight sharing increases the parameter count from 0.04 M to 0.07 M, while GFLOPs remain at 0.11. The effect on per-task accuracy is uneven: A2 performs better on IQ (71.97% vs 67.42%), whereas A1 performs better on MATH (75.76% vs 69.70%), with similar GAME accuracy (76.14% vs 76.89%). For the ALL setting, A1 achieves 74.75% accuracy compared with 70.20% for A2, a descriptive difference of 4.55 percentage points. However, the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test does not reach statistical significance ( W = 12 .5, p = 0 .083 ), with a rank-biserial correlation of r = 0 .62 . Numbers of parameters in Table I are reported in millions and rounded to two decimal places. 

Depthwise-separable convolution (D2). Replacing the depthwise-separable convolution with a standard Conv1d (A3) raises the parameter count from 0.04 M to 0.05 M and adds 0.02 GFLOPs. Accuracy differences across the three tasks are small descriptively: IQ increases from 67.42% to 69.70%, MATH decreases from 75.76% to 75.00%, and GAME is unchanged. The ALL accuracy difference between A1 and A3 is 0.13 percentage points and is not statistically significant (W = 17 .0, p = 0.888 , rank-biserial r = 0.06 ). The depthwise-separable factorization is therefore retained for its 2.9 × reduction in convolutional parameters, from 12,288 to 4,288, rather than on the basis of an established accuracy equivalence. 

Model width (D4 — d). Three widths are compared: d =32 (A4), d = 64 (A1), and d = 128 (A5). Reducing the width to d = 32 decreases ALL accuracy from 74.75% to 69.07%, a descriptive difference of 5.68 percentage points; however, this difference does not reach statistical significance (W = 10 .0, p = 0 .075 , rank-biserial r = 0 .64 ). Increasing the width to d = 128 yields 75.00% ALL accuracy, only 0.25 percentage points above A1, with no statistically significant difference ( W = 32 .0, p = 0 .966 , rank-biserial r = −0.03 ), while increasing the parameter count from 0.04 M to 0.14 M and GFLOPs from 0.11 to 0.40. We therefore retain d = 64 

based on the observed cost–performance tradeoff. 

SSM state dimension (D4 — dstate ). Halving dstate from 16 to 8 (A6) yields an ALL accuracy of 70.45% compared with 74.75% for A1, a descriptive difference of 4.30 percentage points. This difference does not reach statistical significance (W = 14 .0, p = 0.102 , rank-biserial r = 0.58 ). Lower accuracy is also observed for MATH (70.08% vs 75.76%) and GAME (69.32% vs 76.14%). Thus, although the results suggest sensitivity to the SSM state dimension in the present configuration, they do not establish a statistically significant performance difference or a specific temporal mechanism. 

Final configuration. We adopt A1 as the final FOCUS architecture, with d = 64 , dstate = 16 , depthwise-separable convolution, and weight-shared encoder and bottleneck blocks. A1 achieves 74.75% ALL accuracy with 0.04 M parameters, 0.11 GFLOPs, and a model-forward latency of 0.08 ms per sample. Although A5 ( d = 128 ) achieves a slightly higher ALL accuracy of 75.00%, the difference relative to A1 is only 0.25 percentage points and is not statistically significant ( W = 32 .0,

p = 0 .966 , rank-biserial r = −0.03 ), while A5 requires 0.14 M parameters and 0.40 GFLOPs. A1 is therefore selected based on the cost–performance tradeoff. V. C OMPARISON WITH EXISTING WORK 

Table II compares FOCUS with three published references, EEGNet [30], 1BT [21], and the multimodal framework proposed in [31], as well as with a plain Mamba-2 baseline evaluated in the present study. EEGNet and 1BT provide CNN-and Transformer-based EEG reference architectures, respec-tively, while the framework in [31] employs five modalities, namely ECG, EDA, RESP, SpO 2, and EEG. The plain Mamba-2 baseline provides a direct state-space-model comparison for assessing the overall benefit of the proposed FOCUS design. The published EEGNet and 1BT results were obtained on the same EEG data under the same subject-independent LOSO protocol, while the multimodal framework employs the same participants and task protocol with the additional peripheral modalities. Plain Mamba-2 and FOCUS were evaluated in the present study. Following the convention established in [21], the mean score represents the unweighted average across all three tasks and all three evaluation metrics. FOCUS achieves the highest accuracy on MATH and GAME among the evaluated models. On MATH, FOCUS reaches 75.76% accuracy, compared with 67.42% for plain Mamba-2, 67.05% for 1BT, 64.39% for the multimodal framework, and 63.64% for EEGNet. On GAME, FOCUS reaches 76.14% accuracy, compared with 70.83% for the multimodal framework, 69.70% for 1BT, 68.56% for Mamba-2, and 64.39% for EEGNet. IQ shows a different pattern, with the multimodal framework achieving the highest accuracy of 72.35%, followed by 1BT at 69.32%, Mamba-2 at 68.56%, FOCUS at 67.42%, and EEGNet at 65.15%. Thus, the advantage of FOCUS is primarily observed on MATH and GAME, while the multimodal framework achieves the highest performance on IQ. In the pooled ALL setting, FOCUS achieves 74.75% accuracy, compared with 62.88% for the multimodal framework, with a measured GPU model-forward latency of 0.08 ms per sample for FOCUS. The five models exhibit different cost–performance profiles. EEGNet is the most computationally compact, with 0.01 M parameters and 0.02 GFLOPs, but has the lowest mean performance of 64.47%. 1BT achieves a mean score of 69.37% with 0.45 M parameters and 0.02 GFLOPs, while the multimodal framework reaches 69.77% with 5.61 M parameters and 0.36 GFLOPs. Plain Mamba-2 achieves 68.78% with 0.06 M parameters and 0.14 GFLOPs, whereas FOCUS achieves the highest mean score of 73.57% with 0.04 M parameters and 0.11 GFLOPs. Compared with plain Mamba-2, FOCUS has a 4.79 percentage-point higher mean score while using approximately 33% fewer parameters and 21% fewer GFLOPs. Compared with 1BT, FOCUS has a 4.20 percentage-point higher mean score while using approximately 11 × fewer parameters, although 1BT has lower GFLOPs. Additionally, compared with the multimodal framework, FOCUS achieves a 3.80 percentage-point higher mean score while requiring substantially fewer parameters and GFLOPs. These results indicate that FOCUS provides a favorable trade-off between predictive performance and computational cost rather than minimizing computational cost alone. VI. D ISCUSSION & C ONCLUSION

The selected A1 configuration has a measured GPU model-forward latency of 0.08 ms per sample. The study has two main limitations: the dataset comprises 

11 participants recorded in a single controlled laboratory environment, and the generalisability of the findings to clinical or operational settings with different EEG headsets or longer recording sessions has not been tested. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as pilot evidence of the feasibility and computational efficiency of the proposed architecture rather than as evidence of broad population-level generalization. The binary targets represent controlled easy/hard task-difficulty conditions used to induce differences in cognitive demand; therefore, the reported results should not be interpreted as direct estimation of subjective workload. The present study does not isolate the contribution of the fixed FFT decomposition relative to alternative EEG front-ends. Accordingly, the FFT stage should be interpreted as a parameter-free, physiologically motivated design choice rather than as an empirically estab-lished improvement over alternative representations. Future work should directly compare the fixed FFT decomposition with time-domain EEG without fixed band decomposition, learned temporal filters, wavelet-based representations, and learnable filterbanks, and evaluate FOCUS on larger and more demographically diverse cohorts. The present study intentionally focuses on EEG-only modeling to isolate the efficiency of the proposed architecture. Extending FOCUS to multimodal physiological sensing is a relevant direction for future work, particularly for social and companion robots in educational or healthcare settings [32], where recognition accuracy, model size, and inference time are all critical for practical deployment [33].
