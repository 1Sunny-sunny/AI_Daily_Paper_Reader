# Abstract 

Auditory attention decoding (AAD) seeks to identify the attended talker from brief noninvasive brain record-ings by comparing neural responses with competing speech streams. Recent electroencephalography (EEG) decoders report nearly perfect accuracy from one sec-ond of data, yet we show that this performance fails to generalize to unseen speech because models exploit trial leakage, eye gaze, and acoustic cues instead of speech evoked neural activity. We introduce Couple-Flow , which decodes attention through temporal cou-pling between neural and acoustic embeddings. Its predictions remain at chance when the brain signal contains no informative coupling, preventing shortcuts based on speech content or talker identity. A condi-tional flow matching head implements a likelihood ratio decision rule based on pointwise mutual information between brain and speech. This rule is Bayes opti-mal when the recording depends only on the attended talker and removes preferences for speech that is likely a priori. We evaluate CoupleFlow with content dis-joint splits recovered from the audio and null tests that distinguish genuine temporal coupling from trial recognition. Across nine EEG and magnetoencephalog-raphy (MEG) datasets processed through a common pipeline, spanning four languages, high density EEG recordings, listeners with hearing impairment, and a room with multiple talkers, CoupleFlow outperforms linear and deep baselines while all null tests remain at chance. Finally, we derive an evidence accumulation law that predicts accuracy across window lengths from a single parameter per dataset and predicts that pa-rameter from the strength of neural speech tracking. This law identifies recording signal to noise ratio as a limit on decoding speed and reliability and estimates the window length required to reach a target accuracy. 

# 1 Introduction 

When several people talk at once, a listener naturally isolates one voice. The auditory cortex reflects this selection: low-frequency cortical activity tracks the at-tended talker’s speech envelope far more strongly than ignored speech. (Ding and Simon, 2012; Mesgarani and Chang, 2012; O’Sullivan et al., 2015). Auditory attention decoding (AAD) asks whether the attended talker can be identified from this activity recorded non-invasively, with electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG). EEG is promising because it offers millisecond time scale resolution and works with a few electrodes near the ear (Mirkovic et al., 2015; Fiedler et al., 2017). This makes AAD a candidate for neuro-steered hearing devices that am-plify the attended talker (Geirnaert et al., 2021), since hearing-impaired listeners track the attended talker more strongly than the competing one, as normal-hearing listeners do (Petersen et al., 2017; Decruy et al., 2020; Fuglsang et al., 2020). Building such a device is beyond the scope of this paper, but we study the decoding problem in terms of its ability to general-ize to unheard speech using limited data from unseen subjects, solely from brain activity. Using a few seconds of multichannel neural data and the speech of K candidate talkers, the decoder identifies the attended voice. The problem is difficult because the neural response to speech is weak (a linear decoder’s reconstruction correlates with the attended envelope at only r = 0 .07–0 .17 on our two-talker EEG datasets), differs across listeners, and is mixed with activity unre-lated to the audio stimulus, while the candidates often differ in ways a decoder can exploit without reading the brain: loudness, voice, or the identity of the recording session. AAD decoders follow two strategies: Stimulus re-construction and Direct classification. Stimulus recon-struction uses the EEG to estimate of the attended speech envelope, and it selects the candidate talker that correlates best with this envelope. Direct classification 1arXiv:2610.32950v1 [eess.AS] 23 Oct 2026 maps EEG to a label, usually the side of the attended talker. In both cases, however, performance usually fails short of expectations (details in App. A). With stimulus reconstruction, linear backward models and canonical correlation analysis (O’Sullivan et al., 2015; Crosse et al., 2016; de Cheveign´ e et al., 2018) are ro-bust, but they require long signal durations (i.e., tens of seconds) for high accuracy, which is not practical. Deep nonlinear reconstructions have not changed this picture. The best of them still reconstruct the envelope weakly (r ≈ 0.19 for VLAAI; Accou et al., 2023), nonlinear decoders did not consistently outperform linear ones in an independent comparison on two public datasets (Geirnaert et al., 2021), and many reported gains come from evaluations in which training and test data over-lap (Puffay et al., 2023). Conversely, direct classifiers (Vandecappelle et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2024; Ni et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025) report 95–97% at 1 s, but their accuracy rests largely on shortcuts: training and test windows come from the same trial, so the classifier can recognise the trial rather than decode attention (leakage), and it can read eye gaze toward the attended talker. When training and test data come from differ-ent trials, their accuracy drops to near chance (Rotaru et al., 2024). Furthermore, datasets replay the same stories to every listener, and test windows are often cut from training trials (Puffay et al., 2023). It is also not inherently clear that decoders are not overfitting to audio cues, such as loudness, spectral differences, or trial identity, indicating that the decoder is ignoring the brain signal.Most studies report accuracy at only one or two window lengths, so the window needed for a given accuracy cannot be predicted. Each study also preprocesses each dataset its own way, so numbers can-not be compared across studies or datasets. Finally, in one widely used dataset (NJU) we find that the EEG is not time-aligned with the audio at any offset, so results on it cannot reflect speech tracking (App. C). Our approach addresses the AAD problem by ensur-ing that decoding is driven only by the brain’s time-locked response to speech, while quantifying the effects of window length and recording quality on accuracy. Our approach scores candidates based solely on the tem-poral covariance between learned acoustic and EEG em-beddings. The EEG representation is centered so that signals without time-varying information assign equal scores to all candidates. A flow-matching model adds a likelihood-ratio decision rule, while content-disjoint splits and null analyses quantify the contribution of neural activity to decoding performance. 

Contributions. (i) A decoder that can only use the brain’s response to speech. CoupleFlow (Sec. 2) chooses the attended talker using only how the EEG follows each talker’s speech over time. It is therefore guaranteed to be at chance when the EEG carries no information about the speech. Examples are EEG from a different trial, EEG shifted in time so that it no longer lines up with the speech, or random noise. Differences in loudness, voice or trial identity cannot raise its ac-curacy. A generative head adds a new decision rule for AAD, which is optimal under a standard assumption. (ii) A leakage-free benchmark. We evaluate on nine public EEG and MEG datasets under one preprocessing pipeline. Test speech is never heard in training, and control tests check that accuracy comes from the brain. Under this protocol, CoupleFlow outperforms linear and deep baselines trained on the same data, and we show where previously reported accuracies came from. (iii) A law for accuracy versus window length. Asimple formula predicts decoding accuracy at any win-dow length from one number per dataset, and predicts that number from how strongly the brain tracks speech. It shows that fast, reliable decoding is limited by the quality of the recording, and it gives the window needed for any target accuracy. 

# 2 CoupleFlow 

A decision window of T seconds ( L = 64 T samples at 64 Hz) gives the recording x ∈ RC×L of C channels, the speech envelopes a1, . . . , a K ∈ RL of K candidate talkers, and the index y of the attended one. Fig. 1 shows how CoupleFlow turns them into a decision. A neural path (blue) embeds the recording and an acoustic path (orange) embeds each candidate, both as time series of the same size. The two paths meet only in a coupling score (green), which makes the decision, and a flow head (pink) supplies a second decision rule. App. D gives every layer. The EEG first goes through a listener correction , a small low-rank adjustment added to a model shared by all listeners. It absorbs individual differences such as head shape and cap placement without fitting a separate model to each listener’s few minutes of data. A layer of virtual sensors then reduces the channels to a few learned spatial filters, as backward models do, and a lag-window encoder turns them into the neural embedding e. Each time step of e sees only the second of EEG that follows the corresponding sound (from a lag δ ∈ { 0, 125 } ms on), which is where a cortical response to that sound must lie. EEG before the sound cannot be a response to it, and serves as our lag control. The acoustic path starts from candidate features :each talker’s envelope and onsets and, where the audio is available, sub-band envelopes and glimpsed/masked 

channels that mark where the talker is louder or qui-eter than the competing one (Brodbeck et al., 2020). In training, a quantile match gives the candidates’ en-velopes identical value distributions, which removes loudness and level cues. # 4 Experiments 

Data. We test on nine public datasets (Table 7), cho-sen so that a gain cannot come from one recording setup. Six are two-talker EEG studies in Danish, Dutch, Man-darin and English: KU Leuven (Das et al., 2016), DTU (Fuglsang et al., 2017), Fuglsang’20 (Fuglsang et al., 2020), whose 44 older listeners include 22 with hearing loss, ESAA (Li et al., 2022), and two high-density sets with 255 (Mundanad Narayanan et al., 2021) and 128 electrodes (Broderick et al., 2018). SparrKULee (Ac-cou et al., 2024) and MEG-MASC (Gwilliams et al., 2023) have a single talker. They test whether a decoder can tell the heard speech from other speech, and MEG-MASC shows how far decoding goes when the recording is much cleaner than EEG. MAESTRO (Hassan et al., 2026) adds a room in which the listener attends one of four talkers. 

Preprocessing. Published results preprocess each dataset differently, so differences between datasets partly reflect the pipeline. We therefore run every raw recording through one pipeline (App. C). It keeps 0.5–32 Hz, which contains the slow cortical tracking of the speech envelope (Ding and Simon, 2012), and removes drift and most muscle noise. It maps every cap onto the same 64 channels, so one network serves all datasets, and resamples to 64 Hz, enough for this band. We compute the speech envelope with a gam-matone filterbank, a standard model of the cochlea; two datasets ship no audio, and for them we use the authors’ envelopes. Finally, we check that the EEG of every dataset tracks its audio at a plausible latency. One widely used dataset fails this check and is excluded (App. C). 

Protocol. Test speech is never heard in training. Most datasets play the same stories to every listener, and some replay them, so holding out trials does not hold out speech. We therefore group the trials that contain the same speech, found by correlating their envelopes since the releases lack a reliable stimulus key, and build each test fold from whole groups ( content-disjoint folds; App. C). We report three candidate sets: competing (comp.), the attended vs. the other talker; matched , the same pair after quantile matching, which removes loudness cues; and same-talker (match– mismatch, mm), the attended speech vs. the same talker’s speech from another window of the trial, the only task for single-talker data. Every accuracy comes with nulls that make the EEG uninformative: another trial’s recording of the same listener ( across-trial ) and, at 5 s, the trial’s own recording shifted by at least 2 s 4(circular ), which keeps trial identity but breaks time-locking. Baselines. We compare with one decoder from each main family, all trained on the same folds, windows and inputs and stopped early on validation data: the per-listener linear backward model, still the standard for AAD; VLAAI (Accou et al., 2023), a deep reconstruction model trained across listeners; and a dilated-convolution match–mismatch model (Dil. MM; Accou et al., 2020), which, like CoupleFlow, compares the EEG with each candidate directly. A recursive decoder (DECAF) stays near chance on the two-talker sets and appears only in App. Table 13; training details are in App. C. 

Main results (Table 1; leave-one-listener-out, LOSO: listener and content held out together). At 5 s CoupleFlow is best on seven of eight datasets. Its margin over the strongest baseline is +6.8 points on KU Leuven, +3.8 on DTU, +2.3 on Fuglsang’20, +3.8 on ESAA, +3.9 on the 255-channel set, +8.9 on Spar-rKULee and +2.8 on MEG. On Broderick it ties the per-listener linear decoder (-0.1, within one seed s.d.) and stays within one point of it at every window. On unseen listeners CoupleFlow keeps 2.0–8.3 points over the pooled linear model wherever either transfers; on Broderick neither does (App. F.6). # 5 Discussion and Limitations 

Leakage-free evaluation lowers reported AAD accura-cies, but the remaining signal is real and time-locked across nine datasets. The accumulation law turns the 5-s target many applications ask for into a statement about the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the recording: EEG would need κ ≈ 0.74, which only our MEG data reach ( κ = 1 .02). The decoder transfers to unseen listeners at a cost of 3.4–9.2 points (App. F.6), and se-quential decisions on calibrated 1-s scores gain 5.3–7.9 points at the same mean decision time (App. G). On MAESTRO, where the attended talker is always the loudest, removing any one safeguard leaves every null at chance (App. Table 18): the time-centred score closes that shortcut by construction. Limitations. Prop. 2 7assumes the recording depends on the attended stream only; unattended speech is weakly tracked, which the matched and same-talker sets partly address. Two datasets use published envelopes; AV-GC (no content-disjoint split) and NJU (no time-locking) are excluded; the 255-channel set has two content folds; MEG is evaluated by match–mismatch only.

Neural basis. Cortical activity phase-locks to the slow (1–8 Hz) envelope of speech. With two competing talkers the attended talker’s envelope is represented more strongly, in MEG (Ding and Simon, 2012), intracranial recordings (Mesgarani and Chang, 2012) and single-trial EEG (O’Sullivan et al., 2015). The ignored talker is still weakly represented, most clearly where it is masked (Brodbeck et al., 2020). This is the premise of every envelope-based decoder, and the weak representation of the ignored talker is why the attended-only assumption of Prop. 2 is an approximation. We measure its strength γ on every dataset (Table 20). 

Stimulus reconstruction. Backward (decoding) models map time-lagged EEG to the envelope by ridge regression (O’Sullivan et al., 2015; Crosse et al., 2016), or project EEG and audio into a shared space by canonical correlation analysis (CCA) (de Cheveign´ e et al., 2018). The talker whose envelope correlates better with the reconstruction is chosen. These methods are interpretable, need little data and transfer across listeners, and they cannot use spatial or identity shortcuts. Their weakness is speed: one reconstructed correlation is a noisy statistic, and high accuracy needs tens of seconds (Geirnaert et al., 2021). Prop. 3 explains why: accuracy grows with √T at a rate set by the per-sample tracking margin. Deep reconstructions such as VLAAI (Accou et al., 2023) raise the reconstructed correlation modestly. Under our content-disjoint folds VLAAI stays below the per-listener linear decoder on every two-talker dataset (Table 1), plausibly because its subject-independent training cannot use the listener-specific channel weights that the linear model fits. CoupleFlow keeps the time-locked principle of reconstruction but learns the neural and acoustic representations jointly, with a per-listener correction; this is the component that carries its accuracy (Table 14). 

Match–mismatch. The match–mismatch task asks whether a speech segment is the one that evoked a given EEG segment (Accou et al., 2020; Puffay et al., 2023). It is free of spatial confounds and applies to single-talker data. It is usually evaluated on unseen time windows of seen stimuli, and its negatives are often taken from nearby in the same recording. We use it on held-out stimuli as our same-talker candidate set and as the task for single-talker datasets. 

Direct classifiers of the attended side. Convolutional networks (Vandecappelle et al., 2021) and their successors with attention, graph and dual-branch architectures (Yan et al., 2024; Ni et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025) predict whether the attended talker is left or right from EEG alone, with 95–97% at 1 s on KU Leuven. No audio is used, so any EEG signal that differs between left and right trials can be exploited. This includes eye movements toward the attended loudspeaker, lateralised muscle activity and slow trial-specific drifts. On a dataset where gaze and attention were dissociated, accuracy followed gaze (Rotaru et al., 2024). Under their within-trial split we reproduce the published numbers. On held-out stimuli, the same code falls to 40–62% on DTU, ESAA and the 255-channel set (Table 16). On KU Leuven, attended side and story are confounded, so the side task is ill-posed there without shared stories. 

Brain decoding with learned representations and generative models. Contrastive alignment between M/EEG and pretrained speech representations identifies which of many speech segments was heard (D´ efossez et al., 2023). Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) and diffusion models give tractable conditional generative models, and conditioning dropout lets one network represent both p(a | x) and p(a) (Ho and Salimans, 2021). Likelihood-ratio classification with generative models is classical. We use the ratio for a different purpose: it cancels the audio prior p(a), which would otherwise favour acoustically typical candidates regardless of the brain (Prop. 2; Table 3). Our InfoNCE term follows contrastive decoding, restricted to windows of one listener so that identity cannot solve it. 

Evaluation validity. Data leakage through non-independent splits inflates results across machine-learning (ML) based science (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). In AAD, reviews have noted within-trial splits and repeated stimuli (Puffay et al., 2023), and Rotaru et al. (2024) demonstrated gaze and trial-identity confounds. Table 6 lists the failure modes we found or controlled, and the control we use for each.

Content groups. Table 9 summarises the groups behind the content-disjoint folds (Sec. 4). Holding out trials does not hold out audio: most datasets play the same stories to all listeners, and KU Leuven replays each story pair five times per listener, so a trial split puts the same speech in training and test, where a decoder with an audio pathway can memorise it. The audio, not the metadata, defines the groups because stimulus keys are missing, inconsistent or per-listener in the releases (e.g. Fuglsang’20 numbers its files per listener, yet its 1401 trials contain only 4 distinct contents). The search has two stages. First, every candidate envelope is averaged to 8 Hz, cut into non-overlapping 10-s chunks, and each chunk is correlated with every 10-s window, at every 1/8-s offset, of every other envelope; pairs with r > 0.9 are candidates. Second, each candidate is re-correlated at 64 Hz over the full overlap at the implied offset ( ±1/8 s) and kept if r > 0.95. A copy of one recording gives 

r ≈ 1 (every kept link has r ≥ 0.964). Different contents stay well below the threshold: the 64-Hz correlation at the best 8-Hz match of a 10-s chunk in another group has a median of at most 0.53 per dataset (one listener’s chunks against all envelopes of a second listener). The closest cases (Table 9, last column), up to 0.93, have identifiable causes that are not shared speech: in Fuglsang’20 every competing-talker envelope starts with 3–5 s of zero padding, and in SparrKULee one 10-s stretch of a podcast resembles stretches of three other podcasts. Two envelopes are linked if both stages pass. Let G be the graph whose nodes are trials, with an edge between two trials whenever any of their envelopes are linked; its connected components, found with union–find, are the content groups (e.g. the 320 KU Leuven trials form 4 groups). 

Proposition 4 (content disjointness) . No test stream is linked to any training or validation stream. 

The proof is immediate from the component construction (App. B). Folds are unions of whole groups (min(5 , groups) folds); validation uses whole training groups when at least 10 remain, otherwise the last 15% of each training trial of at least 200 s (Fuglsang’20, whose trials last about 50 s: the last 20% of each trial of at least 30 s), separated from the training part by a gap of one context margin on each side. Such validation data share content with training and serve only for early stopping and the lag choice; no reported number uses them. 

Alignment checks. For every bundle, a per-listener ridge backward model (lags 0–250 ms, leave-trials-out) tracks the attended envelope (held-out r = 0.07–0.27, against r ≈ 0 for the envelope shifted by 1–4 s), and a global offset scan (125-ms steps) peaks at 0 s, or at +125 ms on KU Leuven and Broderick. For SparrKULee the audio was re-timed to the EEG clock using the recorded 1-Hz trigger pulses (drift 3 .4–5 .5 × 10 −5). For MEG-MASC, sound onsets were corrected by the delay measured on the audio channel recorded in the MEG file (40–56 ms).

MAESTRO synchronisation. Each stream is trimmed to the release’s common window, from the latest start among EEG and audio playback to the earliest end. The playback start is known only from software timestamps. The glasses’ scene microphone recorded the room audio, and a label-free search by generalised cross-correlation with phase transform (GCC-PHAT, 300–4000 Hz) located every talker’s audio in it. On 1,240 trials with clear peaks, the three playback devices start within a median 14 ms of each other (90th percentile 118 ms), while their timestamps differ by a median 162 ms. Slicing each device from its own timestamp therefore adds misalignment; it lowers tracking. We place all talkers at the earliest timestamp plus one global latency of 0.781 s (50 samples at 64 Hz). Odd and even listeners give the same estimate (0.75 and 0.81 s). EEG tracking of the six-talker mixture, which uses no attention label, peaks at 0.000 s after the correction. There is no per-trial or per-listener adjustment. Without the correction, a window that nominally follows the sound in fact covers EEG from 0.78 s before it, and per-trial accuracy rises to 77.9%. A correctly aligned model given the same pre-sound EEG reaches the same 78.0% (vs. 70.2% causal), so the extra accuracy comes from EEG that precedes the sound. It is consistent with the lag control: pre-sound EEG alone gives 46.3% per trial on MAESTRO. The attended talker is always the loudest, by 3–18 dB, but per-trial accuracy does not depend on that margin (Spearman ρ = 0 .03, p = 0 .22). Bad channels are those listed in the release, plus flat channels and channels at the amplifier’s clipping level in at least 1% of samples. M1/M2 are dropped by the 64-channel mapping, leaving 28.0 channels on average.

Training details. AdamW (learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −4), gradient-norm clipping at 1, batches of up to 32 windows from one listener (a listener’s last partial batch is kept if it has at least 4), 5-s training crops, up to 100 epochs with early stopping (patience 20) on validation contribution, learning rate halved when it has not improved for more than 5 epochs. Nulls. The across-trial null uses 20 permutations at 5 s and 3 at other windows; when a listener has a single test trial, windows of that trial are permuted instead. Candidate features and quantile matching. Each candidate is described by its broadband gammatone envelope ( | · | 0.6), its onsets (half-wave-rectified derivative, since cortex responds strongly to acoustic edges), 8-and 28-band sub-band envelopes (spectral detail), and glimpsed and masked channels: the candidate’s window-standardised sub-band envelope where it exceeds, or falls below, the largest standardised envelope of the other candidates (on the broadband envelope for the two envelope-only datasets). Ignored speech is tracked mainly where it is glimpsed (Brodbeck et al., 2020), so these channels let the model treat the two cases differently. In training, and for the matched and same-talker candidate sets, the broadband envelope and onset of each window (with its context) are quantile-matched across candidates; every other channel is z-scored within the window. The competing set is evaluated unmatched. The candidates then have identical envelope value distributions, which removes loudness and level cues that would identify the attended talker without the brain (Fig. 7). 

Audio encoder. Four causal convolutions (Fig. 6, orange) (dilations 1–8, receptive field 31 samples, 0.48 s) map each candidate to ˆ ak ∈ R16 ×L. Causal means ˆ ak,t depends only on audio up to t, so together with the EEG window the lag direction is fixed by the architecture: EEG must follow the sound it is matched to. One set of weights is shared by all candidates. This is required by Prop. 1 (assumption (a)) and means no candidate can be favoured by its position. 

Coupling score. The score (Eq. 1) is a weighted sum of per-dimension Pearson correlations over time, scaled by a learned temperature (initialised at 0.07) and centred over candidates. Correlation is invariant to the scale and offset of either embedding, so the score depends only on how the two signals co-vary in time. Centring the embeddings over time makes the score exactly zero for a time-constant neural embedding. This is what makes the decoder null-calibrated: without time-varying neural input there is nothing for the audio pathway to use. The weights ωd start equal (1 /D ) and learn which embedding dimensions carry coupling. Fig. 8 follows one test window through the score.

The weights, λNCE = 1, λnull = 0 .5, λadv = λFM = λside = 0 .3, λVIC = 0 .1, λenv = 0 .2, were set once and are the same for every dataset: the two decoding terms at full weight, the others below them so that the decision shapes the embedding. 

Why more than one term. Cross-entropy over the candidates is all the task requires, but on its own it accepts any route to the label. AAD data offer several routes that do not go through the brain response: the attended talker may be louder or have a recognisable voice, every window of a trial has the same label, and a listener may attend the same side throughout. A decoder that takes such a route scores well on a leaky split and fails on new content. The architecture already closes the main route: the time-centred score is zero for every candidate without time-varying neural input (Prop. 1). The remaining terms make the useful route easier to find (InfoNCE), guard against degenerate solutions (null hinge, VICReg, adversary), and add outputs that the decision does not use but that we report (flow head, side head, envelope read-out). 

Decoding terms. LCE is the task: given one window and its candidates, put the highest score on the attended one. Label smoothing (0.1) reflects that the label is an instruction, not an observation: listeners lapse, so a fraction of windows is mislabelled and should not be fitted with full confidence. With two candidates LCE gives one bit of supervision per window, too little to teach two encoders from scratch. LNCE supplies more: it asks which of the B = 32 windows’ attended speech goes with which window’s recording, a B-way matching problem (ln 32 = 3 .47 nats at chance against ln 2 = 0 .69 for the task). Because all windows in a batch come from one listener, recognising the listener cannot solve it; only the time course of the response can. It is the largest term at the first epoch (Table 12) and falls by 30–83% as the encoders learn to align brain and speech. Both terms act on the same score (Eq. 1), so what InfoNCE learns transfers directly to the decision. 

Anti-shortcut terms. Lnull states the null hypothesis as a constraint: the true recording must give the attended speech a higher score than the recording of another window, and than a constant recording (score 0), each by a margin ξ = 0 .5. A decoder that scores the attended talker high whatever the brain did violates it. The softplus keeps a small gradient after the margin is met. Ladv trains an audio-only classifier to find the attended candidate from summary statistics of the candidate embeddings; the reversed gradient (Ganin et al., 2016) then removes from the acoustic encoder whatever let it succeed. Its value is a direct check: it is 0 .3 ln 2 = 0 .21, chance, at the first and at the selected epoch on every dataset, so after quantile matching the audio carries no usable cue to the attended role. LVIC is a guard against a collapsed neural embedding (constant over time, or with all 23 dimensions alike), which would score every candidate 0 and stall training. Its variance part is zero while every dimension’s temporal s.d. exceeds sd min = 0 .5; the whole term is below 0.005 at the first and at the selected epoch on every dataset. These three terms are therefore insurance: they cost nothing when the model behaves and act only when it starts to take a shortcut. On MAESTRO, where a loudness shortcut exists, removing any one of them leaves every null at chance (Table 18), because the score already closes the shortcut. 

Density and auxiliary terms. LFM (Eq. 3) trains the flow head, which supplies the PMI rule and the per-source residuals (Table 3). Its gradient also reaches the neural encoder through the condition, and asks the embedding to carry what predicts the attended envelope, which agrees with the coupling objective. Lside trains the EEG-only side head. Lateralised EEG can carry attention, but also gaze and, when a listener attends one side throughout, listener identity: on Broderick the term drops to 0 because side and listener coincide. We therefore measure its credit separately and never use it in a reported decision. Lenv = 1 − r trains a linear read-out of the embedding to the attended envelope; it gives the tracking values of Table 2 and slightly regularises the embedding towards the envelope. 

How the weights were set. The weights were set once and not tuned on any dataset; every dataset uses the same values. Where the concurrent MERIT objective (Anonymous, 2026) has a corresponding term we took its weight; the others follow from scale. The task has weight 1 and is the reference. InfoNCE also has weight 1: it is the same log-softmax on the same score, and should dominate early. The null hinge (0.5) is a constraint that should bind only when violated. The adversary (0.3) is kept moderate because a gradient-reversal game becomes unstable when the reversed gradient dominates; the flow, side and envelope terms (0.3, 0.3, 0.2) are kept below the decoding terms so that the neural embedding is shaped mainly by the decision; VICReg (0.1) is a guard. Two facts make the choice uncritical. First, the selected model is chosen on validation contribution 

(accuracy minus the across-trial null), not on the loss, so the weights trade off training signals, not the selection criterion. Second, removing any one term changes 5-s accuracy on KU Leuven, DTU and MEG-MASC by at most 0.8 points (Table 14; one seed per variant); only the per-listener correction, an architectural component, matters there. On MAESTRO, InfoNCE does matter (-3.6 points per trial, Table 18). ## F.2 Two kinds of leakage 

Table 15 and Table 17 can look contradictory: one finds that a leaky split inflates accuracy by at most +2.0 points, the other by 16.7–31.5. They measure different leaks. 

Content leakage (Table 15). In a trial split, whole trials are held out, so no test sample, and no EEG from a test trial, is seen in training. Only the stories repeat: a test trial’s audio was heard by the listener in another, training trial. A model can profit from this only if it can recognise the audio and map it to a label, as an audio-only or stimulus-identity classifier could. To measure what a content-sharing split does, we retrain every model on KU Leuven and DTU with one group per trial , the split used in most prior work, so test stories also occur in training (Table 15). No model gains more than +2.0 points: -0.3 to +2.0 for the linear decoder, VLAAI, the match–mismatch model and CoupleFlow alike. Sharing stories across trials is therefore not what inflates these decoders. Each of them reaches the decision through the neural response to the candidate audio, with no path that can memorise a story’s identity. The small KU Leuven gain for CoupleFlow (+2.0) is consistent with its trial split exposing all four story pairs in training instead of three. 

Trial-identity leakage (Table 17). In a within-trial split, every trial contributes windows to both training and test: our earlier decoders used a random 5-fold split of overlapping windows, the side classifiers’ protocol holds out the last 10% of each trial. Each test window then has training windows from the same trial , with the same label, overlapping it or seconds away. EEG has slow, trial-specific structure: electrode impedances, drifts, posture and gaze, and an attended side that is constant within the trial. A decoder that recognises which trial a window comes from therefore knows its label without decoding attention. This is the much larger effect: 16.7–31.5 points for our earlier decoders, and 96.8–98.1% at 1 s on KU Leuven for the side classifiers (Table 16; on DTU our runs under their split come out about 12 points below the published numbers, plausibly because of our stricter preprocessing: 0.5-Hz high-pass, per-trial z-scoring, no electrooculography (EOG) channels). NJU, whose EEG is 27 not time-locked to the audio at all, reached 86.0% this way. ## F.5 Further analyses 

Where the attention signal lives. Decomposing the PMI attention effect (PMI att − PMI comp ) by modulation band of the candidate envelope (seed 0), the delta band (0.5–4 Hz) carries 71–91% of it on every dataset, theta (4–8 Hz) 7–22%, and alpha and beta together at most 9%. This matches the known spectral profile of cortical speech tracking (Ding and Simon, 2012), and it matches for MEG and EEG alike. 

Hearing-impaired listeners. On Fuglsang’20, 5-s accuracy is 80.6% for the 22 hearing-impaired and 78.5% for the 22 normal-hearing listeners (mean over 3 seeds; Mann–Whitney p = 0 .06–0 .46 across seeds). Decoding is not worse with hearing loss, consistent with the enhanced envelope tracking reported for these listeners (Fuglsang et al., 2020). We use the release’s envelopes of the unamplified stimuli for every listener.

# G Confidence and Sequential Decisions 

Everything so far scores fixed windows. Here we ask how the decoder’s per-window scores should be combined over time, when a decision should be made, and whether overlapping windows or a different training window help. Nothing in this appendix retrains the paper’s models except Sec. G.6; the decision rules only read their per-window scores.

The sequential rule reaches the fixed-window accuracy in about half the time, with the same κ. This is the classical result of Wald (1945); its continuous-time limit is the drift–diffusion model of perceptual decisions (Bogacz et al., 2006). If the information rate varies between stretches of a trial (attention lapses, noise bursts), the sequential rule spends its time on the weak stretches, which a fixed window cannot do.

## G.4 Anatomy of the sequential decisions 

Table 24 and Figs. 12 and 13 open up the sequential rule on CoupleFlow’s calibrated 1-s log-odds (decisions of the three seeds pooled, test folds). Four things stand out. 

The threshold sets the accuracy. Decisions that reach the threshold are about as accurate as the guarantee Λ( A)of Prop. 9 (Fig. 12a; deviation -2.2 to +9.9 points over all datasets and thresholds). At small A they are above it, because a 1-s step overshoots the threshold; at large A slightly below it, the signature of the mild positive dependence between consecutive windows, which makes long sums a little over-confident. At A=1 decisions are 74.9–80.2% correct (Λ(1) = 73.1%), at A=2 86.1–90.6% (Λ(2) = 88.1%), on every dataset: a target accuracy fixes 

A, to within a few points, without looking at the data. 

The chosen length adapts widely. The same threshold yields short and long decisions (Fig. 13a). At A=1 the median decision uses 3–7 s and the 90th percentile 7–21 s; 3–20% of decisions are taken after a single second. At A=2 the median is 6–17 s and the 90th percentile 14–49 s. The mean length grows with A roughly as 

A(1 − 2α)/(2 κ2) (Prop. 8), so datasets with small κ (Broderick, KUL-255ch) wait longest (Fig. 12b). 

Accuracy hardly depends on the chosen length. As Prop. 9 predicts, fast and slow decisions are about equally accurate (Fig. 13b): decisions of at most 8 s are at most 1.9 points below Λ(2). The exception is the longest decisions ( ≥17 s), up to 9.7 points below Λ(2). They come from stretches in which the recording tracks the speech poorly, and one global calibration scale over-states the evidence of such windows: calibration holds on average, not conditionally on the local signal quality. A calibration that depends on the recent evidence rate would close this gap. 

Finite trials force some decisions. When a trial ends before |S| reaches A, the leftover is decided anyway (Fig. 12c). These forced decisions are less accurate (58.4–79.6% at A ∈ { 1, 2}) and frequent on the short trials of Broderick (up to 37.6% at A=2); the “all” column includes them. In continuous listening there is no trial end and they do not arise. 1 2 3 4

## G.5 Overlapping windows 

Table 25 and Fig. 14 repeat the analysis with 1-s windows every 0.5 s and 0.25 s (CoupleFlow, seed 0). The data follow Prop. 10 closely. The log-odds of windows jh apart correlate as 1 − jh/w (KU Leuven, hop 0.25 s: 0.74, 0.48, 0.25 at j = 1 , 2, 3; prediction 0.75, 0.5, 0.25), fixed-length accuracy changes by at most 0.7 points, and the summed log-odds are over-confident (5-s ECE up to 0.20) until scaled by h/w (ECE ≤ 0.02). The corrected sequential rule gains 0.1–1.6 points over the non-overlapping one at the same mean time, the small effect of checking the threshold more often. One detail matters: after a decision the rule must restart at the first window that shares no data with that decision. Short crops help short windows. Training on 1-s crops raises 1-s accuracy by +0.8 to +5.9 points ( n = 8 datasets), but changes 5-s accuracy by -1.3 to +0.9 and 30-s accuracy by -1.8 to +0.8. Long crops help nothing :10-s training changes 1-s accuracy by -4.3 to -0.2 and 5-s accuracy by -1.4 to -0.1 points. 

Mixed crops are best overall. Redrawing the crop length raises 1-s accuracy on every dataset (+0.4 to +3.8 points, n = 8) and lowers neither 5-s accuracy (0.0 to +2.6) nor 30-s accuracy (0.0 to +3.8) by more than 0.1 points. In the terms of Prop. 6 it raises the information rate of the 1-s evidence: κ of the summed 1-s log-odds grows by a factor 1.05–1.22, and the sequential rule at A=2 needs 0.76–0.88 of the time (0.74–0.84 for 1-s training). A plausible reading, which we have not tested further: a model that only sees long crops learns a coupling that is reliable when averaged over many seconds but noisy within one; short crops force every second to carry evidence, and mixing lengths keeps the long-window accuracy.
