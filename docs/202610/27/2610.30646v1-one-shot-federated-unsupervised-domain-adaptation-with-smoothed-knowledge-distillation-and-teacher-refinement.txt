Abstract 

Federated unsupervised domain adaptation (FUDA) addresses the challenge of adapting models to an unlabeled target domain using decentralized source domains while preserving data privacy. However, existing FUDA methods typically require multiple communication rounds, rely on complex aggre-gation strategies, and often struggle with noisy pseudo labels and incon-sistent source knowledge. To address these challenges, we propose SKD-ETR, a novel one-shot FUDA framework that combines Smoothed Knowl-edge Distillation (SKD) and Exponential moving average-based Teacher Re-finement (ETR). SKD trains a student model on the target domain using smoothed soft pseudo labels generated by the ensemble of source models. This reduces overconfidence, mitigates noise, and improves robustness. ETR further refines each source model by interpolating its parameters toward the student via exponential moving average (EMA), thereby transferring target-domain knowledge back to the teachers. This bidirectional refine-ment enhances pseudo-label quality and student generalization without ad-ditional communication overhead. SKD-ETR eliminates complex aggrega-tion by initializing the student model randomly and performing a single-round distillation process. Extensive experiments on OfficeHome, Office-Caltech, and DomainNet demonstrate that SKD-ETR achieves competi-tive performance while being communication- and computation-efficient, and robust under noisy supervision. Federated learning (FL) is a decentralized learning framework where mul-tiple clients collaboratively train a shared global model without exchanging their raw data [1, 2, 3]. This approach is critical in scenarios where data is distributed across multiple centers and cannot be shared due to privacy regulations, such as in medical imaging [1, 4, 5]. However, FL faces two major challenges in real-world deployments. First, conventional FL meth-ods rely on multiple rounds of communication between the server and clients to form the global model [6], which results in high communication overhead and an increased risk of privacy leakage during transmission [7, 8, 9]. Second, in practice, the data distributions across clients are often non-identical and domain-specific. This distributional heterogeneity, also referred to as domain shift [10, 11, 12, 13], can severely degrade the generalization performance of the global model when applied to unseen target domains. To overcome these limitations, recent studies have proposed strategies to improve both scalability and generalization in FL. One-shot FL [7, 14, 15] offers a communication-efficient alternative by performing only a single com-munication round. This design reduces bandwidth requirement and mini-mizes the risk of privacy leakage, making it suitable for resource-constrained and privacy-sensitive settings [16]. Unlike traditional multi-round FL, which frequently exchanges parameters between server and clients, one-shot FL eliminates repeated exposure of intermediate model states and thus reduces the attack surface. Federated unsupervised domain adaptation (FUDA) [17] extends FL to address domain shift by adapting a model trained on distributed labeled source domains to an unlabeled target domain while maintaining data pri-vacy. Each client trains locally on its labeled source domain, and the server aggregates knowledge from source models to generalize to an unlabeled target domain [18, 19, 20, 21]. FUDA can be viewed as a decentralized and privacy-preserving extension of multi-source-free domain adaptation (MSFDA) [22], which addresses the challenge of domain adaptation without access to source data by leveraging pretrained source models. MSFDA itself builds upon the multi-source unsupervised domain adaptation (MSUDA) [10] framework, which assumes access to labeled data from multiple source domains during the adaptation on an unlabeled target domain. While MSUDA relies on cen-2tralized access to both source and target data, and MSFDA assumes access to all source models in a central location, such assumptions are often im-practical due to privacy and communication constraints. This motivates the development of FUDA. Existing FUDA methods [23, 17, 18, 19, 21] have achieved notable progress but still face several fundamental limitations. Source-to-target knowledge transfer remains unstable because consensus voting or confidence thresholds may either discard valuable signals or propagate noisy supervision. Aggre-gation strategies are often heuristic, which can introduce bias. Moreover, many approaches are communication-inefficient, limiting their scalability in real federated settings. As summarized in Table 1, these issues highlight the gap between existing multi-round and one-shot designs and motivate the need for a more communication-efficient and robust framework that can sta-bilize pseudo-labels while reducing reliance on costly aggregation mechanisms [24, 25]. These limitations motivate our one-shot FUDA framework, SKD-ETR, which integrates Smoothed Knowledge Distillation (SKD) and Exponential moving average-based Teacher Refinement (ETR). The framework builds on Knowledge Distillation (KD) and consists of two key modules. The first module (SKD) trains a randomly initialized student model from scratch on the unlabeled target domain using soft pseudo labels generated by an ensem-ble of independently trained source models. To handle the impact of noise and uncertainty inherent in these teacher-generated pseudo labels, we incor-porate label smoothing [26] into the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss. This acts as an implicit regularizer and improves the robustness and gener-alization of the student model [27]. The second module (ETR) updates each source model by gradually interpolating its parameters toward the student through an exponential moving average (EMA) mechanism. This process allows the teachers to absorb target-specific knowledge and produce more robust and target-aware pseudo labels, thereby enhancing adaptation per-formance. The effectiveness of SKD-ETR is validated on three standard benchmarks: OfficeHome [28], Office-Caltech [29], and DomainNet [30]. 2. Related Work 

In this section, we review related works across several areas relevant to our approach, including MSUDA, FUDA, and KD. 42.1. Multi-source Unsupervised Domain Adaptation 

SSD [32] is a MSUDA method that selects informative source samples and ranks source domains through a two-step distillation process using pseudo-labeled target data. A domain discriminator assigns weights and identifies the dominant source domain for final prediction. SImpAl [33] enforces clas-sifier agreement on pseudo-labeled target samples, which enables implicit feature alignment without additional alignment losses. MDAMA [34] aligns the target domain with a mixture of source domains using a classifier-aware margin-based discrepancy. These methods assumes access to all source data during adaptation, which is often impractical due to privacy or communica-tion limits. MSFDA [22] overcomes this limitation by adapting using only pretrained source models, without source data access. SHOT [35] freezes the source classifier and optimizes a target-specific feature encoder using information maximization and self-supervised pseudo-labeling. DECISION [36] performs multi-source-free domain adaptation by learning optimal source model weights and adapting only feature extractors using information maximization and pseudo-labeling strategies. TransDA [37] injects a transformer module into the feature extractor and applies self-knowledge distillation with pseudo-labels to guide attention toward object regions in the target domain. 

2.2. Federated Unsupervised Domain Adaptation 

Unlike MSFDA, which assumes centralized access to all source models, FUDA distributes them across clients and supports privacy-preserving adap-tation with unlabeled target data. FADE [18] uses adversarial debiasing to align feature distributions across non-iid clients without requiring group la-bels or data sharing. FDKD [19] uses confidence-weighted KD with adaptive teacher weighting and feature-level alignment to improve generalization on the target domain. KD3A [31] distills knowledge from source model predic-tions using consensus voting and reweights domains based on their contribu-tion to reduce negative transfer. FDAC [23] applies domain- and category-level contrastive learning to align features and preserve class semantics across source domains. SEA+MSPL [21] introduces one-shot FUDA by aggregat-ing source models using scaled entropy-based aggregation and adapting the global model via smoothed pseudo labels from source predictions. 52.3. Knowledge Distillation 

Knowledge distillation (KD) [38, 39, 40], which was originally introduced as a model compression technique to transfer knowledge from a large and well-performing teacher model to a smaller student model, has shown promis-ing results in domain adaptation tasks. KD3A [31] uses a knowledge voting scheme that aggregates confident predictions from multiple teachers to form a consensus signal for the student. FDKD [19] introduces confidence-based multi-teacher distillation by adaptively weighting teachers based on their agreement with smoothed ensemble predictions. SSD [32] performs dual distillation by weighting both source models and individual samples based on cross-domain consistency and reliability during target training. DECI-SION [36] trains a student by combining weighted information maximization with refined pseudo labels from fixed classifiers. TransDA [37] applies self-distillation using soft pseudo labels to improve attention alignment in target adaptation.

4. Experimental settings 

4.1. Datasets 

We evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method on three widely used multi-domain benchmarks for unsupervised domain adaptation. Office-Home [28] contains approximately 15,500 images categorized into 65 classes spanning four distinct domains: Art, Clipart, Product, and Real-World. This dataset presents significant domain shift, making it a standard and challeng-ing benchmark for domain adaptation. Office-Caltech [29] combines the Office-31 [47] dataset with the Caltech-256 1 dataset, resulting in a unified benchmark with 2,533 images from four domains: Amazon, Webcam, DSLR, and Caltech, restricted to 10 common object categories. DomainNet [30] is the largest and most diverse benchmark in our evaluation, comprising 48,129 images across six highly diverse domains: Clipart, Real, Sketch, Infograph, Painting, and Quickdraw, with a shared label space of 345 categories. These datasets collectively cover a wide range of domain shifts, image styles, and semantic spaces, allowing for comprehensive evaluation of adaptation perfor-mance under both mild and severe distribution shifts. Figure 3 illustrates sample images from different domains and classes across the three bench-marks.

4.3. Baselines and Evaluation Metric 

We compare our method against three categories of baselines. MSUDA methods include SSD [32], SImpAl [33], and MDAMA [34]. MSFDA meth-ods include TransDA [37], SHOT [35], and DECISION [36]. FUDA methods include FedSD2C [49], FedPFT [15], FADA [17], FADE [18], KD3A [31], FDKD [19], SEA+MSPL [21], and FDAC [23]. Among these methods, FedSD2C [49] and FedPFT [15] are one-shot FL approaches that we extend to the FUDA setting, while SEA+MSPL [21] is a one-shot FUDA method. We evaluate all methods on OfficeHome, Office-Caltech, and DomainNet 15 using the standard protocol in FUDA literature, where each domain is treated as the target once and the remaining domains serve as sources. Each source domain is assigned to a separate client, resulting in M = L − 1 clients per experiment, where L is the total number of domains. The evaluation metric is top-1 classification accuracy on the unlabeled target domain. We report per-domain results as well as the average accuracy across all domains. 

5. Results and Analysis 

5.1. Results 

Tables 3 and 4 compare the performance of our proposed SKD-ETR method with state-of-the-art baselines on the OfficeHome, DomainNet, and Office-Caltech datasets. SKD-ETR consistently achieves the highest average accuracy on OfficeHome and DomainNet, demonstrating its effectiveness in handling diverse and complex domain shifts. On Office-Caltech, it delivers performance comparable to the best-performing baseline while significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters, communication overhead, and the size of sharable model weights. Additionally, Figure 4 presents the representations learned by the student model using t-SNE before and after domain adaptation with SKD-ETR. The visualization clearly shows that our approach leads to better feature align-ment and class separability in the target domain. This highlights the effect of adaptation on the student model. To further quantify these representa-tional shifts, we analyzed the movement of the decision boundary using Flip Rate and KL Divergence between the student model before and after adapta-tion. The Flip Rate measures the fraction of samples whose predicted labels change after adaptation, while the KL divergence quantifies the average shift in prediction distributions. As shown in Table 2, all domains exhibit high Flip Rate and KL divergence values, indicating substantial prediction and distributional changes following training. These results confirm that SKD-ETR effectively adapts the decision boundaries toward the target domain, resulting in improved alignment and generalization.

5.2.2. Effect of Different Student Model Initializations 

Table 6 compares several strategies for initializing the student model, in-cluding random initialization, averaged-teacher initialization, lowest-entropy source, and random-source initialization. In random initialization, the stu-dent starts with entirely new parameters, independent of any source model. 18 The averaged-teacher initialization computes the mean of all source teach-ers’ bottleneck and head parameters to provide a unified starting point. The lowest-entropy source initialization selects the source model with the most confident predictions, i.e., the source model yielding the lowest output en-tropy, as the student’s initialization. Additionally, the random-source initial-ization simply chooses one source model at random for initialization. Based on the results, random initialization achieves the highest average performance across the four OfficeHome domains. Transferring parameters from source models, either individually or through averaging, often introduces domain-specific biases or noisy supervision that impede adaptation. In contrast, random initialization provides an unbiased starting point, allowing SKD to guide the student toward more domain-invariant representations. We there-fore adopt this initialization strategy in all subsequent experiments.

5.3.4. Effect of ETR on Teacher models 

To examine the impact of ETR, we evaluate the target-domain perfor-mance of each source model before and after the adaptation process, during which multiple rounds of teacher refinement are applied. As shown in Fig-ure 7, ETR consistently improves the target-domain accuracy of all teacher models, indicating that the refinement process effectively pulls each source hypothesis toward the target domain. For example, when Art is the target domain, the source model trained on Clipart improves from 72.7% accu-racy before adaptation to 81.2% after the refinement process. Additionally, ETR reduces the pairwise KL divergence between teacher predictions on the target domain (see Table 11). This improvement demonstrates that ETR progressively decreases inter-teacher disagreement and enhances hypothesis alignment prior to pseudo-label aggregation. 23 Although refined teachers exhibit clear performance gains, their accuracy remains lower than that of the adapted student model. This is expected, as the student is directly optimized on the target-domain, while teachers are updated indirectly via parameter interpolation. These results suggest that ETR primarily serves as a training-time alignment mechanism that improves teacher quality and pseudo-label reliability. While ensembles of refined teach-ers may offer additional robustness in safety-critical scenarios, the student provides a more favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-off and is therefore re-tained for inference. 5.4. Computational Overhead and Resource Efficiency 

To analyze the computational cost of SKD-ETR, we measured the train-ing time, GPU memory footprint, floating-point operations (FLOPs), and energy consumption of both teacher and student models on an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 Ti GPU. Results are summarized in Table 12. Each teacher model requires approximately 70.7 GFLOPs per forward–backward pass, 364 MB of parameters, and peaks at 0.81 GB of GPU memory, with training time averaging 1,560 seconds. The student model, which performs 24 the cross-domain adaptation, incurs a moderate increase in memory usage and training time while maintaining the same FLOP count and parameter size. The total estimated energy consumption for training student model re-mains low at approximately 0.05 kg CO 2. The inference time for a batch of 32 samples is 0.01 seconds. It is also important to note that, during adaptation, the teachers remain in non-training mode and contribute only lightweight forward passes. Consequently, both the ensemble pseudo-label generation and the EMA refinement scale linearly with the number of clients M . These operations are performed only once during adaptation and do not impact the 

O(1) inference cost after the student model is trained. These results confirm that SKD-ETR achieves effective adaptation with modest computational and energy requirements.

5.5. Limitations and Failure Modes 

Although SKD-ETR performs competitively across most domains, cer-tain cases reveal some limitations of its design. The Quickdraw domain in DomainNet poses a particular challenge due to its extreme stylization and substantial distribution gap from the sources. In Figure 8, we provide a quantitative measure of the domain shift by computing the pairwise Fréchet Distance between all domains in DomainNet using embeddings extracted from the frozen DINOv2 backbone. As illustrated, the Quickdraw domain exhibits the largest distances relative to all others, forming a clear outlier in the overall domain structure. SKD-ETR employs random initialization to avoid bias toward any spe-cific source; thus, the student begins without domain-aligned priors. While this unbiased start generally enhances robustness and fairness, it can hinder adaptation when the target distribution differs drastically from all sources. 6. Conclusion and Future Work 

In this paper, we introduced SKD-ETR, a novel one-shot FUDA frame-work that effectively transfers knowledge from multiple decentralized source models to an unlabeled target domain without requiring access to source data. Our method consists of two key modules: SKD, which regularizes train-ing by mitigating the effects of noisy pseudo labels using label smoothing; and ETR, which updates the source models after each iteration of student updates using the student’s parameters. This process enables the teachers to gener-ate increasingly better and more robust pseudo-labels after each iteration, without requiring additional communication. We also provide a theoretical justification for the EMA update and show that our framework avoids com-plex aggregation mechanisms. Extensive experiments on OfficeHome, Office-Caltech, and DomainNet benchmarks demonstrate that SKD-ETR achieves superior or comparable performance to state-of-the-art methods while re-26 quiring significantly fewer trainable parameters and communication rounds. These results validate the effectiveness and the communication and compu-tation efficiency of our approach in practical federated settings. For future work, we plan to extend our framework to more complex scenarios such as heterogeneous model architectures and semi-supervised target domains. Moreover, exploring adaptive smoothing strategies and integrating model compression techniques may further enhance scalability and deployment in resource-constrained environments.
