## Abstract 

EEG foundation models report downstream gains, yet their protocols seldom include three controls. These are the same encoder untrained, spectral features fitted on the same data, and a layer picked without looking at test scores. We therefore evaluate five publicly released EEG foundation models and an in-house JEPA-style model under one protocol with all three controls and several seeds, on five benchmarks and four added task families. The controls change the reading in three ways. First, picking the probed layer by its test score raises the layer-wise peak by an average of +0.034 and reverses the peak-versus-output gap in 9 of 36 cells. Second, with the layer picked on validation, random initialisation matches or beats pretraining for half of the models on sleep and emotion, and no model beats a five-band log-power baseline on emotion or motor imagery. Third, after full fine-tuning only 5 of 30 pairs gain beyond twice their seed spread, and the one clear loss of −0.290 AUROC persists under matched learning rates. From these results we draw four reporting practices, and we release every cell for re-analysis. 

## 1 Introduction 

Recent EEG foundation models such as LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024), EEGPT (Wang et al., 2024), CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025), LUNA (Döner et al., 2025) and LuMamba (Broustail et al., 2026) each report downstream gains under their own protocol, with its own splits, heads, schedules and hyperparameters. These protocols differ, so the published numbers cannot be compared across papers, and none of the papers evaluates the other models under its own protocol (Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Döner et al., 2025). Three controls are missing from most of these reports. The three are a matched encoder of the same architecture at random initialisation, a band-power baseline on the same split, and a probed layer chosen on validation instead of on test (Section 3.4). Each is standard in representation-learning evaluation (Alain & Bengio, 2017; Musgrave et al., 2020; Pineau et al., 2018). Such controls have changed conclusions before. In other fields they shrank the reported gains, as metric-learning improvements nearly vanished under one fixed protocol (Musgrave et al., 2020) and better ImageNet models did not always transfer better (Kornblith et al., 2019). The same happened in time-series forecasting, where linear models rivalled transformer forecasters (Zeng et al., 2023) and the pretrained language model could be removed from language-model forecasters without loss (Tan et al., 2024). Whether EEG foundation models keep their gains once all three controls are in place is the question this paper measures. We came to this question from the model side, after building an in-house JEPA-style model for arbitrary montages (Assran et al., 2023; Balestriero & LeCun, 2025; Maes et al., 2026) and pretraining it on the REVE 1arXiv:2610.32067v1 [cs.LG] 23 Oct 2026 corpus (El Ouahidi et al., 2025) (Figure 1). Its frozen output-layer probe on Lee2019 motor imagery (MI) scored 0.507 AUROC, below the 0.741 of a five-band log-power baseline, band power, computed on the same split (Lee et al., 2019). Probing every internal stage then located the loss, because an early front-end stage reached 0.708 and the later stages lost that signal again (Figure 3, left). That observation turned the project into a controlled evaluation of five public EEG foundation models. The in-house model stays in the panel as one row, under the same adapter interface, splits, probe and hyperparameters as the others. The paper makes three contributions. First, it evaluates six models under all three controls, covering 30 model-dataset pairs under frozen probing and full fine-tuning at 5 seeds (Section 3.3). It extends them with four task families (event-related potential (ERP), sleep, emotion and within-subject MI) at 3 seeds wherever the seed changes the result, with LuMamba absent from within-subject MI. Second, it reports what survives the controls: test selection inflates layer-wise peaks and the band-power baseline is rarely beaten (Section 4). It also finds that random initialisation often matches pretraining under a frozen probe and that most fine-tuning gains sit within seed noise (Sections 4.5 and 4.6). Third, it describes the diagnostic protocol that exposed the in-house failure and turns it into reporting practices for evaluating EEG foundation models (Sections 3.5 and 5). One distinction runs through all three. Representation quality, read by a frozen probe, is kept apart from trainability, the fine-tuning gain over random initialisation (Section 3.4). Performance after per-model tuning is a third notion, which a shared protocol bounds from below and does not measure (Section 4.6). 

## 2 Related work 

EEG foundation models differ in objective and input handling: LaBraM predicts masked codes from a learned neural tokenizer (Jiang et al., 2024), and CBraMod reconstructs masked patches with criss-cross attention (Wang et al., 2025). EEGPT likewise combines masked prediction with reconstruction (Wang et al., 2024), LUNA compresses channels through learned queries (Döner et al., 2025), and LuMamba pairs a joint-embedding objective with a state-space backbone (Broustail et al., 2026). Earlier and parallel lines include contrastive pretraining (Kostas et al., 2021), multi-modal biosignal transformers (Yang et al., 2023), generative pretraining (Cui et al., 2023) and signal-level joint-embedding prediction (Guetschel et al., 2024). These papers compare against published numbers from other protocols, whereas we run every model ourselves under one protocol with the three controls (Appendix A). Controlled re-evaluations are an established genre: reproducibility checklists (Pineau et al., 2018), re-labelled benchmarks (Beyer et al., 2020), and fixed-protocol comparisons (Musgrave et al., 2020; Kornblith et al., 2019). Benchmarks of EEG foundation models already report that large models gain little over compact deep architectures (Lee et al., 2025), standardise comparisons across paradigms (Xiong et al., 2026), and find specialist models trained from scratch competitive (Liu et al., 2026). A second tool comes from probing, where linear probes read how much of a label is linearly decodable at each layer (Alain & Bengio, 2017) and masked autoencoders rank differently under linear probing and fine-tuning (He et al., 2022). We apply that genre to EEG and add the layer-selection control, since a layer-wise probe reports as many candidate values as there are layers (Alain & Bengio, 2017). The baselines a foundation model has to beat are older than the models. Classical EEG decoding rests on band power in the canonical frequency bands (Babiloni et al., 2009; Pfurtscheller & Lopes da Silva, 1999), on compact convolutional networks such as EEGNet (Lawhern et al., 2018), and for MI on filter-bank spatial filters (Ang et al., 2008). We use all three on the same splits as the foundation models, so that a gain is measured against the simplest method that could have produced it (Table 2).

3.3 Benchmarks and seed semantics 

The original matrix covers Alzheimer’s disease on APAVA (Escudero et al., 2006), motor imagery on BCI IV 2a (Tangermann et al., 2012) and Lee2019 (Lee et al., 2019), TUH slowing events (TUSL) and abnormal TUAB recordings (Obeid & Picone, 2016; López, 2017). All five are cut into 5-second windows at 200 Hz under one preprocessing chain (Table 1). These five are extended by four task families on data outside TUH, namely P300 detection on BNCI2014-009, five-class sleep staging on ISRUC, eight-class emotion recognition on FACED, and within-subject MI on BCI IV 2a. Here the public FACED release we read carries eight of the nine FACED emotion categories, without the neutral one, so the emotion task is eight-class (Chen et al., 2023). Each added task is reduced to one five-second window, the centre of each labelled epoch for sleep and emotion and a zero-padded window for the sub-second P300 epochs. The reduction is the same for every model and for band power. Then each adapter passes the windows to its checkpoint in the input format that checkpoint’s code expects, which matches pretraining only in part (Table 5). Of the 5 public models, 4 receive their pretraining sampling rate, while EEGPT receives the rate of the released port we load rather than that of its original release. 3.4 Controlled protocols 

Each model is wrapped in an adapter that exposes its internal stages; at each stage we mean-pool the tokens, standardise on the training split and fit a logistic-regression probe (Figure 2). Every pretrained cell, the pretrained checkpoint, has a random-init twin, the same architecture at random initialisation with the library’s default initialiser, probed identically. The second reference, band power, is the five-band log-power baseline: log band power per channel, followed by the same logistic regression on the same split. The gain ∆ is the pretrained minus random-init test metric at the best-validation epoch, averaged over 5 seeds with the population standard deviation. Within that schedule, the learning rate is lower for pretrained than for random-init, because a shared rate under-trains the random twin and biases ∆ upward; Section 4.6 re-runs the most negative cell under matched rates. This single schedule gives a fair-comparison lower bound on each model’s best, and Section 4.6 measures the gap with a per-model learning-rate grid (Musgrave et al., 2020). To read ∆ against its seed noise, we call a mean gain clear when it exceeds twice its seed standard deviation, a descriptive bar across 30 cells. It is a heuristic and not a corrected test, because with 5 seeds a rank test cannot reach conventional significance in any single cell (Pineau et al., 2018). 

3.5 Diagnostic protocol 

Everything above was assembled while diagnosing the in-house model, where every architectural iteration was kept or dropped by the same stage-wise probes (Section 4.1). That diagnosis also produced two further 5Figure 2: Layer-wise probe protocol. (a) A linear probe at every internal stage of the frozen encoder (twelve for the in-house model), with the band power anchor on the same split. (b) Per-stage mechanics: mean-pool over tokens, standardise on the training split, logistic regression. (c) The gap G is the peak minus the output stage, with the peak ˆ s selected on validation (Eq. 5; measured curves in Figure 3). tools. First, each model-dataset pair is labelled by the signs of its frozen-probe gain and its fine-tuning gain, which separates a representation defect (both negative) from a trainability defect (frozen positive, fine-tuning negative). Second, the same probes run on every checkpoint saved during pretraining, so that a run can be stopped when its downstream probes turn down rather than when its training loss stops falling. By contrast, on the five public models the protocol is applied after the fact as an evaluator; we did not use it during their development and make no claim that it would have changed it.

4.2 Choosing the layer on test inflates layer-wise results 

Re-selecting the probed stage on validation lowers the reported peak by +0.034 on average and by up to +0.191 in one cell (Table 8). These 36 cells cover the three leakage-free benchmarks, six models under both initialisations. These include 9 cells in which the peak-minus-output gap is positive under test selection and below −0.01 under validation selection. In those cells, therefore, the reading that an intermediate layer beats the output was an artefact of choosing the layer on test. LuMamba on APAVA shows the pattern: its test-selected gap of +0.006 becomes −0.139 once validation picks the stage (Figure 3, middle). Validation selection does not remove every intermediate peak. A genuine gap survives in 11 cells, the largest for the in-house model on Lee2019 at +0.202. That cell’s random-init curve follows the pretrained one, so its gap belongs to the front-end architecture rather than to pretraining (Figure 3, left). A layer-wise probe therefore supports a claim about intermediate layers only when the layer is chosen on validation and the random twin is plotted beside it (Figure 3).

4.4 A model that sees mostly slow components 

The front-end error also explains where the in-house model does well. Under validation-selected frozen probes the in-house model ranks first among the evaluated models on all four motor-imagery and event-related tasks (Table 8, Figure 4). Its margin over the next model is 0.709 against 0.570 on Lee2019 and 0.795 against 0.768 on P300. It ranks last on all three tasks whose classical markers are band powers, APAVA, sleep staging and emotion (Babiloni et al., 2009). This split is consistent with what the front-end passes, since slow and event-locked potentials survive a sub-hertz low-pass filter far better than band power above a few hertz (Section 3.2). On motor imagery it also suggests that the frozen representations of the five public models expose little of the mu and beta power that band power reads (Section 4.3). A model that sees almost none of that power outranks them, while band power outranks every model (Table 8). The comparison holds for frozen representations only, since the in-house model was developed against Lee2019 (Section 5.3). 4.5 Random initialisation often matches pretraining 

On the added tasks random-init equals or beats pretrained for 3 of 6 models on sleep, 3 of 6 on emotion, 2 of 5 on within-subject MI, and 1 of 6 on ERP (Figure 4). In the other direction, the pretrained probe on ERP detection sits above its random-init twin for 5 of the 6 models, by +0.032 to +0.065. Within sleep staging, the one large frozen-probe gain in the panel is CBraMod’s +0.135, while LUNA, EEGPT and LuMamba score higher at random initialisation than pretrained. Two checks bound how far this reading goes. The random twin is itself a sample: two independent validation-selected draws of the same random-init cell differ by 0.030 on average (Appendix F). The largest difference between random-init draws is 0.115, for LaBraM on APAVA (0.857 against 0.741), so a single draw bounds a frozen gain only to that precision. A second check cuts the label budget to 10% of the training set and finds no low-label advantage (Appendix J). A gap that grows by more than 0.02 over its full-budget value occurs in 5 of 18 cells on the seed mean, and only 2 of those are positive at the low budget. Under a frozen probe, then, much of what reads as a pretrained representation is already present at random initialisation, which is why the twin has to be reported with every cell (Figure 4). 

4.6 Fine-tuning gains mostly sit within seed noise 

Of the 30 pairs, 23 have a positive mean gain after full fine-tuning, but only 5 positive pairs are clear of twice their seed standard deviation and 1 negative pair is (Figure 5, Table 3). Paired over seeds, the 95% interval of the gain excludes zero in 7 positive and 2 negative of the 30 cells. After Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all cells (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995), 5 cells remain, 2 of them on the TUH-derived benchmarks that overlap most pretraining corpora. These gains are also easy to misread from one seed, as LaBraM on 9TUSL gives −0.060 at one seed but +0.059 ±0.075 over 5, and CBraMod on BCI IV 2a moves from +0.102 to +0.052 ±0.030. Fine-tuning therefore buys a clear gain only in isolated cells, spread over 3 models with no model clear on more than 2 of the five original benchmarks (Figure 5). The clear negative pair, LUNA on APAVA at −0.290 ±0.035, persists under three shared learning rates (−0.184, −0.287 and −0.124, 5 seeds each), so the asymmetric schedule did not manufacture it (Appendix I). A fresh asymmetric run of that pair gives −0.272, within seed noise of the matrix value. Its frozen-probe gain is fragile under validation selection: +0.011 or +0.104 in two runs, because near-tied validation values pick different stages, and +0.040 test-selected. At the full label budget, though, every reading finds the pretrained representation no worse than its random-init twin, so the loss appears only once the weights are trained. That frozen-positive, fine-tuning-negative label is the only one of the four modes stable across thresholds (3 pairs at every threshold, while both-positive moves between 12 and 17; Appendix L). All of these gains were measured under one shared schedule. Tuning the learning rate per model on validation would have added +0.031 on average (range −0.019 to +0.148 over 15 pairs at one seed, Appendix H). The largest penalty falls on the in-house model on APAVA, so the canonical numbers are a modest lower bound. 

4.7 Nonlinear readouts recover little 

Replacing mean pooling by a trainable attention-pooling layer in the style of the models’ own heads does not recover the output layer (Appendix G). Run for two models on the three leakage-free benchmarks, its score minus the mean-pool probe lies between −0.069 and −0.007 in all 6 cells. The other readout, a one-hidden-layer MLP probe, lifts the output stage by more than three points in at most 1 of 18 cells at any of 3 seeds (Appendix G). The losses measured by the linear probe are thus not an artefact of mean pooling or of linearity (Appendix G). 

## 5 Discussion 

5.1 Reporting practices 

The results suggest four habits for anyone who evaluates an EEG foundation model. First, report the matched random-init encoder and band power on the same split for every cell (Section 4.5). Without the random-init twin 3 of 6 sleep cells would read as pretraining gains, and without band power 6 of 6 emotion cells would read as usable representations. Second, select the probed layer on validation and publish the full layer-wise curve, because test selection added +0.034 on average here (Section 4.2). Third, report seeds together with what they vary, since a fixed split turns repeated frozen probes into replicates and a single seed can reverse the sign of a fine-tuning gain (Section 4.6). Fourth, present shared-protocol numbers as the floor of a fair comparison, not as a ranking of architectures, and report the tuning penalty where it can be measured (Musgrave et al., 2020; Pineau et al., 2018). None of the four needs new data or large compute, because random-init, band power and the validation split are already part of any evaluation that reports a pretrained model. 

5.2 Scope of the conclusions 

The results do not establish a causal ranking of pretraining objectives or input-handling designs, because in a panel of six those choices are confounded with corpus, tuning and implementation (Appendix O). Nor do they cover every EEG paradigm, since the added tasks enter in reduced single-window form and clinical event detection and sleep sequence models lie outside the protocol (Section 3.3). Finally, they do not show that the diagnostic protocol helps developers of other models, since it was used during development on one model only (Section 3.5). 

5.3 Limitations 

The evaluation covers five public checkpoints and the in-house model, one linear-probe family with two nonlinear checks, one fine-tuning recipe and five-second single windows (Section 3.4). The original matrix also overlaps with pretraining data, since five of the six models and the in-house corpus contain TUH-derived 10 recordings (Obeid & Picone, 2016). TUSL and TUAB may therefore have been seen in pretraining, which is why the added task families were chosen outside TUH (Section 3.3). That released matrix also still uses test-selected stages in two places, the frozen probe on TUSL and TUAB and the layer-wise re-probe after fine-tuning, which we cite only as upper bounds (Appendix M). The in-house front-end did not implement its design, and we report the model as released rather than retrain it with corrected filters, so the in-house rows describe a model that sees mostly sub-hertz content (Section 3.2). A limit of its own comes from how the protocol was produced. The probe protocol was fixed while diagnosing the in-house model, whose iterations were judged on held-out pretraining windows and on Lee2019. The in-house Lee2019 cells therefore carry developer contact, and the validation rule and the added tasks came after the first results were seen. This makes the study exploratory rather than pre-registered, and its conclusions are scoped to the tested panel and protocols (Pineau et al., 2018). 

## 6 Conclusion 

With a matched random twin, a matched band-power baseline and a validation-selected layer in place, most of the gains that EEG foundation models report under their own protocols shrink or vanish in this panel. Fine-tuning gains mostly sit within seed noise, and the one clearly negative pair survives matched learning rates. The four practices cost little, and the per-cell results are released at https: //github.com/fbdeme/eeg-fm-controlled-audit so that each claim here can be checked against its own numbers. The natural next step is to run the same controls during pretraining, where they can still change a design.

## O Input handling as a hypothesis 

The fine-tuning tally follows input handling only loosely, as the full-resolution tokenisers (Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025) reach 5, 5 and 4 positive datasets of five, the channel-compressed LUNA and LuMamba (Döner et al., 2025; Broustail et al., 2026) 3 and 3, and the fixed-vocabulary EEGPT (Wang et al., 2024) 3 (Table 3). Corpus, tuning maturity and implementation maturity vary together with input handling in this panel, so the ordering is a hypothesis for a study that varies input handling alone (Döner et al., 2025; Jaegle et al., 2021). 21
