# NeuroCodec: EEG-Guided Latent-Domain Generative Model for High-Fidelity Target Speaker Extraction 

Julian Silva , Jaliya Kodithuwakku , Navindu Sirimanna , Shaveen Herath ,Wageesha N. Manamperi , Member, IEEE , and Joshua P. Kulasingham , Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Driven by recent advancements in hearing assistive devices, target speaker extraction (TSE) in multi-talker environ-ments has drawn significant attention. To address this, hearing technologies identify attention using neural signals recorded via electroencephalography (EEG) to guide TSE, enabling neuro-steered auditory attention decoding. However, most existing techniques rely on time-frequency masking, which suffers from low fidelity in spectro-temporally overlapping regions and is often restricted to narrowband audio. In contrast, we introduce 

NeuroCodec , which, to the best of our knowledge, is the first generative EEG-guided TSE framework operating in the latent space of a neural audio codec. Specifically, NeuroCodec models spatial EEG dependencies via graph convolutions and fuses cross-modal EEG and speech representations through attention mechanisms and Mamba-based temporal modeling. We further introduce a six-phase training curriculum that progressively incorporates latent reconstruction, multi-scale spectral, and ad-versarial objectives to ensure stable, high-fidelity speech gen-eration. Experiments on the public Cocktail Party and KUL datasets show NeuroCodec outperforms baselines, across several perceptual metrics. A listening experiment with 20 participants validated that NeuroCodec offers greater perceptual accuracy, achieving a mean score of 82.24 compared with 49.63 for M3ANet. Additionally, the proposed method is seen to enable 44.1 kHz reconstruction with spectral content extending up to 20 kHz, further supporting the perceptual results. Finally, we extend NeuroCodec with a causal streaming formulation suited for real-time processing, providing a basis for hearing assistive devices. 

Index Terms —Target speaker extraction, audio codec, gen-erative speech reconstruction, electroencephalography (EEG), hearing assistive devices

However, this selective auditory attention is severely compromised in listeners with hearing impairments [3], lead-ing to degraded speech intelligibility and elevated cognitive load [4], [5]. While advanced hearing aid technologies and digital acoustic prostheses have successfully restored broad auditory accessibility for millions of individuals, enabling these devices to operate effectively in dynamic, multi-speaker

scenes remains a critical hurdle. For modern speech enhance-ment systems, one fundamental challenge lies not merely in isolating overlapping speech audio, but in decoding the listener’s attention to determine which speaker to extract from the acoustic mixture. Target Speaker Extraction (TSE) aims to address this prob-lem by isolating a desired speaker from a speech mixture using auxiliary information about the target speaker [6]. Re-cent approaches exploit a variety of cues, including acous-tic embeddings, directional or binaural spatial cues [7], [8], and visual signals [9], [10], to guide the extraction process and improve robustness in challenging acoustic environments. Although these approaches have achieved strong extraction performance, they rely on external reference information that may be unavailable or unreliable in dynamic real-world envi-ronments. An alternative approach is to decode the attentional target from the listener’s brain activity, which has been shown to be strongly correlated with the attended speech signal. Several studies in the field of auditory neuroscience have demonstrated that the electroencephalography (EEG) signal tracks the at-tended speech, which is also called neural speech tracking [11], [12]. Consequently, EEG serves as a direct, non-invasive, and robust indicator of attention, enabling TSE without re-lying on restrictive external cues. While early computational approaches leveraged this neural-acoustic correlation solely to detect the attended speaker [13] without speech extraction, a task known as Auditory Attention Decoding (AAD), recent advances have established integrated neuro-steered TSE frame-works [14]–[17]. By utilizing the continuous EEG signal as the reference cue, these systems directly isolate and reconstruct the attended speech waveform from the multi-talker acoustic mixture [18]. Neuro-steered TSE can be categorized into two main pro-cessing paradigms: offline and online extraction. Offline neuro-steered TSE processes complete audio-EEG sequence before producing the target speech, allowing the model to exploit full temporal context during extraction. Early work, such as brain-informed speech separation (BISS) [18], used an EEG-decoded speech envelope to guide TSE. The brain-assisted speech enhancement network (BASEN) [19] later introduced convolutional cross-attention for direct EEG-guided speech enhancement, while NeuroHeed [15] employed self-attention-based EEG encoding and an end-to-end extraction framework. NeuroHeed+ [17] extended this approach through joint au-ditory attention detection and extraction. NeuroSpex+ [16] further improves extraction through dual-task training on speech-envelope and waveform objectives and is evaluated arXiv:2610.29815v1 [eess.AS] 21 Oct 2026 2

under a subject-independent (SI) setting, where test subjects are unseen during training. Although SI evaluation is more challenging than subject-dependent (SD) testing, it provides a rigorous measure of cross-subject generalization for practical deployment. More recently, M3ANet [14] incorporated graph-based EEG modeling and GroupMamba blocks. Despite these architectural advances, the inherent reliance on offline processing renders these models unsuitable for real-time deployment in hearing assistive devices that require con-tinuous, low-latency operation. To bridge this gap, online pro-cessing paradigms handle the input audio stream using short, overlapping causal windows. NeuroHeed [15] introduced an online formulation for causal neuro-steered TSE, while CA-NeuroSpex [20] more recently explored a related causal set-ting. However, causal neuro-steered extraction remains highly challenging, with existing online systems generally showing lower performance than their offline counterparts [15]. Taken together, prior work leaves two main gaps. First, existing EEG-guided TSE systems still rely on discriminative mixture-domain mask prediction [14], [15]. When target and interfering speech overlap, an inaccurate mask may retain components of the interfering speaker, resulting in speaker leakage. Second, because prior systems have been evaluated at limited audio sampling rates, at a maximum of 8 kHz or 14.7 kHz [14], [15], their ability to reconstruct wider-band attended speech has not been established. This narrowband constraint yields an output with a muffled, robotic acoustic quality that fails to meet the perceptual naturalness required for real-world applications. Generative approaches have recently shown promising per-formance in speech enhancement and for TSE conditioned on acoustic or visual cues [21]–[23]. However, these models are computationally intensive, rely on slow iterative generation, and typically benefit from large-scale training data. These re-quirements are particularly challenging for EEG-guided TSE, where paired EEG-speech datasets are limited and low-latency processing is desirable. To address these constraints, neural audio codecs such as SoundStream [24], EnCodec [25], and the Descript Audio Codec (DAC) [26], offer an alternative by using Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) to compress high-rate audio into compact, low-frame-rate representations that can be reconstructed by a learned decoder. Predicting these target speech latents fundamentally transforms the extraction task from discriminative mixture-domain mask estimation to direct, generative latent synthesis. In this paper, we propose NeuroCodec , a generative neuro-steered TSE framework that addresses the limitations of time-frequency mask estimation using a generative neural audio codec. Specifically, the framework predicts target speech representations within the latent space of a frozen, pre-trained DAC [26]. This generative formulation aims to reduce masking-related artifacts such as spectral holes and residual speaker leakage, while enabling substantially wider-band re-construction. Unlike prior neuro-steered systems limited to an output bandwidth of approximately 4-7 kHz, NeuroCodec 

reconstructs spectral content up to 20 kHz. Cross-modal at-tention and Mamba-based temporal modeling fuse EEG and mixture audio representations at the compact codec frame rate. To the best of our knowledge, there has not been prior work on generative EEG-guided TSE frameworks. Moreover, we present OnlineNeuroCodec , a causal streaming extension tailored for real-time hearing assistive devices. Our main contributions are as follows:  

> •

A generative EEG-guided target speech extraction frame-work, NeuroCodec , that predicts target speech in the latent space of a neural audio codec, featuring a six-phase training curriculum to stabilize latent, spectral, and adversarial objectives while mitigating traditional time-frequency masking artifacts.  

> •

A causal streaming extension, OnlineNeuroCodec , in-corporating previous target-latent estimates through an auditory attractor to enable real-time, neuro-steered ex-traction.  

> •

A comprehensive experimental evaluation under the SI setting on the Cocktail Party [27] and KUL [13] datasets, featuring objective evaluations, ablation studies, stream-ing benchmarks, spectrogram analysis, and a perceptual listening study demonstrating reconstruction of spectral content up to approximately 20 kHz. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion II introduces the proposed NeuroCodec framework, Sec-tion III presents OnlineNeuroCodec , the causal extension for online streaming. Section IV describes the implementation details and datasets, Section V presents the results and dis-cussion, and Section VI is the conclusion. II. N EURO CODEC FRAMEWORK 

A. Model Overview 

We formulate the TSE problem as a generative mapping from a multi-speaker mixture and multi-channel EEG sig-nals to the target speaker’s latent representations, which are subsequently decoded into a high-fidelity speech waveform. By predicting these latents within the structured space of a frozen pretrained DAC [26], the framework shifts from mixture-domain interference suppression to cross-modal latent alignment. This enables the model to leverage pretrained perceptual priors for speech extraction while focusing its learning capacity on EEG-guided attention detection. The codec’s decoder is utilized for final waveform synthesis. As shown in Fig. 1 the architecture consists of four stages: (i) audio encoder, (ii) EEG encoder, (iii) cross-modal selective state space block (CM-S3), and (iv) codec latent generator and decoder. The complete offline training algorithm is provided in the supplementary materials. 

B. Audio Encoder 

A pretrained and frozen DAC [26] encoder Ec maps the mixture waveform x ∈ RB×1×T to a compact latent rep-resentation Zmix ∈ RB×D×Tc , where B is the batch size, 

T is the number of waveform samples, Tc is the number of latent samples, and D = 1024 is the codec latent dimension size. The compact codec representation substantially reduces the temporal resolution relative to the raw audio, enabling efficient subsequent EEG-audio fusion. The DAC encoder remains frozen throughout training to preserve its pretrained acoustic representation. 3

C. EEG Encoder 

Adapted from M3ANet [14], the EEG encoder applies third order Chebyshev graph convolutions [28] to model spatial dependencies across electrodes. Unlike prior approaches that upsample EEG toward the audio rate [14]–[16], NeuroCodec operates at the compact codec latent rate (50 or 86 Hz), close to the native 128 Hz EEG resolution, thereby avoiding the initial upsampling and substantially reducing interpolation and computational overhead. The graph features are then processed by parallel 1-D convolutions with kernel sizes 3, 7, and 11, followed by a temporal encoder stack of 1-D convolutions to capture deeper sequential dependencies, that yields a latent output ¯E ∈ RB×H×T ′

D. Cross Modal Selective State Space Block 

Positional encodings are then injected into both modalities and the temporal sequences are aligned via multi-head cross-attention [29], defined as 

Z1 = Zmix + Attn(LN( Zmix ), LN( ¯E), LN( ¯E)) (1) where LN (·) denotes layer normalization and Attn (·, ·, ·)

denotes cross-modal attention with inputs of queries, keys and values. The acoustic tokens act as queries, while the normalized EEG features serve as keys and values to steer the extraction. Long-range temporal dependencies are subse-quently modeled using a Mamba block [30], defined as 

Ztgt = Z1 + Mamba(LN( Z1)) (2) By replacing self-attention with selective state-space modeling, Mamba provides efficient linear-time temporal modeling of codec-latent sequences. 

E. Codec Latent Generator and Decoder 

The output of the four stacked CM-S3 blocks is projected back to the codec latent dimension to yield the continuous tar-get latents ˜Ztgt ∈ RB×D×Tc . These predicted latents are then quantized using the residual vector quantization (RVQ) [26] codebooks ( Q1-Q9) of the frozen DAC, yielding the discrete latent representation defined as ˆZtgt = Q(˜Ztgt ), where Q

denotes the quantization operation. Finally, a pretrained and frozen DAC decoder Dc generates the target speech waveform from these quantized latents, providing the target reconstruc-tion defined as ˆs = Dc(ˆZtgt ).

F. Loss Functions 

Unlike conventional models that optimize scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR) loss directly on the decoded audio [14]–[16], [18], Neurocodec is trained using a composite objective function for accurate latent alignment and high-fidelity acoustic reconstruction. Reconstruction loss, Lrecon , is computed as the mean squared error between the predicted and ground-truth target continuous latents and is used to guide the model to reconstruct the target speaker representation in the codec latent space. However, reconstruction loss alone can yield perceptual ar-tifacts. Spectral loss, Lmel [26], [31], improves spectral fidelity by minimizing the L1 distance between log-Mel spectrograms at multiple resolutions. We use seven scales up to 20 kHz for the Cocktail Party [27] dataset and four scales up to 4 kHz for KUL dataset [13] to capture both transient and steady-state spectral characteristics across their respective bandwidths. To further improve perceptual realism, we use an adversarial ensemble of Multi-Period Discriminators (MPD) [32] to model waveform periodicity and a Multi-band Multi-scale Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) Discriminator (MBMSD) [26] to enforce multi-band spectral consistency. The generator is optimized using hinge adversarial loss Ladv and L1 feature-matching loss Lfm on intermediate discriminator features, while the discriminators are trained with the standard hinge objective. 

G. Multi-phase Training Curriculum 

The overall generator objective is given by, 

LG = λrecon Lrecon + λmel Lmel + λadv Ladv + λfm Lfm , (3) where λrecon , λmel , λadv , and λfm are weighting coefficients balancing their respective loss terms throughout training. We set λrecon = 1 to be fixed throughout training. 4

To preserve GAN stability and mitigate mode collapse during training, we employ a six-phase curriculum that pro-gressively introduces spectral and adversarial objectives. In Phase 1, the model optimizes exclusively Lrecon until the validation latent mean squared error (MSE) falls below 0.87, establishing a stable latent mapping prior to multi-modal supervision. Phase 2 gradually introduces the Mel loss ( Lmel ), ramping λmel from 2 to 15 to progressively incorporate spectral supervision without destabilizing the learned latent representation. Upon reaching λmel = 15 , Phase 3 maintains joint latent and spectral optimization until the validation loss decreases for at least one epoch. In Phase 4, the generator parameters are frozen for 2 epochs to allow warm-up pre-training of the discriminators, providing stable adversarial supervision. Phase 5 then jointly optimizes the generator and discriminators, progressively increasing (λadv , λ fm ) in steps of 

(0 .25 , 0.5) until the target values of (1 .0, 2.0) are reached. Fi-nally, Phase 6 conducts full adversarial training until validation performance plateaus, employing a ReduceLROnPlateau 

scheduler with patience of 3 epochs and a reduction factor of 0.5. In the following section, we extend this framework into a causal streaming formulation, OnlineNeuroCodec , which integrates an auditory attractor mechanism to enable real-time target speech extraction. III. C AUSAL STREAMING EXTENSION OF NEURO CODEC

OnlineNeuroCodec . Training follows the streaming setting by applying the reconstruction and spectral objectives of Section II-F to each output hop. IV. D ATASETS AND EXPERIMENTS 

In this section, we detail the experimental setup and the datasets used to evaluate the proposed method. 

A. Datasets 

To evaluate our proposed method, we used two publicly available multi-speaker EEG-audio datasets: Cocktail Party and KUL. The Cocktail Party dataset [27] includes EEG recordings from 33 subjects (128 channels, 512 Hz) who completed 30 dichotic listening trials (60 s each), attending to one of two simultaneous narrative speech streams presented to opposite ears. The KUL data set [13] consists of 16 subjects with normal-hearing with 20 trials each; in our experiments, we used the first eight trials (similar to prior work for fair comparison [15], [16]), such that subjects do not attend to the same speech stimulus repeatedly, where participants selectively attended one of two concurrent Dutch narrative stories presented dichotically. 

B. Implementation Details 

EEG signals are sampled at 128 Hz and were band-pass filtered between 0.1-45 Hz, followed by ICA-based artifact removal [36]. All experiments use subject independent (SI) evaluation, where test subjects are unseen during training, providing a more stringent assessment of cross-subject gen-eralization. For the Cocktail Party dataset, EEG is additionally re-referenced to the average mastoids. Subjects are randomly split with a fixed seed into 23/5/5, training/validation/test subjects, and the same SI split is used for all compared models. For fair comparison with M3ANet [14], BASEN [19], and MSFNet [37], trials are segmented into 2s non-overlapping windows. The native 44.1 kHz audio is processed using the pretrained 44.1 kHz DAC encoder [26], which has a down-sampling factor of 512 and yields an approximately 86 Hz latent frame rate. For KUL, we use the preprocessed data released with NeuroHeed [15] and perform 16-fold SI cross-validation. For fair comparison with the SI NeuroSpex+ setting [16], recordings are segmented into 4s windows with a 1-s hop. The original 8 kHz audio is upsampled to 16 kHz and processed using the pretrained 16 kHz DAC encoder [26], which has a downsampling factor of 320 and yields a 50 Hz latent frame rate. Note that the difference in window sizes between the two datasets is to ensure fair comparison with the prior work on each dataset. Additional implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in the supplementary material. The source code will be released upon acceptance. 

C. Evaluation Metrics 

Performance is evaluated using standard objective speech enhancement metrics: Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI) [38], Extended Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (ESTOI) [39], and Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ) [40]. To ensure fair comparison with existing baselines that only operate at 8 kHz sample rate, all outputs are resampled to 8 kHz before objective evaluation. This prevents the wider output bandwidth of NeuroCodec from providing an inherent advantage in the reported objective metrics. V. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION

To understand the contribution of each architectural com-ponent, we conduct controlled ablation experiments on the KUL dataset. 2) EEG encoder guidance and components: Next, we val-idate the necessity and design of the EEG encoder. To assess the model’s dependence on EEG guidance, we replace the EEG input with Gaussian noise. C. Perceptual Evaluation 

To verify the perceptual quality of the generated out-put, we conducted a controlled multi-stimulus listening test with 20 participants. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the University of Moratuwa (No EDN/2025/012) and all participants provided written informed consent. We used web Multiple Stimulus with Hidden Refer-ence and Anchor (MUSHRA) [43] to compare NeuroCodec’s native 44.1kHz output with the native output of the strongest reproducible baseline, M3ANet [14]. The Cocktail Party dataset was used; KUL was excluded because NeuroSpex+ [16] has no public implementation. Eight 2s samples were randomly selected from the held-out test set.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper we introduce NeuroCodec, a generative EEG-guided target speaker extraction framework that predicts latent representations of a frozen high-fidelity audio codec instead of estimating time-frequency masks. By combining graph-based EEG modeling, cross-modal attention, and Mamba-based temporal modeling, NeuroCodec achieves effective EEG-audio fusion in a compact latent space. Experiments on two datasets demonstrate improved speech intelligibility and perceptual quality over existing approaches, as measured by both objective metrics and a subjective listening study. NeuroCodec also enables 44.1kHz reconstruction with spectral content extending to approximately 20 kHz with minimal speaker interference, unlike prior work with limited output bandwidth and speaker leakage. We extend the model to a causal streaming version, OnlineNeuroCodec, which enables streaming TSE using a causal auditory attractor derived from previous target estimates. This model achieves vastly superior computational speed while retaining competitive extraction quality. These results demonstrate the potential of generative latent modeling for neuro-steered speech extraction and future assistive hearing systems. However, OnlineNeuroCodec, like most other contemporary models, remains far from deploy-ment on embedded hearing-aid hardware. Future work will explore autoregressive codec-token prediction for fully causal generation with reduced buffering requirements, potentially lowering algorithmic latency, alongside model compression and low-power implementation. 8
