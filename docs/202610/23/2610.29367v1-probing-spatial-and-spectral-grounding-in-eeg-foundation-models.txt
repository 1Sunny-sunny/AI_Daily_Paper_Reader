ABSTRACT 

EEG foundation models are advancing at an unprecedented pace, yet their evaluation is driven by downstream accuracy, which re-veals little about whether their predictions rely on physiologically interpretable signal structure. We introduce a set of perturbation probes in two grounding axes: spatial grounding in channel iden-tity and topographic locality, and spectral grounding in oscillatory content and waveform morphology. We apply these probes to five published EEG foundation models across motor-imagery and seizure recordings. Our experiments reveal differences in both representa-tional sensitivity and downstream reliance. Electrode identity may be encoded without influencing downstream classification: permut-ing LaBraM’s positional codes heavily displaced its embeddings yet reduced motor-imagery accuracy by only 1.2 points, while spectral details may be discriminable without producing specific responses to simulated clinical markers, as only REVE exceeded a spectral baseline on this task. These dissociations are hidden in common benchmarks, demonstrating how grounding probes can complement performance-based evaluation by revealing what EEG foundation models encode and what their predictions depend on. 

Index Terms — Electroencephalography, EEG foundation mod-els, perturbation, spatial grounding, spectral grounding 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Electroencephalography (EEG) offers a non-invasive, low-cost mea-sure of brain activity. EEG foundation models (EEG-FMs), trained on thousands of unlabeled recordings, have achieved state-of-the-art performance across domains ranging from motor imagery and sleep staging to emotion recognition and clinical event detection [1, 2, 3]. However, task performance alone does not guarantee that a model captures properties used in expert EEG interpretation. Such interpre-tation often relies on spatial context across electrodes and waveform characteristics when spectral information alone is insufficient. For example, clinicians distinguish muscle contamination from epilepti-form activity using the spatial distribution and morphology of a dis-charge, despite overlapping spectral properties and the dominance of muscle activity in scalp power [4]. More generally, electrode lo-cation carries physiologically meaningful information [5, 6], while clinically relevant EEG phenomena are often defined by waveform morphology [7]. We refer to these complementary requirements as 

spatial and spectral grounding, where spectral grounding encom-passes sensitivity not only to oscillatory power, but also to phase-dependent temporal structure and waveform morphology. Such grounding may not be apparent from conventional down-stream benchmarks: accuracy indicates how often an adapted model is correct, but not which signal properties support its predictions. This ambiguity is particularly relevant to EEG because recordings

contain limited high-level semantic structure and are affected by environmental noise, device-specific artifacts, and between-subject variability. Consequently, competitive performance may be achieved partly through artifact-related shortcuts. Conversely, a representa-tion may preserve meaningful spatial or spectro-temporal informa-tion that a downstream prediction head does not use. We therefore ask three separate questions: Can the architecture access a signal property? Is that property encoded in the learned representation? And do downstream predictions rely on it? We investigate these distinctions through controlled perturbation-response experiments. To evaluate spatial grounding, we intervene on channel-to-location assignments by independently manipulating electrode identity, channel order, and learned positional encod-ing. Spectral probes test whether models distinguish recordings from surrogates that preserve power spectra while disrupting phase-dependent temporal structure, and whether their representations decode injected oscillations across canonical frequency bands. We further test whether representational sensitivity affects downstream predictions. We applied this framework to five EEG-FMs: BIOT [8], LaBraM [9], CBraMod [1], CSBrain [10], and REVE [2], and relate their observed dissociations to differences in model architecture and pre-training. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce spatial interventions that disentangle electrode identity and positional encoding, and characterize distance-dependent cross-channel influence across model depth. • We introduce spectral interventions that evaluate sensitivity to phase-dependent structure beyond spectral power and to os-cillatory activity across canonical frequency bands, calibrated against handcrafted, non-learned baselines. • We show that architectural access to signal information, its preservation in learned representations, and its use in down-stream predictions are distinct properties that cannot be in-ferred from performance benchmarks alone. 

Evaluation Studies : Prior evaluation studies have found incon-sistent gains over lighter supervised decoders: fine-tuned on mem-ory and sleep benchmarks, EEG-FMs improve over these baselines by around 1% while carrying millions of additional parameters [11], whereas when the labels are scarce they do not outperform lighter decoders [12, 13]. Evaluation has accordingly moved past accu-racy metrics. Benchmarking EEG-FMs on a suite of eight datasets yielded attribution maps that stay spatially stable under perturbation while the predictions degrade [14]. On the spectral side, a recent qualitative study injected synthetic oscillations and found that em-beddings were dominated by the aperiodic power spectrum, with the oscillatory components increasingly underrepresented toward higher frequencies [15]. It remains unclear whether representations are grounded in interpretable EEG properties, such as electrode location or waveform structure beyond spectral power. arXiv:2610.29367v1 [cs.NE] 21 Oct 2026 Foundation models for EEG. EEG-FMs pretrain encoders on large unlabeled corpora and adapt them to diverse downstream tasks [16], differing in input tokenization and in how they encode electrode topology. We select 5 publicly released models that jointly cover the main design choices. BIOT [8] tokenizes a fixed bipo-lar montage as STFT magnitudes indexed by learned slot tokens. 

LaBraM [9] trains a quantization-based tokenizer whose targets are the Fourier amplitude and phase, then re-trains the encoder on masked code prediction. CBraMod [1] adds a magnitude-spectrum pathway and applies a depthwise convolution over the channel axis, consuming order but not electrode identity. CSBrain [10] shares that magnitude pathway while grouping electrodes into anatomical regions, thus attending over coarse topography. Finally, REVE [2] embeds raw temporal patches and conditions attention directly on continuous 3D electrode coordinates. The models further differ in size, ranging from 3.2M parameters for BIOT to 69 M for REVE, and are trained on corpora ranging from a few to thousands of hours. 

2. DATA AND PRE-PROCESSING 

We used two datasets that differ in montage, environment, and task. 

PhysioNet-MI [17, 18] is a 4-class motor imagery corpus with 109 participants at 64 channels, of which we only probe the 20 partici-pants (1,805 samples) that define the class-balanced test set in prior studies [1] to avoid leakage. PhysioNet-MI is pre-processed in line with previous studies. Recordings are average-referenced, high-pass filtered at 0.3 Hz, notch filtered at 60 Hz, resampled to 200 Hz, and epoched from cue onset to 4 s without baseline correction. 

Siena Scalp [19, 18] provides a 2-class clinical setting of seizure versus non-seizure windows, recorded from 14 participants with 29 electrodes. Recordings are band-passed at 0.1–75 Hz, notch filtered at 50 Hz, and resampled to 200 Hz. Our probing uses a subject-stratified balanced subset of 374 windows of 10 s from the 4 held-out subjects from [1, 10]. Across both corpora, the cached waveforms remain unnormalized so that each encoder applies its own input scal-ing. Electrode coordinates are drawn from the standard 10–05 mon-tage, mean-centered, and divided by the largest norm. All five EEG-FMs are kept frozen throughout our protocol and only linear readouts are probed. Unless noted, f (X) denotes penul-timate encoder activations: tokens are pooled within output channel and the channel vectors concatenated. BIOT is probed over the 16 bipolar derivations, each assigned the midpoint of its electrode pair. 

3. SPATIAL GROUNDING 

Each EEG channel reflects a position-dependent mixture of under-lying neural sources [5]. We therefore test whether pretrained model representations retain channel identity and topographic locality, and whether sensitivity to the spatial location may influence downstream predictions. EEG-FMs encode this information differently: BIOT assigns learned channel-slot tokens, LaBraM indexes electrode names, CSBrain groups electrodes into anatomical regions, while REVE explicitly encodes positional embeddings of 3D coordinates on the scalp. CBraMod on the other hand operates on a fixed or-der without explicit electrode identities. We design three probes to distinguish these mechanisms and test their downstream influence.

4. SPECTRAL GROUNDING 

EEG-FMs are commonly pretrained through masked reconstruction in either the temporal or spectral domain [9, 2, 20], encouraging them to follow the dominant 1/f aperiodic spectrum [15]. Clin-ically relevant information, however, also depends on oscillatory activity and waveform structure not determined by spectral power alone [21]. The five considered models impose different spectral biases: BIOT consumes only STFT magnitudes, LaBraM trains its tokenizer against Fourier amplitude and phase, CBraMod and CSBrain add magnitude-spectrum projections to their embeddings, while REVE operates solely on temporal patches. We design three probes for information beyond phase-free power, oscillatory sensi-tivity, and decision-level specificity to clinical morphology. Fig. 1 . SPA1 — Sensitivity to spatial encoding. Normalized la-tent displacement after permuting electrode labels while holding the waveforms fixed ( Sid ; left) and after breaking the correspondence between signals and their location ( Ssig ; right). 

4.1. Spectral Probes SPE1: Information beyond spectral power. For each record-ing, we generate an iterative amplitude-adjusted Fourier-transform (IAAFT) surrogate [22] that preserves each channel’s amplitude distribution and approximately its power spectrum. Phases are ran-domized with the same random phase sequence across channels, approximately preserving inter-channel phase differences [23]. Alinear head is then trained to distinguish original from surrogate embeddings; above-chance accuracy indicates that the representa-tion encodes waveform structure beyond amplitude distribution and power spectrum. Because IAAFT leaves a small spectral residual and removes nonstationary power fluctuations, we benchmark each encoder against phase-free spectral power on the same task. 

SPE2: Frequency-specific oscillatory sensitivity. We inject a narrowband oscillation into one of four canonical bands, namely delta, theta, alpha, or beta (ranges are given in Figure 4), and train a four-way linear head to identify the injected band from the latent dis-placement. Every channel receives an independent carrier produced by passing white Gaussian noise through a fourth-order Butterworth filter at the band boundaries. Injection power is measured against the recording’s original power in that same band, preventing the aperi-odic spectrum from disproportionately weighting frequencies. The latent displacement was also unit-normalized so that band identity cannot be inferred from displacement magnitude alone. 

SPE3: Sensitivity to clinical morphology. Finally, we test whether spectral sensitivity carries over to clinical predictions. A lin-ear seizure classifier is fit on clean Siena Scalp representations, and at test time we inject into held-out non-seizure segments (at 0 dB) either a rhythmic ictal discharge, a spike-wave pattern, or power-matched pink noise control. The ictal marker is a chirp decreasing from 10 to 3 Hz with increasing amplitude; the spike-wave marker repeats at 3 Hz, each event being a Gaussian-derivative spike with extrema 70 ms apart [7], followed after 100 ms by a 100 ms Gaussian slow wave. Each waveform is injected identically into a contiguous 20–40% channel subset formed by a seed electrode and its nearest neighbors. We report specificity as the mean change in P (seizure) 

for the two epileptiform patterns minus that for pink noise.

5. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 5.1. Spatial grounding 

The spatial probes reveal distinct mechanisms for representing channel location. EEG-FMs with explicit positional encoding were largely invariant to channel order, whereas CBraMod was built di-rectly to process it. Among identity-aware models, BIOT’s learned slot tokens produced the largest identity displacement (0.687 on PhysioNet-MI, 0.517 on Siena Scalp) whereas REVE’s 3D coor-dinates produced the smallest (0.272 on PhysioNet-MI, 0.140 on Siena Scalp). Breaking the signal-to-location correspondence pro-duced a similar Ssig ordering on both datasets, reflecting consistent model-specific sensitivity across domains (Fig. 1), with lower Siena values consistent with its fewer channels. SPA2 also found spatial locality to be strongly depth-dependent, as shown in Fig. 2. Perturbations in REVE and LaBraM affected nearby electrodes more than distant ones ( ρ = -0.829 and -0.811, respectively), with the strongest topographic organization appear-ing in intermediate layers for the latter. CSBrain and CBraMod showed little distance structure, while BIOT was approximately flat. The task-level intervention on PhysioNet-MI further separated representational sensitivity from functional reliance (Table 1). Permuting positional codes substan-tially altered the representations of REVE, LaBraM, and CSBrain, but LaBraM did not show a corresponding loss in motor-imagery accuracy. BIOT’s large SPA1 identity displacement could not be as-sessed at the decision level, as its readout performed near chance. 

5.2. Spectral grounding 

SPE1 showed that most EEG-FMs only partially retain structure beyond spectral power on the two corpora (Fig. 3). REVE and LaBraM in particular clearly distinguished EEG from the phase-randomized surrogate, whereas BIOT was marginally better than a phase-free control derived from spectral power alone. CSBrain and CBraMod scored at or below that baseline, consistent with representations dominated by their magnitude-spectrum pathways. Sensitivity to band injection in SPE2 followed a similar pattern, shown in Fig. 4. REVE and LaBraM were able to identify the injected frequency band at substantially lower perturbation levels than the other models, while CSBrain and CBraMod collapsed un-der weaker injections. Sensitivity was also frequency-dependent: CSBrain and CBraMod degraded most for weaker theta and alpha injections, while performance recovered in the beta band. Finally, according to SPE3, changes in seizure probability in the Siena Scalp test set were less specific than the embedding-level re-sults suggest (Table 2). All encoders increased their seizure-class probability following at least one epileptiform intervention, but sev-eral also responded strongly to matched-power pink noise. REVE was the only model for which noise moved predictions consistently away from the seizure class and whose epileptiform specificity ex-ceeded the log-PSD control. LaBraM responded to the perturbations almost as strongly as to noise, illustrating that spectral sensitivity alone does not establish clinically meaningful waveform selectivity. 

5.3. Representations do not predict decision reliance 

Together, our spatial and spectral probes reveal model-specific pro-files that are not recoverable from architecture descriptions or down-stream rankings alone. BIOT’s slot tokens provided the strongest channel identity displacement but these changes carried no measur-able decision-level reliance in this task. CSBrain relied on its region code for motor-imagery decisions despite showing little topographic locality, whereas CBraMod showed little sensitivity to either spatial or spectral conditioning. 6. CONCLUSION 

We introduced a set of perturbation probes to evaluate whether EEG foundation models encode and use physiologically meaningful spa-tial and spectral structure. Across five models, the resulting profiles were not determined by architecture or captured by downstream accuracy. Channel identity could be strongly encoded without topographic locality (BIOT) or without influencing classification (LaBraM), or be relied upon only at a coarse regional level (CS-Brain), while oscillatory sensitivity did not imply selectivity for pathological morphology. What a representation encodes therefore need not govern its predictions. That said, our analysis is constrained by the limited number of Siena Scalp participants and by using only linear readouts on frozen encoders, which capture only linearly ac-cessible information and may not reflect fully fine-tuned models. Broader ecological and clinical validation is therefore needed. 7. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS 

This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified human EEG data. No new human-participant data were collected. Ethical approval and informed consent for the original data collec-tions are documented in the corresponding dataset publications.
