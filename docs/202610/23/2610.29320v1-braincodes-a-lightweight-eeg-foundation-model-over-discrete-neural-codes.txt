# ABSTRACT 

Recent electroencephalography (EEG) foundation models increasingly emphasize model scale, potentially limiting their deployment on resource-constrained de-vices and in everyday environments. We argue that transferable EEG features can instead be learned with a compact architecture and introduce BrainCodes , a lightweight foundation model pretrained through self-supervised learning on more than 800 hours of recordings from over 1,000 participants. Our architecture com-bines a quantization-based neural tokenizer with a spatiotemporal encoder that supports arbitrary electrode configurations. The pretraining objective explicitly encourages representations that capture spatial connectivity through channel po-sitional encoding, and sensitivity to narrowband neural oscillations through an auxiliary training objective. Our model matches or surpasses the state of the art across diverse neurophysiological monitoring tasks, including sleep staging, atten-tion assessment, and cognitive load estimation. BrainCodes transfers effectively to low-resource wearable devices while using up to 97% fewer trainable parameters than comparable architectures. Beyond predictive accuracy, our evaluation frame-work characterizes the geometry and invariances of learned EEG representations across measurable spatial, temporal, and spectral signal properties. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent self-supervised models for electroencephalography (EEG) are typically trained on patch re-construction, either in the signal waveform or in a spectral representation (Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023a; El Ouahidi et al., 2025). This objective is inherited from language and computer vision, where a masked token can be reliably estimated from its local context. EEG however presents a different setting, in which a masked interval is only weakly constrained by its neighborhood, while neighboring electrodes often contain redundant mixtures of the same underly-ing sources due to volume conduction. Consequently, components that carry task-relevant properties may occupy small fraction of the total variance, so that a model need not preserve them to reduce re-construction error. Whether common EEG pretext tasks produce representations grounded in those properties is an empirical question that downstream benchmarks alone can only partially answer. Consider reconstruction under a squared-error objective in the frequency domain. EEG typically exhibits an aperiodic power spectrum S(f ) ∝ f −α (He, 2014; Donoghue et al., 2020a), and the contribution of a frequency band to the reconstruction loss depends on its residual power. High-power broadband components can therefore dominate the objective, whereas narrowband, oscilla-tory components may contribute relatively little to the optimization process. A pretrained model may consequently achieve low reconstruction error by accurately fitting only the aperiodic background, without faithfully preserving rhythmic structure. Spatial reconstruction presents an analogous ambi-guity. Scalp potentials are linear lead-field mixtures of underlying source currents, and their spatial autocorrelation is shaped not only by source dynamics but also by head geometry and electrode density (Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006). Because neighboring electrodes often contain redundant mix-tures, a masked channel can be approximated accurately from its neighbors; even spherical spline

Masked-channel reconstruction may therefore be solved partly through spatial interpolation without requiring the representation to encode physiologically informative spatial dynamics. Although these ambiguities are well established in neurophysiology, standard downstream bench-marks do not directly determine which properties of EEG signals are retained by a foundation model. We therefore introduce a compact model, trained to predict a finite set of discrete latent codes produced by a vector-quantized tokenizer. The quantizer allocates capacity according to a rate–distortion trade-off, concentrating representational capacity on distinctions between samples. Because this prediction objective is categorical rather than a squared error on the waveform level, its weighting is not directly governed by absolute signal power. To promote sensitivity to physiologi-cally meaningful oscillatory and spatial structure, we supplement masked-token prediction with an auxiliary objective based on localized narrowband perturbations, and we explicitly encode relative electrode distances as positional encoding. The central novelty is therefore an objective designed to preserve oscillatory and spatial structure without relying on scale. Our contributions are as follows: • A compact foundation model on top of discrete codes. BrainCodes combines a vector-quantized tokenizer and a series of factorized transformer layers trained on masked code prediction under an oscillation-injection auxiliary. With 1.9M trainable parameters in its backbone, our model achieves the highest mean performance among the compared models across 14 downstream benchmarks in both linear probing and full fine-tuning. • Transfer to sparse, consumer-grade montages. To our knowledge, we provide the first systematic evaluation of EEG foundation models on consumer-grade hardware, using three datasets spanning in-ear EEG and the Muse headband. BrainCodes substantially outper-forms the published baselines in these settings. Through controlled sparse-montage simula-tions, we further demonstrate that BrainCodes retains, on average, 91% of its full-montage performance when using only a sparse set of 4 electrodes. • Evidence of retained spatial and spectral structure. We introduce controlled probes that measure representational sensitivity to electrode geometry and spectral perturbations. Relative to the compared models, BrainCodes exhibits stronger geometry-consistent spatial locality and greater sensitivity to oscillatory components than to the aperiodic slope of the EEG spectrum. These results demonstrate that its representations retain measurable spatial and spectral properties of EEG beyond those captured by downstream performance alone. 

# 2 METHODS 

We introduce BrainCodes , a self-supervised EEG model designed to preserve measurable spatial and spectral properties of scalp-recorded signals in a reduced parameter budget. A frozen RVQ to-kenizer first converts each EEG channel into continuous embeddings associated with discrete code indices (Avramidis et al., 2025), and then a hierarchical factorized transformer is trained over those tokens under a masked-reconstruction primary objective and an oscillation-prediction auxiliary. A transformer U-Net backbone with factorized temporal and channel attention processes the RVQ em-beddings and predicts discrete code indices at masked positions. Auxiliary heads applied to its time-compressed bottleneck predict the frequency, duration, and spatial extent of synthetic narrowband perturbations. The resulting bottleneck features are used for all downstream tasks. # 3 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

3.1 PRETRAINING CORPUS 

BrainCodes is pretrained on the Healthy Brain Network EEG dataset (HBN-EEG) (Alexander et al., 2017), a developmental corpus of high-density recordings from children and adolescents acquired across multiple sites under a common protocol. We pretrain on a subset of HBN-EEG, comprising releases R3, R4, R7 and R8, which include about 820 hours from 1,131 participants. We chose this corpus because of its multiple axes of variability (age-wise, task-wise, and site-wise), and because of its dense 128-channel montage that allows training an extensive range of channel subsets. Each recording was preprocessed in accordance with the requirements of the frozen tokenizer. We band-pass filtered the waveforms between 0.5 and 75 Hz with a 60 Hz notch, interpolated flat chan-nels from their closest neighbors, resampled to 250 Hz, and robust-z-scored each channel indepen-dently. The only departure from the configuration of Avramidis et al. (2025) was the low-pass cutoff, which we moved from 100 to 75 Hz as it improved convergence without regressions in reconstruc-tion quality. Recordings were segmented into 30 s windows and tokenized once with BioCodec. The tokenizer emits a 128D embedding from the RVQ output at a code-frame rate of 21 Hz, so a 30 swindow yields T = 625 frames. Windows of randomized length (2 to 30 s) and channel set were drawn during training, for which we split a 20% participant-disjoint validation set. 5Preprint. Under review. 3.2 DOWNSTREAM DATASETS 

We evaluate our model on a total of 14 downstream tasks spanning three benchmark regimes (Ta-ble 1). The first is a standard suite used across the EEG foundation-model literature: abnormal-ity detection and event classification on the Temple University corpora, TUAB and TUEV (Obeid & Picone, 2016); sleep staging on ISRUC (Khalighi et al., 2016); seizure detection on Siena Scalp Database (Detti et al., 2020); motor imagery on PhysioNet-MI (Schalk et al., 2004) and BCI Competition IV-2a (Brunner et al., 2008); and cognitive load on the mental-arithmetic dataset (Zyma et al., 2019). The second is the N EURO RVQ benchmark suite (Barmpas et al., 2025), which adds High-Gamma motor (Schirrmeister et al., 2017), OpenBMI-ERP (Lee et al., 2019), working memory (Pavlov et al., 2022) and eyes-open/closed resting (Schalk et al., 2004), presented in Appendix B. The third targets consumer-grade hardware: in-ear-EEG sleep staging on EESM23 (Bjarke Mikkelsen et al., 2025) and two tasks recorded with the four-electrode Muse head-band (Elkaffas, 2024; Bird et al., 2018), which together let us test transfer to montages an order of magnitude sparser than the pretraining layout. Each dataset is processed by the same pipeline as the pretraining corpus, and electrode coordinates are resolved from the same standard montages, allowing one model to be fine-tuned across layouts of arbitrary number of electrodes without architectural changes. Splits are grouped so that no par-ticipant contributes to more than one partition. A representative selection of the aforementioned datasets is further used to quantify linear-probing robustness and the interpretability study. 3.3 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Baselines. We compare against five models, namely BIOT (Yang et al., 2023a), LaBraM (Jiang et al., 2024), CBraMod (Wang et al., 2025), CSBrain (Zhou et al., 2025) and REVE (El Ouahidi et al., 2025), spanning 3.2M to 69 M parameters. We additionally compare against EEGPT (Wang et al., 2024), NeuroGPT (Cui et al., 2024), and N EURO RVQ (Barmpas et al., 2025) on the sec-ond benchmark (Appendix B). On the standard suite and the N EURO RVQ benchmark we quote published numbers where a directly comparable protocol exists. The wearable benchmark has no extensive published baselines, hence we adopt those from Pradeepkumar et al. (2025) and addition-ally rerun the same models with the recipe used for BrainCodes. 

Hyperparameters. All downstream runs optimized on AdamW with a cosine-decay schedule and linear warmup, weight decay 0.01, label smoothing 0.1, and square-root inverse-frequency class weights. Peak learning rate was chosen between 10 −4 and 5 × 10 −4 across datasets, and training lasted 20 epochs for the majority of tasks (10 on TUAB, TUEV). Model selection was by balanced accuracy on the validation set. Every reported number is the mean over 3 seeds; cross-validation entries are additionally averaged over 4 folds in Section 4.3 and 5 folds in Appendix B. 

Evaluation. Both bottleneck features bc and ¯bc are assessed under two fine-tuning protocols. Under 

full fine-tuning , the U-Net backbone (excluding the tokenizer) is optimized end to end along with a linear head that reads {bc}Cc=1 or {¯bc}Cc=1 . This protocol establishes the benefit from the pretrained initialization. Under linear probing , the backbone is held frozen and a single linear head φ is fitted to the same bottleneck features. Any performance recovered in this regime is thus attributable to the pretrained encoder, which makes it the most representative test of representation quality. For a C-class downstream task we match the convention of the domain literature and report balanced accuracy (BAC) as the primary metric, together with AUROC and AUPRC when C = 2 , or Weighted F1 and Cohen’s κ when C > 2. To render results comparable across tasks with differing numbers of classes, we report a fair average model performance through chance-normalized BAC, i.e., 

nBAC = (BAC − 1/C) / (1 − 1/C) , (9) which maps chance-level performance to 0 and perfect classification to 1.

4.3 APPLICATION ON WEARABLE DEVICES 

To our knowledge, we provide the first benchmark evaluation of EEG foundation models on con-sumer wearable devices. Specifically, we evaluate transfer to two forms of consumer hardware: the four-channel Muse headband (TP9, AF7, AF8, TP10) and EESM23 sleep staging from in-ear elec-trodes (Bjarke Mikkelsen et al., 2025). All models were evaluated on identical splits, i.e., four-fold cross-validation over participants for the two Muse tasks and the fixed participant split of Pradeep-kumar et al. (2025) for EESM23, and the results are shared in Table 4. On the motor imagery task, BrainCodes reaches 0.5889 BAC against 0.5083 for the best baseline, whereas Muse-mental is a tie for all frontier models, as every model’s standard deviation exceeds the between-model spread. On EESM23, BrainCodes reaches 0.5254 against 0.4176 for REVE, i.e., a clear head over the strongest baseline. Ear-EEG is the setting furthest from the pretraining montage, yet a frozen linear probe still reached 0.4791, i.e., 91% of the full fine-tuning performance. 

# 5 GROUNDING ANALYSIS 

The downstream results establish that BrainCodes is transferable and parameter-efficient, but predic-tive performance alone cannot validate the motivation of its objective, i.e., whether the learned rep-resentation preserves oscillatory and spatial structure that typical reconstruction may underweight. The following analyses probe whether channel interactions follow electrode geometry and whether the representation preferentially exposes narrowband rhythms or merely broadband variation. 5.1 RETAINED SPATIAL DYNAMICS 

A desirable property of a foundation model for EEG is the ability to reflect spatial dependencies between channels that follow the scalp geometry. We test this explicitly through two properties: 

mixing , whether perturbing one electrode changes the representation of other channels at all (Fig-ure 2a), and locality , the rank correlation between the size of that change and inter-electrode distance (Figure 2b), which dissociate in practice (Figure 2c). CSBrain, BrainCodes and LaBraM propagate induced perturbations most widely across the montage, while REVE and CBraMod confine them more narrowly, as shown in the 2D channel matrices. 8Preprint. CBraMod and CSBrain inherit no grounded spatial structure, so their mixing spreads indiscriminately across the scalp rather than decaying with distance. BrainCodes and LaBraM respect scalp geometry the most, which we attribute to their explicit channel positional encoding. Because broadband aperiodic activity contributes substantial signal power, squared-error reconstruc-tion may prioritize it over lower-power oscillatory structure (Donoghue et al., 2020b; Kommineni et al., 2026). We tested this effect by injecting controlled perturbations of two kinds into 512 held-out test segments of PhysioNet-MI and identifying them through linear probing. The first was a narrowband burst drawn from one of four canonical rhythms ( δ, μ, β, spindle), i.e., a four-way discrimination that required the representation to encode oscillatory information of physiological interest. The second was a noise control task between white and pink noise, which only required the slope of the broadband spectrum. Sweeping the injected SNR from 35 to 15 dB traces how each property becomes recoverable as the perturbation strengthens (Figure 2 d,e). Rhythm identification was comparable across models, with BrainCodes reaching the highest accu-racy at the strongest injection (0.95 against 0.88 for REVE). On noise color though, BrainCodes was the least accurate model at every SNR, rising only from 0.53 to 0.76, while CBraMod reached 0.99. Taking the difference between the two curves in chance-normalized balanced accuracy, which accounted for their chance levels of 0.25 and 0.5 (Figure 2f), gives a single interpretable index of what each latent space preferentially encodes. BrainCodes was the only model for which this index is positive across the entire sweep, followed by REVE. Interestingly, these models also performed the best across the benchmarks of Section 4. # 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced BrainCodes , a lightweight EEG foundation model combining discrete neural to-ken prediction with montage-aware spatial encoding and oscillatory supervision. Across 14 bench-marks, BrainCodes matched or outperformed larger models, transferred effectively to sparse con-sumer montages, and preserved measurable spatial and spectral structure. Our findings show that grounded objectives can support EEG representation learning at substantially smaller scale. # A RELATED WORK 

Self-supervised Learning for EEG. Most EEG foundation models learn by reconstructing masked segments of the input. LaBraM first trained a vector-quantized tokenizer to encode the Fourier am-plitude and phase of EEG patches, then pretrained a transformer on approximately 2,500 hours of recordings to predict the indices of masked patches (Jiang et al., 2024). CBraMod instead recon-structed masked waveform patches using a criss-cross transformer with separate spatial and tem-poral attention, pretrained on more than 9,000 hours from the TUEG corpus (Wang et al., 2025). REVE scaled masked autoencoding to more than 60,000 hours from 25,000 participants across 92 datasets, with model sizes ranging from 12M to 408M parameters (El Ouahidi et al., 2025). BIOT represented each channel independently as a sequence of segment embeddings processed by a linear-attention transformer, allowing it to train across datasets with different channel sets (Yang et al., 2023a). CSBrain, in turn, combined cross-scale spatiotemporal tokenization with structured sparse attention (Zhou et al., 2025). Despite their architectural differences, these models optimize reconstruction targets whose contribution varies with spectral power. Reconstruction-based EEG models consequently tend to emphasize aperiodic and low-frequency content (Kommineni et al., 2026), motivating objectives whose weighting is less directly tied to signal amplitude. 

Discrete Tokenization of Biosignals. Neural audio codecs and vector quantization have also mo-tivated discrete representations of physiological signals. BioCodec applied residual vector quan-tization to single-channel biosignals and showed that its codes support downstream decoding at a fraction of the original bitrate (Avramidis et al., 2025). NeuroRVQ used a multi-scale residual quantizer within a generative EEG foundation model (Barmpas et al., 2025), while TFM-Tokenizer learned a vocabulary of time–frequency motifs from single-channel EEG and evaluated it on tasks including in-ear sleep staging (Pradeepkumar et al., 2025). These tokenizers were evaluated primar-ily through downstream accuracy, providing actionable insights on using vector quantization as a denoising method for EEG representations. 

Spatial Encoding of Electrodes. Transfer across heterogeneous montages requires electrode po-sition to be represented in a form that remains meaningful across layouts. LaBraM learned a sep-arate spatial embedding for each electrode name (Jiang et al., 2024), while CBraMod generated positional encodings from the input through a convolutional module (Wang et al., 2025). REVE jointly encoded electrode coordinates and time using a four-dimensional Fourier positional encod-ing (El Ouahidi et al., 2025). Rotary position embeddings offer a complementary approach: they encode position through rotations of query and key pairs, making the resulting attention logits de-pend on relative offset (Su et al., 2024). Their mixed-axis extension learns the rotation frequency and orientation of each pair jointly over two-dimensional image coordinates (Heo et al., 2024). Extend-ing this formulation to three-dimensional electrode coordinates makes the positional component of channel attention depend only on inter-electrode displacement, the geometric quantity most directly related to volume conduction at the scalp (Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006). 

EEG on Consumer Hardware. Low-cost headbands and ear-EEG systems have been validated for event-related potential research (Krigolson et al., 2017) and sleep monitoring (Bjarke Mikkelsen et al., 2025), and mental states have been classified from the four electrodes of the Muse head-band (Bird et al., 2018). These devices nevertheless present a substantial transfer challenge: their montages contain only four electrodes, often at positions that are sparse or absent in the recordings used for pretraining. Existing EEG foundation models have been evaluated almost entirely on clini-cal and laboratory montages. Even in-ear sleep staging has been studied mainly by using BIOT and LaBraM as front ends to TFM-Tokenizer (Pradeepkumar et al., 2025). Direct transfer of pretrained EEG representations to consumer hardware therefore remains largely unexplored.

# C MODEL PERFORMANCE : S PARSE MONTAGE ABLATION 

Figure 3: Balanced accuracy as electrodes are removed by farthest-point sampling, with shading marking the standard deviation over seeds. Left: absolute performance, with dotted lines at chance. Right: fraction of each full-montage score, where each full montage sits at its own electrode count. ∗

A foundation model that accepts variable electrode montages is most useful if its performance re-mains stable as spatial sampling becomes increasingly sparse. We therefore evaluated BrainCodes under a controlled montage-reduction protocol designed to separate the effect of electrode count from that of electrode placement. For each of the considered datasets, we constructed nested, task-agnostic subsets of 32, 16, 8, 4 and 1 electrodes using farthest-point sampling over the 3D electrode coordinates. This procedure preserves broad and approximately uniform scalp coverage at every 15 Preprint. Under review. budget while ensuring that each smaller montage is contained within the preceding one. The model was then fine-tuned separately on each reduced montage over three seeds, following the same pro-tocol used for the corresponding full-montage benchmark. BrainCodes retained 83% of its full-montage BAC with four electrodes on PhysioNet-MI, 87.0% on OpenBMI-ERP and scored even better than full fine-tuning on Siena, yielding an average retention of 91% (Figure 3b). Performance generally declined gradually as the montage was reduced, with a pronounced drop emerging only in the single-electrode condition. On PhysioNet-MI, for example, balanced accuracy decreased from 0.550 with four electrodes to 0.261 with one, as a single channel cannot preserve the lateralized spatial contrast needed to distinguish imagery of the left and right hands, making this task particularly sensitive to the final reduction. Siena, by contrast, matched its full-montage performance even with four electrodes and none of its reduced-montage results differed significantly from the full montage ( p > 0.2, two-sample t-tests across seeds). 

# D MODEL PERFORMANCE : L OW -RESOURCE FINE -TUNING 

Label efficiency is commonly summarized by the total number of samples. In EEG, however, the same budget can be allocated either broadly across participants or densely within fewer participants, and these strategies need not be equivalent when label-relevant variability is distributed differently within and across individuals. To separate these effects, we varied participant coverage and within-participant label density independently on PhysioNet-MI and OpenBMI-ERP. PhysioNet-MI crossed participant counts {16 , 24 , 32 , 48 , 60 } with {24 , 30 , 45 , 60 , 90 } windows per participant, while OpenBMI-ERP crossed {8, 16 , 24 , 32 , 40 } participants with {24 , 30 , 45 , 60 , 90 , 180 , 360 , 720 } win-dows. For each condition, participants were sampled without replacement and windows were sam-pled within participant without class stratification. BrainCodes was fully fine-tuned over three seeds under our standard protocol, with validation and test partitions held fixed. 

Figure 4: Low-resource fine-tuning under independently varied participant and within-participant label budgets. (a,b) Mean balanced accuracy of BrainCodes across three seeds on PhysioNet-MI and OpenBMI-ERP. (c) Estimated gain in balanced-accuracy points per doubling of each budget axis. Error bars denote coefficient standard errors. Performance improved along both budget axes, with different sensitivities across datasets (Figure 4). On PhysioNet-MI, balanced accuracy rose from 0.386 with 16 participants and 24 windows each to 0.652 with 48 participants and 90 windows each, approaching the full-partition score of 0.664. The progression was smooth across the grid, indicating that both additional participants and addi-tional trials from the same participants contributed consistently. On OpenBMI-ERP, performance increased from 0.511 with 8 participants and 24 windows each to 0.690 with 40 participants and 720 windows each. Gains were modest at the smallest within-participant budgets but became clearer as the number of windows increased. To compare the two budget dimensions directly, we regressed the mean accuracy of each condition on the logarithms of participant count and windows per participant. On PhysioNet-MI, each doubling contributed 7.9 balanced-accuracy points for participants and 6.3 points for windows per participant. The corresponding gains on OpenBMI-ERP were 3.0 and 2.0 points. # F THE LEARNED CHANNEL POSITIONAL ENCODING 

BrainCodes learns how differences in three-dimensional electrode position should affect channel at-tention. For attention head h and rotary pair r, a learned vector ωh,r ∈ R3 converts the displacement between electrodes i and j into a relative phase, 

∆ϕh,r (i, j ) = ω⊤ 

> h,r

(pi − pj ). (10) The direction of ωh,r determines the spatial axis to which the pair responds, while its magnitude determines how rapidly the phase changes with distance. A near-zero vector is therefore insensitive 17 Preprint. Under review. to electrode position, whereas a large vector produces stronger spatial differentiation. BrainCodes learns 8 such pairs for each of its eight attention heads, initialized at geometrically decreasing mag-nitudes. The same learned rotations are shared across all channel-attention layers, so each head applied a consistent spatial code throughout the backbone. Pretraining retained a compact set of spatial modes. Of the 64 head–pair vectors, 26 grew beyond their initial magnitudes, reaching norms of 0.907–1.878, increased by a median of 2.85 times. Most of this spatial structure was concentrated in three rotary pairs per head; the remaining pairs produced little positional variation (Figure 7). The resulting positional similarity remained between 0.61 and 0.70 even at the largest possible inter-electrode distance, indicating a smooth spatial bias rather than a hard separation between distant channels. Within each attention head, the retained directions formed an approximately orthogonal three-dimensional frame: the mean ratio between their smallest and largest singular values was 0.770, compared with 0.257 for size-matched random sets ( p < 

0.001 ). Collectively, these directions covered the left–right, anterior–posterior and inferior–superior axes evenly. Their normalized spatial gains were also nearly isotropic (0.353, 0.360 and 0.286), despite the unequal geometric extent of the montage along those axes (0.491, 0.358 and 0.150). Overall, pretraining transformed the initial multiscale encoding into a compact, approximately isotropic three-dimensional distance code that was replicated across attention heads. The result-ing bias depends primarily on relative electrode separation rather than absolute scalp location, so seeding it at different electrodes produces similar smoothly decaying spatial fields (Figure 5). This geometry is consistent with the isotropic spatial mixing produced by volume conduction.

# G SPECTRAL SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS 

Since the auxiliary objective explicitly targeted narrowband activity, we tested whether BrainCodes makes naturally occurring EEG rhythms more accessible without simply encoding the broadband spectral slope. We analyzed 800 PhysioNet-MI windows from 19 participants and evaluated on 800 windows from 20 separate participants. For every channel, the log spectrum from 1 to 45 Hz was decomposed into periodic and aperiodic components following Donoghue et al. (2020b). Specif-ically, we fitted a line in log–log coordinates and iteratively refitted it using bins below the 40th percentile of the residual distribution, preventing spectral peaks from biasing the aperiodic fit. Its slope defined the 1/f exponent, while the mean residual log power within each canonical frequency band defined the oscillatory targets. Linear regression heads were fitted to the time-averaged tokens of each frozen model. Targets were centered within each channel using only the fitting set, hence preventing electrode identity from supporting prediction. R2 = 0 .270 to 0.340, while decoding of the 1/f exponent remained effectively unchanged (Fig-18 Preprint. Under review.

The improvement was concentrated in the lower-frequency rhythms: R2 increased by 0.096 in delta, 0.075 in theta and 0.160 in alpha, significantly above zero by participant-bootstrap confidence intervals. Importantly, this pattern was ob-served in real EEG rather than synthetically perturbed proxies. 

# H SIGNAL CAPACITY & M ODEL SCALING 

BrainCodes is designed around two forms of efficiency: a compressed discrete (code) representa-tion and a lightweight U-Net backbone. The BioCodec tokenizer imposes an explicit information bottleneck, encoding approximately 1,000 bits/s per channel compared with 4,000 bits/s for the original 16-bit signal. However, we showed in Table 7 that the tokenizer outperformed a random quantizer at the same bitrate and, after pretraining, BrainCodes surpassed the random projection on all considered datasets. Thus, the discrete bottleneck reduces input capacity without constraining the performance of the pretrained model. We did not directly study parameter scaling and BrainCodes was pretrained at a single capacity of 1.9M parameters. Existing results nevertheless support this design choice. Scaling LaBraM from 5.8M to 369M parameters improved TUEV balanced accuracy from 0.6409 to 0.6616 (Jiang et al., 2024), which corresponds to less than a participant-level bootstrap standard deviation. Notably, participant-level resampling produced 9.2 times greater variability than window-level resampling, indicating that the apparent scaling gain is small relative to uncertainty across individuals. CBraMod likewise found that performance improved with model size, while gains from additional pretraining data slowed beyond 1,000 hours (Wang et al., 2025). On the other hand, REVE reported contin-ued linear-probing gains from 12M to 408M parameters (El Ouahidi et al., 2025). Together with our finding that increasing participant coverage yields greater returns than collecting more data per participant (Appendix D), these results motivated a complementary focus on efficiency. 19 Preprint. # K IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS & R ESOURCES 

Pretraining. BrainCodes was pretrained on the codes of the frozen BioCodec tokenizer, i.e., six residual quantization stages of 256 codewords at 20.8 frames per second, extracted from the corpus described in Section 3 after band-pass filtering between 0.5 and 75 Hz with a 60 Hz notch, interpo-lation of flat channels, resampling to 250 Hz and robust z-scoring of each channel. Training crops were drawn with equal probability among lengths of 2, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 s, and each crop re-tained a random subset of 16 to 64 channels, so that the model encountered both short windows and sparse montages during pretraining. The backbone used a model width of 128 with 8 attention heads, three blocks of two time-attention layers and one channel-attention layer, temporal pooling by factors of 2 and 2, encoder dropout of 0.1 and U-Net skip-connection dropout of 0.2. Optimiza-tion used AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95), a peak learning rate of 5×10 −4 , weight decay of 0.05, 2,000 warmup steps followed by cosine decay and gradient clipping at 1.0, for 20,000 steps at a batch size of 256 samples, realized through gradient accumulation in bfloat16. Each batch was assigned to one of the two objectives, i.e., 60% to masked code prediction and 40% to oscillation injection, with unit weight on every loss term. The block mask selected between 10% and 25% of channels, gave each a contiguous span whose length was drawn log-uniformly between 20% and 80% of the batch length, and extended every span to the 12.5% of electrodes nearest to that channel, so that a masked code could not be copied from an adjacent contact. Injected bursts had amplitudes between 0.1 and 0.5, a bandwidth of 2 Hz, durations between 10% and 50% of the batch length, and were applied to regions of 20% to 40% of the channels; the location head was trained with binary cross-entropy and the frequency and duration heads with squared error on unit-normalized targets. Electrode co-ordinates were further perturbed with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.05, capped at half the mean nearest-neighbor spacing of the montage. Every downstream experiment used the checkpoint at step 20,000. Pretraining required about 190 GPU-hours, realized on 4 NVIDIA RTX 6000 GPUs. 

Downstream Adaptation. Unless stated otherwise, BrainCodes was fully fine-tuned with AdamW at a weight decay of 0.01, label smoothing of 0.1 and class weights set to the square root of the inverse class frequency, normalized to unit mean, which damps the weighting of rare classes relative to inverse-frequency weights. Inputs were z-scored per channel and window, the learning rate rose linearly during the warmup epochs and then followed a cosine decay, and the checkpoint with the highest validation balanced accuracy was evaluated on the test data. The classification head read ei-ther the time-pooled bottleneck feature bc through a projector shared across channels or the flattened bottleneck ¯bc through a single linear layer, and the per-dataset settings are listed in Table 17.

Grounding-probe implementation. Both probes in Section 5 used the test split of PhysioNet-MI under the standard BrainCodes preprocessing, with 128 windows for the spatial probe and 512 for the spectral probe. For the spatial probe, we perturbed one electrode at a time by adding Gaussian white noise at an SNR of 10 dB and measured its influence on channel i as ∥fi(˜ x)−fi(x)∥/∥fi(x)∥,where fi denotes the features of channel i and the norms run over windows and feature dimensions. Mixing was defined as the share of the total influence carried by pairs of distinct electrodes, locality as the Spearman correlation between that influence and inter-electrode distance, and the decay length 

λ as −1/b for the slope b of a least-squares fit of log influence against Euclidean distance, with coordinates centered on the montage and scaled to a maximum electrode norm of 1. For the spectral probe, band-limited noise was injected into a spatially contiguous 20–40% of the channels, i.e., a seed electrode and its nearest neighbors, as delta (0.5 to 4 Hz) and spindle (11 to 16 Hz) bursts lasting 0.5 to 2.0 and 0.5 to 1.5 s under Hann and Gaussian envelopes, and as continuous mu (8 to 13 Hz, central electrodes only) and beta (13 to 30 Hz) activity, while white and pink noise were matched in power; the SNR of 35 to 15 dB was defined as the ratio of signal to marker power per injected channel over the injection window, before any normalization. Standardized logistic regressions were fitted to the change in channel-averaged features between the injected and the clean window, reduced to 64 principal components, under leave-one-participant-out validation. 

Computational Cost. BrainCodes holds 1,877,312 trainable parameters, and the BioCodec tok-enizer it reads from contributes a further 3,152,770 that remained frozen throughout, so the released checkpoint stores the encoder alone in 7.19 MiB. With its classification head the fine-tuned model held between 1.9 and 2.5 million trainable parameters across the datasets of Table 17, from 1,917,510 on EESM23 to 2,520,744 on TUAB. Tables with results only mention the size of the encoders. Be-cause the tokenizer was frozen and shared across every downstream task, each corpus was tokenized once and its code indices were cached. Hence the fine-tuning runs of Appendix D and the arms of Table 7 shared a single tokenization pass per corpus.
