Abstract 

Neuroimaging analysis commonly involves classifying cognitive conditions, identifying task-relevant brain regions, and examining functional connectivity. These objectives are typically addressed separately, without a model-based framework. In contrast, we propose a Bayesian distance-weighted Ising model that integrates classification, regional importance, and connectivity analysis. The model captures dependence among task labels based on connectivities between functional signals, while region-of-interest (ROI)-specific interaction parameters account for spatial heterogeneity and quantify regional contributions to task discrimination. However, inference is challenging because the signals may be observed on 

1arXiv:2610.25656v1 [stat.AP] 19 Oct 2026 unequal time grids and the likelihood function contains an intractable normalising constant. To address this, we use a basis expansion and an auxiliary variable Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm. We apply the method to functional near-infrared spectroscopy data from younger and older adults, comparing resting-state and task-related activity. The model achieves high classification accuracy and reveals age-dependent patterns of regional importance and connectivity. Among younger adults, the left orbitofrontal cortex contributes more strongly to task discrimination than the dorsolateral prefrontal and frontopolar cortices, whereas regional differences are less evident among older adults. Older adults also exhibit greater bilateral connectivity between corresponding ROIs, consistent with reduced hemispheric lateralisation. 

Keywords: auxiliary variable Markov chain Monte Carlo; functional connectivity; functional near-infrared spectroscopy; Ising model; regional importance 

# 1 Introduction 

Neuroimaging techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) provide valuable tools for investigating brain activity. By measuring task-related changes in brain signals across multiple cortical locations, researchers can characterise how neural activity varies across experimental conditions and brain regions. Consequently, the statistical analysis of neuroimaging data has become an important component of studies aimed at understanding brain function and its relationship with cognitive processes. Various methods have been developed for neuroimaging data, with several major analytical objectives, including classification and prediction, where brain signals are used to distinguish between experimental conditions or subject groups. For example, multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA) treats the decoding of experimental conditions from fMRI signals as a supervised clas-sification problem and has become a widely used approach for analysing distributed patterns of neural activity (Haxby et al., 2001; Norman et al., 2006). Related classification approaches have also been extensively studied for fNIRS data, particularly in brain–computer interface, clinical diagnosis, and cognitive-task applications (Naseer and Hong, 2015). Another major objective is to identify brain regions that contribute most strongly to distinguishing different cognitive states 2or tasks (Haufe et al., 2014). Such analyses can be summarised through spatial importance maps, which highlight regions associated with task discrimination. Another major objective is connec-tivity analysis (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009; Zhang et al., 2010), which investigates relationships among different brain regions and reveals coordinated patterns of neural activity. However, these objectives have typically been studied separately. Even when multiple objec-tives are considered within a single analysis, they are often addressed sequentially rather than simultaneously. For example, functional connectivity measures may first be estimated between pairs of brain regions, followed by feature selection and the application of a separate classifier that uses the estimated connectivity measures as input features (Du et al., 2018). Similarly, regional importance is often assessed by interpreting feature weights or spatial patterns obtained from fitted classifiers (Hebart et al., 2015). Existing approaches are limited in addressing these objectives simultaneously. Also, uncertainty from earlier stages is not necessarily propagated to subsequent analyses. Motivated by this limitation, we propose a Bayesian distance-weighted Ising (BDWI) model, which is a unified probabilistic model for analysing functional neuroimaging data. Our model incorporates region-of-interest (ROI)-specific interaction parameters to account for heterogeneity across brain regions and uses distance-based weights between functional observations to charac-terise their relationships. This framework enables task classification, quantification of the relative contributions of ROIs to task-label prediction, and investigation of inter-ROI distance-weighted connectivity patterns within a single statistical model. The Bayesian approach also allows us to quantify uncertainty through posterior samples. Our approach builds on probabilistic nearest-neighbour methods for classification. Holmes and Adams (2002) introduced a probabilistic formulation of the k-nearest-neighbour ( k-NN) classifier, and Cucala et al. (2009) developed a probabilistic k-NN model with a valid joint distribution for the class labels. These approaches retain a neighbourhood defined by the k

nearest observations, with observations within the neighbourhood contributing equally regardless of their distances. Their performance also depends on the choice of k. Friel and Pettitt (2011) introduced the distance nearest-neighbour (d-NN) model, which assigns distance-based weights to all other observations, avoiding the need to specify the neighbourhood size k. However, directly applying the d-NN model to functional neuroimaging data presents two important challenges. 3First, the original d-NN model assumes a common interaction parameter across all observations and therefore does not account for regional heterogeneity. In functional neuroimaging data, the contribution of functional observations to task-label prediction may vary across brain regions. Second, neuroimaging signals are high-dimensional, with each functional observation consisting of a large number of time points. Moreover, the number and timing of measurements may vary across observations or experimental conditions, complicating the direct computation of distances between the observed time series. To address these challenges, we account for regional heterogeneity by introducing ROI-specific interaction parameters. We also employ basis expansion to obtain a common low-dimensional representation of the functional signals, enabling distance-based comparisons between high-dimensional observations measured on different grids. The likelihood for our proposed model contains an intractable, parameter-dependent normalising constant. Thus, standard Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms cannot be applied directly for Bayesian inference. We address this computational difficulty using an auxiliary variable MCMC (Murray et al., 2006; Caimo and Friel, 2011) approach that avoids direct evaluation of the normalising constant. The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In Section 2, we describe the fNIRS data from younger and older adults and present exploratory analyses. In Section 3, we introduce the BDWI model and describe an auxiliary variable MCMC algorithm for posterior inference. In Section 4, we apply our proposed method to the fNIRS data and examine regional importance and distance-weighted functional connectivity. In Section 5, we conclude the paper with a discussion and summary. 

# 2 Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy Data 

# 2.1 Data Description 

In this study, we analyse functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) data, which consist of time-series measurements of changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin concentra-tions and provide information about cortical haemodynamic activity. The fNIRS recordings were collected in the Department of Psychology at Yonsei University from two separately re-4cruited cohorts of younger and older adults. The younger cohort included participants aged 19–33 years, whereas the older cohort consisted of participants aged 65 years or older. Younger adults were recruited from four-year universities in South Korea and were either current under-graduate students or recent graduates. Older adults were recruited from a senior programme in Seongnam-si, Gyeonggi-do. For older adults, cognitive impairment was screened using the Ko-rean Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE). In both cohorts, participants were excluded if they had a current or previous Axis I psychiatric disorder, were taking medication that could alter neural activity, or had uncorrected visual impairment. In this manuscript, we analyse the two cohorts separately. Cortical haemodynamic activity was recorded in the prefrontal cortex using a continuous-wave fNIRS system (NIRSIT; OBELAB Inc., Seoul, South Korea), which consists of 48 measurement channels sampled at 8.138 Hz. Each channel records changes in oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated (HbR) haemoglobin concentrations, and we use the HbO signal for the subsequent analysis. The recordings were preprocessed following standard fNIRS procedures (Takizawa et al., 2014; Pfeifer et al., 2018; Y¨ ucel et al., 2021). Each participant completed three experimental conditions: (1) a resting-state condition, dur-ing which the participant sat quietly with their eyes closed; (2) the Iowa gambling task (IGT), a repeated-choice task designed to assess decision-making under uncertainty (Bechara et al., 1994); and (3) the Stroop task, a colour–word interference task that assesses cognitive control and selec-tive attention (Stroop, 1935). Detailed information on participant characteristics, experimental design and procedures, task protocols, and fNIRS data acquisition is provided in Section 1 of the Supplementary Material. Our interest lies in how cortical activity changes when a participant engages in a cognitive task relative to rest. We therefore compare the IGT and Stroop conditions separately with the resting state to characterise task-related differences in cortical activity. In our study, we assign each of the 48 measurement channels to one of eight anatomically defined ROIs according to its cortical location, using Brodmann area (BA) mapping (Brodmann, 1909). Thus, this grouping does not select or aggregate the 48 channel-level signals into eight observa-tions; rather, it partitions the 48 channels into eight broader cortical regions while retaining each channel as an individual functional observation. The eight ROIs comprise the dorsolateral pre-frontal cortex (DLPFC), ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), frontopolar prefrontal cortex (FPC), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) in both hemispheres. Specifically, the left-hemisphere 5ROIs are denoted by L-DLPFC (ROI 1L), L-VLPFC (ROI 2L), L-FPC (ROI 3L), and L-OFC (ROI 4L), while the corresponding right-hemisphere ROIs are denoted by R-DLPFC (ROI 1R), R-VLPFC (ROI 2R), R-FPC (ROI 3R), and R-OFC (ROI 4R) (Figure 1). These regions pro-vide an anatomically interpretable partition of the prefrontal cortex and are used to characterise regional variation in the relationship between the fNIRS signals and experimental conditions. 

Figure 1: Anatomical partition of the prefrontal cortex into eight ROIs. Each of the 48 fNIRS measurement channels is assigned to one ROI according to its cortical location. 

# 2.2 Exploratory Data Analysis 

In this section, we conduct an exploratory data analysis to motivate our model by examining regional and cohort-specific variation in task-related HbO signals. We first compare the task-specific mean HbO curves within each ROI. For each curve, we normalise the time domain to [0 , 1] and linearly interpolate the HbO time series at 100 equally spaced time points. This transformation places recordings of different durations—approximately 300, 856, and 63 seconds for the resting, IGT, and Stroop conditions, respectively—on a common time grid. For each ROI–condition combination, we compute the mean curve by averaging the corresponding channel-by-participant observations at each normalised time point, as illustrated in Figure 2. We observe that the temporal patterns differ across conditions and ROIs, with the Stroop task showing particularly distinct trajectories compared with the resting and IGT conditions. Corresponding left–right ROIs generally exhibit similar patterns, although their magnitudes vary across ROIs 6Figure 2: Mean HbO curves for each condition within each ROI, shown separately for younger and older adults. Accuracy is evaluated by 5-fold cross-validation, with all observa-tions from a given participant assigned to the same fold. Blue and red bars represent younger and older adults, respectively. Across most ROIs, the mean HbO curves show larger temporal fluctuations in the younger cohort than in the older cohort. In the older cohort, differences between Stroop and the other conditions remain apparent despite the smaller overall amplitude range. These findings motivate a model that accommodates ROI-specific heterogeneity. Furthermore, we examine the extent to which individual ROIs are informative for distinguish-ing among the three experimental conditions. For each ROI and cohort separately, we train a 

k-nearest-neighbour classifier ( k = 25) using Euclidean distances between the curves, with each observation corresponding to a participant–channel–condition combination. Performance is eval-uated using 5-fold cross-validation. Figure 3 shows the held-out classification accuracies, with 1/3 denoting the expected accuracy under uniform random assignment to the three experimen-tal conditions. All ROIs achieve accuracies above this baseline, although accuracy varies across ROIs and cohorts. In the younger cohort, ROI 3L achieves the highest accuracy (72%), followed by ROI 1L (68%) and ROI 3R (67%), whereas ROI 1R performs best in the older cohort (65%). Performance also differs by cohort: ROIs 3L and 3R perform better in younger adults, whereas ROIs 1R and 2R perform better in older adults. Together, these exploratory analyses suggest that the extent to which HbO signals distinguish between experimental conditions varies across 8ROIs and age cohorts. Although these exploratory analyses provide useful evidence of spatial heterogeneity in task-related signal patterns, they have several limitations. The mean-curve comparisons do not quan-tify uncertainty in differences between ROIs. In addition, fitting a separate classifier for each ROI does not allow information from other ROIs to inform condition-label prediction. Moreover, differences in classification accuracy do not directly quantify the relative contribution of each ROI to task-label prediction in a joint model. These limitations motivate a Bayesian model for condition-label prediction with ROI-specific interaction parameters.

13 3.3 Neuroimaging Interpretation 

The BDWI model supports three complementary analyses of neuroimaging data. First, the model enables task classification based on fNIRS data. This demonstrates that the BDWI model can capture task-specific patterns in neuroimaging signals and use them to distinguish among different experimental conditions. Second, by estimating ROI-specific interaction strengths βrℓ , the model provides an ROI-level measure of each ROI’s contribution to task-label prediction. A larger βrℓ strengthens distance-weighted interactions between observations from ROI rℓ and other observations, whether they belong to the same or different ROIs. These interactions favour agreement in condition labels between observations with similar functional signals. An importance map of βrℓ identifies ROIs that are most informative for task discrimination. # 4 Application 

In this section, we apply the proposed BDWI model to the fNIRS data. We consider two binary classification problems, comparing the IGT with the resting state and comparing the Stroop task with the resting state. Each comparison is conducted separately for the younger and older cohorts, resulting in a total of four datasets. Inference is performed using the AEA 15 described in Section 3.2. For each dataset, we run the algorithm for 20,000 iterations, discard the first 10,000 iterations as burn-in, and retain every 10th draw thereafter. Following the heuristic of Park and Haran (2018), we set L = 10, beyond which the posterior distributions change little. The sensitivity analysis in Section 2 of the Supplementary Material shows accurate parameter recovery for both L = 10 and L = 20. The code for the proposed BDWI model, along with the simulation code and datasets used in this study, is publicly available at https: //github.com/codinheesang/BDWI

# 4.2 Importance Analysis 

We investigate the relative importance of individual brain regions in distinguishing each cognitive task from the resting state. In the proposed BDWI model, the ROI-specific interaction parameter 

βrℓ controls the strength of local label agreement associated with ROI rℓ. Accordingly, posterior 17 inference on this parameter measures the relative contribution of each ROI to task discrimination. Larger values of βrℓ indicate stronger task-discriminative local dependence associated with the corresponding ROI. For each ROI, we summarise its posterior distribution using the posterior mean together with the corresponding 95% credible interval. 

Figure 4: ROI-specific importance maps for the IGT versus resting and Stroop versus resting comparisons in the younger and older cohorts. The values represent the posterior means of 

βrℓ , with the corresponding 95% credible intervals shown in parentheses. Red ROIs indicate significantly larger βrℓ values than the corresponding blue ROIs based on the 95% credible intervals of their pairwise differences. Figure 4 shows distinct patterns in the ROI-specific interaction parameters between the younger and older cohorts. For the older cohort, no significant differences among ROIs are observed for either the IGT or Stroop comparison, with the 95% credible intervals overlapping 18 for every pair of ROIs. This lack of regional differentiation can be interpreted in the context of age-related neural dedifferentiation (Park et al., 2004). Neural dedifferentiation refers to a reduc-tion in the functional specificity or selectivity of neural responses with ageing, such that neural representations become less differentiated across ROIs or conditions. MVPA studies comparing patterns of brain activity between younger and older adults have reported similar age-related reductions in neural distinctiveness (Carp et al., 2011). Moreover, MVPA-based measures of memory specificity have provided evidence of neural dedifferentiation in older adults in both the PFC and hippocampus (Abdulrahman et al., 2017), suggesting that age-related dedifferentiation may extend across brain regions involved in higher-order cognitive and memory processes. Although the older cohort shows no significant ROI-specific differences, the estimated inter-action parameters exhibit different bilateral patterns across tasks. In the IGT comparison, the left and right ROIs show relatively similar values of βrℓ , whereas greater left–right differences are observed in the Stroop comparison. The relatively similar bilateral interaction strengths, particu-larly for the IGT comparison, are broadly consistent with the hemispheric asymmetry reduction in older adults (HAROLD) model (Cabeza, 2002). The HAROLD model describes an age-related reduction in the lateralisation of prefrontal activity during cognitive performance, with older adults tending to exhibit more bilateral patterns of prefrontal recruitment than younger adults. Consistent with this interpretation, studies using reward-based decision-making tasks similar to the IGT have reported greater bilateral and contralateral recruitment in older than in younger adults (McCarrey et al., 2012), suggesting reduced hemispheric specialisation during decision-making with ageing. In contrast, the younger cohort exhibits significant regional differences in the IGT comparison. The interaction parameters for the DLPFC (ROI 1) and FPC (ROI 3) are significantly lower than that for the L-OFC (ROI 4L). This pattern may reflect functional differences among the OFC, DLPFC, and FPC regions. The OFC is closely involved in value representation, reward evaluation, and decision-making under uncertainty, processes that are particularly relevant to the IGT (Bechara et al., 1994, 2000). In particular, Lawrence et al. (2009) reported selective activation of the lateral OFC during advantageous relative to disadvantageous deck choices in healthy adults performing the IGT. Accordingly, the stronger interaction associated with the L-OFC (ROI 4L) may indicate that signals from this ROI discriminate more strongly between 19 IGT and resting conditions. In contrast, the DLPFC is involved in working-memory-related processes (Li et al., 2010), whereas the FPC has been associated with higher-order integrative and control processes across multiple information sources (Ramnani and Owen, 2004). These functional characteristics may be consistent with the significantly lower interaction parameters for ROI 1 and ROI 3, suggesting weaker condition-specific discrimination in these regions than in ROI 4L. No significant pairwise differences among ROIs are observed in the younger cohort for the Stroop comparison. This finding may reflect differences in the neural demands of the two tasks. Whereas the IGT strongly engages value-evaluation processes for which the OFC has a relatively specialised role, Stroop performance relies on a broader cognitive-control network involving mul-tiple prefrontal regions, including the DLPFC and VLPFC. This interpretation is consistent with the superordinate cognitive control network described by Niendam et al. (2012), in which diverse executive-control tasks recruit a distributed set of frontal and parietal regions. 

# 4.3 Connectivity Analysis 

We further investigate the pairwise connectivity structure based on the distance-based weights in equation (5) by presenting the posterior mean and 95% credible interval of eCjk (σ) for each pair of ROIs j and k. Figure 6 illustrates the top five ROI pairs for each dataset, highlighting those with the strongest pairwise connectivity. The complete connectivity graph across all ROI pairs is provided in Section 3 of the Supplementary Material. Several estimated connectivity patterns can be interpreted in terms of the known functional and hemispheric organisation of the prefrontal cortex. First, the bilateral connectivity between the left and right FPC (ROI 3) and OFC (ROI 4) is generally stronger than that between the corresponding DLPFC (ROI 1) and VLPFC (ROI 2). This pattern is broadly consistent with previously reported regional differences in interhemispheric organisation of the prefrontal cortex. Electrophysiological evidence from intracranial cortico-cortical evoked potential (CCEP) recordings has demonstrated strong homotopic connections involving the OFC, characterised by short interhemispheric response latencies (Starkweather et al., 2026). Complementary evidence from meta-analytic task-based co-activation data has also shown substantial regional variation in 20 Figure 5: The five ROI pairs with the highest posterior mean distance-weighted connectivity for the IGT versus resting and Stroop versus resting comparisons in the younger and older cohorts. Each annotation gives the posterior mean eCjk (σ) and 95% credible interval, defined in equation (5), for the corresponding ROI pairs. 21 homotopic connectivity within the prefrontal cortex. In particular, regions near the medial OFC exhibit relatively strong homotopic connectivity, whereas lateral prefrontal regions, including the DLPFC and VLPFC, show comparatively weak homotopic connectivity (Mancuso et al., 2019). Second, strong connectivity between the left FPC (ROI 3L) and left OFC (ROI 4L) is consis-tently observed across all settings. This pattern may be explained by their anatomical proximity and direct structural connections. In particular, the fronto-orbitopolar tract provides an asso-ciative pathway between the posterior OFC and anterior orbitofrontal and frontal polar regions, including connections between areas corresponding to the OFC and FPC (Catani et al., 2012). Functionally, the strong FPC–OFC connectivity may also be consistent with their complemen-tary involvement in value-based decision making, with the OFC closely associated with value and outcome evaluation (Bechara et al., 1994, 2000) and the FPC with the integration of infor-mation across multiple cognitive processes (Ramnani and Owen, 2004). The consistently strong connectivity between ROI 3L and ROI 4L may therefore be consistent with both anatomical and functional coupling between these neighbouring prefrontal regions. Finally, the older cohort generally exhibits stronger connectivity between corresponding left and right ROIs than the younger cohort. This age-related pattern is consistent with the reduced hemispheric lateralisation described by the HAROLD model (Cabeza, 2002). As discussed above, older adults tend to show more bilateral patterns of prefrontal recruitment during cognitive processing, reflecting reduced hemispheric asymmetry with ageing. 

# 5 Discussion 

In this study, we proposed a unified probabilistic model for classification, ROI-specific importance analysis, and connectivity analysis. To address the computational challenges posed by high-dimensional functional data and an intractable normalising constant, we utilised basis expansion with auxiliary variable MCMC. The proposed model achieved accurate classification across all task comparisons. The importance analysis revealed significant differences among ROIs for the IGT comparison in the younger cohort and no significant differences in the older cohort. The connectivity analysis identified group-specific patterns, including greater bilateral connectivity in the older cohort. 22 Our findings are consistent with age-related neural dedifferentiation and reduced hemispheric lateralisation, suggesting that ageing may be associated with less regionally differentiated and more bilaterally similar patterns of prefrontal activity. Beyond these neuroscientific implications, the inference results demonstrate that the proposed model can jointly characterise task-related differences, ROI-specific importance, and connectivity. Although our study focused on fNIRS data, the proposed framework could be extended to other neuroimaging modalities, such as fMRI, with suitable representations of functional observations and spatial regions. Future research could address the computational cost of the auxiliary simulations required at each MCMC iteration, particularly for large datasets. One direction is to investigate whether divide-and-conquer methods (Srivastava et al., 2018; Scott et al., 2016) can be adapted to improve the scalability of posterior inference for the proposed model.

# A.1 Participants 

Participants were recruited separately for the younger and older adult groups. The younger adult sample consisted of 43 participants recruited from four-year universities across South Korea. Eligible participants were current undergraduate students or college graduates between 19 and 33 years of age. The final younger adult sample ranged in age from 19 to 32 years (mean age = 23.37 years) and included 28 female participants. For the older adult group, 42 participants were recruited through a senior program in Seongnam-si, Gyeonggi-do. Cognitive eligibility was assessed using the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE) (Kang et al., 1997), with scores below 24 serving as an exclusion criterion. Of the 62 initially recruited participants, one was excluded due to suspected mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and one was excluded for not meeting the minimum age requirement of 65 years. The final older adult sample therefore consisted of 60 participants, ranging in age from 66 to 88 years (mean age = 74.58 years), of whom 53 were female. Across both age groups, participants were excluded if they (a) had a current or past Axis I psychiatric disorder, including substance abuse or dependence; (b) were taking medication on an ongoing basis that could plausibly alter neural activity relative to the general population; or (c) had uncorrected visual impairment that could interfere with viewing the on-screen stimuli. All eligible participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. 

# A.2 Experimental Design and Procedures 

For the older cohort, eligibility was first assessed using the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE) (Kang et al., 1997), with participants scoring below 24 excluded from participation. Eligible participants provided written informed consent and subsequently completed a set of paper-based questionnaires. Participants then completed a keyboard-based version of the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT). Task instructions were presented via slides, and partic-ipants responded using the index finger of one hand. The functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) device was fitted according to a standardised manual. Before the main task, a 5-minute 24 resting-state baseline was recorded while participants sat quietly with their eyes closed. Par-ticipants were informed when the resting period had concluded. They subsequently completed a 20-trial IGT practice block followed by the 100-trial main IGT block. The Stroop task was administered after the IGT. Instructions were presented via slides, and participants responded using the keyboard arrow keys as quickly and accurately as possible. Participants completed a practice block until they were familiar with the response procedure, after which the main Stroop task was administered. The main task consisted of a 30-second congruent condition followed by a 30-second incongruent condition. For the younger cohort, Eligible participants provided written informed consent and sub-sequently completed a computerised questionnaire battery using a mouse. The experimenter explained the initial instruction screen and informed participants that instructions would be presented before each subsequent scale. Participants then proceeded through the questionnaires independently and notified the experimenter upon completion. Participants next completed a keyboard-based version of the IGT. Task instructions were presented via slides, and participants were instructed to respond using the index finger of one hand. After completing a 10-trial practice block, the fNIRS device was fitted according to a standardised manual, and the corresponding PsychoPy experimental file was configured. A 5-minute resting-state baseline was then recorded, during which participants were instructed to remain still and either maintain fixation or keep eyes closed without falling asleep. Following the baseline recording, participants completed the 200-trial main IGT block. The Stroop task was subsequently administered using PsychoPy for stimulus presentation and response collection. Participants were instructed to respond using the keyboard as quickly as possible while maintaining accurate performance. The experimenter monitored participants during the practice block to ensure familiarity with the response proce-dure. The main Stroop task then followed the same structure as that used for the older cohort, consisting of a 30-second congruent condition followed by a 30-second incongruent condition. 

# A.3 Tasks 

Participants completed a computerised version of the IGT using a keyboard, responding with the index finger of one hand. The reward and loss contingencies followed the original payoff 25 scheme introduced by Bechara et al. (1994). However, unlike the original paradigm, we did not impose a maximum number of selections from each deck. If the number of selections from a given deck exceeded the length of its predefined payoff sequence, the sequence was repeated from the beginning. Decks A and B were disadvantageous, providing a constant reward of 100 USD per selection but imposing relatively large losses, resulting in a net expected loss of 250 USD per 10 selections. Conversely, In contrast, Decks C and D were advantageous, providing a smaller constant reward of 50 USD per selection but imposing smaller cumulative losses, resulting in a net expected gain of 250 USD per 10 selections. The decks also differed in loss frequency: losses occurred on 5 of every 10 selections for Decks A and C and on 1 of every 10 selections for Decks B and D. To facilitate interpretation of the monetary outcomes by Korean participants, all monetary values were proportionally rescaled to Korean Won (KRW). For example, the 100 USD reward associated with Decks A and B was represented as 10,000 KRW. The behavioural data were collected as part of a concurrent fNIRS study. Accordingly, we used a fixed trial duration to maintain a consistent temporal structure for the neuroimaging experiment. Previous research has shown that imposing time constraints on the IGT does not substantially alter behavioural patterns or decision-making trajectories (Bowman et al., 2005). Because younger adults responded substantially faster than older adults, the trial duration was adjusted by age group to avoid prolonged inactive periods within trials while maintaining a comparably constrained task structure. Older adults completed 100 trials with a fixed duration of 6 seconds per trial, whereas younger adults completed 200 trials with a fixed duration of 4 seconds per trial. If a participant failed to make a selection within the response window, a warning message instructing the participant to respond within the allotted time was displayed for the remainder of the trial. The trial was recorded as a non-response (NA) and excluded from the behavioural analysis. No outcome feedback was presented for non-response trials; consequently, these trials provided no new information about the reward or loss associated with any deck. In the subsequent behavioural analysis, deck-specific value representations were therefore carried forward unchanged until the next valid response. Selective attention and cognitive interference were assessed using a computerised Stroop task (Stroop, 1935), which was administered using the same procedure in both age groups. On each trial, a colour word was presented in either a congruent condition (e.g., the word ’RED’ displayed 26 in red) or an incongruent condition (e.g., the word ’RED’ displayed in blue). Participants were instructed to identify the colour in which the word was displayed as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing the corresponding directional arrow key on a keyboard. Each of the four arrow keys was mapped to one of four response colours: red, blue, green, and yellow. Before the main task, participants completed a fixed 16-trial practice block under the supervision of the experimenter. The main task consisted of a 30-second congruent block followed by a 30-second incongruent block, resulting in a total task duration of 60 seconds. Response time (RT; ms), accuracy (proportion correct), and the number of completed trials were recorded separately for each block. To quantify the Stroop interference effect, an interference score was calculated for each measure as the difference between performance in the incongruent and congruent blocks (incongruent − congruent; Cothran and Larsen, 2008; Dittrich and Stahl, 2012). This yielded three measures: accuracy interference, trial-count interference, and RT interference. A positive RT interference score indicates slower responses in the incongruent condition, whereas negative accuracy and trial-count interference scores indicate lower accuracy and fewer completed trials, respectively, in the incongruent condition. Thus, larger positive values for RT interference and larger negative values for accuracy and trial-count interference reflect greater Stroop interference. 

# A.4 fNIRS Data Acquisition 

Cerebral hemodynamic activity was recorded using a continuous-wave functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) system (NIRSIT; OBELAB Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea). The portable, wireless system uses two wavelengths of near-infrared light (approximately 780 and 850 nm) to measure changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin concentrations (HbO and HbR) in cortical tissue. The system comprises 24 laser diodes and 32 photodiodes, providing 48 mea-surement channels with a source–detector separation of 3 cm and a sampling frequency of 8.138 Hz. Measurements covered the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), frontopolar cortex (FPC), ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Data acquisition was performed using NIRSIT SCAN (OBELAB Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea), the manufacturer’s dedicated acquisition software. fNIRS measurements were conducted in a quiet, 27 controlled environment with minimal interference from strong artificial light sources or electro-magnetic signals. Participants were seated comfortably and instructed to remain still during data acquisition. Before positioning the NIRSIT probes over the forehead, care was taken to ensure that hair did not obstruct contact at the forehead and temporal regions. The system’s built-in gain calibration procedure was then performed automatically to optimise signal quality before recording. The fNIRS data were preprocessed using a standardised sequence of quality-control and signal-processing procedures. First, invalid samples were identified at the raw intensity level. When five or fewer invalid samples occurred consecutively, they were replaced using nearest-neighbour interpolation; channels containing more than five consecutive invalid samples were rejected. Ad-ditional channel-quality criteria were then applied to the raw intensity data. Channels were rejected if their median intensity was below 30 (Y¨ ucel et al., 2021), if their coefficient of varia-tion exceeded 20% (Pfeifer et al., 2018), or if identical consecutive values occurred over more than 5% of the entire time series, indicating potential signal saturation. An additional quality-control criterion was applied after conversion to haemoglobin concentrations: channels exhibiting an extreme negative correlation between the HbO and HbR time series ( r ≤ − 0.9), indicative of a potential mirroring artefact, were rejected (Takizawa et al., 2014). Following the initial quality-control procedures, the retained raw intensity data were con-verted to optical density (Y¨ ucel et al., 2021), and motion artefacts were corrected using Temporal Derivative Distribution Repair (TDDR) (Fishburn et al., 2019). The corrected optical density data were subsequently converted to changes in haemoglobin concentration using the modified Beer–Lambert law (Delpy et al., 1988; Cope and Delpy, 1988). Following the ISO guideline, concentration changes were calculated without differential pathlength correction and were there-fore expressed in units of mm · mM. Molar extinction coefficients based on the data reported by Moaveni were used for this conversion (Zhao et al., 2017). The resulting haemoglobin con-centration signals were then digitally filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth bandpass filter with cutoff frequencies of 0.01 and 0.2 Hz to attenuate low-frequency drift and higher-frequency physiological and measurement noise (Y¨ ucel et al., 2021). Finally, channels rejected during the quality-control procedures were reconstructed using a predefined padding procedure. For each rejected channel, replacement values were first obtained from designated backup channels with 28 the same source–detector distance and corresponding target location. When no suitable backup channel was available, the rejected channel was replaced using the mean signal of the channels belonging to the same Brodmann area group. 

# B Simulation Study
