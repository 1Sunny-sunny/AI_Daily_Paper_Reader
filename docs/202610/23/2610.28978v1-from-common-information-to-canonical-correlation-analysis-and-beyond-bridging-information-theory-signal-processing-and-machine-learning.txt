# From Common Information to Canonical Correlation Analysis and Beyond: Bridging Information Theory, Signal Processing, and Machine Learning 

Paris A. Karakasis* and Walid Saad 

Institute for Advanced Computing, Bradley Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, Alexandria, VA 22305, USA; {parisk, walids}@vt.edu 

* Correspondence: parisk@vt.edu 

Abstract 

Many problems in signal processing, machine learning, and neuroimaging provide several jointly observed measurements of one process, each mixing a component common to all of them with a component private to itself, and require the common component to be recov-ered without supervision. This problem has been studied in several communities, largely independently. Statisticians proposed canonical correlation analysis (CCA), introduced by Hotelling in 1936. Information theorists proposed two notions of common information, due to Gács and Körner and to Wyner. Engineers developed subspace methods for sensor arrays and multiuser communications to design multiview receivers. This tutorial presents the main results and tools each community developed for this problem in a single exposition, and explains the connections that emerge where they meet, distinguishing exact equiva-lences from special cases and analogies. We develop the information-theoretic foundation from first principles. We then introduce CCA and its extension to several views, followed by the engineering thread from blind multiuser detection to CCA as a blind receiver. Next, we turn to nonlinear CCA, covering kernel, deep, and probabilistic formulations, the condi-tions under which the common factor is identifiable, and connections to self-supervised learning. We conclude with a discussion of further extensions and open directions. The exposition is self-contained, so that readers from each of these communities can follow the parts developed by the others. 

Keywords: common information; canonical correlation analysis; generalized CCA; identifi-ability; Gray–Wyner network; blind multiuser detection; multiview learning; multimodal learning; latent variable models; conditional independence 

# 1. Introduction 

1.1. A Question That Crosses Disciplines 

A wide range of applications, from wireless communications and neuroimaging to speech processing and machine learning, rely on observing the same phenomenon through several measurements. These measurements, which we call views, may come from different instruments, from different locations, or from different modalities, and each view records something the others miss. What should be extracted from such multiview measurements depends on the application. In some applications, each view is incomplete on its own, and the goal is to merge the views into a single representation that describes the phenomenon more fully than any one of them does alone. This process is known as multiview fusion. In arXiv:2610.28978v1 [cs.IT] 20 Oct 2026 Accepted for publication in Entropy 2 of 100 

other applications, the opposite holds. Each view captures the phenomenon completely, but most of what it records is unrelated to the phenomenon, and the goal is to keep only the part that is due to the phenomenon. Prior information that points to the interesting part of the recordings is often unavailable, and the only side information is that jointly observed samples of the different views share something. Since the phenomenon is the only thing common to all views, the task becomes to recover what the views have in common. This problem, which we call common factor recovery, is the subject of this tutorial. The problem is important because it arises across engineering and the sciences, typically when the signal of interest is weak and no supervision is available, and it has been posed independently in fields that share little else. We open with three instances of it, from wireless communications, neuroimaging, and machine learning. 

Wireless communications. A fundamental problem in wireless communications is to recover a transmission that is received buried under others that are orders of magnitude stronger. For example, consider two base stations, each equipped with several antennas, receiving uplink transmissions from the users in their vicinity, as in Figure 1. The trans-missions of users near the boundary between the two cells, the cell-edge users, reach both stations, but at low power, since those users are far from either one. A cell-center user close to a given base station is received there at high power and lies near the noise floor at the other. Every user has to be decoded, and the ones near a station present no difficulty. The cell-edge users are the challenging case, since their signals sit underneath those of the nearby users. Neuroimaging. A fundamental problem in neuroimaging is to estimate the spa-tiotemporal response to a stimulus. This is usually done by extracting task-related pairs of temporal signatures and brain activation maps from recorded brain signals. For exam-ple, consider multiple subjects performing the same visuo-motor task while functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) records their brain activity. A visuo-motor task is one in which the subject produces a movement in response to a visual stimulus, so that Accepted for publication in Entropy 3 of 100 

visual and motor areas are engaged together. A common instance is pressing a button whenever a checkerboard pattern appears on a screen. For each subject, the measurements can be arranged in a matrix of size space × time by stacking the time series of all volume elements, or voxels. Each recording mixes the response to the task, which is shared across subjects, with spontaneous activity private to each individual subject. The goal is to infer the task-related temporal and spatial components of the response from the recordings without knowing what the stimulus was or what the two components look like. The only assumption available is that the measurements of all subjects share them. 

Multimodal machine learning. A fundamental problem in multimodal learning is to build a representation of two modalities that keeps what they agree on and discards what belongs to only one. For example, consider a speaker recorded in two modalities while talking. In the first one, sensors track the positions of the tongue, lips and jaw as the vocal tract moves. In the second one, a microphone records the resulting sound. Several common factors characterize both, among them the phoneme being produced and the speaker’s identity, speaking rate, age and sex. Each recording also carries variation the other modality does not. For example, the articulatory traces carry sensor noise and involuntary movement, and the waveform carries the acoustics of the room. Each also carries measurement noise independent of the other. Learning from both modalities isolates the factors they share. A few labels are then enough to build a representation of the factor a downstream task needs. Naturally, one wonders whether common factor recovery, in these three examples and in general, is just an ordinary estimation task. As we will see, the problem is much more challenging, for three technical reasons. The first is that we do not know which part of the measurements corresponds to the common factor and which to the private ones, since we observe only their mixture. In the most general case, we also lack any kind of side information about their shape, statistics, or any other characteristics. The only thing we know is the coupling of the joint measurements, i.e., that jointly observed multiview samples share something. Infinitely many valid decompositions are consistent with the same measurements, each assigning some factors to both views and others to only one. As will become clear, what counts as common and private has to be defined more rigorously than this. The second reason is the absence of supervision. The cell-edge users are not identified in advance, their number can even be unknown, and their symbol sequences may be partially or fully unknown. In fMRI, the expected brain response is unknown, though a canonical shape is often assumed. In a controlled experiment the stimulus timing is known but is deliberately not used, so that the recovered response is not constrained by the assumed model. Under naturalistic stimulation, such as watching a film or listening to continuous speech, there is no design to use at all. The speech recordings may come with no labels, or with very few. The third is that the common factor need not dominate in the measurements. For example, in the wireless cellular network use case, cell-edge users are received at lower power than the interference, so any criterion that ranks structure by energy discards exactly their transmissions. Any methodology must therefore provide four elements, namely a rigorous definition of the shared structure, an objective that allows us to design a well-posed mathematical problem for the extraction of the common factor, an estimator that computes it from finite samples, and conditions under which the estimator returns the defined object rather than an artifact of the data. This tutorial is organized around those four requirements. Accepted for publication in Entropy 4 of 100 

1.2. Four Communities, One Problem 

Remarkably, the problem set out above has been studied by four communities, us-ing different semantics, and the resulting bodies of work developed with few explicit connections between them. 

Statistics. Hotelling introduced canonical correlation analysis (CCA) in 1936 [1], seeking the linear combinations of two sets of variables that correlate most strongly with each other. His examples were reading and arithmetic scores in schoolchildren, and prices and quantities across related commodities. Kettenring [2] later cataloged five criteria for extending the formulation to more than two sets, two of them his own, with all five coinciding for two sets of variables and differing beyond that. 

Information theory. Gács and Körner asked in 1973 what two random variables hold in common in the strict sense of a quantity that each can compute on its own. They showed that such a quantity can be far smaller than the mutual information, vanishing entirely for most distributions of practical interest [3]. Two years later, Wyner posed a different question, namely how much information a latent variable must carry in order to render the two random variables conditionally independent, and answered it with the common information [4]. The Gray–Wyner network [5] gave that quantity an operational meaning as the minimum rate on a shared communication link, subject to lossless reconstruction at a total rate equal to the joint entropy. Tandon, Sankar, and Poor [6] later extended the Gray–Wyner network to several sources under a privacy requirement. 

Signal processing. Wang and Poor showed in 1998 that a receiver can estimate the subspace spanned by the users’ signatures directly from the received data, and use that estimate to suppress interference from users whose signatures it does not know [7]. Two decades later, Ibrahim and Sidiropoulos showed that two receivers observing a common subset of users can recover the subspace that subset spans using CCA, with no knowledge of any signature [8]. 

Machine learning. Lai and Fyfe published a first kernel version of CCA in 2000 [9], and Akaho proposed in 2001 the formulation in use today, in which the linear combinations of each view are replaced with functions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space [10]. In 2013, Andrew et al. replaced the kernel with a neural network per view, which yielded deep CCA [11]. Later, the same correlation objectives formed a basis for current self-supervised learning, where the two views are constructed from one sample rather than observed [12,13]. CCA remained largely confined to statistics for a long time. Borga observed in 2001 that CCA was surprisingly little known in learning and signal processing, and wrote a tutorial addressed to those two audiences [14]. Tutorials aimed at machine learning [15,16] and neuroscience [17] followed. These treatments establish how CCA is formulated and how to compute it efficiently, which leaves two questions open. The first is what the computed quantity means. A large canonical correlation says that some linear combination of the first view agrees with some linear combination of the second. Going beyond this requires an assumed generative model that names what the common factor is, such as a cell-edge user or a response common to several subjects. The second is whether the recovered structure corresponds to anything real. A high correlation between two transformed views shows that the transformations agree. However, it does not necessarily show that the views share something concrete, since the agreement can be manufactured by the transformations themselves, especially when the class they are drawn from is rich. Answering that requires more than a generative model. It requires conditions under which what is recovered is a common factor rather than an artifact, which a correlation criterion by itself does not supply. Attempts to answer the first question were made twice, and neither answer identifies the common factor. Information theory answered it as a rate. Gács and Körner and Wyner Accepted for publication in Entropy 5 of 100 

each wrote down a quantity in bits, and the Gray–Wyner network turns Wyner’s into the rate that a link shared by two receivers must carry [3–5]. These quantities settle how much the two views have in common and what describing it costs. However, they say nothing about what the common factor is. Bach and Jordan answered the question with a generative model [18]. Each view is its own affine function of one shared latent vector plus zero-mean Gaussian noise with its own arbitrary covariance. Under this assumption, the maximum-likelihood estimates of the model parameters are given by the canonical directions, and the posterior mean of the shared latent vector given either view is a linear function of the canonical variates of that view. Naming the common factor, as a cell-edge user or a task response, remains application specific. The second question was answered much more recently [19–22], and in the later ones one key condition is the same conditional independence that Wyner wrote down in 1975. In this tutorial, we take those information-theoretic definitions as the foundation. We develop them first and then introduce CCA. For jointly Gaussian views, the canonical correlations determine Wyner’s common information exactly, whereas the link between CCA and the Gács–Körner common information is only one-sided. We then trace the line from subspace methods in communications through CCA to the identifiability theory that came later, in both linear and nonlinear settings. 

1.3. Tutorial Objectives and Contributions 

The main objective of this tutorial is, thus, to make four bodies of literature accessible to a reader who wants to understand how shared structure appears across multiple jointly observed sources. Each of the four communities has developed a substantial body of theory and tools, largely without reference to the others. We present the main principles behind common information and canonical correlation in detail, together with examples of applications, so that a reader can see how the concepts of the four literatures relate, apply the resulting tools, and assess what the outcome of an analysis means. A further objective is to encourage progress on these topics, and for that reason the tutorial also discusses open problems and research directions. Specifically, the tutorial makes the following contributions. • We develop the Gács–Körner and Wyner common information from first principles, together with the Gray–Wyner network, their extensions to several and to continuous variables, and worked examples for binary and Gaussian sources (Section 2). • We present CCA from Hotelling’s formulation, show that for jointly Gaussian views the canonical correlations determine both the mutual information and Wyner’s common information, and give the conditions under which CCA and generalized CCA recover the common subspace (Section 3). • We follow the signal processing thread from the blind multiuser detection of Wang and Poor to CCA-based receivers, for cell-edge users and for a second view designed at the transmitter (Section 4). • We cover kernel, deep, and probabilistic formulations of nonlinear CCA, explain their trivial solutions, state the conditions under which the common factor is identifiable, and relate these conditions to the feasible sets of the Gács–Körner and Wyner common information. We also connect these formulations to self-supervised learning (Section 5). • Throughout, boxes labeled Connection relate the information-theoretic and the statisti-cal developments, each labeled with the type of relation it states, and Section 6 collects further extensions and open problems. 

1.4. Relation to Existing Tutorials & Monographs 

The prior art focuses mainly on one of the two main topics, common informa-tion and canonical correlation. On the statistics and learning side, several tutorials on Accepted for publication in Entropy 6 of 100

Borga’s tutorial [14] covers the statistical formulation and its links to related second-order methods, with information theory entering only through the mutual information of Gaussian views. Hardoon et al. [15] introduce CCA and kernel CCA to machine learning researchers and sketch an extension of CCA to more than two sets. Uurtio et al. [16] add regularized and sparse variants together with a brief account of the deep one. Wang et al. [17] write a primer for neuroscientists, with worked examples that link brain imaging to behavior, psychopathology and genetics. Yang et al. [23] give the broadest coverage of the formulations, sorting them into multi-view, probabilistic, deep, kernel, discriminative, sparse and locality preserving families, and pairing each with representative applications and datasets. None of them treats the information-theoretic definitions as the foundation of the subject. Only Yang et al. and Wang et al. raise identifiability, Yang et al. for Bayesian CCA and Wang et al. for linear CCA when the number of samples is small relative to the number of variables. However, neither examines the conditions under which the recovered factor is guaranteed to be a common factor. On the information-theoretic side, the monograph of Yu and Tan [24] surveys Wyner’s and the Gács–Körner common information in great depth. The authors cover the opera-tional meanings of both notions in distributed source simulation and common randomness extraction, as well as their extensions, together with noise stability and the associated functional inequalities. The Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi maximal correlation enters that monograph as a tool in the analysis of the Gács–Körner common information and of noise stability, but the monograph does not reach CCA or the identifiability of the common factor. The monograph of Huang et al. [25] starts from the information-theoretic side and shares the most common ground with the present tutorial. That monograph builds on the modal decomposition of the joint distribution of a pair of variables, mainly on finite alpha-bets, together with a local information geometry perspective. From there, the monograph devotes a chapter to Hotelling’s CCA for jointly Gaussian vectors under linear features, and relates the features of the modal decomposition to both Wyner’s and the Gács–Körner common information. In addition, the monograph surveys only briefly the kernel and deep formulations of CCA as well as multivariate extensions. Finally, the monograph does not address the identifiability of the common factor, and the detection and estimation covered there are statistical formulations, outside the wireless communications setup. # 2. The Information-Theoretic View of Common Information 

2.1. What Does “Shared” Mean? 

In Section 1.1 we considered three problems. In the first, we are interested in combining the signals received at two base stations to decode the cell-edge users, which both stations receive at low power. In the second, several subjects perform the same task during fMRI scanning sessions, and we are interested in estimating the response to the task that is shared by all subjects. In the third, a speaker is recorded by vocal-tract sensors and a microphone, and we are interested in a representation of the factors the two recordings share, such as the phoneme being spoken. One may notice that all three are instances of the same, more abstract problem. In each, several jointly observed views are available. Each view is generated by a factor common to all views together with factors private to that view, and we are interested in recovering the common one. Many other problems share this form. Two microphones record one speaker, two cameras watch one scene, and gene expressions and clinical measurements come from one patient. The views may differ in form, because each is recorded by a different instrument, at a different location, or from a different subject. In this section, we lay the foundations for studying the shared structure of two or more random variables, which information theorists quantify as common information. This is the first step toward the question that runs through the tutorial. What do the views share, and how can we recover what they share? 

Two examples show what the answer can look like. Suppose we install two perception systems at an intersection, to support autonomous driving and traffic monitoring. For each detected object, such a system returns a label from its own label set. The two systems come from different manufacturers, and their label sets differ significantly. We therefore want a third system, on top of the two, that aligns their outputs. Unlike the perception systems, the third system never processes sensor data. Notice that this requirement alone is met by the trivial pair of maps that sends every label to a single common label, such as “object”, and possibly by coarse labelings such as vehicle and person. Among all such pairs of maps, we want the pair whose common labeling describes the detected objects as finely as possible. What is shared, in this case, is then a factor that explains the dependence between the views, and the most parsimonious such factor is sought. The two readings of “shared” lead to different quantities, and information theory makes each precise. Gács and Körner formalized the first, and Wyner the second. Both quantities are measured in bits and derive their meaning from coding problems. We develop them in turn, beginning with the earlier one, due to Gács and Körner.

Hence, W carries as much information as X and Y themselves, and KGK (X; Y) = I(X; Y) = H(X) = H(Y). At the other extreme, f

and g could both be constant, mapping all values to a single label. This always satisfies the constraint W = f (X) = g(Y) trivially, but gives H(W) = 0 and carries no information, as in the second example, where this was the only feasible option. The interesting case lies in between, where f and g define a coarse grouping of the values of X and Y, respectively, into matching clusters. Then, W simply carries the cluster index. The maximization of H(W)

searches for the finest such grouping that each of X and Y can determine on its own, i.e., the richest shared partition of the two alphabets that is consistent with the joint distribution. are the labels that Systems A and B assign to that object. Consider a sedan passing through the intersection. System A labels the sedan as car, so the object lies in the atom {X = car } of 

σ(X), which collects every object that System A calls a car. System B labels the same object as sedan, so the object lies in the atom {Y = sedan } of σ(Y). The join σ(X, Y) records both labels, and the object lies in the atom {X = car, Y = sedan }. The meet records only what both systems agree on. The object lies in the atom light vehicle, which equals {X = car }

and also equals {Y = sedan } ∪ { Y = SUV }. Hence each system determines from its own label that the object is a light vehicle, while only System B can tell a sedan from an SUV. KGK (X; Y) ≤ I(X; Y), (2) with equality if and only if X and Y are conditionally independent given the common part W∗ [3]. In general, the inequality can be strict, and the gap can be arbitrarily large, as 

KGK (X; Y) can vanish entirely even when I(X; Y) is arbitrarily large. This vanishing is not a technicality but a fundamental limitation of the Gács–Körner notion, and is precisely the observation encapsulated in the title of their paper, “Common Information is Far Less Than Mutual Information” [3]. 2.2.1. Connection to Maximal Correlation The definition in (1) relies on the Shannon entropy H(W), which is infinite for any continuous random variable. Quantizing such a W at resolution ∆ gives an entropy of approximately h(W) − log ∆, where h denotes differential entropy, and this value diverges as ∆ → 0. Replacing H(W) by h(W) does not resolve the issue. The common part 

W∗ is defined only up to relabeling, since any invertible function of a common part is again a common part, whereas differential entropy is not invariant under such maps. For Accepted for publication in Entropy 12 of 100 

instance, h(aW ) = h(W) + log |a| for any a̸ = 0, so the maximum of h(W) subject to 

W = f (X) = g(Y) is unbounded. Differential entropy can also be negative. Hence, once X

and Y share a continuous common part, the entropy objective no longer measures how rich the shared structure is. A more tractable question to study is whether a nontrivial common part exists at all. In the general case, such functions f and g specify a random variable c(W∗) for some deterministic function c,which is not necessarily invertible. The Gács–Körner notion thus captures no common structure in jointly Gaussian pairs. Inter-estingly, the analysis of Witsenhausen is geometric. Applications The Gács–Körner common information has found applications across several areas. In machine learning, the Gács–Körner framework provides a principled information-theoretic foundation for multi-view representation learning, where the goal is to separate shared from view-specific factors of variation [36,39]. 2.2.4. Limitations and the Need for a Richer Notion The Gács–Körner common information, while mathematically elegant, captures only deterministic shared structure and is blind to any probabilistic common information. As discussed above, KGK (X; Y) = 0 for most distributions of practical interest, including all nondegenerate jointly Gaussian pairs, even when I(X; Y) is arbitrarily large. This fundamental limitation motivates the search for a richer notion of common information, one that allows the shared variable W to be probabilistically rather than deterministically related to each view. Instead of requiring W = f (X) = g(Y) exactly, one could ask for the minimum-rate description of W that renders X and Y conditionally independent. This is precisely the question that Wyner’s common information, introduced in the next subsection, addresses. 

2.3. Wyner’s Common Information 

Wyner [4] proposed a more flexible definition. Instead of requiring the common variable to be computable from each observation alone, Wyner required a common factor that explains the dependence between them.

The interest in this network lies in the fact that the total rate can remain at its minimum H(X, Y) while each receiver obtains less than H(X, Y), which saves bandwidth, storage and decoding effort at the receivers at no extra cost to the transmitter. The goal is to characterize all achievable rate triples (R0, R1, R2) that allow lossless reconstruction. Among these, we seek, at the minimum total rate, the encoding that keeps the common rate R0, which represents the information that must necessarily be shared between the two receivers, as small as possible. Gray and Wyner showed that H(X, Y) remains optimal in the distributed setting described above, since every achievable triple satisfies R0 + R1 + R2 ≥ H(X, Y), and some triples attain equality ([5], Theorems 2 and 3). Going Beyond Wyner’s Common Information Thus far, we have treated X and Y as given and asked how efficiently they can be compressed. The reverse question is also important. How would one engineer two variables to exhibit a desired correlation, and how much shared randomness does that take? This is the problem of distributed channel synthesis, analyzed by Cuff [59], where Wyner’s common information C(X; Y) is the rate the two sides must communicate to build the correlation without sharing any randomness. A different direction varies how faithfully the synthesized distribution must match the target PXY . Measuring that mismatch by a Rényi divergence of order α, which for α > 1 is stronger than the Kullback–Leibler one, gives the Rényi common information of Yu and Tan [60,61]. As α grows, this quantity interpolates between Wyner’s common information, which is recovered for every α ≤ 1, and the exact common information of Kumar, Li, and El Gamal [42], reached at α = ∞ [62]. Finally, when the common information of two variables is infinite, a real-valued vector w is needed to carry the shared randomness. Li, Hanna, Diggavi, and Fragouli [63] consider the common information dimension, dim (w), and compute it in closed form for jointly Gaussian vectors. Applications Common information arises in several problems of information theory. The Gray– Wyner network [5] is the original setting, where Wyner’s common information is the smallest shared rate at minimum total rate for lossless reconstruction [4] and, under suit-able conditions, for lossy reconstruction [56]. Viswanatha, Akyol, and Rose used this network to model a database that stores two correlated sources and serves requests for each source separately [67]. Storing the sources jointly minimizes the storage rate but makes retrieval costly, whereas storing them separately does the opposite. They showed that the optimal trade-off between storage and retrieval connects the operating points of Wyner and Gács–Körner, which correspond to these two extremes. Sula and Gastpar [68] solved two related problems explicitly for Gaussian sources. They showed that Gaussian codebooks are optimal for the Gray–Wyner network with a sum-rate constraint on the private links, which gives the rate region in closed form. Moreover, they considered Wyner’s common in-formation, together with a relaxation that only bounds the conditional mutual information, for scalar and vector sources. Another field where common information appears is information-theoretic security. In secure source coding, a transmitter describes a source to a legitimate receiver and uses a secret key shared with that receiver to limit what an eavesdropper can infer. Satpathy and Cuff studied secure source coding with Gaussian sources [69]. Characterizing the trade-off between the communication rate and the key rate requires finding an optimal auxiliary vari-able, similar to the one in Wyner’s common information. Their analysis also gives Wyner’s common information of jointly Gaussian vectors in closed form, generalizing the scalar result of Xu, Liu, and Chen [53]. On the Gács–Körner side, the common information is the rate of shared randomness that two parties can extract from correlated observations without communicating. Allowing public communication leads to the common-randomness and secret-key capacities studied in [70–73]. Common information also appears in caching, where part of the data is delivered to a user before the user’s request is known. Wang, Lim, and Gastpar considered a user who requests one of two files with equal probability. Once the cache is large enough, the optimal cache content is a description that renders the two files conditionally independent, and the smallest cache rate at which this is possible is Wyner’s common information [74]. When the files need only be reconstructed within a given distortion, lossy versions of the Gács–Körner and Wyner common information also play an important role in caching [75]. In machine learning, common information has also been learned directly from data. Kleinman et al. [39] define a notion of common information that can be estimated from samples and recovers the Gács–Körner common information as a special case. They use this notion to train a variational autoencoder that separates the information shared by two views from the information unique to each. On the Wyner side, Ryu et al. [76] learn a compact common representation of two modalities for joint and conditional generation. Accepted for publication in Entropy 29 of 100 

Their motivation is channel synthesis, where Wyner’s common information is the smallest rate needed to build the correlation, as discussed above. Huang and El Gamal [77] develop efficient solvers for two relaxations of Wyner’s problem and apply them to multimodal clustering. Related information-theoretic objectives have been used to improve adversarial robustness [78] and to measure the dependence among several variables without estimating their distributions [37]. 

2.8. How Many Kinds of Common Information Are There After All? 

Thus far, we have covered several distinct quantities under the same name, and it is worth stepping back to see how they relate. In particular, this section introduced the Gács–Körner common information KGK , Wyner’s common information C, its K-variable extension, the common entropy GCE , and the Tandon–Sankar–Poor quantity CTSP . Maximal correlation entered as the test for whether KGK is positive. A few others were mentioned only in passing, such as the exact common information and the Rényi common information. This raises a natural question: How many kinds of common information are there, and what separates them? 

Despite the variety, they fall into two families that pull in opposite directions. The first family is intersective. Quantities in this family ask what the sources hold in common, in the sense that each source can produce the shared part on its own. Gács–Körner is the prototype here. The Gács–Körner common information seeks the richest variable that every source determines, so the quantity maximizes an entropy. The Tandon–Sankar–Poor quantity is its stochastic, privacy-driven cousin, since CTSP also captures only information that is available from every source individually. Maximal correlation belongs here as well, as the criterion for whether any such shared variable exists at all. The Gács–Körner and Tandon–Sankar–Poor quantities do not increase as more sources are added, because agreement across a larger set can only get harder to reach. The second family is generative. Quantities in this family ask what must be shared to explain or reproduce the dependence, rather than what each source can recover alone. Wyner’s common information is the prototype. Wyner’s common information seeks the most parsimonious variable that explains the dependence by rendering the sources conditionally independent, so the quantity minimizes a rate. The K-variable extension 

C(X1; . . . ; XK ), the common entropy GCE , and the exact common information are of the same kind. The extension C(X1; . . . ; XK ) does not decrease as more sources are added, because a single variable must now account for potentially more dependence. The two pro-totypes therefore move in opposite directions with the number of sources. The intersective quantities never increase, and the K-variable Wyner quantity never decreases. The two families coincide only in special cases. For two discrete variables with finite alphabets, the prototypes satisfy KGK (X; Y) ≤ I(X; Y) ≤ C(X; Y), and both inequalities hold with equality exactly when the joint PMF, after a relabeling of the symbols, is block-diagonal with product blocks. In general, the intersective and generative values differ, and jointly Gaussian variables make the gap extreme. For a correlation 0 < |ρ| < 1, the Gács–Körner common information vanishes, while Wyner’s common information is finite and positive. Partial information decomposition (PID) [79] offers a third and complementary lens. PID splits the information that two sources X and Y jointly carry about a third variable T

into four nonnegative parts, 

I(X, Y; T) = R(X, Y; T) + UX (X; T) + UY (Y; T) + S(X, Y; T), (40) where R is the redundant information that each source carries about T, UX and UY are the unique information that only one source carries, and S is the synergistic information Accepted for publication in Entropy 30 of 100 

that only the two together reveal. The redundant part R belongs with the intersective family, since the information about T that R measures is available from each source alone, in the spirit of Gács–Körner. The synergistic part S is information that the two sources hold only jointly, since S appears only when the two sources are combined. Synergy has no counterpart in either family of common information. If X and Y are independent uniform bits and T = X ⊕ Y, then KGK (X; Y) = C(X; Y) = 0, yet the full bit of I(X, Y; T) is synergistic, since neither source alone carries any information about T. PID is therefore complementary to our story rather than a refinement of the story. The main difference is that PID rests on a target variable T, whereas the common-information notions we discussed describe the dependence between the sources themselves, aiming to discover or engineer shared structure and then exploit that structure. The synergistic term S(X, Y; T) in (40) names an older idea, namely that information can appear only in combination. McGill [80] studied three variables and defined their interaction as the change in the mutual information between two of them when the third is held fixed. Hu [81] later extended mutual information to any number of variables through an inclusion–exclusion formula on entropies. For two variables, mutual information is 

I(X1; X2) = H(X1) + H(X2) − H(X1, X2), and for three variables the formula of Hu reads 

I(X1; X2; X3) = H(X1) + H(X2) + H(X3) + H(X1, X2, X3)

− H(X1, X2) − H(X1, X3) − H(X2, X3), (41) which equals I(X1; X2) − I(X1; X2 | X3). The interaction of McGill is the same quantity with the opposite sign. Unlike mutual information, I(X1; X2; X3) can be negative, as both authors observed, and a negative value is the footprint of synergy. In the XOR example above, I(X; Y) = 0 while I(X; Y | T) = 1 bit, so I(X; Y; T) = −1 bit. More generally, the decomposition (40) gives I(X; Y; T) = R(X, Y; T) − S(X, Y; T), so the three-variable quantity turns negative exactly when synergy outweighs redundancy. The common-information notions, by contrast, keep only the shared part and stay nonnegative. This line of work is a rich topic in its own right, but lies outside the scope of this tutorial.

This section developed the information-theoretic foundation for the rest of the tutorial. Throughout, the objects of study were jointly distributed random variables, and the ques-tion was how to quantify what they share. # 3. Canonical Correlation Analysis 

3.1. Three Problems to Set the Scene 

Before the mathematics, we start this section by introducing three problems from different fields, each asking the same underlying question. For the first problem, consider a wireless sensor network (WSN). Each node carries several sensors and produces a bundle of recordings. Bandwidth is scarce, so we would like to find pairs of nodes whose recordings are strongly correlated and encode such pairs jointly rather than separately. The question here is which pairs of nodes genuinely share structure, and how we can quantify their correlation in order to sort all pairs and pick the pairs we can afford to encode jointly. For the second problem, we switch to functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). There, the brain volume is imaged as a grid of small volume elements, or voxels. Each voxel carries a time series of its blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal, a proxy for local neural activity. Neighboring voxels tend to fluctuate together, partly through shared blood-supply factors and partly because the single-voxel signal is spatially smoothed, to suppress noise, which correlates adjacent voxels. Hence, it is natural to treat a neighborhood of voxels as a single unit rather than a lone voxel. We then ask whether two different nonoverlapping neighborhoods exhibit correlated activity, as a step toward mapping how functional brain networks are organized. For the third problem, consider an urban cellular network, where each multi-antenna base station forms a cell and serves the users within the cell. A user near the boundary of two adjacent cells is typically received weakly by both stations. Each station alone may be incapable of decoding that user, but since both stations receive a weak copy of the same transmission, the stations can collaborate to detect such users and decode their messages. The questions that arise are how two base stations can discover that a faint common signal is present in both received signals, and how the stations can cooperate to decode a message that neither could recover alone. The three problems differ in every surface detail, yet are instances of the same abstract form. In each of them, a view is described not by a single scalar random variable, as before, but by a vector of channels or features collecting a set of related signals, gathered at a multi-sensor node, over a voxel neighborhood, or at a base station. The question is whether two such multivariate views share hidden common structure, and how strongly. The concepts of common information developed in Section 2 can still be applied here in many different ways. For example, one could measure the common information between scalar-valued signals from different views and then report an average. However, two nodes may share a weak source that no single pair of their sensors reveals, because noise and interference bury the common signal in each single-channel measurement. Most of the time, the natural grouping of the channels that make up a view carries a mathematical structure that can be exploited. When the same source signals appear mixed throughout the different channels of a view, processing all channels together can approximately extract a wide range of these signals, from stronger ones to weaker ones. Processing every channel on its own does not provide these degrees of freedom. As a result, in detecting multiview correlations, jointly processing all channels within a view can be significantly more effective, as we will see. Mathematically speaking, the joint processing of the channels of each view will be performed here by taking linear combinations of the channel signals and looking for the combinations whose outputs are most correlated. The question of this section then becomes whether some combination of the first set agrees with some combination of the second, which treats the two sets as wholes rather than in parts. An ideal answer has to provide two things. The first is a number reporting how strongly the two multivariate views are Accepted for publication in Entropy 33 of 100 

related, which allows the pairs of sensor nodes above to be sorted and the strongly related ones encoded jointly. The second is the resulting combinations themselves, since they carry the shared structure. This is exactly the question canonical correlation analysis was built to answer, as we see next. 

3.2. Hotelling’s Original Formulation 

Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) was introduced by Harold Hotelling in 1936 [1], nearly four decades before the common-information notions of Gács–Körner [3] and Wyner [4]. Hotelling was a statistician and economic theorist whose motivations were explicitly empirical. In a study by Truman L. Kelley, the measurements on each schoolchild fell into two groups. The reading group held reading speed and reading power, and the arithmetic group held arithmetic speed and arithmetic power. Hotelling was interested in finding what reading and arithmetic abilities have in common. In a second, economic setting, the variables were the prices and the quantities of a group of related commodities such as wheat, potatoes, rye, and barley, each commodity contributing its price to one set and its quantity to the other. There, the question was how prices and quantities move together across the whole group. In each case, the variables split into two sets, and the interest lies not in either set alone but in what the two sets share. In the scholastic example, Hotelling concluded that reading and arithmetic involve one common mental factor, and in the economic one, the prices and quantities of related commodities must be studied as a group rather than one commodity at a time. He argued that the classical one-variable-at-a-time approach could not reveal such shared structure, and his idea was to reduce each set to a single linear combination, one from each set, chosen so that the two are as correlated with each other as possible. That maximal correlation measures how strongly the two sets share their common factor. Despite its power, CCA largely remained confined to statistics, with a few exceptions. As late as 2001, Borga [14] noted that although canonical correlation was by then standard in economics, medical studies, meteorology, and even the classification of malt whisky, it was “surprisingly unknown in the fields of learning and signal processing.” That is no longer an accurate description of either field. In machine learning, CCA entered through its kernel and deep formulations, and deep CCA together with its autoencoding and identifiable variants was published at the main machine learning conferences [11,21,82,83]. The agreement objectives of current self-supervised learning rest on the same criterion [12,13]. In signal processing, CCA underlies the geometry of linear estimation, filtering, and Gaussian channel capacity [26], blind detection of unknown users in cellular uplinks [8,27], underlay spectrum sharing [84,85], signal alignment in IoT networks [86,87], scalable formulations for many views [20,88], and multisubject fMRI analysis [89–91]. Tutorials aimed at these audiences followed in learning, computing, and neuroscience venues rather than statistics ones [15–17]. So far, CCA has given us the canonical correlations and the aligned representations 

Z1 and Z2. It is worth pausing to ask a more basic, almost philosophical question. Why should two views be correlated at all, and what ties them together? As we see next, Bach and Jordan [18] proposed a rigorous answer. Two views can be correlated because they share something, a common latent factor that drives both. Each observation is then a noisy, linearly transformed glimpse of the same hidden cause, and the correlation we measure is the trace this factor leaves on the two views at once. Once the factor is known, nothing further links the views, so they become conditionally independent given the factor, which is exactly Wyner’s feasibility condition. This is a generative stance rather than a descriptive one. Instead of asking how aligned the two views are, a generative model posits the mechanism that makes them aligned, and turns CCA into the estimation of that mechanism.

Generalized CCA: From Two Views to K Views 

Thus far, we have studied the two-view setup, and we now understand that setup well. We know what CCA estimates, as well as models and conditions under which CCA recovers the true common latent subspace. The problems that opened this section, though, may involve more than two jointly observed random vectors, or views. A sensor network usually has many nodes, an fMRI study has many subjects, and a cellular uplink is heard by several base stations. Generalized canonical correlation analysis (GCCA) conceptually extends CCA to K ≥ 2 views. Rather than aligning a single pair, GCCA aims to align all views at once, producing reduced views XkQk that are close to each other. Unlike the two-view case, though, GCCA is not a single method. The quality of a K-view alignment can be measured in several inequivalent ways, and for K > 2 these give genuinely different formulations, and different solutions. 3.8. Summary 

This section developed CCA as the second-order, sample-based counterpart of the common information of Section 2. Here, the protagonists were no longer scalar random variables but random vectors. Then, the question shifted to how many correlated variables can be formed by taking linear combinations within each view, and how to estimate them from data. Before moving on, it is worth collecting the main ideas. • CCA finds the most correlated directions between two views. Hotelling’s problem is solved in closed form through an eigenvalue decomposition. The canonical correla-tions are the cosines of the principal angles between the two view subspaces, so CCA is at heart a statement about the relative geometry of those subspaces. Accepted for publication in Entropy 48 of 100 

• For the Gaussian case CCA computes Wyner’s common information. The canonical correlations determine both the mutual information, as shown by Scharf and Mullis and later by Borga, and Wyner’s common information, as shown by Satpathy and Cuff. The balanced latent of the Bach–Jordan probabilistic model attains Wyner’s common information. By analogy, the directions with canonical correlation one span the common subspace and play the role of the Gács–Körner common part of Section 2. Wyner’s common information is finite and positive when some canonical correlations are nonzero and all are below one, that is, when the views are correlated but share no common subspace. • CCA identifies the common subspace under a generative model. When each view is a common component plus a private one, simple rank conditions say when the shared subspace is recoverable from noiseless data, separating what CCA can and cannot disentangle. • The framework extends to K views. The five formulations collected by Kettenring coincide for two views and diverge beyond. MAXVAR yields D consensus variables, the columns of an orthonormal basis G, through an eigenvalue problem. Finally, 

K-view identifiability, cast as an intersection of subspaces, generically improves as more views are added. The three problems that opened this section can now be addressed. For the sensor network, the pairs of nodes are sorted by their canonical correlations, and the strongly correlated pairs can now be encoded jointly rather than separately. For the two voxel neighborhoods, the same quantities report whether the activity is correlated, while the canonical variates carry the shared activity itself and not only its strength. For the detection and decoding of cell-edge users, neighboring base stations can forward their received measurements to a central unit. That unit computes the canonical correlations and uses them as a detection criterion, i.e., a high canonical correlation indicates the presence of one or more weak users. The extracted variates can then be processed further to decode those users. Section 3.6 gives the conditions under which the recovered subspace is the one spanned by the cell-edge users. Generalized CCA handles the many nodes of a real network, and the many subjects of an fMRI study, in one step rather than pair by pair. Our vantage point in this section was mostly statistical. Canonical correlation analysis and its generalizations were estimators of a shared structure from second-order statistics, and the probabilistic reading tied that structure back to the common-information notions of Section 2. Signal processing reaches the same object from a different starting point. There the common subspace arises from a concrete detection problem, where several sources are received across an array and the task is to tell them apart. The tools are the ones we have already used, common subspaces and the singular value decomposition, but there the guarantee that matters is operational, namely how little a receiver must know in advance to recover the sources. The next section follows that thread. That section starts with a single array and adds a second, at which point the common subspace of CCA reappears. 

# 4. The Signal Processing Thread, from Subspace Methods to CCA 

We now leave the statistical perspective of the previous section and take up the one of signal processing. Subspace methods are a cornerstone of signal processing, and their starting point is a single observation about multichannel data. A multichannel receiver records M channels per snapshot, which may be the elements of an antenna array or, as we see below, the chips of a spreading code. Gathering a whole recording into a matrix, we let each row hold one snapshot across all channels and each column hold the entire history of one channel. As a result, M channels observed over N snapshots give a matrix 

X ∈ RN×M. All quantities in this section are real valued. The real convention costs nothing, Accepted for publication in Entropy 49 of 100 

since a complex baseband record splits into its real and imaginary parts, which stack into a real matrix with twice as many channels. What the receiver records is a superposition of a few sources, such as communication users, radar echoes or acoustic sources. When the sources are few and the channels many, the noiseless data matrix has low rank. The row space of the noiseless data matrix is a signal subspace and everything orthogo-nal to that subspace is a noise subspace (array processing usually arranges the snapshots as columns of the data matrix, in which case the signal subspace is the column space; with the snapshots as rows, as here, the same subspace is the row space). The signal subspace matters because every source contributes to the data only through its direction across the 

M channels, and all these directions lie in the signal subspace. Restricting attention to the signal subspace therefore keeps everything the sources contribute, discards the noise outside that subspace, and reduces the problem from M dimensions to the number of sources. As long as the sources are strong enough, the two subspaces can be read off the eigendecomposition of a sample covariance matrix, or equivalently the SVD of X, with-out knowing anything about the sources themselves. This subspace estimate is the first step of a broad family of methods, from high-resolution direction-of-arrival estimation to interference suppression, blind channel estimation, and the multiuser detection we develop here. The signal subspace holds the contributions of all sources together, while a receiver is often interested in only some of them, such as the users that the receiver has to decode. To recover those users, the receiver must suppress the interference of all other users, who can be quite strong. Suppressing that interference amounts to finding the subspace that the desired users occupy, after which the receiver decodes the individual users within that subspace. Both steps rely on prior information, and this section asks how little a receiver must know in advance to carry them out. The question becomes most challenging for the weak users of interest, whose low received power makes their detection hard, especially in the presence of strong interference. We progress from receivers that rely on prior information about the users of interest to multiview receivers in which a second recording of the same transmission takes the place of most of that information. The second recording is provided either by a second receiver or by the transmitter itself, which sends its data twice. We can already see from this discussion how this problem connects to Sections 2 and 3. The cell-edge users of Section 1.1 are hard to decode, because every station receives them at low power. However, two stations that both receive a cell-edge user record the same transmission of that user. Similarly, when a user repeats a block, the two halves of the received block carry the same transmission. These recordings are natural candidates for views. Estimating the subspace occupied by the weak users then becomes the problem of estimating a common subspace that the views share. The problem of interest here therefore has an intersective character, aligned with the Gács–Körner notion of common information from Section 2. At the same time, the several channels of each recording form a random vector, which serves as a view. A limited number of snapshots for learning the joint distribution, together with an object of interest that is itself a subspace, then point to algebraic methods for computing the subspace intersection with CCA. The sufficient conditions for recovering the common subspace, established abstractly in Section 3.6, therefore apply here as well, with the dimensions counted in channels and block lengths. The rest of this section is organized as follows. Section 4.1 states the signal model and the problem. Section 4.2 begins with a single-antenna receiver and code-division multiple access (CDMA), where each user spreads its symbols with a signature known to the receiver, and presents the subspace approach of Wang and Poor, in which only the signature of the user of interest must be known. Section 4.3 turns to a receiver with multiple Accepted for publication in Entropy 50 of 100

Section 4.5 applies this idea to recovering cell-edge users. Finally, Section 4.7 compares what each receiver must know and what that knowledge costs, and discusses two assumptions whose relaxation leads to the rest of this tutorial.

4.2. Multiuser Detection in CDMA: The Wang–Poor Approach 

Subspace methods did not begin in communications. They were originally developed for sensor array processing and spectral estimation, and they were carried into multiuser detection later [7]. As a first step toward recovering the symbol streams of some users, we assume structure that makes the problem easier. Specifically, we assume that the subspace col (H) is fixed by design, and that the receiver knows the column of H that corresponds to a user of interest, up to a scalar. This assumption is realistic in the classical setting of multiuser detection, code-division multiple access (CDMA), where the prior information of the receiver takes a concrete form, the signature and timing of each user. Under CDMA, several users transmit simultaneously over the same frequency band, and each is assigned a distinct signature waveform, or spreading code, that the receiver uses to tell them apart. A signature is a sequence of M chips, where a chip corresponds to the elementary pulse from which the code is built, and M is called the processing gain. Here, we assume a single-antenna receiver and synchronous users. User r is assigned a signature dr ∈ RM, normalized to unit norm, and transmits each of its symbols br [n] by multiplying the symbol with dr, so that each symbol is spread over M chips. Each user therefore sends one symbol per M chips, and its rate drops to 1/ M of what an unspread transmission would achieve. The channel scales the transmission of user r by a gain hr,and the receiver records the sum over all users. 4.4. The Multi-Array Leap from Signal Subspace to Common Subspace 

Consider again the cell-edge problem of Section 1.1, illustrated in Figure 1. Users near the shared boundary of two cells are attenuated by path loss, so their signals arrive at each base station at low signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio. At the same time, they are received by both stations at comparable power, whereas a user close to one station is received strongly there and lies near the noise floor at the other. This is the near–far problem of Section 4.2, with the cell-edge users in the far position at both stations at once. The receivers of the previous subsections handle that problem only when they are handed the signature of the user of interest or long pilots, which are exactly what may not be known for a cell-edge user. Without them, the cell-edge transmissions cannot be extracted from the signal subspace that they span together with the strong cell-center users. Even when the pilots of a cell-edge user are known, the strong cell-center users corrupt both the timing and the estimated response of that user unless the pilots are sufficiently long, as discussed in Sections 4.2 and 4.3. Ibrahim and Sidiropoulos [8,19] proposed to combine the recordings of the two sta-tions in order to recover the cell-edge users, and they named their strategy base-station interferometry. They assume that both stations receive the same cell-edge transmissions, so, as noted in Section 4.1, the cell-edge waveforms lie in col (B1) ∩ col (B2). If each cell-center user is received essentially by one station only, the cell-edge transmissions are the only ones that appear in col (B1) ∩ col (B2). A single station has nothing that singles out Accepted for publication in Entropy 55 of 100 

the cell-edge users, but the two stations together do, since the cell-edge users are exactly the ones they share. Finding the subspace shared by two data matrices is the problem that CCA solves in Section 3. Under the identifiability conditions of Section 3.6, CCA recovers col (Bce ) = col (B1) ∩ col (B2), where the columns of Bce are the cell-edge transmis-sions, without a signature or pilots, and regardless of the power of the cell-center users at either station. The indifference to interference power follows from the invariance property of Section 3, by which canonical correlations are unchanged under any invertible linear transformation of either view. The power of each user enters a view through its mixing matrix, which is such a transformation, so a cell-center user is discarded for being private to one station, however strongly that user is received. Noise, on the other hand, is added to a view rather than applied to it as a transformation, so noise does affect the canonical correlations. Hence, the cell-edge users must still be received above the noise at each station for a common subspace to emerge. The second station therefore removes the effect of interference power, but not the effect of noise. Next, we present the work of Ibrahim and Sidiropoulos [8,19] in detail.

Each user then carries a unique identification sequence, and correlating the recovered streams with these sequences resolves both ambiguities [8,19]. 4.5.1. When Is a User Common? Theorem 3 is noiseless, and in practice every user is overheard, to some degree, at both stations. The appendix of [8] analyzes this case and shows that, for nearly orthogonal sequences and large arrays, the canonical correlation of each user is the product of two factors, one per station. Each factor lies between zero and one and increases with the signal-to-noise ratio at which that station receives the user. A high canonical correlation therefore requires both factors to be high, and a single high factor does not suffice. A cell-center user is received strongly by its serving station but weakly by the other, so its canonical correlation stays low regardless of how strong its signal is at the serving station. A cell-edge user is received by both stations, so its canonical correlation is limited only by its signal-to-noise ratios. Strong interference is thus harmless to CCA precisely because it is one-sided. 4.5.2. The Same Computation Synchronizes the Two Stations So far, the two recordings were assumed aligned in time. If they are not, the cell-edge streams in X1 and X2 no longer match row by row. Since independent symbol streams shifted by even one symbol are already uncorrelated, no common subspace remains and Accepted for publication in Entropy 57 of 100 

even the largest canonical correlation is small. Computing the largest canonical correlation over a range of relative delays and selecting the delay at which it peaks therefore recovers the alignment [8]. Unlike the correlation-based synchronization of Section 4.3, which the strong cell-center interference corrupts, the canonical-correlation-based approach requires no pilots. As a result, without any side information, CCA can (i) reveal whether cell-edge users are present, (ii) synchronize the two stations when they are, and (iii) estimate the cell-edge subspace, leaving potential prior information to be used only for unmixing the cell-edge streams. Revealing the presence of cell-edge users is a hypothesis testing problem. A closely related problem has been studied in the context of passive radar [115]. There, two multi-antenna arrays, playing the role of our two stations, record signals, and the question is whether they share a common subspace signal of known dimension. The noise at each array may be arbitrarily correlated across its antennas but is uncorrelated with the noise at the other array. This setting is similar to the cell-edge one, as the cell-center interference can be seen as noise of this kind when its leakage to the other station is neglected. Modeling the common symbols and the noises as Gaussian, Santamaría et al. [115] derived a generalized likelihood ratio test that can be used for deciding whether a common signal is present by comparing a product of functions of the leading canonical correlations to a threshold.

4.7. Summary 

Throughout this section, the problem was the factorization X = BH ⊤ + E of (73), and the goal was to retrieve a subset of the columns of B, namely the symbol streams of the users of interest. We began with the most favorable case, in which the directions of the columns of H are fixed by design. Such a case arises in CDMA with a single antenna. There, all users follow the same protocol, and their spreading signatures provide the observation dimensions that the missing antennas cannot, at the cost of rate. For this case, the classical zero-forcing and MMSE detectors are written in terms of the signatures of all users, since nulling an interferer requires knowing its direction. From that point on, the receiver was given less prior information, and the coordination among users became weaker. Cell-edge detection motivated why retrieving only a subset of the columns of B is of interest, and it Accepted for publication in Entropy 59 of 100 

transferred the coordination from the users to the infrastructure, namely two multi-antenna base stations and a central processing unit. Section 4.6 carried the same idea to a single multi-antenna receiver, where prior coordination is needed only between the receiver and the users of interest, as the other users need not follow the same protocol. Apart from this coordination, the price is a multi-antenna receiver and half the transmission rate of the users of interest. In exchange, the receiver asymptotically attains the linear MMSE detector knowing only the interleavers of the desired user. Table 3 lays the sequence out. The first two rows are the classical detectors for CDMA. In its classical form, the zero-forcing detector needs the signatures of all users, and the MMSE detector also needs the received amplitudes and the noise variance. Wang and Poor [7] showed that the signal sub-space, estimated from the data, provides the directions of the interferers. As a result, both detectors can be written in closed form from this subspace, with the desired user’s signature as the only prior knowledge, since the timing can then be estimated from the data [109,110]. These receivers can detect only users that spread their symbols with their own signatures, since the detectors are built from these signatures. Each such user sends one symbol per M

chips, so its rate drops to 1/ M. Other CDMA users must be assigned distinct signatures, but the CDMA users can also share the band with preexisting narrowband systems, since the linear MMSE detector suppresses a rich variety of narrowband interference [106,107]. With antennas in place of chips, the columns of H are set by propagation rather than by design, so the designed knowledge must enter elsewhere. Pilots can take this role, as the designed knowledge from which the MMSE detector is built. The two CCA receivers also come with different kinds of guarantees. Theorem 3 is an identifiability statement. It ensures that, in the absence of noise, CCA recovers the subspace of the cell-edge users. Theorem 4 is an optimality statement. It ensures that, with ensemble correlations, the canonical directions coincide, up to scaling, with the linear MMSE detector, which maximizes the output signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio among all linear detectors. Together, the two results suggest that a multiview receiver with little prior knowledge can do more than detect a potentially weak desired user. When the transmitter creates a second view by design, such a receiver can also attain the best linear performance. Finally, the signal processing thread of this section arrives at the same object that Section 3 reached from the statistical and information-theoretic directions. There, CCA appeared as the maximum-likelihood solution of a latent-factor model, its canonical correla-tions determined Wyner’s common information for jointly Gaussian views, and Section 3.6 stated its identifiability conditions for a general two-view generative model. Here, CCA ap-pears as a detector, reached from a low-rank data matrix and a question about interference. As we saw, the same identifiability conditions re-emerge as requirements on the numbers of antennas and the block length. The statistical, information-theoretic, and signal processing threads thus meet at CCA, each contributing its own reading of the common component. Two assumptions ran through this section, and relaxing each leads to a problem that the rest of this tutorial takes up. First, both routes to a second view assume that the pairing of observations is known in advance. The two base stations are fixed by the deployment, and the two halves of a repeated block are fixed by the code. Ibrahim and Sidiropoulos name the case of more than two stations as future work in the conclusion of [8], and take it up in [116], where generalized CCA is applied to a selected subset of stations. More views relax the identifiability conditions, as Theorem 2 shows. However, a realistic deployment has several groups of cell-edge users, each received by a different subset of stations, and some of those stations may themselves be mobile, as with base stations mounted on unmanned aerial vehicles. Which stations share which users is then unknown and must be estimated jointly with the shared structure, a problem with close ties to subspace clustering. Section 6 briefly covers the work of Karakasis and Sidiropoulos [117] on unifying subspace clustering and generalized CCA. At each transmitter, the power amplifier compresses as it approaches saturation [118], so a map f acts on each entry of B. # 5. Beyond Linear: Nonlinear and Deep CCA 

To this point, we have restricted ourselves to linear transformations of the observed views. However, relaxing this assumption is essential in many real-world use cases, as illustrated next. We begin with the communication models of Section 4. Every model there takes the received signal to be a linear mixture of the transmitted symbols, which assumes ideal radio front ends. As discussed in Section 4.7, practical front ends are nonlinear, which turns the model into (87), and where the nonlinearity acts decides whether a common subspace survives. The transmitter map f keeps the mixture linear in the distorted symbols f (B). The receiver map g, which models a saturating amplifier or a low-resolution analog-to-digital converter that clips and quantizes, acts on each entry after the mixing. A linear mixture followed by such an entrywise map is known as a post-nonlinear mixture [121,122]. The common subspace that CCA recovered in Section 4 is then no longer a subspace of the received data, and linear CCA may no longer recover the cell-edge users. A similar issue arises in neuroimaging. Consider two neighborhoods of voxels in the brain, each recorded over time, as in Section 3, and suppose a common neural process drives both. These neighborhoods can be from the same person, or from two different subjects presented with the same stimulus. Here, the information recorded is not that process, but the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal it induces. The map from neural activity to the BOLD signal passes through the hemodynamic response, i.e., the changes in blood flow and oxygenation that the activity triggers, and this map is nonlinear. The usual linear convolution model is only its first-order approximation [123,124]. Because these hemodynamic responses may vary from region to region [125], and even more across different subjects, the two neighborhoods project the shared process through two different nonlinear maps. As a result, each recording is a distinct nonlinear function of the common process together with activity private to that neighborhood. A linear analysis, such as the canonical correlations of Section 3, may understate, or miss, the coupling that the shared process induces. Finally, consider two views of one phenomenon from which we would like to extract the common factors. A natural instance arises in the study of speech production. One view can be a set of articulatory measurements, namely the positions of the tongue, the lips, and the soft palate as the vocal tract moves. Another can be a set of features extracted from a recording of the acoustic waveform. Figure 12 shows an example of the two views over the same time interval. The phoneme is realized as a particular configuration of the vocal tract, and the speaker’s anatomy, which varies with identity and age, sets the geometry of that vocal tract. Hence the phoneme, the identity, and the age are all imprinted on the articulatory measurements. The same factors shape the sound as well, since the phoneme and the speaker’s vocal characteristics jointly determine the sound that is heard [126]. There are two reasons for considering CCA with nonlinear transformations. The first is to measure how strongly the views are correlated. The canonical correlations answer this question, and the measure they provide is useful in its own right. In the fMRI example, a measure of correlation between brain regions can help improve our understanding of how the brain functions. The second is the recovery of the common latent factors. In the nonlinear communications example, CCA with nonlinear transformations, together with a few known symbols to resolve the remaining ambiguities, could recover the weak cell-edge users. More broadly, the recovered common factors can be used for a downstream task. The benefit is clearest in a particular regime, when samples from jointly observed high-dimensional views are plentiful, the low-dimensional common factors are known to carry the information a task needs, but the labeled data at hand are scarce. There, requiring the learned functions of the two views to agree on the unlabeled samples restricts the set of candidate pairs of functions, and with it the number of labeled examples needed to find a pair that performs the task well [128]. Both of these reasons rest on the same premise, which Section 3 made explicit for linear maps. Behind each collection of views lies a common factor that drives all of them, as in the probabilistic model of Bach and Jordan [18] and the common-plus-private model (63). Here, each view is formed from the common factor and a private factor of its own through its own, possibly nonlinear, function. This is the modeling assumption we follow in the rest of this section. It is motivated by examples like those above, where in some cases a common factor is easy to name, though extracting it is not trivial. A more general problem is to recover both the common and the private factors, a task known as latent disentanglement. Although that is an active research topic, in this tutorial we focus mostly on what it takes to recover the common factor. Under this model, the task of this section is to rigorously recover the common factor, or at least to reliably gauge how strongly the views express it. The natural way to look for the common factor is to find view-wise transformations that make the views maximally correlated. When the transformations are linear, this is Hotelling’s canonical correlation. When the views are single variables and the transforma-tions are arbitrary functions, normalized to have zero mean and unit variance outputs, we obtain the Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi (HGR) maximal correlation of Section 2. This section works at their meeting point, maximizing correlation over nonlinear transformations of multi-variable views. Accepted for publication in Entropy 63 of 100 

Maximizing correlation is an objective, not a guarantee, and the gap between the two is what this section is really about. Two challenges define that gap. First, correlation alone is too permissive. Once arbitrary transformations are allowed, correlation can be driven high by degenerate or overfitted choices that agree on nothing meaningful. Hence, the transformed views may agree without reflecting the common factor we assumed. The normalization constraints of HGR, zero-mean and unit-variance outputs, help but do not fully resolve this. Recall from Section 2 that a pair of functions attaining S(X, Y) = 1certifies that the Gács–Körner common information is positive, but need not recover the common part W∗, only a possibly coarser function c(W∗) of it. This observation brings us to the second challenge. Even when the transformations agree on a common variable, the variable may be any coarsening c(W∗) of the common part, from a coarse grouping of its values up to W∗ itself, and these coarsenings carry different amounts of common information. It is not clear which of them the objective singles out. Finally, learning the transformations from finite samples sharpens both difficulties. The rest of this section is organized as follows. Section 5.1 presents optimal scaling, an early form of nonlinear CCA in which each variable of a view is transformed separately. Section 5.2 presents kernel CCA, which maps each view into a space of functions where ordinary CCA applies, and Section 5.3 shows why that approach must be regularized. Sections 5.4 and 5.5 replace that fixed map with a neural network learned from data, known as deep CCA, and show that the networks can agree on the training samples while carrying nothing the views share. Section 5.6 turns to formulations that fit a probabilistic model of the views rather than maximize a correlation. Section 5.7 gives two sets of conditions under which the common factor is provably recovered, and Section 5.8 relates these methods to self-supervised learning, where the views are constructed from one sample rather than observed. Finally, Section 5.9 summarizes the section. 

5.1. Optimal Scaling: The Pre-Neural Nonlinear CCA 

The first step beyond linear CCA was a half-step, taken through a method known as 

optimal scaling . Optimal scaling is a general method for handling variables that are categori-cal or recorded on an unknown scale. Each observed variable of a view is replaced by a transformed version, where each transformation is drawn from a class fixed by the type of the variable and chosen so as to maximize the correlation. All the transformations are esti-mated jointly with the canonical weights. Three variable types are distinguished [129,130]. A numerical variable takes values on a numerical scale, such as a temperature. An ordinal variable takes categories with a natural order but no meaningful distances between them, such as low, medium and high. A nominal variable takes categories with no order at all, such as eye color. Accordingly, a numerical variable admits any affine transformation, an ordinal variable any monotone nondecreasing one, and a nominal variable any assignment of values to its categories. The idea goes back to Fisher, who assigned scores to the cate-gories of a contingency table so that the two sets of scores were maximally correlated [131], and psychometrics developed it into the general method described here [132]. Placing the transformed variables inside the MAXVAR consensus objective (70) gives a nonlinear version of CCA, proposed for two views by Young, de Leeuw and Takane as CORALS [132] and extended to K views by van der Burg, de Leeuw and Verdegaal as OVERALS [133]. A variant called CANALS [134] applies optimal scaling to two views but maximizes the sum of the squared canonical correlations rather than their sum, and coincides with CORALS for a single component. A direct consequence of the admissible classes above is that optimal scaling extends CCA beyond the linear case only through ordinal and nominal variables, since with all variables numerical the method reduces to ordinary CCA [133]. 5.2. Kernel CCA 

Optimal scaling relaxed linearity under two restrictions that are worth separating. Its encoders in (88) nonlinearly transform each coordinate of a view separately and then mix the transformed coordinates linearly. Hence, no nonlinear transformation may act on several coordinates at once. The second restriction is that each coordinate transformation, although estimated from data jointly with the linear reduction, is confined to a restricted class fixed in advance by the type of the variable. When every variable is numerical the admissible class is affine and optimal scaling reduces to ordinary CCA [133]. Hence, nothing is gained on precisely the continuous views that motivated this section, the voxel time series, the received signals, and the acoustic features. Kernel CCA lifts the first restriction and relaxes the second. For each view, a single transformation is learned and applied to the view as a whole rather than coordinate by coordinate. The view is first mapped into a space of very high, possibly infinite, dimension, in which the transformation becomes a linear operation. The class of candidate transformations is then a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), fixed by the choice of a kernel for each view, and it is far larger than the classes of optimal scaling. The price is that, once the class is this rich, agreement between the transformed views can be produced by the transformations alone. This is the difficulty already met in Section 5.1, where it was an empirical hazard under extreme nonlinearity and high noise. In the kernel setting it can be Accepted for publication in Entropy 65 of 100 

detected, as we will see in Section 5.3, and for the kernels in common use it is the generic outcome rather than an edge case. The Unrestricted Problem Has Its Own Lineage Every method of this section so far draws the transformations from a restricted class, and Section 5.2 noted that the Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi maximal correlation ranges over all measurable functions instead. That problem was studied in the population setting by Lancaster [143] and is solved algorithmically by alternating conditional expectation, the ACE procedure of Breiman and Friedman [144], which alternates between conditional expectations of one transformed view given the other until the transformations stop chang-ing. Michaeli, Wang and Livescu return to Lancaster’s theory and show that the optimal transformations are given by the singular value decomposition of an operator built from the joint density of the views [145]. Replacing the joint density by an estimate from the training samples turns this into a finite eigenvalue problem. In their implementation, a Gaussian kernel density estimate restricted to a few nearest neighbors yields a sparse matrix nor-malized by a diagonal one, so that, unlike (100), no kernel matrix has to be inverted. They report that the resulting method outperforms kernel CCA and is competitive with methods that learn the transformations with neural networks, at a lower computational cost. Accepted for publication in Entropy 71 of 100 

Note where this line of research leaves the lesson of this subsection. Nothing bounds the transformations in the Lancaster-based approach, so the penalty of (99) has no coun-terpart. What takes its place is the density estimate. The resolution of the estimated joint density is what limits how finely the transformations can distinguish points, so the safe-guard against the degeneracy of (102) is not removed but relocated, from a norm penalty to the bandwidth or neighborhood size of a density estimator. Two caveats attach to that relocation. The first is that estimating the joint density is a harder problem than the one we are after, since the canonical transformations are a functional of the density and not the density itself. Vapnik’s principle advises against exactly this, stating that “when solving a given problem, try to avoid solving a more general problem as an intermediate step” ([146], Section 1.9). The second is quantitative. The sample size needed to estimate the joint density of the views grows exponentially in their total dimension M1 + M2, and the views that motivated this section, the voxel time series, the received signal blocks and the acoustic features, are all high-dimensional. Michaeli, Wang and Livescu work with a kernel density estimate over a small number of nearest neighbors, which is what keeps the method practical, and the recovered transformations rest on that estimate regardless. 

5.4. From Kernels to Networks 

The consistency question opened by Bach and Jordan was answered by letting the penalty vanish slowly enough as the sample size grows [141]. Computationally, the N × N

Gram matrices remained the binding constraint, since decomposing them costs O(N3)

if done directly, and a succession of approximations was developed to avoid it, among them incomplete Cholesky factorization [137], partial Gram–Schmidt [15], the Nyström method [147], randomized nonlinear CCA [148], and incremental SVD [149]. Arora and Livescu [150] brought the last of these to kernel CCA, reducing the decomposition to 

O(m2 N) time and O(mN ) space for a truncation rank m ≪ N, and applied it to the speech production setting discussed at the beginning of this section. Deep CCA was proposed against that computational background. Andrew et al. [11] give two reasons for replacing the kernel by a network. The kernel is chosen rather than learned, so it limits the representation. Moreover, by (93), kernel CCA carries one coefficient per training sample, so training scales badly with N and every new point has to be compared against the whole training set. On the other hand, a network has a fixed number of weights, set by the architecture, so it trains well on large data and can be evaluated on a new point without using the training set. What is given up in the exchange is the closed-form geometry of Section 5.3, and with it the ability to detect degeneracy by counting dimensions. Next, we turn to approaches that use neural networks.

It is tempting to read this as the coarsening of Section 2.2, but the two differ in what feasibility means. 5.6. Probabilistic and Variational Formulations 

Most of the nonlinear methods so far have learned transformations that make the views correlate as much as possible, and have treated the transformed views as estimates of the common latent factor. Section 5.5 ended with the formulation of DCCAE, which asks each encoding to reconstruct its own view, through a deterministic decoder, while the two encodings achieve high cross-correlation. Although this requirement makes the learned encodings carry information about their own views, it also creates a potential conflict. This is because the learned encodings are now required to serve two purposes that do not necessarily align. A deterministic decoder is a function, so it assigns the same reconstruction to all view samples mapped to the same encoding. To keep the reconstruction error small, the encoding itself has to tell those samples apart. A stochastic decoder, i.e., a conditional distribution of the view given the encoding, handles those samples differently. It spreads its mass over them, so the variation among them is represented by the conditional distribution, evaluated at that encoding, rather than by the encoding itself. The problem that emerges now is that reconstruction error is no longer a reasonable measure of fit for learning an encoder paired with a stochastic decoder, since the decoder returns a distribution rather than a single reconstruction. The natural measure is instead the likelihood this distribution assigns to the observed view. Learning under stochastic decoders therefore calls for likelihood-based objectives. The starting point is the probabilistic model (60) of Section 3.5 rather than Hotelling’s ratio, and no objective below is defined in terms of correlation. The methods of this subsection reverse the logic of the deterministic ones. They assume a probabilistic generative model of the views that contains a common latent factor. Then, they fit that model to the training samples by maximizing their likelihood, or a lower bound on it, and infer the common factor from the fitted model. 5.6.1. Variational CCA with Private Factors A more complete generative model also considers a private factor for each view, re-sponsible for the variation that the common factor does not explain. The limitation of considering a common factor alone shows up in reconstruction. For Gaussian observation models, the expectation term of the bound is a reconstruction error, and the best reconstruction it admits is E[xk | g], so all realizations of view k sharing the same g receive the same reconstruction. variations make the bound harder to optimize. When this happens, it may be beneficial to model private factors explicitly, alongside the common one. can reconstruct view k on its own while g is ignored, since nothing in the bound requires the fitted p(xk | g, ck) to depend on g. They argue that applying dropout to the samples of g, c1 and c2 helps prevent this, and find dropout on these samples to give most of their empirical gain. No theoretical guarantee is provided that the fitted model separates the common factor from the private ones. We left deterministic nonlinear CCA having seen that requiring each encoding to reconstruct its own view helps to avoid trivially correlated encodings. Moving from DCCAE to the probabilistic formulations of this subsection removes the conflict those encodings face, since reconstruction now arrives as a likelihood rather than as a competing term. However, generative models of high quality need decoders that capture the variation of each view, which leads to models that carry both common and private factors. VCCA with private factors, and the more recent approaches of Karami and Schuurmans [160] and Damianou et al. [161], still leave open the question of when common and private factors can be reliably separated in the latent space. A crucial assumption throughout this subsection is that the observed views are conditionally independent given the common factor g, which is the conditional independence that defines the feasible set of Wyner’s common information. Next, we turn to two works that provide conditions and algorithms with disentanglement and identifiability guarantees, under deterministic mappings. 

5.7. What Makes the Common Factor Recoverable 

Every method so far has left the same question open. Maximizing either correlation or a likelihood produces a latent factor, but in neither case could we say whether what is learned is purely common, or whether all of the common information is extracted. Both assume that the views are generated from a common factor together with a private factor per view, both take the views conditionally independent given the common factor, and both give conditions under which the learned factor is the common one up to an invertible reparameterization. Trivial Solutions Theorems 5 and 6 are population statements, while the objectives, losses and con-straints are all evaluated on finitely many training samples. 5.8. Connection to Self-Supervised Learning 

Everything in this section has assumed that the views are given by the problem, such as the acoustic and articulatory measurements of the speech example. Self-supervised learning drops that assumption. Its goal is to learn, from unlabeled data, representations that are useful for downstream tasks such as classification. To that end, self-supervised learning manufactures the views, usually by applying two random augmentations to the same image, designed to leave its semantic content unchanged, and then learns encodings that capture that content. The semantic content that an augmentation preserves thus plays the role of the common factor, and what the augmentation changes plays the role of the Accepted for publication in Entropy 85 of 100 

private one. What self-supervised learning optimizes then has the structure we have been working with throughout. One term rewards agreement between the two encodings, and a second term stops that agreement from being achieved on a constant. This subsection highlights how strong the similarities are between self-supervised learning and nonlinear CCA, and where they stop. An early work in this line, by Becker and Hinton, already named canonical correlation as what it extends, and the identifiability questions of Section 5.7 reappear in the self-supervised literature in nearly the same form. 5.8.1. The Original Argument Becker and Hinton [162] trained two small networks, which they call modules, on inputs that share only a common cause. In their multiview setup, two neighboring patches of a stereo image serve as views, and the only property they share is the local depth of the scene. The idea is to train the networks to agree, so that, without any labels, they learn to report depth. How agreement is measured, however, matters. The simplest measure admits the trivial solution of Section 5.5. As Becker and Hinton note, penalizing the squared difference of the outputs “usually causes each module to produce the same constant output that is unaffected by the input to the module and therefore conveys no information about it”. What they propose instead is that the outputs “agree closely relative to how much they both vary as the input is varied”. With a and b the outputs of the two networks, this corresponds to maximizing 

I(a, b) = 12 log V(a + b)

V(a − b) , (128) where V is the variance over the training cases. When a and b have equal variances σ2 and correlation ρ, we have V(a ± b) = 2σ2(1 ± ρ), so the ratio equals (1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ). Hence, maximizing (128) maximizes the correlation of the two outputs, and a constant output is no longer optimal. The paper itself describes the procedure as “a multilayer nonlinear extension of statistical methods such as canonical correlation or alternating conditional expectation because it maximizes the normalized covariation between the outputs”. In (128), agreement and the prevention of collapse are combined in a single ratio. A small denominator rewards agreement, and a large numerator rules out a constant output. 5.8.3. When the Views Are Given After All In multimodal learning, the views are again given by the data rather than constructed. CLIP [165] trains an image encoder and a text encoder jointly, maximizing the cosine similarity of the N matched image–caption pairs in a batch against the N2 − N mismatched ones under a symmetric cross-entropy loss. ImageBind [166] extends this to six modalities, namely images, text, audio, depth, thermal, and inertial measurement unit readings, but does not align all pairs. It aligns each modality to images only, using the InfoNCE loss on pairs (I, M) of an image and an observation of another modality, and reports that modalities M1 and M2 become aligned even though no (M1, M2) pair was ever seen in training. The topology is worth noting. Every modality is aligned to images alone, rather than to every other modality as in the all-pairs sum of correlations of SUMCOR. By analogy with MAXVAR, where all views are fitted to a single common representation G, the image embedding plays the role of G, and the emergent alignment is then what one would expect of a common representation that every view is required to match. 5.8.4. What Is Recovered Zimmermann et al. [167] consider views generated as follows. A latent is drawn uniformly from the latent space, a second latent is drawn from a conditional centered at the first, and the two views are obtained by applying the same mixing to the two latents. The mixing is never assumed known, only injective and differentiable. Under this model, they show that, in the limit of infinitely many negative samples, an encoder that minimizes a contrastive loss recovers the latent variables that generated the views. When the latent space is a hypersphere and the conditional is von Mises–Fisher, the minimizer of (129) recovers the latent up to an orthogonal map. When the latent space is a convex body, the cosine similarity of (129) is replaced by a distance induced by a norm, and the latent is recovered up to an affine map. What must be known is the form of the conditional that produced the positive pair, because the similarity used in the loss has to be based on the same metric as that conditional, so a contrastive objective identifies only the generative model it was built for. Von Kügelgen et al. [168] consider views generated as follows. The latent is split into content c ∈ Rnc and style s, and style may depend on content. A second latent is obtained by keeping the content and changing a random subset of the style variables, with every style variable changed with positive probability. The two views x and ˜ x are obtained by applying the same mixing to the two latents, and the mixing is smooth and invertible with a smooth inverse. When the views are given, as in Section 5.7, the corresponding conditions are hypotheses about the data that have to be argued for case by case, and the guarantees of a self-supervised method do not carry over to given views. This is also why the two fields focus on different things. Self-supervised learning focuses on designing the augmentation, and multiview learning focuses on justifying the assumptions. 

5.9. Summary 

This section replaced the linear maps of Sections 3 and 4 with functions drawn from broader classes. The main points are summarized below. • Rich function classes allow overfitting and trivial solutions. Optimal scaling, kernel CCA, and deep CCA restrict the function class in different ways, and without a sufficient restriction, the learned representation carries no information about the data, because learning criteria are evaluated on finitely many samples. In optimal scaling, nominal and monotone transformations can merge distinct values, and degenerate results have been reported under extreme nonlinearities and high noise. In kernel CCA without regularization, every canonical correlation can equal one regardless of the data. In the deep formulations, with a sufficiently rich class of networks, the empirical constraints can be met by representations unrelated to the views, because agreement is imposed only at the N training samples. • Probabilistic formulations assume conditional independence but do not single out the common factor. The variational methods assume that the views are conditionally independent given a shared latent factor, and fit the resulting model by maximizing a lower bound on the likelihood. Conditional independence places the factor in the feasible set of Wyner’s common information, but does not single it out, since the feasible set ranges from a minimally informative factor to the views themselves, and two fitted models with the same likelihood may sit at different points of that range. Adding private factors makes the model more expressive, but the private factor of a view can reconstruct that view while the shared factor is ignored. As a result, the question of when the shared and private factors can be reliably separated remains open. • Agreement alone does not determine the representation. Proposition 1 shows that a representation that agrees across views and also renders the views conditionally independent is the common factor, up to an invertible map. The criteria of this section reward agreement, but do not impose conditional independence, which enters only as an assumption of the generative model. Hence, even in the population setups, every function of the common factor agrees across views, including functions that discard part of the common factor. As a result, additional requirements are needed for the representation to retain the whole common factor. Reconstructing each view from its own representation makes the representation more informative, but private variation may leak into the representation. Reconstructing each view from the representations of the other views keeps private variation out, while requiring the representation to retain what predicts the other views. • The common factor is identifiable up to an invertible map, in the population. Lyu et al. show identifiability for two views, each an invertible function of the common factor and a private factor, with the common and private factors mutually independent. Karakasis and Sidiropoulos show identifiability for K views. They require only that the common factor be a function of each view, and allow the private factors to be Accepted for publication in Entropy 89 of 100 

dependent, provided that they are conditionally independent given the common factor. In exchange, they require at least one view to be mean dependent on the common factor, which makes the learned representation a nontrivial function of the common factor alone. Moreover, they show that when the stacked conditional mean of the views given the common factor is an invertible function of the common factor, the common factor is recovered up to an invertible map. At any fixed N, the trivial solutions remain available. • Self-supervised learning seeks nontrivial agreement between constructed views. 

Many of its losses pair an agreement term with a term intended to prevent collapse to a constant representation, which agrees perfectly across views but carries no informa-tion. The second term is realized by negative samples in contrastive learning, by a soft whitening of the cross-correlation matrix in Barlow Twins, or by a variance floor in VICReg. The identifiability result of von Kügelgen et al. assumes that the augmen-tation leaves content unchanged and changes style, and the choice of augmentation implicitly defines what content and style are. 

# 6. Further Extensions and Open Problems 

This section is in two parts. The first collects results that bear directly on the problem of this tutorial but fall outside what the preceding sections develop, so that a reader who wants to go further knows where to go. The second outlines open directions. 

6.1. Further Extensions 

6.1.1. Unifying Generalized CCA with Subspace Clustering Generalized CCA assumes that all K views share one common subspace. In many deployments they do not, and the views fall into clusters, with the views inside a cluster sharing a subspace. Karakasis and Sidiropoulos formulate this as subspace clustering of subspaces (SCoS) [117], in which the assignment of views to clusters and the common subspace of each cluster are estimated together. The formulation contains GCCA as the single-cluster case, and it extends subspace clustering from vector-valued samples to matrix-valued ones, which is why it unifies the two. 6.1.2. Tensor CCA and the High-Order Correlation Criterion Every criterion in Section 3 is assembled from pairwise correlations, so the extension to 

K views sums or otherwise combines quantities defined two views at a time. Luo et al. [169] replace that with a criterion defined on all K views at once, the high-order correlation of a covariance tensor built from the views. They show that maximizing it is equivalent to computing the best rank-one approximation of that tensor. The CP machinery of Section 2 therefore returns as the solver rather than as a representation of a distribution. Wong et al. [170] give the deep version, learning nonlinear per-view maps whose images are correlated in high order. Girka et al. [171] address a different tensor setting, in which each view is itself a higher-order tensor, and constrain the canonical vectors to admit an orthogonal rank-R CP decomposition within the regularized GCCA framework. To the best of our knowledge, none of the three comes with an identifiability result. 6.1.3. Identifiability Without Paired Samples The results of Section 5 assume that sample n of view k and sample n of view l come from the same draw. Timilsina et al. [172] remove that assumption for linear mixtures and establish identifiability of the shared components by minimizing a divergence between the cross-modality distributions, so the alignment they need is distributional rather than sample by sample. Accepted for publication in Entropy 90 of 100 

6.1.4. Identifiability Without Known Latent Dimensions The dimensions of the common and private factors are fixed in advance throughout this tutorial. Shrestha and Fu [173] show that content-style identifiability holds without prior knowledge of either dimension, provided sparsity constraints are imposed on the learned representations. Their setting is unaligned multi-domain data, in which a content variable is shared across all domains and each domain has its own style. As in the previous paragraph, identifiability is established by matching the latent distributions across domains. 6.1.5. Disentangling the Private Factors from the Common One Section 5.7 showed that, under the assumptions of Lyu et al. [21], an independence constraint between the shared and private blocks of each encoder also recovers the pri-vate factors. Sristi et al. [174] pursue a separation of this kind in multi-region neural recordings, partitioning the latent space of each brain region into orthogonal shared and private subspaces. There both subspaces are estimated with coupled transformer en-coders and decoders, which also handle the non-stationary temporal dynamics that a static model ignores. 6.1.6. Preventing Collapse in Deep CCA Section 5 shows that a rich enough transformation class can manufacture agreement between the views. He et al. [175] report the training counterpart of this, in which the performance of deep CCA drops sharply as training proceeds, and they relate it to the weight matrices of the networks becoming low-rank. As a remedy, they propose a noise regularizer that encourages each network to keep its weight matrices full rank, and the same regularizer also applies to deep GCCA. 6.1.7. Adaptive Estimation Wang and Poor also implement their blind detector adaptively, tracking the signal subspace as snapshots arrive with the projection approximation subspace tracking algo-rithm of Yang [176], rather than recomputing it from a whole block. The CCA estimators of Section 3 have no such counterpart and need the full record before processing. Peppas and Sidiropoulos provide one, an adaptive CCA that updates the canonical directions as samples arrive, applied to time synchronization for signal alignment [177]. 6.1.8. Applications Beyond the Three Examples The formulation of Section 1.1 has been carried into settings the tutorial does not develop, and we name a few of them here, grouped by field. In neuroimaging, a two-stage GCCA separates the task-related response shared across subjects from resting-state activity private to each one [89,91]. In network analysis, a related problem is graph alignment, where two graphs must be matched with no node correspondence given, and Karakasis et al. [178] show how subspace intersection can be used to align them. In wireless communi-cations, an underlay scheme places short-range secondary transmissions beneath a primary signal and recovers them without coordinating with the primary. Ibrahim et al. [84] show how CCA can be combined with repetition coding for this purpose, and the construction is carried to analog FM radio with several receivers [85]. Packet repetition is also part of many popular protocols in frequency-hopped IoT networks. Peppas et al. [86,87] exploit that repetition to create a multiview setup in which CCA applies directly to align packets received in different hops, and the same alignment is carried to multicarrier waveforms to make them resilient to interference [179]. 6.2. Open Problems 

6.2.1. Relaxing the Invertibility Condition The identifiability results of Section 5 assume each mixing function is partially in-vertible with respect to the common factor. The common factor is then a function of any single view and can be recovered from one view at a time. The next weaker assumption is that some joint function of all K views determines the common factor, with no single view sufficient on its own. To the best of our knowledge, neither an identifiability theory nor an algorithm is available in that case. Note that the weakening costs more than analytical convenience. A model identified this way cannot be applied to a single view at test time, so the weaker condition returns us to the multiview fusion problem set aside in Section 1.1. 6.2.2. Identifiability of the Private Factors In the most general model of Section 5, the private factors are required only to be conditionally independent given the common factor, and, to the best of our knowledge, no identifiability theory for them is written directly in terms of that conditional independence. The available guarantee, by Lyu et al., instead assumes that the private factors are inde-pendent of the common factor and of each other, and enforces this in learning through an independence constraint. What is missing is a framework that recovers the private factors under conditional independence alone. 6.2.3. Estimating the Clustering of the Views Removing the pairing assumption and removing the clustering assumption are dif-ferent problems, and they should not be conflated. The first has been addressed for linear mixtures. The second has not. Section 4.7 sets out the deployment that raises it, in which several groups of cell-edge users are each received by a different subset of stations and some of the stations are themselves mobile. Ibrahim and Sidiropoulos select a subset of stations and apply generalized CCA to it [116], which addresses the problem for a fixed candidate subset and leaves the estimation of the partition open. Note also that SCoS assumes the number of clusters is given, and neither that deployment nor the underlay setting above provides such a number. 6.2.4. Finite-Sample Behavior and Minimax Limits Kernel CCA comes with consistency results (Section 5.3), but, to the best of our knowledge, no comparable result exists for deep CCA itself. The minimax rates, i.e., the smallest estimation error for the common subspaces achievable by any estimator, as a function of the number of samples, the number of views, the subspace dimensions and the noise level, are a separate question and remain unknown. The multi-user extension of Tandon, Sankar and Poor described in Section 2 is a starting point, since the rate on the common link bounds how much information about the common factor reaches any decoder. Converting that rate into an estimation error bound is open. The obstacle is that the Gray–Wyner region is stated in entropy and mutual information while estimation theory works in mean squared error, and the I-MMSE relationship of Guo, Shamai and Verdú [180] is a natural bridge between the two formalisms. 6.2.5. Invariance, Domain Shift and Causality Correlation across views does not establish a causal relation. The factor these methods recover is the one that co-varies across the views, and none of the formulations surveyed here distinguishes the factor of interest from any other variable that the views happen to share, such as a confounder introduced by the acquisition process. The invariance that CCA enforces is indexed by the view, while the invariance that domain generalization requires Accepted for publication in Entropy 92 of 100 

is indexed by the environment, and a factor stable across views need not be stable across environments. Whether the first can be made to serve as the second is open, and answering it needs a model in which the views and the environments appear as separate indices. 

# 7. Conclusions 

This tutorial followed one question through information theory, statistics, signal processing, and machine learning. Given several views of the same phenomenon, what do the views have in common, and under what conditions can the common part be recovered from data? Section 2 gave two answers to the first part of the question for scalar-valued views. The Gács–Körner common information is defined through the highest-entropy function that each view can compute on its own, and Wyner’s common information through the least informative variable that renders the views conditionally independent. The two quantities bound the mutual information of the views from below and from above, and all three coin-cide when the views share a common part given which they are conditionally independent. Section 3 turned to vector-valued views. CCA finds, within each view, the linear combinations that are most correlated with each other. The solution follows in closed form from the SVD of the coherence matrix, whose singular values are the canonical correlations, the cosines of the principal angles between the subspaces of the two views. In this sense, CCA is the second-order counterpart of the common information of Section 2, and Section 3 related both notions to a single set of canonical correlations. For jointly Gaussian views, the canonical correlations determine both the mutual information and Wyner’s common information. The relation to the Gács–Körner notion is weaker. A canonical correlation equal to one certifies that Gács–Körner common information exists, and the directions with canonical correlation one play the role of the common part by analogy. On the side of recovery, CCA returns the common subspace exactly in the noiseless case, under rank conditions that require the common and private subspaces of the views to be linearly independent. Generalized CCA extends the analysis to K views, where identifiability, cast as an intersection of subspaces, generically improves as views are added. Section 4 reached the same problem from multiuser detection, where the goal was to recover selected users under strong interference. Our first stop was the result of Wang and Poor, who showed that, in CDMA with a single antenna, the signal subspace allows the zero-forcing and MMSE detectors to be built blindly, with the signature of the desired user as the only prior knowledge. With multiple antennas, the directions of the users are set by propagation rather than by design, and pilots take the role of the signatures. However, when only the pilots of the desired user are known, strong interference degrades the channel estimate, and longer pilot sequences are required, at the cost of rate. A second view allows the interference to be suppressed without pilots, and changes the question from which directions carry the most energy to which directions the views share, which is the question CCA answers. When the second view is provided by a second base station, CCA recovers the subspace of the cell-edge users in the noiseless case. When the second view is provided by repetition at the transmitter, the canonical directions converge, up to scaling and as the number of samples grows, to the linear MMSE detector of the desired user, and no channel estimation is required. Section 5 replaced the linear maps of CCA with nonlinear ones, drawn from optimal scaling, from a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, or from a class of neural networks. The richer the function class, the easier agreement becomes. Without regularization, every kernel canonical correlation can equal one regardless of the data, and a sufficiently rich class of networks can meet the deep CCA constraints at the training samples with representations unrelated to the views. Even in the population, when each view determines the common Accepted for publication in Entropy 93 of 100 

factor, every function of the common factor can be reproduced by all views, including functions that discard part of the common factor, so agreement alone does not determine the representation.The probabilistic formulations add conditional independence of the views given a shared latent factor, which places that factor in the feasible set of Wyner’s common information without singling the factor out. Under explicit generative models, Lyu et al. for two views and Karakasis and Sidiropoulos for K views showed that the common factor is identifiable up to an invertible map. Self-supervised learning pursues the same agreement between views that are constructed rather than given, and in the result of von Kügelgen et al. the recovered factor is whatever the augmentations leave unchanged. Of the open problems collected in Section 6, we single out four. Partial invertibility of each mixing function with respect to the common factor has not been relaxed, and relaxing that assumption to a joint function of all the views would return the problem to multiview fusion. No identifiability theory for the private factors is written directly in terms of their conditional independence. Finite-sample analyses cover only a few formulations, and minimax limits are unknown. Finally, the recovered factor is defined by agreement across views, and none of the formulations surveyed here separates a common cause from a confounder that the views share. The practical lesson is the same across the tutorial. A high canonical correlation, or a high agreement between learned representations, does not necessarily indicate shared structure, since the agreement can be produced by the transformations themselves. The recovered factor can be read as the common factor only when a well-posed learning criterion is used under conditions that make the common factor identifiable, and these conditions should be checked against the application before the result is interpreted.
