MINIMAX AND ADAPTIVE TRANSFER LEARNING FOR SPARSE CANONICAL CORRELATION ANALYSIS 

BY BO FU1,a AND DANDAN JIANG *1,b   

> 1School of Mathematics and Statistics, Xi’an Jiaotong University, a311342@stu.xjtu.edu.cn; bjiangdd@xjtu.edu.cn

We develop a transfer learning framework for high-dimensional sparse canonical correlation analysis (CCA) with multiple heterogeneous source datasets. Our goal is to improve estimation of the target canonical coefficient matrices by borrowing information from related sources. We characterize source-target similarity through discrepancies in the joint covariance struc-ture and establish minimax optimal rates under both joint and separate predic-tion losses, explicitly quantifying the gain from enlarged effective sample size and the cost of heterogeneity. To attain these rates, we first construct an oracle transfer estimator based on debiasing and truncated normalization. We then develop a computationally efficient procedure that adaptively achieves the minimax rate without prior knowledge of the sparsity levels, using aggrega-tion to control transfer bias and a truncated normalization scheme to preserve transfer gains under the scale-sensitive prediction loss. A data-driven source detection procedure is further proposed to identify potentially informative auxiliary datasets. Our analysis also yields a generalized sin-theta perturba-tion theorem that removes a commonly imposed comparability condition on leading canonical correlations in the sparse CCA problem. 

1. Introduction. Modern scientific studies increasingly collect data from multiple re-lated populations, cohorts, or sites. In many such applications, the target population of pri-mary scientific interest may have only a limited sample size, while auxiliary datasets from related populations are available and may contain useful information about the target popu-lation. Classical Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA; Hotelling, 1936) is a fundamental tool in multivariate analysis for characterizing the association between two sets of variables through maximally correlated linear combinations. It has found important applications in neuroimag-ing, imaging genetics, and multi-omics studies (Butler et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2023). How-ever, it is commonly encountered in practice that the data dimensions can be larger than the sample size (Bai et al., 2022; Bao et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2015; Gao, Ma and Zhou, 2017), in which classical CCA breaks down, and the leading canonical coefficient matrices are commonly assumed to be row sparse (Waaijenborg and Zwinderman, 2009; Hardoon and Shawe-Taylor, 2011; Lê Cao et al., 2009; Parkhomenko, Tritchler and Beyene, 2009; Gao et al., 2015; Gao, Ma and Zhou, 2017; Witten, Tibshirani and Hastie, 2009) to improve esti-mation and interpretability, which is called sparse CCA. Most existing theory and methodology for classical CCA and sparse CCA, nevertheless, have been developed for a single dataset, which can be restrictive in modern applications where the target sample size is limited but related auxiliary datasets are available. In practice, classical CCA and sparse CCA can exhibit substantial instability in small samples (Helmer et al., 2024), while modern genomic, neuroimaging, and multimodal studies are often con-ducted across related cohorts, hospitals, or sites that may share common cross-view asso-ciation structures. Despite this potential transferability, directly pooling related datasets may lead to biased estimation when their underlying distributions are heterogeneous. Transfer learning (Pan and Yang, 2009) has become a powerful tool for leveraging auxiliary datasets to improve perfor-mance while accounting for such heterogeneity. A common message from this literature is that auxiliary samples can substantially improve estimation when source and target popula-tions are sufficiently similar. It is therefore natural to ask whether related source datasets can be used to improve sparse CCA estimation for a target population. 1.1. Related work. In the conventional single-dataset setting, sparse CCA has been ex-tensively studied from both methodological and theoretical perspectives (Witten, Tibshirani and Hastie, 2009; Gao et al., 2015; Gao, Ma and Zhou, 2017; Mai and Zhang, 2019). More re-cent work has extended sparse CCA to broader distributional settings, including mixed data through latent Gaussian copula models (Yoon, Carroll and Gaynanova, 2020) and heavy-tailed data through sparse Gaussianized CCA (Di and Zou, 2026). These methods, however, primarily focus on estimating canonical structures from a single population. Several extensions of CCA have considered distributed learning or federated learning un-der differential privacy (Bertrand and Moonen, 2015; Chen and Zhu, 2024; Luo et al., 2026). These methods primarily aim to estimate a common canonical structure from decentralized data, rather than selectively exploiting heterogeneous auxiliary datasets to improve estima-tion for a prespecified target population. In parallel, transfer learning has been extensively studied in a variety of statistical prob-lems, including nonparametric classification (Cai and Wei, 2021), transfer reinforcement learning (Chai, Chen and Fan, 2025), principal component analysis (PCA; Li et al., 2024a), covariance matrix estimation (Cai et al., 2026), precision matrix estimation (Li, Cai and Li, 2022; Zhao, Ma and Kolar, 2026), and parametric regression models (Li, Cai and Li, 2022; Bastani, 2021; Tian and Feng, 2023; Jin et al., 2024; Fu and Jiang, 2025; Li et al., 2024b). Despite these advances, to the best of our knowledge, the statistical problem of target-specific sparse CCA estimation with heterogeneous auxiliary populations and its fundamental mini-max limits, has not been systematically studied. 1.2. Following Gao, Ma and Zhou (2017), we use this prediction loss to measure how accurately the estimated canonical variates recover their population counterparts for a new target observation, up to an orthog-onal transformation. It captures errors in both the estimated canonical directions and their normalization, hence containing more information than the projection loss. When auxiliary source datasets are available, it is expected that leveraging these datasets will enhance performance compared to employing the target dataset only. Our goal is to con-struct a computationally efficient and minimax optimal estimator for U (0) under the loss function (1.4) (and symmetrically, for V (0) ). However, transfer learning for CCA is intrin-sically more delicate than for problems involving a single covariance matrix. Unlike PCA, where the principal subspace is determined by a single covariance matrix and is orthogo-nal under Euclidean geometry, the canonical subspaces in CCA depend on both marginal and cross-covariance matrices and are normalized for unknown marginal covariance struc-tures. This additional dependency makes CCA more challenging; see Gao et al. (2015) for a detailed comparison of sparse CCA and sparse PCA. Consequently, heterogeneity across datasets can affect the target canonical directions through the two marginal covariance ma-trices and the cross-covariance matrix. Naively pooling source and target data can therefore introduce substantial transfer bias. To attain the separate minimax rate for U (0) , two key techniques are required. First, the transfer bias induced by pooling must be corrected without losing the improvement provided by the source datasets. We propose debiasing and aggregation approaches for this purpose. Second, the resulting estimator must be normalized without reintroducing an error deter-mined solely by the target sample size. As illustrated in Example 1.1, exact normalization with respect to the target sample covariance matrix can reintroduce additional stochastic er-ror with a rate limited by target sample size, while normalization with respect to the pooled covariance matrix using all data may retain bias caused by source-target heterogeneity. We therefore develop a truncated normalization scheme that balances these two effects. Our trun-cated normalization preserves the gains from transfer under the scale-sensitive prediction loss while also achieving smaller population-level normalization loss (2.6). This truncated normalization also improves numerical stability by avoiding a potentially unstable matrix in-version when the target sample size is small relative to r. In conclusion, our contributions are summarized as follows: • We develop a transfer learning framework for sparse CCA that leverages auxiliary source datasets to enhance the estimation accuracy and establish the minimax rates under both joint and separate loss functions. • We propose an oracle transfer sparse CCA estimator (Algorithm 1) that employs trans-fer, a debiasing technique under ℓ0-constraint, and a truncated normalization scheme and achieves the minimax-optimal rate under the separate loss function (1.4) when the set of informative source datasets and the sparsity levels are known. We further propose a com-putationally efficient estimator (Algorithm 2) when the informative source set is known, which, under additional sample size conditions, adaptively matches the minimax-optimal rate without knowledge of the sparsity levels, through an aggregation step and the same normalization procedure. Both estimators also yield smaller population-level normaliza-tion error. To prevent negative transfer, we apply a source detection algorithm. • We provide a more general version of the generalized sin-theta theorem that does not require the leading singular values to be comparable to the smallest signal singular value imposed in Theorem 5 of Gao et al. The results are exhibited in Figures 1–2. From Figures 1 and 2, we draw several conclusions. First, across all considered di-mensional and sample-size settings, Oracle-Trans-sCCA substantially improves upon 

Target-sCCA once informative source datasets are available. Moreover, its prediction er-rors for both U (0) and V (0) decrease rapidly as |A h| increases, which is consistent with the theoretical gain from the enlarged effective sample size provided by informative sources. Sec-ond, Pooled-sCCA can perform substantially worse than Oracle-Trans-sCCA , and even worse than Target-sCCA when only a small proportion of the source datasets are informative. This demonstrates that indiscriminately pooling heterogeneous source datasets may introduce considerable transfer bias and lead to negative transfer. As the number of informative sources increases, the performance of Pooled-sCCA gradually improves and eventually approaches that of Oracle-Trans-sCCA , reflecting the diminishing impact of non-informative sources. Third, similar results are observed under both the exact and ap-proximate settings and for the estimation of both canonical coefficient matrices. This suggests that our estimator is robust to model misspecification arising when the number of canonical correlations exceeds 2. Overall, these results demonstrate the potential efficiency gain from transferring information across related datasets, while also highlighting the importance of identifying informative sources rather than naively pooling all available source data. 6. Conclusion. In this paper, we developed a transfer learning framework for high-dimensional sparse CCA. By measuring source-target similarity through the spectral discrep-ancy between joint covariance matrices, we established minimax rates under both joint and separate losses, thereby characterizing the gain from informative source data and the price of heterogeneity. We proposed statistically optimal procedures that combine transfer, debias-ing/aggregation, and truncated normalization, and showed that they attain the minimax rates in the sense of prediction loss under suitable conditions. A generalized sin-theta theorem was also developed to support the perturbation analysis without requiring comparability between the leading and smallest signal singular values.
