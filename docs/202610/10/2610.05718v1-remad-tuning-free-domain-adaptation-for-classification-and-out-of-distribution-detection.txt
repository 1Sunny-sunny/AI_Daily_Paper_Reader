Abstract — We introduce Reduced-rank Mahalanobis Distance (ReMaD), a novel prototypical distance-based refinement to classification and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection using pretrained models without finetuning. We use embeddings of the target dataset to fit closed-form distribution statistics in the 

model’s latent space which can classify in -distribution samples and detect OOD samples, all without training or prior knowledge of the OOD data. Building on prototype classification and OOD detection, we analyze the distribution properties of large pretrained models when processing new datasets; based on this analysis, we formulate a simple modification to Mahalanobis 

Distance to adapt models’ latent space distributions to new 

domains by removing unused features, without the finetuning or hyperparameter searches required by other adaptation procedures. We demonstrate the efficacy of this method to adapt existing large pretrained image embedding models to new classification domains outside their trained capabilities by testing across four target datasets, with competitive performance in both classification and OOD detection. 

Impact Statement — Neural network image classifiers deployed in real tasks must often handle unexpected data from far outside their training sets, especially in safety-sensitive applications where an inappropriate response could cause harm. While large pretrained models have become prevalent in classification tasks, current literature on methods to handle anomalous data has mostly focused on traditional trained classifiers or models which have at least observed relevant data during training. Our method allows these large models to be more safely deployed without the high cost of retraining by enhancing their ability to identify and handle out-of-distribution data. In contrast to other methods, we also do not require any hyperparameter tuning, further minimizing deployment cost and complexity. By adding this capability without significant added cost, our method can help to fortify existing systems against unexpected inputs. 

Index Terms —Anomaly Detection, Classification, Domain Adaptation, Large Pretrained Models, Mahalanobis Distance, Out-of-Distribution Detection

We introduce a novel training-free and hyperparameter tuning-free approach for the domain adaptation of large pretrained models to new classification tasks, with accompanying OOD detection capability. II. RELATED WORK 

A. Distance-Based OOD Detection for Trained Classifiers 

OOD detection using Mahalanobis distance [11] assumes that a trained classifier learns to cluster samples belonging to 

each class within the latent space produced by the model’s penultimate layer. The mean location of each class’s training 

samples within the latent space and a tied covariance matrix of 

the sample’s distribution around these class means define the 

samples’ distribution in the latent space. When test samples are 

classified, the relative likelihood that they do not belong to any known class is calculated as the squared Mahalanobis distance 

from the sample’s latent vector to the nearest class center. 

Other performant OOD detection methods for trained classifiers have more stringent requirements which make them ill-suited to our goals. For example, Maximum Softmax 

Probability relies on the model’s final classification logit output 

[4] [5], while K-Nearest Neighbors for OOD detection requires comparison to individual known samples at inference time, which can become expensive for large datasets [15]. 

B. Zero-shot classification and OOD detection methods for Contrastive Language Image Pretrained (CLIP) models 

While most large pretrained embedding models must be 

finetuned to function as classifiers, CLIP models’ multimodal 

alignment enables zero-shot classification on image tasks where descriptions of the classes are within the learned domain of the model [3] [2]. However, this capability does not extend to specialized cases where the target classification task falls 

outside the model’s experience. 

Several OOD detection methods have been proposed for CLIP models [7]; some learn new tokens for the text encoder through training or employ other finetuning methods. Others can operate in a training-free setting, but only in cases where the class labels are within the model ’s pretraining [8] [9]. 

C. Mahalanobis Classification and Prototypical Networks 

Mahalanobis distance has been widely used for classification in classical machine learning [16]. In the context of neural networks, it has been applied to classification on latent space representations in methods related to Prototypical Networks [14] , where mean representations are used as “prototypes” of 

the corresponding classes, and classification is performed by comparing new samples to these centroids. While the original method used Euclidean distance, improved methods in this vein can outperform baseline distance metrics on training-free domain adaptation for classification and operate predominantly in few-shot settings. However, these extensions generally require training model parameters or tuning hyperparameters to adapt to new domains [17] [18] [19]. This represents a trade-off in comparison to our method, which uses the information from more data samples to adapt to new domains with no finetuning or hyperparameter searches. Many improved prototypical methods also focus specifically on classification, whereas we prioritize high performance in OOD detection for robustness. 

III. METHOD 

We seek to adapt large pretrained models as classifiers with robust OOD detection capability, without the need for finetuning, hyperparameter searches, or prior knowledge from training. We base our method on OOD detection using Mahalanobis distance; although it was introduced for trained classifiers, this method is fully post-hoc and does not require specialized training modifications. Additionally, it uses only the latent space and not the classification logits of trained models, unlike many widely used OOD detection methods [4] [5] [20]. However, the OOD method was still formulated for trained classifiers, and its core mathematical assumption of clustering within the latent space is predicated upon the model being trained as such [11]. We will show that this method can be adapted to our use case through low-cost modifications.

C. Covariance Matrix Inversion Analysis 

We can apply this method directly to large pretrained models (see Section IV), but the latent space’s non -specialized feature set may cause issues in practice due to discrepancies between the sample distribution and the overall span of the latent space. Because it measures a continuous value rather than a simple discrete assignment, OOD detection may be especially sensitive to any noise introduced by this potential misalignment. To understand and address this gap without finetuning or hyperparameter searches, we examine the mechanics of Mahalanobis distance to find the pitfalls of this method when the model has not been trained exclusively on the in-distribution. 

Mahalanobis distance is equivalent to the Euclidean distance between projections through a whitening transform, where samples are decorrelated and standardized to a mean of zero and a variance of one; the whitening matrix of Mahalanobis/ZCA whitening [21] for a centered dataset with covariance  Σ is given as:

Observe that, analogously to z-score normalization in uncorrelated data, the features are divided by the inverse square root of the eigenvalues. Consider the task at hand, where the latent distribution of the training set may span only a small 

subset of the model’s latent space. Many features may be 

unused, resulting in a lower effective dimensionality and some eigenvalues approaching zero magnitude. Mahalanobis distance would magnify these features, degrading our metric with noise.

IV. EXPERIMENTS 

A. Datasets and Models 

We test our method on four target classification datasets: CIFAR-10 [23], CIFAR-100 [23], ImageNet1k [24], and MIDOG [25]. For CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet1k, we use the testing data splits introduced in OpenOODv1.5 [26] [27], an out-of-distribution detection benchmark which provides standardized testing splits across a large set of benchmark image datasets (Table I). To assess a case outside of most general pretrained models’ knowledgebases, we employ 

the in- and out-of-distribution data splits for the MIDOG dataset introduced by OpenMIBOOD [28], an extension of OpenOOD for OOD detection in biomedical images (Table I). The ID samples include three labeled classes from breast carcinoma imaging: mitotic cells, imposter cells, and background tissue; this represents a difficult task which falls far outside the regularly tested range of zero-shot classification tasks on CLIP models. Both benchmarks divide out-of-distribution testing sets into two broad categories: Near-OOD contains image sets that are similar to the in-distribution, while Far-OOD includes less similar sets. 

We employ three widely available large pretrained CLIP models: the original CLIP from OpenAI [3], and the more recent and performant SigLip2 [29] and MetaCLIP2 [30]; we 

use the “base” version of each of these models with a 16px 

patch size (commonly referred to as ViT-B/16), offering a 

balance between capability and size. We measure these model’s 

zero-shot classification capabilities as a baseline for training-free performance by a simplified and data-agnostic method, comparing test images to a set of captions formatte d as “a photo of a {class label}.” This also allows us to verify our assumption 

that MIDOG lies outside the knowledge of these models; the accuracy of this method ranged from 22-32% (Table II). For OOD detection, we compare our method to two latent distance-based baselines: Euclidean distance, and unmodified Mahalanobis distance. 

B. Results 

Across the three CLIP image embedding models, our 

method’s average performance in Near - and Far-OOD task categories exceeded both baselines in 22/24 cases (Table III). Our rank-reduced Mahalanobis OOD metric consistently achieved higher performance in OOD detection, as measured by Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC) and false positive rate at 95% true positive rate (FPR). In classification, our method greatly exceeded the zero-shot text embedding baseline; these methods are not closely comparable 

due to the baseline’s use of the text encoder and our method’s 

use of the training data, but these results provide some insight into the limitations of text label-based classification and the effectiveness of our method at domain transfer into unknown tasks.

V. ANALYSIS 

These results demonstrate that our method can effectively utilize large pretrained image embedding models for new classification and out-of-distribution detection tasks, refining classifier performance without training to increase robustness to anomalous data. Our rank reduction removed significant numbers of features in every case (Fig. 1), lending credence to our hypothesis that a specific dataset may effectively occupy a lower dimensional span of a model ’s latent space. 

A single inference pass over the training set generates the statistics, requiring far less computational resources and lower device memory than finetuning using backpropagation, and no hyperparameter searches are required. Further, it allows domain adaptation to entirely new classification tasks without relying on any part of the trained model possessing relevant prior knowledge. While data is required, training is not, facilitating the rapid deployment of new classifiers as new tasks arise.

VI. CONCLUSION 

We introduce ReMaD, a novel refinement method for domain adaptation of large pretrained models to new classification domains using only training data, without finetuning through backpropagation or relying on multimodal prior knowledge. We demonstrate competitive performance across four classification tasks, with high robustness to anomalous data as measured by OOD detection scores on a broad spectrum of benchmark datasets. In future work, our method could potentially be augmented to handle few-shot settings without introducing any tuning requirements; this would further expand its applicability to data-restricted tasks while retaining its ability to refine latent distributions for new domains with a single inference pass.
