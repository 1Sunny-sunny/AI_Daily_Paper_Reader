# CoHyFuse: Condition-wise Hypergraph Fusion with Global Connectome in Task-fMRI 

Boseong Kim 1, Haejun Chung 1, and Ikbeom Jang 21Hanyang University, Seoul, Republic of Korea

Abstract —Task-fMRI connectomes reveal state-dependent neu-ral reconfigurations, yet conventional methods marginalize these signals by aggregating distinct conditions into static pairwise graphs, thereby obscuring condition-specific multi-ROI organiza-tion. We introduce CoHyFuse, a condition-aware ROI-centered hypergraph framework that constructs a task-state-specific inci-dence matrix from condition-wise functional connectivity (FC)-profile embeddings, allowing the same ROI to form different multi-ROI hyperedges across task phases. Condition-specific neighborhood sizes Kq further adapt the hyperedge scale to each task state, and the resulting condition embeddings are fused with a complementary whole-session FC branch for prediction. In the AABC cohort (N=1,074), CoHyFuse achieved the best mean out-of-fold predictive performance among evaluated baselines on FACENAME Fluid Cognition Composite (FCC) prediction (7.83 ±0.10 MAE, 0.439 ±0.026 R2) and VISMOTOR age predic-tion (7.52 ±0.37 MAE, 0.592 ±0.022 R2). In an auxiliary CMI-HBN attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) classifica-tion benchmark (N=223), CoHyFuse obtained 72.0 ±2.1% macro-AUC and 74.2 ±2.9% accuracy. Ablation studies support the con-tributions of condition-wise incidence construction and dual-view fusion, suggesting that state-resolved ROI-set structure provides complementary predictive information beyond whole-session FC alone. Occlusion analysis identifies the Distraction condition as the primary driver of model prediction, pointing toward the Salience/Ventral Attention Network (SAN)–FrontoParietal Net-work (FPN) and within-SAN hyperedge-defined ROI-set motifs as candidate model-relevant patterns. This framework provides an interpretable, state-resolved view of the connectome for down-stream cohort analysis. Code will be released upon acceptance. 

Index Terms —Task-fMRI, hypergraph neural network, condition-specific connectivity, functional connectivity 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Population-scale neuroimaging initiatives provide task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (task-fMRI) together with standardized behavioral and cognitive phenotyp-ing, enabling connectome-based modeling of inter-individual variability. Accumulating evidence suggests that task-evoked brain states can increase behaviorally relevant variance in func-tional connectivity (FC) and, for some traits and paradigms, task-derived connectivity can improve out-of-sample behav-ioral prediction relative to resting-state connectivity [1]. These findings motivate task-fMRI connectome models for cognitive phenotype prediction that preserve structured condition dy-namics across paradigms and cohorts [2]. Most connectome-learning approaches represent FC as pair-wise graphs or FC matrices and learn a single, condition-agnostic representation of an entire scan [3], [4]. However, task paradigms are inherently heterogeneous: FC reconfigures across conditions, and collapsing repeated phases into a single representation can attenuate condition-wise signatures. More-over, pairwise graphs restrict message passing to dyadic region of interest (ROI)–ROI edges and do not directly aggregate information over ROI sets. Hypergraphs generalize graphs by allowing a hyperedge to connect multiple nodes; in this work, each hyperedge is operationally defined as an FC-profile neighborhood, enabling incidence-based aggregation over condition-specific ROI sets [5]. Prior hypergraph connec-tomics studies suggest that such ROI-set topology can carry behaviorally and clinically relevant information [6]. To address this gap, we propose CoHyFuse, a condition-aware ROI-centered hypergraph fusion framework that builds condition-wise top-Kq hypergraphs. CoHyFuse integrates attention-fused hypergraph neural networks (HGNN) embeddings with a whole-session FC branch, capturing both condition-wise reconfiguration cues and session-level connectivity structure for task-fMRI prediction. Our contributions are threefold: (i) We propose CoHyFuse , a condition-aware ROI-centered hypergraph fusion framework that constructs condition-wise top-Kq hypergraphs and integrates attention-fused HGNN embeddings with a complementary whole-session FC branch, thereby capturing both condition-wise reconfiguration cues and session-level connectivity structure for task-fMRI prediction. (ii) We evaluate CoHyFuse on two public fMRI datasets, and CoHyFuse achieves the best mean performance among evaluated baselines. Ablation results support the effectiveness of condition-specific hypergraph construc-tion, heterogeneous Kq , and dual-branch fusion. (iii) Applying CoHyFuse to FACENAME, we find that the Distraction condition yields the strongest association between hyperedge-guided subnetwork strength and the FCC ( r=0 .36 ), with dominant network-pair contribu-tions in SAN–SAN and SAN–FPN connectivity. B. Graph-based connectome learning 

Recent deep connectome models represent fMRI connectiv-ity as graphs or FC matrices and learn predictive representa-tions using graph neural networks (GNNs) or Transformers. GCN- and GAT-based models perform message passing over pairwise ROI–ROI edges, while brain-specific architectures such as BrainGNN introduce interpretable graph learning for fMRI analysis [3], [7], [8]. Transformer-based connectome models further aim to capture long-range dependencies in FC matrices [9]. Although these approaches improve representa-tion learning over conventional machine learning baselines, most of them still operate on whole-session or condition-agnostic pairwise connectivity. As a result, they may miss task-state-specific reconfiguration patterns and condition-specific multi-ROI organization. 

C. Hypergraph learning for ROI-set connectomics 

Hypergraphs extend conventional graphs by allowing one hyperedge to connect multiple ROIs, providing a natural representation for ROI-set structure [5]. Hypergraph neural networks perform incidence-based message passing through node–hyperedge memberships [5], and later hypergraph mod-els further improve hyperedge-aware propagation and repre-sentation learning [10], [11]. Recent hypergraph connectomics studies suggest that multi-ROI topological or representational patterns can improve task decoding, individual identification, and brain–behavior association [6]. However, existing hy-pergraph connectome approaches generally do not explicitly construct task-condition-specific hypergraphs or adapt the neighborhood scale across task states. The key distinction is that task condition is not treated merely as an additional feature or branch label; it determines the incidence structure itself, so that ROI-set membership is allowed to change across paradigm-defined brain states. CoHyFuse addresses this gap by building condition-wise ROI-centered top-Kq hypergraphs and fusing their HGNN embeddings with a complementary whole-session FC branch, thereby linking condition-resolved task-fMRI modeling with hypergraph-based ROI-set connectome learning. A. Overview and Problem Formulation 

Given an ROI time series X ∈ RT ×V and task-condition labels c = {ct}Tt=1 where ct ∈ {1, . . . , Q } denotes the condition at repetition time (TR) t, we learn a regressor fΘ to predict y ∈ R:

ˆy = fΘ(X, c). (1) Here, c denotes paradigm timing information available from the task-fMRI design, not subject-level metadata or target-derived information; CoHyFuse uses it only to partition time points into task states and to construct state-resolved FC/hypergraph representations. Our framework first estimates condition-wise FC and constructs a top-Kq neighborhood hypergraph for each condition. An HGNN encodes each condition-wise hypergraph to obtain condition embeddings, which are integrated via multi-head self-attention. Finally, the fused condition representation is combined with a whole-session FC feature vector to predict ˆy (Fig. 1).

A. Dataset and Experiment Settings 1) Dataset: We evaluated CoHyFuse using two publicly available fMRI datasets: Aging Adult Brain Connectome (AABC) Release 1 [15] and the Child Mind Institute Healthy Brain Network (CMI-HBN) [16]. Table I summarizes the AABC analytic cohort used for both FACENAME and VIS-MOTOR benchmarks, and Table II summarizes the CMI-HBN diagnostic cohort. 

AABC: From the AABC cohort, we restricted analyses to the cross-sectional V1 visit and used two paradigms, FACENAME and VISMOTOR, as separate prediction benchmarks. After excluding participants with missing targets or unusable runs, we analyzed 1,074 adults aged 36–100+ years. Using the released HCP-style preprocessing [17], we extracted ROI-mean time series with the Schaefer 100-region atlas [18]. Targets were the NIH Toolbox Fluid Cognition Compos-ite (FCC) for FACENAME [2] and chronological age for VISMOTOR. For condition-wise hypergraphs, timing files were converted to TR-wise condition labels, and valid same-condition samples were pooled across repeated blocks before FC estimation. FACENAME (345 volumes; TR =0.8 s) has encoding (ENC), distraction (DIST), and recall (REC) (4 ×25 TR blocks each; 100 TR/condition), excluding instruction/cue and run-in/out rest from FC estimation. VISMOTOR (194 volumes; TR =0.8 s) has active/rest, where active blocks are 35/34/34 TRs (103 TR total) and intervening rest blocks define rest. 

CMI-HBN: From the CMI-HBN pediatric cohort (ages 5–21) [16], we restricted analyses to children aged 6–10 years from the complete-pass release with structural and blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) data passing quality control (QC), to reduce developmental heterogeneity. For functional QC, we required a median framewise displacement (FD) ≤ 0.2 mm and a normalized cross-correlation to template ≥ 0.8. After QC, age, sex, available site/scanner information, and median FD were inspected as cohort-level nuisance variables but were not used as model inputs; all learning-based models used identical subject-wise splits. Across the diagnostic groups, the Reproducible Brain Charts (RBC)-provided per-subject me-dian FD did not differ significantly (Kruskal–Wallis, p=0.852; ADHD-I vs. ADHD-C, Mann–Whitney, p=0.562), suggesting that group-level motion differences were unlikely to drive classification. Participants were classified into three groups: no diagnosis (NoDx), ADHD inattentive type (ADHD-I), and ADHD combined type (ADHD-C). REST and PEER run labels were converted to TR-wise state labels, and valid same-state samples were pooled before FC estimation. PEER is an MR-based eye-calibration task with 27 fixation events (4 s each; 5 TRs/event at TR =0.8 s; 135 volumes/run); PEER1/PEER3 defined repeated PEER-state runs, and length-matched REST segments defined the REST state. 

2) Experimental Settings and Implementation Details: All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. We performed subject-wise 5-fold cross-validation with a64%/16%/20% train/validation/test split; all hyperparameter and checkpoint selections, including Kq , used only the val-idation split, and the outer test fold was held out for final evaluation. CoHyFuse used a two-layer FC-profile encoder with output dimension D = 64 , one shared-parameter HGNN layer per condition, 4-head self-attention for condition fusion, a two-layer whole-session FC MLP branch, and a two-layer prediction head, with ReLU activation, dropout p = 0.2,and approximately 0.71M trainable parameters. Models were optimized with AdamW using batch size 64, weight decay 

10 −4, gradient clipping with max-norm = 1.0, and early stopping on validation loss for up to 80 epochs. The learning rate was selected from {3 × 10 −4, 5 × 10 −4, 10 −3}, and Kq

was searched over {5, 10 , . . . , 50 } with step size 5. Regression used MSE loss, whereas the CMI-HBN classifier used cross-entropy loss. We report mean ±std across folds. 

B. Comparison with Baseline Methods 

We compared CoHyFuse with classical baselines, including machine learning models (support vector machine (SVM) and multilayer perceptron (MLP)), graph neural networks (graph convolutional network (GCN) [7], graph attention net-work (GAT) [8], BrainGNN [3]), recent task-fMRI-oriented GNN baselines, including TA-GAT [13] and STNAGNN [14], a Transformer-based method (ALTER [9]), and hypergraph neural networks (HGNN [5], HNHN [10], and ED-HNN [11]). All baselines were evaluated using the same subject-wise folds, preprocessing, ROI parcellation, and validation-based model selection. For TA-GAT and STNAGNN, available paradigm/state information was used following their original task-aware designs; CoHyFuse used the same timing only to form condition-wise FC profiles and task-state-specific incidence matrices. As shown in Table III, CoHyFuse achieved the best mean performance among the evaluated baselines on both AABC tasks, reaching 7.83 ±0.10 MAE / 9.91 ±0.25 RMSE / 0.439 ±0.026 R2 on FACENAME (improving MAE by 0.34 over TA-GAT and RMSE/ R2 by 0.39/0.048 over STNAGNN) and 7.52 ±0.37 MAE / 9.61 ±0.36 RMSE / 0.592 ±0.022 R2

on VISMOTOR (improving by 0.39 MAE, 0.31 RMSE, and 0.033 R2 over ALTER). Paired bootstrap analysis on out-of-fold predictions further supported the FACENAME MAE gain over TA-GAT ( ∆MAE = 0.34, 95% CI [0.11, 0.57], 

p = 0.004 ), and the VISMOTOR gain over ALTER was likewise significant ( ∆MAE = 0.39, 95% CI [ 0.18 , 0.60], p =0.018 ). Because FACENAME was evaluated in a broad adult lifespan cohort, we interpret the reported FACENAME R2 as unadjusted total out-of-fold predictive performance rather than a covariate-adjusted effect size. A dedicated confound analysis in Sec. IV-D4 showed that an age/sex/FD-only model was lower than CoHyFuse ( R2 = 0 .25 vs. 0.439 ) and that the FACENAME association remained significant after fold-wise confound adjustment (partial r = 0 .34 , p < 0.001 ). 3) Condition-wise FC reliability and sensitivity: As an ad-ditional FC-estimator sensitivity check, we repeated the FACE-NAME experiment using Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage-regularized condition-wise FCs, while keeping the selected Kq , data splits, and training settings unchanged. Performance remained close to the Pearson-FC setting (MAE = 7 .92 ±0.11 , R2 = 0 .425 ±

0.025 ), suggesting that CoHyFuse is not solely dependent on the unregularized Pearson FC estimator. A within-subject split-half check (odd vs. even blocks) showed moderate-to-good edge-wise consistency of the condition-wise FCs (mean r=0.61), which exceeded the mean between-condition simi-larity (r=0.42), indicating that the state-resolved profiles are reliable yet condition-distinct rather than noise-dominated. 

4) Confound control: Because FC is entangled with age and head motion [19], we tested whether predictions reduce to measured demographic and motion covariates. The con-found set was age/sex/FD for FACENAME and sex/FD for VISMOTOR (age is excluded as it is the VISMOTOR target). A confound-only model yielded R2 = 0 .25 (FACENAME) and R2 ≈ 0 (VISMOTOR), both below CoHyFuse. For the FACENAME confound-adjusted analysis, nuisance-regression coefficients for residualizing the FC-derived features and target were estimated within each outer fold using only the train-ing split and then applied unchanged to validation/test sub-jects [20]. After this fold-wise residualization, the confound-adjusted association remained significant for FACENAME (partial r = 0 .34 , p < . 001 , 10,000 permutations), indicat-ing that FACENAME prediction was not fully explained by measured age, sex, and motion covariates. For VISMOTOR, where age is itself the target, we instead verified that prediction error was not appreciably correlated with motion ( r = 0 .09 ), suggesting that age prediction was not strongly motion-driven. 

E. Interpretability Analysis 

To provide neurocognitively meaningful insights beyond predictive accuracy, we analyzed both condition-wise BOLD activity patterns and the hyperedges that were most influential for prediction in the FACENAME task-fMRI of the AABC dataset, with all summaries reported at the Yeo-7 network level [21]. 

1) BOLD signal analysis: The mean BOLD signal across subjects at the Yeo-7 network level exhibited condition-dependent differences (Fig. 3(b)). The VIN showed high activity during ENC, reached its lowest level during DIST, and recovered during REC. In contrast, the SAN was relatively weak in ENC but became engaged in DIST. These large-scale shifts across task conditions suggest that the data contain distinct condition-dependent regimes. Therefore, treating all TRs as a single homogeneous sequence may mix conditions with different baseline activation contexts and blur condition-wise connectivity patterns [1]. 

2) Condition-wise hyperedge occlusion analysis: To inter-pret which condition-wise hyperedges were most influential for model prediction (Fig. 3(a)), we ranked hyperedges via oc-clusion by removing one constructed hyperedge at a time and measuring the resulting increase in prediction error. Rankings were derived on the training split and assessed on a held-out test split, and hyperedge memberships were summarized using 

Hmean aggregated to Yeo-7 networks. 

ENC (Encoding): In ENC, the Top-5 hyperedges were an-chored evenly across five large-scale systems (VIN, DAN, SMN, SAN, and DMN; n=1 each). Dominant network-pair contributions were within-network VIN–VIN and SMN–SMN, with an additional cross-network SMN–DAN component. Neurocognitively, this pattern suggests that the model’s predic-tions were most sensitive to within-VIN structure and SMN– DAN coupling, consistent with visual processing and goal-directed attention/response-selection demands during encoding [22]. 

DIST (Distraction): In DIST, anchors were concentrated in SAN ( n=3 ; DMN n=1 , SMN n=1 ), and network-pair contributions were SAN–FPN and SAN–SAN, consistent with accounts in which the SAN is engaged by distracting events and interacts with FPN during distraction [23]. Consistent with this motif, DIST subnetwork strength showed the strongest condition-wise association with FCC (Fig. 3(c)). 

REC (Recall): In REC, anchors were distributed across VIN (n=2 ), LIN ( n=2 ), and FPN ( n=1 ). Network-pair summaries highlighted LIN–DMN and LIN–LIN contributions alongside within-network VIN–VIN. F. Application to CMI-HBN Dataset 

To assess the generality of CoHyFuse beyond the AABC regression benchmarks, we further applied it to an exploratory three-class ADHD classification benchmark in the CMI-HBN pediatric cohort. The task distinguishes NoDx, ADHD-I, and ADHD-C using REST and PEER-state fMRI. For this clas-sification benchmark, the scalar regression head was replaced with a three-way softmax classifier trained using cross-entropy loss, while the condition-wise hypergraph construction and fu-sion modules were kept unchanged. All methods were trained and evaluated using identical subject-wise splits and the same ROI time-series inputs. Here, mAUC and ACC denote macro-AUC and accuracy, respectively, and SEN N /SEN I /SEN C de-note sensitivities for NoDx/ADHD-I/ADHD-C. As shown in Table VIII, CoHyFuse achieved the best overall performance, with 72.0% mAUC and 74.2% accuracy. Compared with the strongest baseline, CoHyFuse improved mAUC by 4.0 percentage points over STNAGNN and accuracy by 2.6 percentage points over ALTER. CoHyFuse achieved the highest sensitivity across all three diagnostic groups, sug-gesting that condition-wise hypergraph-based ROI-set fusion provides useful predictive information for pediatric ADHD subtype classification. V. D ISCUSSION 

CoHyFuse was designed to preserve task-state information that is often lost when task-fMRI is summarized as a single condition-agnostic FC graph. Across AABC FACENAME and VISMOTOR benchmarks, CoHyFuse achieved the best mean performance among the evaluated baselines, and ablations showed that both the condition-wise hypergraph branch and the whole-session FC branch contributed to prediction. These results suggest that task-state reconfiguration and session-level connectivity provide complementary information, con-sistent with prior work showing that task-fMRI can amplify behaviorally relevant individual differences and that task-state FC carries functional information beyond resting-state connectivity [1]. The operator-control and Kq analyses fur-ther support modeling task-fMRI beyond pairwise ROI–ROI graphs: replacing HGNN with GCN/GAT variants reduced performance, and condition-specific hypergraph construction was more effective than a shared neighborhood scale. This is consistent with prior hypergraph connectomics evidence that multi-ROI topological patterns can carry task- and behavior-relevant information [6]. In CoHyFuse, the condition-wise hy-pergraph branch captures state-dependent FC-profile neighbor-hood structure, whereas the whole-session FC branch provides a compact summary of session-level connectivity. Hyperedge occlusion and subnetwork-strength analyses highlighted SAN–FPN and within-SAN motifs, and DIST subnetwork strength showed the strongest association with FCC after age and motion adjustment. These findings suggest a model-relevant salience–control pattern during distraction, but should be interpreted as model reliance rather than causal biomarker evidence. The CMI-HBN ADHD experiment pro-vides an auxiliary clinical-task evaluation, suggesting that the same state-wise fusion mechanism can be applied beyond adult cognitive regression under matched splits and ROI inputs. Limitations include reliance on paradigms with sufficiently long condition windows for stable condition-wise FC estima-tion and increased hyperparameter search cost for condition-wise Kq . Future work will explore learnable neighborhood selection, temporally adaptive condition modeling, and broader validation across tasks, cohorts, atlases, and connectivity esti-mators. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented CoHyFuse, a condition-aware ROI-centered hypergraph fusion framework for task-fMRI prediction. CoHy-Fuse constructs condition-wise top-Kq hypergraphs and fuses HGNN-based condition embeddings with a complementary whole-session FC branch. Experiments on AABC and CMI-HBN benchmarks showed the best mean performance among evaluated baselines, and ablations supported the contributions of condition-specific hypergraph construction, heterogeneous 

Kq , and dual-branch fusion. Interpretability analysis further identified DIST-related SAN–FPN and SAN–SAN motifs as model-relevant patterns associated with FCC. Overall, Co-HyFuse provides a compact and interpretable framework for modeling state-resolved hypergraph-defined ROI-set structure in task-fMRI.
