## ABSTRACT 

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) provides portable, low-cost, and spatially localized measurements of cortical hemodynamics, but substantial differ-ences in channel count, probe layout, signal distribution, and task type hinder joint pretraining across datasets. We introduce GeoNIRS, a geometry-aware pretrain-ing framework that uses the physical locations of source–detector pairs as a shared spatial reference across heterogeneous probe layouts. GeoNIRS compresses variable-size channel sets into a fixed-size latent representation and employs bidi-rectional HbO/HbR cross-reconstruction with contiguous temporal masking to learn transferable representations. GeoNIRS-base is pretrained on eight heteroge-neous datasets, while GeoNIRS-large scales pretraining to 22 datasets. Neither the data nor labels of the downstream tasks are used during pretraining, enabling eval-uation under a strict cross-task setting. To the best of our knowledge, GeoNIRS is the first fNIRS framework to explicitly model physical channel geometry dur-ing joint pretraining across heterogeneous channel configurations, and the first to provide both complete source code and downloadable pretrained checkpoints. We evaluate GeoNIRS on eight held-out tasks spanning clinical and auditory as-sessment, pain and affective processing, semantic decoding, and motor function. GeoNIRS achieves the highest task-averaged balanced accuracy among the eval-uated conventional machine-learning, fNIRS-specific, general time-series, and fNIRS pretraining baselines. Compared with random initialization, pretraining improves task-averaged balanced accuracy by 4.12 percentage points under linear probing and 3.30 points under full fine-tuning, with larger benefits when down-stream labels are scarce. GeoNIRS also remains robust when input channels are missing. Geometry perturbations and pretraining ablations further demon-strate that both channel geometry and cross-chromophore reconstruction con-tribute to the learned representations. Overall, GeoNIRS provides an open and reproducible framework for transferable representation learning across hetero-geneous fNIRS datasets. Our code and pretrained checkpoints are available at 

https://github.com/GeoNIRS/GeoNIRS 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) noninvasively measures changes in cortical oxy-genated and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbO/HbR), providing a hemodynamic proxy for neural activity (Ferrari & Quaresima, 2012; Scholkmann et al., 2014). Compared with functional mag-netic resonance imaging, fNIRS is relatively inexpensive, portable, and silent, supporting bedside monitoring, measurements during movement, and experiments in naturalistic settings. Its spatially localized cortical signals also complement the high-temporal-resolution electrophysiological activ-ity recorded by EEG (Pinti et al., 2020; Y¨ ucel et al., 2021). These properties have enabled appli-1arXiv:2610.17845v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026 cations across cognitive and affective neuroscience, brain–computer interfaces, motor and neurore-habilitation research, and the assessment of neurological and psychiatric conditions (Obrig, 2014; Ehlis et al., 2014; Naseer & Hong, 2015; Mihara & Miyai, 2016). In the closely related EEG do-main, neural foundation models and their benchmarking ecosystems are considerably more mature. OmniEEG-Bench and Meta FAIR’s NeuralBench-EEG systematically evaluate pretrained models across multiple datasets, tasks, and standardized adaptation protocols (Lu et al., 2026; Banville et al., 2026). BENDR provided early evidence for large-scale self-supervised EEG pretraining, while LaBraM, EEGPT, BrainOmni, and REVE further demonstrated that aggregating unlabeled recordings across datasets, tasks, and acquisition systems can produce transferable neural represen-tations (Kostas et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a; Xiao et al., 2025; El Ouahidi et al., 2025). Such multi-dataset pretraining alleviates the limited scale of individual datasets while exposing models to variations in populations, paradigms, and acquisition systems, thereby facili-tating transfer to unseen tasks and label-scarce settings. Extending this paradigm to heterogeneous fNIRS datasets is therefore an important step from dataset-specific decoding toward general-purpose neural representation learning. Conventional fNIRS decoding remains largely dataset-specific and supervised, ranging from hand-crafted HbO/HbR features with LDA or SVM to fNIRS-specific deep networks (Naseer et al., 2016a;b; Wang et al., 2022; 2023; 2026b). These methods are typically trained separately under fixed probe layouts and label spaces, preventing joint learning from heterogeneous unlabeled data. Extending multi-dataset pretraining to fNIRS presents two key challenges. First, channel semantics vary across probe layouts: the same channel index may correspond to different cortical locations, while the same region may be covered by different channels. Variable-channel support alone there-fore cannot establish cross-layout spatial correspondence. Second, designing a non-trivial pretrain-ing objective is difficult. Slowly varying hemodynamic signals make within-signal masked recon-struction susceptible to temporal interpolation (Scholkmann et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2026). Self-reconstruction within each chromophore may primarily capture modality-specific signal structure. In contrast, cross-chromophore reconstruction encourages the model to leverage the shared physio-logical information between HbO and HbR, promoting more transferable representations (Cui et al., 2010). As shown in Table 13, existing methods address only parts of these challenges. Early ap-proaches use a single dataset or train separately across datasets (Liu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021; 2024b). Jung et al. jointly pretrain across varying channel counts but do not model physical channel geometry (Jung et al., 2025); EFRM uses only one fNIRS dataset (Jung & An, 2025); and Subedi et al. assume a shared probe configuration and require supervised adaptation to new layouts (Subedi et al., 2026). ASDGPrompt and DSSER likewise do not perform joint multi-dataset pretraining (Cai et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Thus, existing work has yet to jointly address cross-layout spatial unification and physiologically grounded pretraining, while reproducibility remains limited by the scarcity of complete source code and downloadable pretrained checkpoints. The design of our work is inspired by recent advances in EEG foundation models and physiologi-cal signal pretraining. First, BrainOmni shows that incorporating the physical properties of sensors into representation learning and compressing a variable number of sensor inputs into a fixed-size la-tent representation can mitigate structural differences across acquisition systems (Xiao et al., 2025). Second, xMAE performs masked cross-modal reconstruction between synchronously recorded ECG and PPG signals, demonstrating that predicting masked signals from related physiological observa-tions can reduce reliance on local temporal smoothness (Zhou et al., 2026). However, each fNIRS channel is defined by a source–detector pair, while HbO and HbR are coupled but non-equivalent hemodynamic observations within the same modality (Cui et al., 2010). These ideas must therefore be redesigned for fNIRS to unify heterogeneous probe layouts and learn the hemodynamic structure shared between HbO and HbR. Building on these ideas, we propose GeoNIRS, a geometry-aware pretraining framework for het-erogeneous fNIRS data. GeoNIRS explicitly encodes source–detector geometry and compresses variable-size channel sets from heterogeneous probe layouts into a fixed-size latent representation. It is pretrained via bidirectional HbO/HbR cross-reconstruction with contiguous temporal masking, encouraging the model to capture shared hemodynamic structure while reducing reliance on local temporal interpolation. GeoNIRS-base is pretrained on 383.30 hours of recordings across eight datasets, while GeoNIRS-large scales pretraining to 827.04 hours across 22 datasets. ## 2 METHOD 

2.1 OVERVIEW 

As shown in Figure 1, GeoNIRS processes paired HbO/HbR signals and source–detector geometry from datasets with varying channel counts and probe layouts. Separate temporal encoders repre-sent the two chromophores, while a geometry-aware channel compressor maps each dataset-specific physical-channel set into a fixed number of latent-channel tokens. Pretraining masks contiguous patches of one chromophore and reconstructs them using its visible context together with the com-plete complementary chromophore, with the two reconstruction directions optimized jointly. Af-ter pretraining, the reconstruction decoders are discarded and the fixed-length representations are adapted through linear probing, LoRA, or full fine-tuning. 2.2 TEMPORAL AND GEOMETRIC REPRESENTATIONS

2.5 DOWNSTREAM ADAPTATION 

During downstream adaptation, temporal masking is disabled and the reconstruction decoders are discarded. The complete HbO/HbR signals and their channel geometry are processed by the pre-trained temporal encoders, geometry encoder, and channel compressor, producing fixed-length rep-resentations ZO, Z R ∈ RM ×P ×F . The two chromophore representations are then concatenated along the feature dimension:

and passed to a task-specific prediction head. For linear probing, the pretrained backbone is frozen and only the prediction head is trained. LoRA updates the low-rank adaptation parameters and pre-diction head, whereas full fine-tuning jointly updates the entire pretrained backbone and downstream head. 53 EXPERIMENTS 

We conduct experiments to answer six questions: (1) whether GeoNIRS outperforms existing meth-ods on downstream fNIRS tasks that are unseen during pretraining; (2) whether its downstream gains arise from pretraining rather than the model architecture alone; (3) whether pretraining im-proves transfer when downstream labels are scarce; (4) whether geometry-aware representations remain robust when input channels are missing; (5) how channel geometry and cross-chromophore reconstruction contribute to the learned representations; and (6) how performance changes when scaling model capacity and pretraining data. We first describe the experimental setup and then present cross-task benchmarking, random-initialization controls, label-efficient adaptation, channel-loss robustness, and component ablations. 3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Pretraining data. Our dataset inventory comprises 30 fNIRS datasets, of which 22 are used for pretraining and eight are held out exclusively for downstream evaluation. GeoNIRS-base is pre-trained on eight datasets spanning cognitive and executive functions, social interaction, naturalistic stimulation, and functional localization. GeoNIRS-large extends this corpus with 14 additional datasets, resulting in a total of 22 pretraining datasets. Dataset-level descriptions, statistics, and references are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The complete 22-dataset pretraining corpus contains 3,578 recordings totaling 827.04 hours and yields 93,726 30-s windows after preprocessing. All windows are resampled to 5 Hz. The datasets vary substantially in participant population, channel count, probe layout, acquisition system, and experimental paradigm. None of the eight downstream datasets is included in either pretraining corpus. Unless otherwise specified, GeoNIRS refers to GeoNIRS-base; results for GeoNIRS-large are reported in Appendix E.2. 

Downstream tasks. We evaluate GeoNIRS on eight held-out binary classification tasks spanning clinical and auditory assessment, pain and affective processing, semantic decoding, and motor func-tion. All experiments follow a cross-subject setting: each participant is assigned exclusively to the training, validation, or test set, and all compared methods use identical subject assignments. Input-window duration is configured separately for each task according to its experimental paradigm. Complete task definitions, references, and dataset statistics are provided in Appendix A. 

Baselines. We compare GeoNIRS against four groups of baselines. First, conventional machine-learning approaches include an RBF-SVM (Naseer et al., 2016b) and statistical-feature LDA (Naseer et al., 2016a). Second, supervised neural baselines include 1D-CNN (Trakoolwilaiwan et al., 2018), EEGNet (Lawhern et al., 2018), fNIRS-T (Wang et al., 2022), fNIRSNet (Wang et al., 2023), and fNIRS4D (Wang et al., 2026b). Third, MOMENT-1-base (Goswami et al., 2024) serves as a general-purpose pretrained time-series baseline and is evaluated using linear probing and LoRA. Fourth, we evaluate a paper-guided implementation of the multi-dataset fNIRS pretraining framework of Jung et al. (2025), denoted as Jung-inspired MAE. To accommodate heterogeneous channel counts, Jung-inspired MAE uses channel-independent encoding with shared parameters but does not model physical channel geometry. All methods use the same task-specific input windows and cross-subject splits. Implementation details are provided in Appendix B, while the capabilities and availability of existing fNIRS pretraining methods are summarized in Table 13. 

Adaptation protocols. We evaluate GeoNIRS under three downstream adaptation protocols. For linear probing (LP), the pretrained backbone is frozen and only a task-specific classification head is optimized. For low-rank adaptation (LoRA), low-rank trainable matrices are introduced into the encoder, while the remaining pretrained parameters are kept frozen. For full fine-tuning (FT), the entire backbone and classification head are jointly optimized. All three protocols use identical subject splits within each downstream task. The label-efficiency and channel-loss experiments use full fine-tuning. MOMENT-LP and MOMENT-LoRA both use the publicly released MOMENT-1-base backbone. 

Evaluation protocol. We perform subject-level five-fold cross-validation, such that subjects in the test fold are never observed during downstream training or model selection. Table 1 reports balanced accuracy across the eight held-out downstream tasks. The three GeoNIRS adaptation variants achieve the highest overall averages among all evaluated methods: 63.54 for full fine-tuning, 63.42 for LoRA, and 63.06 for linear probing, compared with 61.73 for the strongest non-GeoNIRS baseline. Notably, linear probing already surpasses all baselines while keeping the pretrained backbone frozen, indicating that GeoNIRS learns representations that transfer directly across datasets and tasks. LoRA performs within 0.12 percentage points of full fine-tuning, showing that competitive transfer can also be achieved through parameter-efficient adaptation. The largest margins over the strongest non-GeoNIRS results occur on CI Status and ICH, where GeoNIRS improves balanced accuracy by 4.08 and 3.34 percentage points, respectively. GeoNIRS also ranks first on Tinnitus, Pain Intensity, and Motor Recovery, and second on Emotion, Semantic, and Action Mode. These results demonstrate broad cross-dataset transfer rather than an improvement driven by a single downstream dataset. 3.3 EFFECT OF PRETRAINING 

Table 2 isolates the contribution of pretraining by comparing GeoNIRS with an otherwise identical randomly initialized counterpart. Under linear probing, pretraining improves average balanced ac-curacy by 4.12 percentage points and AUROC by 5.48 points, with positive gains across all eight tasks. Under full fine-tuning, the corresponding average gains are 3.30 and 3.96 points, respectively. The larger gain under linear probing is expected because the frozen backbone more directly reflects representation quality, whereas full fine-tuning can partially compensate for random initialization using downstream labels. Because the compared models share the same architecture, data splits, and adaptation protocol, the observed gains can be attributed to pretraining rather than model capacity. 3.4 LABEL -E FFICIENT ADAPTATION

Under fixed channel loss, the same predefined subset of channels is removed in every run; under random channel loss, a new subset is sampled independently for each seed. All models are trained using complete channel inputs. As shown in Figure 3, GeoNIRS-FT remains stable under both settings. At 50% channel loss, its balanced accuracy decreases by 1.22 points under fixed loss and 1.70 points under random loss. Its advantage over the strongest non-GeoNIRS baseline in each condition increases from 1.81 points with complete channels to 5.62 and 4.99 points under fixed and random 50% channel loss, respec-tively. These results are consistent with the geometry-aware design of GeoNIRS, which may help the model use spatially related measurements when some channels are unavailable. 3.6 ABLATION STUDIES 

Geometry perturbation after pretraining. We perturb only the channel coordinates during downstream inference while keeping the pretrained weights and fNIRS signals unchanged. Co-ordinates are either replaced with zeros or randomly shuffled across channels. These perturbations reduce average balanced accuracy by 2.34 and 3.24 percentage points, respectively, across the three adaptation protocols (Table 3). The larger reduction under shuffling indicates that incorrect channel– location correspondence is more harmful than removing geometry entirely. 

Pretraining components. We retrain two variants using the same pretraining data and down-stream protocols. The w/o Geometry variant removes channel-coordinate encoding, whereas Self-Reconstruction masks the same temporal segments in HbO and HbR and reconstructs each chro-mophore from its own visible context. As shown in Table 4, the two variants reduce average bal-anced accuracy by 2.98 and 1.58 percentage points, respectively, with consistent degradation across full fine-tuning, LoRA, and linear probing. 

## 4 CONCLUSION 

We introduced GeoNIRS, a geometry-aware framework for joint pretraining across fNIRS datasets with heterogeneous channel counts and probe layouts. GeoNIRS uses source–detector geometry to establish cross-layout spatial correspondence, compresses variable channel sets into fixed latent representations, and learns from paired HbO/HbR signals through bidirectional cross-chromophore reconstruction. Across eight held-out downstream datasets, GeoNIRS achieves the strongest task-averaged performance among the evaluated methods and maintains its advantages under linear prob-ing, limited-label adaptation, and missing-channel conditions. The perturbation and ablation results further confirm the contributions of channel geometry and cross-chromophore reconstruction. B.1 SIGNAL PREPROCESSING 

All datasets are first parsed using format-specific adapters for SNIRF, NIRx, CSV, BIDS, and other supported formats, and are converted into a unified recording representation. After format harmo-nization, both pretraining and downstream datasets follow the same signal-processing pipeline and hyperparameters, without dataset-specific preprocessing branches. B.1.1 SIGNAL -L EVEL PREPROCESSING 

Optical density conversion. For each measurement channel c, raw light intensity is converted to optical density (OD) as

Quality control. For each measurement channel, robust outlier detection is independently applied to its optical-density (OD) time series using the median absolute deviation (MAD). The channel-wise OD median is used as the center, and 1.4826 × MAD is used as the robust scale estimate. Time points with |zrobust | > 8 are marked as outliers, and non-finite values are likewise marked as invalid. Robust z-scores are computed only when the channel MAD is finite and greater than zero. Channels with zero or non-finite MAD are not subjected to point-wise outlier detection and are subsequently marked as invalid by the channel-level quality-control criteria. Contiguous outlier segments are then handled according to their duration. Segments lasting no more than 2 s and not touching either boundary of the recording are linearly interpolated using valid observations from the same channel, and the interpolated time points are subsequently treated as valid. Segments lasting longer than 2 s , or extending to either boundary of the recording, are not interpolated. Their corresponding time intervals are marked as invalid for the entire recording, and the resulting temporal-validity mask is shared across all channels. In addition, a channel is marked as invalid for the entire recording if its MAD is zero or non-finite, if its post-quality-control standard deviation is no greater than 10 −12 , or if its standard deviation is greater than 10 times or less than 1/10 of the median standard deviation across channels in that recording. 

Modified Beer–Lambert law. Valid dual-wavelength measurements are converted to oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentrations using the modified Beer– Lambert law (MBLL), with the corresponding source–detector distance and a fixed partial path-length factor of 

PPF = 6 .0. (2) The resulting concentrations are expressed in μmol /L. A consistent physical channel ordering and the corresponding channel-validity mask are established during this step. 

Resampling HbO and HbR signals are separately resampled to 5 Hz only over valid time seg-ments. Validity is determined before computing the two chromophore signals: temporal validity is defined by the quality-control procedure, while channel validity is determined by the channel-level criteria. A signal position is considered valid only when both the corresponding time point and channel are valid. 

Normalization. HbO and HbR are standardized separately for each recording using 

x′ = x − μσ . (3) For each signal stream, a single recording-level mean μ and standard deviation σ are estimated from all valid channels and valid time points. Unlike channel-wise normalization, this preserves relative amplitude differences across channels while reducing recording-level scale variation. 18 B.1.2 SAMPLE CONSTRUCTION 

For pretraining, all samples are constructed using fixed 30 s windows, corresponding to 150 time points at a sampling rate of 5 Hz. Each window is further divided into 15 consecutive 2 s segments, with 10 time points per segment. For downstream tasks, we preserve the temporal window length defined by each original task and do not apply additional temporal padding, cropping, or re-segmentation. Specifically, Shi et al. (2025) and Medvedeva et al. (2026) use 10 s windows, while the remaining six downstream tasks use 12 s windows. During pretraining, a 2 s segment participates in masking and cross-modal reconstruction loss com-putation only when at least 80% of its time points are valid. This prevents heavily corrupted temporal segments from affecting the self-supervised learning objective. For downstream evaluation, the channel set is fixed separately for each dataset. We retain the chan-nels that are jointly available across all quality-controlled recordings included in that dataset, and reconstruct all samples using exactly the same channel set and channel ordering. This design is both necessary and fair: baselines with fixed input dimensionality cannot accommodate recording-specific channel counts, while using a common channel set ensures that all subjects, recordings, and compared models receive identical channel inputs. Consequently, recording-level variation in channel availability cannot serve as a potential subject- or class-specific cue. Invalid samples along the temporal dimension are zero-filled and indicated by the corresponding validity mask. B.1.3 CHANNEL SAMPLING FOR ULTRA -H IGH -D ENSITY PRETRAINING DATA 

To preserve heterogeneous spatial structure while keeping the computational cost of ultra-high-density recordings tractable, we cap the number of physical channels entering the channel-wise attention module at 512 per pretraining window. In our pretraining corpus this cap is reached by five datasets: Fogarty et al. (2026a) (9,543–10,759 channels), Fogarty et al. (2026b) (3,375–3,515), Butters et al. (2026) (1,728), Dubois et al. (2024) (592–2,744), and Duwadi et al. (2026) (567 chan-nels). For these recordings we do not use a fixed subset of 512 channels throughout pretraining. Instead, whenever the number of valid channels in a window exceeds the cap, we sample exactly 512 chan-nels without replacement from that window’s valid channel set; windows at or below the cap keep all of their valid channels. The sampling seed is a deterministic function of the run seed, the record-ing identifier, the window start, and the training epoch, so the selection is reproducible given the configuration while different channel subsets are observed across epochs. For validation windows the epoch is replaced by a fixed constant, so the same subset is used at every validation pass and validation losses remain comparable across epochs. B.2 CHANNEL GEOMETRY REPRESENTATION 

Each physical channel c is represented by a six-dimensional geometry vector, 

gc = [mid c,x , mid c,y , mid c,z , ∆c,x , ∆c,y , ∆c,z ] , (4) where 

mid c = sc + dc

2 , ∆c = dc − sc, (5) and sc and dc denote the coordinates of the source and detector associated with channel c, respec-tively. The midpoint mid c describes the spatial location of the channel, whereas ∆c captures the source–detector separation and orientation. The resulting representation therefore jointly encodes channel position and source–detector geometry. 

Coordinate harmonization and normalization. Coordinate units vary across datasets. Across the 30 datasets used in this work (22 for pretraining and 8 for downstream evaluation), the original source and detector coordinates are represented in millimeters, centimeters, or meters. We first convert all coordinates to a common physical unit and then normalize them using a fixed reference scale of 25.46 mm. (2025). For planar layouts, mid c,z and ∆c,z are therefore identically zero, while the remaining four dimen-sions continue to encode the in-plane channel position, source–detector separation, and orientation. We retain the same six-dimensional representation without introducing synthetic depth coordinates, allowing planar and three-dimensional probe layouts to be processed by the same geometry encoder. 26 E SCALING ANALYSIS 

E.1 SCALING THE PRETRAINING CORPUS 

We conduct a preliminary analysis of how downstream adaptation changes as the pretraining cor-pus is progressively expanded. Three nested configurations contain one, four, and five datasets, corresponding to 16.71, 26.21, and 51.96 hours of recordings, respectively. The one-dataset config-uration contains Medvedeva et al. (2026); the four-dataset configuration additionally includes Lee et al. (2026), Ryb´ aˇ r et al. (2025), and Saini Ferr´ on et al. (2026); and the five-dataset configuration further adds Ajra et al. (2026). All models are evaluated on the same motor-response decoding task using linear probing and full-coverage LoRA. As shown in Figure 5, performance improves consistently as the pretraining corpus expands. Linear-probing balanced accuracy increases from 63.2% with one dataset to 73.2% with four datasets and 76.0% with five datasets, eventually exceeding the randomly initialized linear-probing baseline. Full-coverage LoRA similarly improves from 75.0% to 77.8% and 79.9%. These results suggest that increasing the scale and diversity of the pretraining corpus improves the learned representations and their downstream adaptability. Because this pilot analysis evaluates a single downstream task and changes both corpus size and composition, it provides preliminary scaling evidence rather than a controlled scaling-law analysis.

## G DISCUSSION 

Why GeoNIRS works. The transferability of GeoNIRS may arise from three complementary fac-tors: multi-dataset pretraining, geometry-aware channel unification, and cross-chromophore recon-struction. First, joint pretraining across datasets with different participant populations, acquisition systems, probe layouts, and experimental paradigms exposes the model to substantially broader signal variability than any individual dataset, providing a basis for learning more general fNIRS rep-resentations. The overall improvements observed when expanding the pretraining corpus are also consistent with this interpretation. Second, the geometry-aware channel compressor does more than accommodate a variable number of input channels: it uses source–detector geometry as a shared spa-tial reference and maps heterogeneous probe layouts into a fixed number of latent-channel tokens. Replacing channel coordinates with zeros or shuffled coordinates reduces average balanced accuracy by 2.34 and 3.24 percentage points, respectively, while removing geometry during pretraining causes a 2.98-point reduction. These results indicate that GeoNIRS meaningfully uses the correspondence between channels and their physical locations. Finally, replacing cross-chromophore reconstruction 29 with self-reconstruction reduces average performance by 1.58 points, consistent with the motiva-tion that the complete HbO or HbR signal provides complementary information for reconstructing its masked counterpart. GeoNIRS also outperforms the strongest non-GeoNIRS method selected separately at each label ratio and under each channel-loss condition, suggesting that the learned rep-resentations remain transferable under limited supervision and missing-channel settings rather than benefiting from comparison with a single weaker baseline. 

Limitations. This study has several limitations. First, although we screened nearly 100 publicly available fNIRS datasets, only 30 provided usable signals, the required metadata, and sufficient in-formation to recover channel geometry. The remaining datasets may still contain physiologically informative signals despite lacking reliable source–detector coordinates. Because GeoNIRS cur-rently relies on channel geometry to establish correspondence across probe layouts, incorporating such datasets without compromising the shared spatial representation remains an important chal-lenge for expanding the pretraining corpus. Second, several existing fNIRS pretraining methods do not provide complete implementations or downloadable pretrained weights, preventing their direct evaluation under a unified setting. Jung-inspired MAE is therefore a paper-guided implementa-tion rather than an exact reproduction of the original method. Finally, GeoNIRS-large increases both model capacity and pretraining data, but its average improvement is modest and not consistent across all downstream tasks. The current results therefore demonstrate the potential of scaling the overall configuration but do not isolate the individual effects of model size, data volume, and corpus composition, nor do they establish a reliable scaling law for fNIRS pretraining. 

Outlook. An important direction is to enable geometry-aware pretraining to benefit from both coordinate-complete and coordinate-missing fNIRS datasets. Possible approaches include geometry-optional modeling branches, estimating spatial relationships from available probe meta-data, or using datasets with reliable geometry to guide representation learning on datasets without coordinates. These approaches could substantially expand the amount of data available for pre-training. Another promising direction is to integrate fNIRS with complementary modalities. EEG captures neural electrical activity at a higher temporal resolution, whereas fNIRS measures com-paratively slow hemodynamic responses; joint pretraining on synchronized EEG–fNIRS recordings may therefore help model the relationship between neural activity and its vascular response. Short-separation channels and peripheral measurements such as heart rate and respiration may further help distinguish cortical hemodynamic responses from systemic physiological interference. In ad-dition, aligning fNIRS representations with experimental stimuli, behavioral records, or textual task descriptions may support transfer across paradigms and enable few-shot or potentially zero-shot downstream applications. Future studies should also vary model capacity, data volume, and corpus composition independently to characterize how heterogeneous fNIRS pretraining scales. 30
