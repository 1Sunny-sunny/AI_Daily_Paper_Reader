# Latent-Space Importance Weighting under Covariate Shift 

Yuling Jiao, Lican Kang, Yang Xiang, Jerry Zhijian Yang, Fusheng Zhou 

Abstract 

In this work, we propose a transfer learning algorithm for covariate shift, integrating sample reweighting with representation learning. A key feature of our approach is the use of a lower-dimensional latent space for modeling, in contrast to conventional methods that operate directly in the high-dimensional ambient space. Our method estimates the density ratio of learned latent features for downstream weighted prediction. We establish explicit target-domain excess-risk rates with independent data folds for strictly sufficient learned representations with regular learned-space prediction scores and density ratios. We give dimension comparisons under stated smoothness conditions and a variance comparison for true weights and a fixed predictor. General finite-sample inequalities retain representation-quality errors. Experiments on simulated and real-world datasets demonstrate gains from combining learned representations and latent weighting, and reveal sensitivity to initialization and domain choice.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Transfer learning (TL) uses information from a source domain to improve prediction in a target domain [1]. In this work, we focus on TL under covariate shift [2, 3], a scenario where the marginal distributions of the covariates differ between the source and target domains, while the conditional distributions of the response variable given the covariates remain consistent. This distributional discrepancy poses significant challenges for transferring models from the source domain to the target domain, often leading to degraded generalization performance or even negative transfer. To address the distributional mismatch, reweighting techniques have emerged as effective solutions, with numerous studies offering insightful contributions. For instance, [4, 5] developed model-assisted transfer regression and model-accuracy transfer inference methods. Divergence-based methods, including those based on Kullback-Leibler and other divergences, have been employed to estimate reweighting functions [6, 7, 8]. Additionally, nonparametric methods such as kernel mean matching and log-likelihood estimation have shown promise in estimating resampling weights [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Related approaches include importance weighting in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) [14, 6], weighted likelihood [13], and representation learning for domain adaptation using maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) [15] or structural correspondence learning [16]. Section I-C reviews these methods. While reweighting methods are theoretically appealing and facilitate the derivation of generalization error bounds [17], density-ratio estimation can be difficult when its complexity depends on the ambient dimension. Low-dimensional task-relevant structure motivates studying when representation learning can reduce this estimation burden. To address covariate shift and mitigate the curse of dimensionality, we adopt a reweighting strategy within a latent represen-tation space. Our analysis quantifies the gains in density-ratio estimation dimension and true-weight variance under sufficient learned representations. Our methodology consists of three key stages. First, inspired by [18], we derive a lower-dimensional representation of the high-dimensional raw data in the source domain. Source-domain representation training alone does not correct differences between the latent marginal distributions. The second stage therefore estimates the density ratio between the source and target distributions of the actual learned features. Finally, in the third stage, we train the target predictor in the source domain by minimizing the weighted empirical risk. Tables II and IV report comparisons with baseline methods under random initialization; the pretrained comparisons in Tables V and VI show that the empirical gains depend on initialization and the source-target domains. From a theoretical perspective, while existing frameworks for TL under covariate shift [17, 19, 20, 21, 22] provide valuable insights, our analysis gives explicit target-risk rates that separate prediction, ratio estimation and representation quality. For strictly sufficient, regular learned features, ratio estimation operates in the latent dimension d rather than the ambient dimension p. The general finite-sample bound quantifies information loss and conditional label mismatch when sufficiency fails.

C. Related Work 

We discuss several related works in this section. 

Covariate Shift and Domain Adaptation. TL has been applied across various domains, including machine learning [32, 33, 34], language and text analysis [35, 36, 37, 38], and computer vision [39, 40, 41, 42]. Alongside its application, the theoretical foundations of TL have also advanced. Covariate shift is a specific setting within domain adaptation: the conditional label law is invariant while the feature marginal may change. Importance weighting corrects the risk without requiring the two feature marginals to be equal. [9] proposed using Kernel Mean Matching in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) to estimate resampling weights, instead of directly estimating the distributions. However, RKHS methods are sensitive to kernel function parameters [14, 6]. [12] proposed an instance-weighting framework that distinguishes changes in input distributions from changes in labeling functions. [13] studied the asymptotic properties of the parametric weighted log-likelihood model under covariate shift. [43] analyzed the bias of logistic maximum-likelihood estimation in a high-dimensional asymptotic regime. [44] developed a Bayesian generative mixture-regression approach combining regression and density estimation. [7] proposed importance-weighted cross-validation for model selection under covariate shift. Direct ratio-estimation methods avoid estimating the source and target densities separately [45]. [46, 47] developed discriminative methods for covariate shift without explicitly modeling either feature distribution. [48] proposed least-squares importance estimation with a closed-form solution and analytically computable leave-one-out cross-validation. [49] studied importance weighting on learned features under joint distribution shift, with covariate shift as a special case. They identify a dependence between weight estimation and classifier-based feature extraction, and propose dynamic importance weighting that alternates weight and classifier updates. Their Theorem 1 preserves the joint density ratio under a fixed invertible transformation. Here we study a staged procedure under covariate shift with independent data folds, allowing representations that need not be invertible. Under sufficiency, the marginal ratio induced by the fitted representation corrects the target risk; it need not recover the original-space ratio. Our contribution builds on feature-space weighting through explicit target-risk rates under stated output conditions, dimension and true-weight variance comparisons, and a general bound retaining representation-quality errors. [15] proposed MMD embedding to learn a shared low-dimensional space with reduced marginal discrepancy. [16] introduced structural correspondence learning, using pivot-feature prediction and a low-rank projection to capture correspondences across domains. Our representation objective instead uses source labels and a Gaussian-reference penalty; cross-domain marginal differences are handled by latent weighting. Deep nonparametric mean and quantile regression under covariate shift have also been analyzed in [50, 22]. These studies motivate the complementary question addressed here: how weighting in a learned task-relevant representation affects target-domain risk. 

DNNs and Representation Learning. DNNs have become widely used for extracting latent representations, achieving remark-able results. [51] demonstrated the effectiveness of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in learning good representations for high-dimensional image data, which can be applied to downstream tasks. Representation learning seeks task-sufficient features with independent components [52]. Furthermore, these representations are transferable across similar tasks [53]. Choosing an objective that yields task-relevant representations remains a challenge [52]. [18] proposed a framework for sufficient and disentangled representation learning, which motivates the representation objective used here; preservation of label information is assessed separately in our risk bounds. The convergence rate of DNN-based methods is a crucial topic of study. Many works have investigated the approximation capabilities of DNNs, describing how network structures like width and depth influence performance [54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60]. To control statistical errors, a well-established set of theories based on empirical processes has been developed [61, 62, 63, 64, 65]. These studies focus on bounding statistical errors in terms of the metric entropy of the hypothesis class.

E XPERIMENTS 

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, we conduct the experiments on both simulated and real dataset, divided into two sub-experiments. The first experiment compares the performance between Method (10) and other three methods: Method (11), Method (12), and Method (13), focusing on the effects of low-dimensional representation and density ratio function on TL. This aims to demonstrate the performance difference while controlling for key variable. The second experiment benchmarks our proposed method against several state-of-the-art TL methods, as outlined in [74] which is a professional TL method library. This comparison assesses empirical performance across initialization and domain-transfer settings. For a detailed introduction to these baselines, please refer to the Appendix A. We set the random seed for the runs. All experiments are implemented using PyTorch and Tesla V100 GPU. We compare prediction accuracy and examine how representation learning, weighting and initialization affect performance. 

A. Data set and processing 

We introduce the datasets used in the experiment, comprising two simulated data sets: Circle and Moon, and four real datasets: Office-Caltech 1, Breast cancer, Blood, Haberman where the last three real datasets are obtained from UCI 2 and [75]. The Circle and Moon datasets are generated by the related packages in scikit-learn 3. For Circle and Moon, we first generate the source data with a noise level of 0.1 and a factor of 0.6, where the noise represents the standard deviation of Gaussian noise added to the data, and the factor denotes the ratio between the inner and outer circles. To generate the covariate shift in the rest simulated datasets, we use biased sampling for both source and target datasets by following [9, 75]. After obtaining the covariate shift datasets, we map them into a higher-dimensional space by multiplying them with a random high-dimensional matrix. C. Parameters setting 

We use Adam with a batch size of 64 and train for 200 epochs. The dataset-specific learning rate lr and latent dimension for representation learning (Step 1 in Algorithm 1) are listed in Table VIII. The density-ratio estimation and prediction stages use a learning rate of 10 −3. We use the exponentially-flattened technique [13, 78] to reduce the variance caused by density ratio [78]. This technique involves replacing the estimated ratio ˆr with ˆrαw , where αw ∈ (0, 1] is the weight-tempering exponent, distinct from the representation-penalty coefficient λ in (5). A comprehensive list of all hyperparameters is provided in Table VIII in Appendix A. Tempering with an exponent below one generally introduces bias relative to full-ratio target-risk correction. The rate theorems analyze full-ratio weighting with clipped fully connected network sieves and empirical risk minimization; the experiments use tempered weights, fixed architectures and finite-epoch Adam training. 

D. Baselines 

In our experiments, we select a series of TL methods as comparison baselines, including DANN [26], DAN [27], CDAN[25], AFN [23], BSP [24], JAN [28], MCC [29], MCD [30], MDD [31], and ERM. These TL methods are highly cited and widely recognized. In TL library [74], these methods have been integrated and implemented. To avoid duplicating effort, we use the toolbox directly to obtain the baseline methods. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we propose a TL algorithm designed to address the covariate shift condition. Our method integrates probability classification for density ratio estimation with deep representation learning, resulting in a TL approach in a latent space. The method is built upon two critical intermediate stages: representation learning and density ratio learning. The first stage aims to extract task-relevant latent features with approximately independent components, mapping high-dimensional raw data into a lower-dimensional latent space. For sufficient, regular learned features, this strategy reduces the dimension governing density-ratio estimation. The second stage involves learning the density ratio function between the source and target domains, which directly affects the TL performance. With independent data folds, we establish explicit target-risk rates for strictly sufficient learned representations with uniform learned-space regularity. The resulting bounds improve the density-ratio estimation expo-nent under the stated dimension and smoothness conditions; true latent weights also reduce the loss variance for a fixed predictor. The general finite-sample bound retains representation-quality errors; the appendix also quantifies representation-estimation error and discriminator bias. Experiments demonstrate gains from combining representation learning and latent weighting, with sensitivity to initialization and domain choice. Finite-sample guarantees for learning sufficient, regular representations, and extensions to shared training samples and multiclass classification, remain future work. A limitation of this approach is its reliance on two intermediate stages, which can complicate model training and tuning. We view overcoming these challenges as a promising direction for future research. APPENDIX

G. Persistent error from an insufficient representation 

An explicit fixed-class example illustrates the obstruction even if Assumption A.1 is retained. Let the source feature law be uniform on [0, 1]p, p ≥ 2, and let the target feature density relative to it be r∗(x) = 1 + ε(2x1 − 1), 0 < ε < 1.
