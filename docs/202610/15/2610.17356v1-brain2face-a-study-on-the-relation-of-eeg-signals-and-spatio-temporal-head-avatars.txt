## ABSTRACT 

Driving digital head avatars is classically done by hand-crafted artist design or by image-based facial performance capture. In this work, we ask whether spatio-temporal tracking of the humans’s head geometry and appearance can be performed solely from brain signals, i.e., Electroencephalography (EEG). Since prior work mostly focused on activity and emotion recognition, this task is relatively unex-plored without even having publicly available high-quality datasets and benchmarks. Thus, we contribute a dataset featuring paired EEG and human head geometry as well as appearance annotations. We synchronize 64-channel EEG with per-frame expression latent codes of a personalized Gaussian head avatar. Second, we in-troduce a baseline to the task of expression recovery of a head avatar from EEG signals. To this end, we propose a dedicated EEG-to-expression model that takes a temporal sequence of EEG signals as input and regresses the latent expression parameters of the 3D Gaussian head avatar. Lastly, we validate our capture and algorithmic design choices through a benchmark protocol that we introduce to this task. We believe this work paves the way towards better understanding of the rela-tion between EEG signals and facial surface as well as appearance changes. Project page: https://vcai.mpi-inf.mpg.de/projects/Brain2Face/ .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Digital human avatars play a central role in virtual reality, telepresence, and human-computer interaction, where the ability to faithfully convey facial expressions is crucial for realism and communication. Existing avatar control paradigms typically rely on manual digital artist design or on visual observations, such as monocular or multi-view images, depth sensors, or facial markers, which inherently require a clear line of sight and explicit user engagement. In contrast, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) offer a fundamentally different interaction modality by enabling direct control from neural signals that do not suffer from such limitations: Leveraging brain signals to drive expressive facial avatars would enable hands-free, camera-free avatar control, opening new possibilities for immersive communication, accessibility, and assistive technologies. Among the devices that measure brain activities, electroencephalography (EEG) stands out due to its non-invasive nature and practical usage. However, current EEG technology still poses fundamental challenges for actual deployment, e.g., due to its setup and noisy signals and it remains an open question to what extent EEG signals are already usable for such deployments. On the other hand, image-based facial performance capture has been studied extensively, from multi-view reconstruction to learned facial models and photorealistic avatars (Beeler et al., 2010; Danˇ eˇ cek et al., 2022; Qian et al., 2024). Meanwhile, EEG-based interfaces have primarily focused on discrete prediction tasks, including affective state recognition (Koelstra et al., 2012; Soleymani et al., 2012) and clinical pathology detection (Gemein et al., 2020; Schirrmeister et al., 2017), rather than continuous facial motion. Related synthesis approaches generate facial emotion images (Esmaeili and Kiani, 2024), EEG-conditioned talking faces (Park et al., 2024), or intentional facial expressions through brain decoding and generative image synthesis (Shimizu et al., 2025). Furthermore, multi-view capture systems (Beeler et al., 2010) and expressive photorealistic head models (Qian et al., 2024; Teotia et al., 2025) provide detailed geometry and appearance representations for facial animation. Studies on EEG-driven photorealistic avatars, however, remain limited, e.g., Mind-to-Face (Xiong et al., 2026). Still, the capture strategy focuses on emotion-stimulating videos and does not provide a fixed set of recorded facial expression, limiting systematic analysis. Further, captured modalities remain limited, i.e., limited number of EEG channels and views, and data is not publicly available for research. To address these limitations, we introduce a dataset of paired EEG and spatio-temporally coherent and fine-grained facial performance capture data, including geometry and appearance. We captured multiple subjects in a multi-view capture setup with up to 33 cameras, paired with 64-channel EEG signals. To recover high-quality face data, we fit a state-of-the-art 3D Gaussian avatar to the data and, thus, encode expressions into low dimensional latent codes. An EEG encoder and a decoder map temporal EEG signals to these expression latents. This allows us to decode the EEG driven expression into 3D Gaussians and geometry that can be rendered to image space. For training, supervision is applied in latent and geometry space. We refer to Fig. 1 for an overview. Since this is the first study of its kind, we introduce an evaluation protocol for this task and a first baseline as well as ablations of various EEG foundation models (El Ouahidi et al., 2025; Guetschel et al., 2024), the influence of different prediction heads, and the effect of masking different brain areas. We believe that our benchmark can serve as a starting point for future works to study the relations between EEG data and facial expressions. To summarize, this work makes several key contributions: • First, we present a dataset that provides EEG signals paired with high-resolution facial performance capture data as well as dense annotations that are encoded as latent expression parameters. • Second, we propose a model for predicting facial expressions from EEG signals, which allows driving a Gaussian head avatar. • Third, we ablate several baselines and design factors on our newly introduced benchmark. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

EEG-based Brain-Computer Interfaces. Electroencephalography (EEG) captures the summation of postsynaptic potentials from cortical pyramidal neurons, providing high-temporal-resolution voltage fluctuations measured at the scalp Gkintoni and Halkiopoulos (2025). Since its discovery by Hans Berger in the 1920s, EEG has evolved from a clinical device into a versatile tool for decoding neural activity in both constrained laboratory and everyday environments Gkintoni and Halkiopoulos (2025); Beyrouthy et al. (2024). The majority of EEG-based brain-computer interface 2(BCI) research has focused on classification tasks, mapping neural signals to a small, fixed set of categories. A prominent line of work concerns affective state recognition, leveraging multi-modal datasets such as DEAP Koelstra et al. (2012) and MAHNOB (Soleymani et al., 2012) to classify valence or arousal labels. Recent methods combine EEG with video and audio streams to improve emotion classification through multimodal fusion (Wang et al., 2026). In the clinical domain, deep convolutional and temporal convolutional networks have demonstrated strong performance for discrete EEG pathology detection Gemein et al. (2020); Schirrmeister et al. (2017), and the Braindecode toolbox Schirrmeister et al. (2017) has made such architectures widely accessible. More recently, large-scale EEG foundation models have been proposed to learn general-purpose representations transferable across subjects and tasks (El Ouahidi et al., 2025). A smaller body of work has explored 

continuous regression from EEG, targeting low-dimensional motor outputs. Hosseini and Shalchyan (2022) decode two-dimensional hand-movement trajectories from 63-channel EEG using phase-based connectivity features and linear regression, while Lu et al. (2026) extend continuous decoding to polar-coordinate hand-motion angles. Bridging neural signals and facial motion, Shimizu et al. (2025) interface brain decoding with generative image synthesis to restore intentional facial expressions for ALS patients, and Park et al. (2024) generate lip-synchronised talking faces conditioned on EEG for dynamic communication. Esmaeili and Kiani (2024) synthesize discrete facial emotion images from EEG using conditional adversarial networks. However, none of these works has investigated learning and studying a continuous mapping from EEG signals to facial geometry and appearance parameterized as a latent expression space of a Gaussian head avatar prior. 

Facial Performance Capture. Recovering fine-grained facial geometry and appearance from image streams has been studied extensively. Monocular methods (Feng et al., 2021; Danˇ eˇ cek et al., 2022) fit parametric face models (Blanz and Vetter, 1999; Li et al., 2017) to single RGB images or video, leveraging shape, expression, and appearance priors to regress 3D morphable model coefficients or neural blendshape parameters. Multi-view systems (Beeler et al., 2010) improve reconstruction fidelity by triangulating correspondences across calibrated cameras, enabling denser and more accurate surface recovery. More recent approaches build implicit neural or Gaussian representations directly from video footage, capturing subject-specific geometry and view-dependent appearance with high visual fidelity (Gafni et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2022; Qian et al., 2024). While egocentric setups using head-mounted displays (Zhang et al., 2024) have relaxed the requirement for external camera rigs, all of these paradigms share a common dependency: they require a clear line of sight to the subject’s face and operate under visual sensing assumptions. EEG needs no line of sight, but its setup and noisy, artifact-prone signals (Goncharova et al., 2003) still hinder deployment. Moreover, image-based methods cannot be trivially adapted to EEG inputs, since EEG signals have entirely different statistical properties, spatial structure, and signal-to-noise characteristics compared to pixel data, necessitating dedicated architectures and datasets. 

Photoreal Head Avatars. Recent years have seen remarkable advances in the fidelity of animatable head avatars. Neural radiance field (NeRF) based approaches (Mildenhall et al., 2020; Gafni et al., 2021) and, more recently, 3D Gaussian splatting representations (Kerbl et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024) have enabled photorealistic rendering of dynamic faces at interactive frame rates. A range of methods construct person-specific neural avatars conditioned on expression codes derived from 3DMM fits (Gafni et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2022) or tracked blendshape rigs (Qian et al., 2024), achieving impressive reconstruction quality. Universal avatar priors that generalize across identities have been proposed to disentangle subject appearance from expression control, allowing a single model to animate novel subjects given only a short capture (Teotia et al., 2025). In this work, we adopt such a universal Gaussian head avatar as our rendering backbone and treat its learned expression latent space as the regression target for our EEG encoder. Our goal is to study EEG-to-expression regression using a learned avatar latent space and to compare EEG representations under controlled evaluation protocols. The most closely related work is Mind-to-Face (Xiong et al., 2026), which also reconstructs photo-realistic, dynamic 3D facial expressions from EEG. Their system records synchronized 16-channel EEG and multi-view video during emotion-eliciting stimuli videos, maps EEG through a CNN-Transformer encoder to dense 3D position maps, and renders the resulting geometry with a Gaussian avatar pipeline. Their study evaluates personalized mappings for two subjects. Brain2Face instead maps 64-channel EEG to the 256-dimensional expression codes of a personalized universal Gaussian head avatar and studies six subjects performing a predefined set of 75 expression types across two 3Expressions Views Subjects

Therefore, our dataset offers annotations previously not available, enabling a a wide range of potential analyses to better understand the relation between EEG signals and facial expression.

3.1 CAPTURE SETUP 

Face Capturing. Subjects are recorded by a multi-view setup of 26 to 33 cameras recording video at fv =29 .97 fps. The multi-view images are used to fit a person-alized Gaussian head avatar (Teotia et al., 2025) to each subject (Sec. 3.2), yielding per-frame expression codes and a fixed identity code. The fitted expression codes pro-vide continuous regression targets. Decoding them yields the meshes, 3D landmarks, and avatar renders used for supervision and evaluation (Sec. 3.2). 

EEG Capturing. Electroencephalography (EEG) mea-sures scalp voltage fluctuations arising from summed postsynaptic activity with high temporal resolution (Gkintoni and Halkiopoulos, 2025). We acquire 64-channel EEG at 500 Hz using a Brain 4Products LiveAmp device with an EasyCap and electrode montage according to the 10-10 system (Oostenveld and Praamstra, 2001), with conductive gel controlling electrode impedance. Impedance was measured before the experiment and kept below 15 k Ω.

Recording Sessions. Every subject follows a standardized experimental protocol with a fixed set of expressions (Secs. A.2 and B in the supplementary material). A baseline EEG recording quantifies the subject’s resting neural activity. Then, the subject performs a set of pre-defined facial expressions in two sessions, following video demonstrations of each target expression, separated by a short break. Each session starts and ends with three jaw clenches that serve as a synchronization signal (Sec. A.1 in the supplementary material). 3.2 GAUSSIAN AVATAR FITTING

## 4 METHOD 

The objective of Brain2Face is continuous control of a personalized 3D Gaussian head avatar from EEG signals. Given a preprocessed EEG window X ∈ RC×Ne , a predictor F estimates the corresponding expression-code sequence ˆZexp = F (X) ∈ Rd×Nz , whose target Zexp stacks the codes Zt

Each predicted code drives the frozen personalized avatar decoder defined in Sec. 3.2. Fig. 4 in the supplementary material gives an overview of the pipeline. 4.1 EEG-TO -E XPRESSION ENCODER 

Data preprocessing. We follow the pretraining preprocessing of REVE (El Ouahidi et al., 2025, Sec. 3.1.1): resampling to 200 Hz, 0.5–99.5 Hz band-pass filtering, and clipping at ±15 standard deviations per session. Further, we employ a notch filter at 50 Hz to remove power line noise. 

Model architecture. We factor the predictor as F = Mϕ ◦ Eθ , where Eθ encodes the EEG window and Mϕ maps its features to the expression-code sequence. The encoder can be initialized from an EEG foundation model and either frozen or fine-tuned. For the EEG encoder, we use the base variant of REVE (El Ouahidi et al., 2025). The decoder flattens the encoder’s channel and patch dimensions, projects the features with a 1 × 1 convolution, and applies L residual convolution blocks along the resulting channel-patch token axis. A learned resampler and a framewise output projection produce 

Nz expression codes with d = 256 coordinates each. We use L = 2 and one-second windows (Ne = 200 , Nz = 30 ) for the principal model and study the decoder depth L in Sec. 5.3. During training, an auxiliary head pools the intermediate residual-block features and maps them to a second expression-code sequence ˆZaux exp of the same shape, which supervises the decoder’s intermediate representations and is discarded at inference. The foundation-model comparison instead uses a decoder with two fully connected hidden layers to probe each encoder’s frozen features (Sec. 5.2). Sec. A.5 provides further details. Replacing Zt

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

Next, we study the feasibility of predicting facial expressions from EEG signals. We introduce the evaluation protocol (Sec. 5.1), report our experimental findings (Sec.5.2-5.5), and, finally, show qualitative results of the overall pipeline (Sec. 5.6). 5.1 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Metrics. To quantitatively evaluate the performance of the face predictions, we use the following evaluation metrics: Latent MSE measures the mean squared error between predicted and target expression codes. Mesh MAE measures the mean absolute coordinate error over the decoded mesh vertices. Landmark Wing measures a robust coordinate-wise deviation of the selected facial landmarks (Feng et al., 2018). Blendshape MAE measures the mean absolute error across 52 expression coefficients. LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) measures perceptual distance between predicted and fitted-avatar ground-truth renders. Sec. A.4 defines latent MSE, mesh MAE, and landmark Wing as Lp, Lv , and Lℓ, evaluated on the test frames. All metrics are averaged over all frames within each recording-expression held-out sequence. 

Test splits. We train and evaluate a separate model for each subject using the cross-session split described in Sec. A.2. Both test settings contain sequences excluded from training. For seen expressions (S), the expression type occurs in training in the opposite recording session; for unseen expressions (U), it is absent from training in both sessions. Both sessions contribute training sequences, so this protocol does not evaluate transfer to a completely unseen recording session or participant. 5.2 ON THE EFFECTIVENESS OF EEG FOUNDATION MODELS FOR FACE DECODING 

We compare nine pretrained EEG encoders Eθ by freezing their parameters and training an MLP decoder Mϕ with two fully connected hidden layers to predict expression-code trajectories. 5.5 ON THE INFORMATIVENESS OF DIFFERENT BRAIN AREALS FOR FACE DECODING 

To analyze what the model has learned, we study the effect of removing electrodes corresponding to various brain areas, similar to region-wise electrode ablations in prior EEG decoding work (Song et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2026). For this, we retrain models where the respective channels are masked out, i.e., set to zero. Masking motor or prefrontal channels degrades facial expressions reconstructed from EEG (Hu et al., 2026). Furthermore, the frontal and temporal groups also lie closest to the frontalis and temporalis muscles, whose EMG is strongest at these sites and attenuates toward central and posterior sites (Goncharova et al., 2003), whereas the occipital group lies over the visual cortex (Song et al., 2024). Further, we also study the effect of masking out bad channels, which Bigdely-Shamlo et al. (2015) identify by extreme amplitudes, high-frequency noise, or low correlation with, and poor predictability from, the other channels. Bad channels are detected per subject on training expressions with PyPREP, a Python implementation of the PREP pipeline Bigdely-Shamlo et al. (2015). The results are presented in Tab. 5. The findings reveal that for seen in-distribution expressions, a substantial amount of the performance can be attributed to the temporal electrodes, as removing the influence of the those electrodes results in a substantial performance drop. Performance drops are observed when masking other brain areas, including the motor cortex. In contrast, removing bad channels reduces the effect of artifacts in the EEG, eventually even improving the performance compared to experiment where access to all electrodes is given. Interestingly, for unseen expressions, the effect of removing electrodes capturing the frontal brain areal is contrary compared to the seen expression: Results substantially improve when their effect is removed. We hypothesize that this could be attributed to the fact that the motion artifacts in the frontal brain area observed in the EEG signal provide less consistent information for unseen expressions than for seen expressions. ## 6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Our initial dataset contains only six participants recorded in two sessions each, motivating larger studies with more participants and recording sessions to assess generalization. As recorded EEG signal are substantially noisier than classical image-driven signals, accurate facial tracking is more challenging with current reconstructions not capturing the level of detail available in image-driven approaches. Future work could explore further analyses with complementary sensing modalities, such as functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), to investigate whether they provide additional information for facial expression decoding. Finally, our framework models only facial expressions and does not account for movements of other body parts - movements that could also impact the signal quality. Future work can also analyze further which EEG signal components drive the prediction of which facial articulations. 97 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduced the task of mapping EEG signals to face articulations. For this, we presented a systematically captured dataset that allows studying the relation between the two modal-ities. Subjects were recorded with an EEG and captured in a multi-camera rig that allows highly accurate reconstructions. We further presented a surprisingly effective first baseline to predict face articulations and we studied various design dimensions including the effect of using pre-trained EEG foundational models, the importance of using expressive decoders, and the importance of different brain areals for the prediction of facial expressions. We hope that the dataset and the first analysis serves the community and encourages further analysis on the informativeness of EEG signals to infer facial geometry and articulations.

To synchronize the EEG signals with the facial video recordings, a synchronization event is manually triggered for both the face and EEG recording devices. With one-second windows, REVE returns one token per electrode, so the decoder’s kernel-size-3 residual blocks, with increasing dilation, operate across the 64 electrode tokens. Two strided depthwise-separable convolutions reduce these tokens to 16, linear interpolation resamples them to Nz = 30 frames, and a framewise MLP maps each frame to d = 256 coordinates. The MLP decoder used for encoder probing flattens the encoder features and applies two 512-unit hidden layers with GEGLU, RMSNorm, and dropout probability 0.75 before projecting to 256 Nz values; it has no auxiliary head. EEG models use AdamW under three initialization regimes: pretrained-frozen, pretrained-unfrozen, and scratch. The first keeps the pretrained encoder fixed, the second fine-tunes it, and the third trains it from random initialization. During training, windows are sampled at random positions within the training segments. All results use predictions from consecutive non-overlapping windows, whereas the qualitative clips concatenate them. The channel-masking ablation (Tab. 5) zeroes the following electrodes: motor, FC1–FC6, C1–C6, Cz, CP1–CP6, and CPz; frontal, Fp1, Fp2, AF3, AF4, AF7, AF8, F1–F8, and Fz; occipital, O1, O2, Oz, PO3, PO4, PO7–PO10, and POz; temporal, T7, T8, FT7–FT10, and TP7–TP10.
