Abstract 

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) diagnosis remains challenging due to het-erogeneous symptoms and reliance on clinical assessment, underscoring the need for objective, data-driven approaches that leverage patient-generated physio-logical signals. We propose Siam-ALS, an explainable Siamese deep learning (DL) framework that jointly models synchronized electroencephalography (EEG) and eye-tracking (ET) signals. EEG captures ALS-related changes in brain activity, while ET provides information about oculomotor control, allowing the model to learn complementary patterns associated with ALS. The framework employs two modality-specific encoders: a convolutional EEG encoder that cap-tures spatiotemporal neural patterns and an MLP-based ET encoder that models oculomotor dynamics. These modality-specific features are processed through weight-sharing Siamese branches and fused into a compact 64-dimensional joint embedding. A lightweight classifier is used to predict ALS, while pairwise metric learning helps the model learn more effective patterns from the small, diverse ALS datasets. Performance was evaluated on the public EEGET-ALS dataset using strict subject-level cross-validation, where Siam-ALS achieved an accuracy of 0.988 and an AUROC of 0.998, demonstrating strong performance compared with 

1arXiv:2610.15351v1 [cs.LG] 11 Oct 2026 classical machine learning (ML), DL, and non-Siamese fusion approaches. Cross-dataset evaluation on the independent BigP3BCI dataset achieved an AUROC of approximately 0.98, supporting the generalization capability of Siam-ALS across different BCI paradigms. To enhance interpretability, six XAI methods were eval-uated in the learned embedding space, with SHAP achieving the highest, though statistically comparable, fidelity in identifying important EEG and ET patterns that contribute to ALS predictions. Overall, Siam-ALS provides an accurate, generalizable, and explainable framework for multimodal ALS detection.

# 1 Introduction 

ALS is a rapidly progressing disease of the nervous system that destroys motor neu-rons, gradually diminishing a person’s ability to move, speak, and communicate [1]. The diagnostic process today is slow: doctors rely on clinical judgment, electromyo-graphy, and the exclusion of other look-alike conditions [2], a process that can take months and delay access to early treatment, clinical trials, and assistive devices [3]. Early diagnosis matters because ALS progresses quickly, and every month lost to diag-nostic delay is a month of missed access to disease-modifying therapies, clinical trial enrollment, and timely assistive support [4, 5]. Yet early detection remains difficult: symptoms overlap with other neuromuscular disorders, and non-invasive biomarkers such as EEG and ET signals are still being validated for routine clinical use [6–9]. Compounding this, the ML models built on such signals often serve as opaque pre-dictors, offering little insight into the underlying biology that drives their decisions [10, 11]. These gaps have increased interest in non-invasive, data-driven screening tools that may help identify ALS earlier than traditional diagnostic approaches. Current ALS detection methods have progressed from classical ML on handcrafted clinical or motor-imagery features [3, 12–14] to DL directly on raw EEG or EMG using CNNs, RNNs, and attention-based architectures [4, 5, 15, 16]. Although these models improve detection accuracy, they provide limited information about the fea-tures responsible for their predictions [10, 17]. Separately, ET has been recognized as a non-invasive biomarker for detecting motor and cognitive changes in ALS patients [18]. Previous studies combining ET with EEG have mainly focused on applications such as attention classification using simple early or late fusion approaches [19]. These methods combine features from both modalities but do not explicitly model the com-plex temporal relationship between brain activity and eye movement patterns. Deeper cross-modal architectures such as Siamese and attention-based networks address this coupling problem in other domains [20–22] but have not been applied to ALS, and explainability tools like SHAP and attention weights remain largely unused in this specific context [23–25]. This left two gaps unaddressed together: no ALS-specific model captures the deeper EEG–ET coupling, and none pairs this with clinically interpretable output. 2EEG captures ALS-related changes in brain activity [6, 7], while ET provides informa-tion about eye-movement control affected by the nervous system [8, 9]. Thus, these two signals provide complementary information for ALS detection, and their integration into a joint model can yield a more complete view of ALS-related changes than either signal alone. Inspired by this, we propose a multimodal EEG–ET Siamese architec-ture with embedding-level explainability. The framework is designed to directly learn interactions between neural and oculomotor signals while producing interpretable representations consistent with known ALS biology. Beyond ALS, EEG–ET fusion has been explored in applications such as emo-tion recognition and attention classification [19]. However, many existing approaches rely on shallow feature fusion strategies [26], where features from different modalities are combined without explicitly learning a shared representation or capturing their underlying interactions. Recent transformer-based EEG–ET models have improved multimodal representation learning for tasks such as depression detection, yet they remain task-specific and have not been extended to neurodegenerative disease screening [27–29]. Within ALS, EEG and ET have primarily been investigated as separate modalities, despite gaze-controlled BCI spellers used by many ALS patients naturally generating both signals simultaneously [30, 31]; however, their integration for early ALS pre-diction remains largely unexplored. Theoretically, the two (EEG and ET) are not redundant: EEG reflects central motor and cortical degeneration, while ET reflects downstream oculomotor output that remains relatively preserved even in late-stage ALS [18], so modeling how these two levels of the motor pathway co-vary should reveal information neither signal captures alone. Moreover, existing ALS detection models, typically EEG-only, offer no explanation of their predictions [4, 5, 32], no reliabil-ity guarantees on small, imbalanced cohorts [4, 32], and no exploration of EEG-ET fusion for this task [31]. Together, these limitations limit the ability to build accurate, explainable, and robust ALS detection models that support clinical decision-making. To address these limitations, we introduce Siam-ALS, a Siamese DL framework that jointly learns an explainable EEG–ET representation for early ALS detection. The framework uses two weight-sharing branches to encode EEG and ET signals into a shared 64-dimensional embedding space, where sample pairs are compared through metric learning rather than relying solely on individual classification. This approach enables robust and generalizable predictions while maintaining clinical inter-pretability. The main idea of combining EEG and ET is to capture complementary aspects of ALS-related changes, where EEG reflects underlying neural dysfunction and ET provides information about oculomotor control alterations. Additionally, the pairwise formulation with the Siamese framework is more data-efficient under small cohorts than standard classifiers, which tend to fit patient-specific quirks rather than disease-general patterns [20, 33]. Because both modalities are projected into the same embedding space, the network learns their cross-modal coupling directly, rather than merging two separate feature sets only at the final decision layer as shallow fusion does. Furthermore, to improve model transparency and clinical trust, we apply explain-able artificial intelligence (XAI) methods to identify which learned EEG–ET patterns 3contribute to ALS predictions. We investigate six XAI techniques, including SHAP, LIME, Integrated Gradients, Saliency, Occlusion, and Permutation Importance, to interpret the learned representation and examine whether the identified brain and eye-movement features align with known ALS-related biological mechanisms [34]. Therefore, our proposed Siam-ALS provides a unified framework that addresses gen-eralization, multimodal fusion, and explainability through pairwise similarity learning in a shared, low-dimensional embedding space. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: i) We introduce Siam-ALS, a novel explainable Siamese DL framework that jointly models EEG and ET signals for early ALS detection. By combining EEG-based neural activity patterns with ET-based oculomotor control information, the frame-work captures complementary manifestations of ALS-related changes, thereby improving disease characterization. ii) We develop a pairwise metric-learning strategy with weight-sharing branches to improve representation learning on the small, heterogeneous datasets commonly found in ALS research. This approach enables robust generalization while reducing reliance on conventional individual sample classification. iii) We compare Siam-ALS against a wide range of classical ML and DL models on the EEGET-ALS dataset, where it matches the highest AUROC and achieves the best threshold-dependent metrics while being the only explainable model. iv) We demonstrate cross-dataset generalizability on EEGET-ALS and BigP3BCI datasets using the same encoder and investigate six XAI methods, where SHAP provides meaningful interpretations by identifying key EEG–ET features associated with ALS prediction. 

# 2 Literature Review 

Early detection of ALS remains difficult because the disease progresses quickly, varies across patients, and lacks a single reliable non-invasive biomarker. Researchers have explored EEG-based ML/DL [35], while EEG–eye tracking fusion has separately proven effective in eye-based interaction systems for individuals with disabilities [22]. ET has also attracted interest, as eye movements often remain intact even in late-stage, locked-in ALS. More recently, Siamese neural networks (SNNs) and contrastive representation learning have opened new possibilities for models that work well with very little labeled data. This review covers five relevant areas: i) EEG biomarkers and neurophysiology of ALS ii) Multimodal EEG–ET fusion in BCI systems iii) Siamese network architectures for biosignal learning iv) Explainability in EEG and biomedical DL. v) Deep EEG models and cross-dataset generalization 

2.1 EEG biomarkers and neurophysiology of ALS 

EEG is widely used to study brain dysfunction in ALS, especially damage to upper motor neurons, which is hard to measure clinically [36]. Studies consistently show that 4ALS disrupts normal brain rhythms, producing distinct patterns. Dukic et al. ana-lyzed resting-state EEG and found four distinct ALS sub-types based on brainwave and connectivity patterns [6], suggesting EEG can capture the disease’s variability and support more personalized diagnosis. Chmiel et al. reviewed multiple studies and found that reduced alpha-wave power is the most consistent EEG marker of ALS [37], widely seen as a sign of brain hyperexcitability and motor neuron damage [38]. Beyond simple brainwave power, researchers have also studied brain connectivity during motor and speech tasks using related electrophysiological modalities. For example, Dash et al. showed that beta-band connectivity changes during speech tasks, measured via magnetoencephalography (MEG), could reliably separate ALS patients from healthy controls [39], complementing EEG-based spectral findings. These findings motivated ML and DL applications: Pancotti et al. used recurrent neural networks on longitu-dinal clinical data to predict how a patient’s condition would decline over time [40]. However, most of these methods rely on manually designed features or require large labeled datasets, limiting their generalization, which motivates architectures that can learn patient-independent representations without heavy manual feature engineering. 

2.2 Multimodal EEG–Eye Tracking Fusion in BCI Systems 

As gaze-controlled communication devices become common in ALS care, ET has become more than a cursor-control tool: eye movements themselves carry physio-logical information, and abnormal patterns, such as unstable fixation, appear across several neurodegenerative diseases. Peters et al. showed that ET remains functional even in late-stage ALS [30], making it practical to capture EEG and ET together using existing spelling devices without additional patient burden. Synchronized multimodal datasets, historically scarce, are now emerging: Ngo et al.’s EEGET-ALS dataset pro-vides synchronized 32-channel EEG and ET from ALS patients and controls during a spelling task [31] (the main dataset used here), while Guttmann-Flury et al. released a larger EEG–ET–video dataset across multiple BCI tasks [41]. Outside ALS, EEG–ET fusion consistently improves performance, in attention decoding [19], neuromarketing [42], emotion recognition [26], and multimodal biosignal-based mental-state detection, with related work confirming EEG’s sensitivity to visual processing [43]. Yet multi-modal fusion for ALS remains largely untested, and most hybrid BCI systems still rely on shallow fusion, concatenating features or averaging outputs, which misses the deeper brain–eye relationship and assumes more data than ALS research can provide. 

2.3 Siamese Neural Networks for EEG and Biosignal Learning 

A core challenge in ALS-based BCI research is the shortage of large labeled datasets: standard CNNs and Transformers need large training sets and tend to overfit on small, heterogeneous patient groups. SNNs offer an alternative: learning whether two samples belong to the same class by comparing their representations rather than classifying each one directly, allowing the network to learn patient-independent features from very little data. Beyond biosignals, hierarchical attention mechanisms have similarly strengthened Siamese architectures for real-time discrimination tasks such as visual tracking, underscoring the generality of the Siamese paradigm for similarity-based 5learning. More advanced designs have addressed multimodal and cross-subject tasks, dual-attention SNNs for human-robot collaboration [44], EEG-signature fusion for identity verification [45, 46], and therapy progress tracking in cerebral palsy [47]. SNNs also reduce the cross-subject "calibration bottleneck" in BCI systems. Despite this progress in sleep staging, seizure detection, and motor imagery, no prior work has applied SNNs to multimodal ALS detection, even though their ability to fuse different signal types under limited data makes them well suited for exactly this problem. 

2.4 Explainability in EEG and Biomedical Deep Learning 

Alongside the push for higher accuracy, a separate line of work has focused on mak-ing biomedical DL models interpretable, since clinicians are often unwilling to trust a prediction they cannot understand. SHAP has become one of the most widely used tools for this purpose, assigning each input feature a contribution score to the final prediction [34]. In EEG research, SHAP and similar attribution methods have iden-tified which channels, frequency bands, or time windows drive a model’s decision in tasks like seizure detection and sleep staging, helping confirm that models rely on physiologically meaningful signals rather than noise, while saliency- and attention-based methods have similarly highlighted important spatial and temporal regions in raw EEG. However, this kind of analysis remains rare in ALS research and virtually absent in multimodal EEG–ET work: most ALS studies reviewed above report accu-racy or AUROC without explaining which features or brain regions drove the output. This gap matters especially for ALS, where an incorrect or unexplained prediction carries real clinical consequences, motivating the embedding-level SHAP analysis used in this work. 

2.5 Deep EEG Models and Cross-Dataset Generalisation 

DL has reshaped EEG decoding: CNNs and, more recently, Transformers have largely replaced pipelines built on manually designed features. Altaheri et al. [48] showed temporal-spatial convolutions outperform earlier motor-imagery methods by learning features automatically from raw signals, and this was extended by fusing multi-scale convolutional branches with attention [49] and by combining channel attention with continuous-time temporal modeling for emotion recognition from raw multi-channel EEG [50]. The pattern holds elsewhere: deep models beat classical correlation analysis for SSVEP classification, Transformers capture long-range temporal patterns that standard convolutions miss, and hybrid CNN-LSTM [51] and graph-based models have modeled brain connectivity effectively [52], with related hybrid frameworks performing well in clinical applications such as neurological disorder detection [53]. Still, a major challenge remains: models trained in one lab often fail in another due to hardware, electrode, or population differences, the generalization gap. Transfer learning can help, but it still usually requires some labeled data from the new setting, limiting its true plug-and-play use, which is why single-dataset validation is no longer sufficient proof of clinical usefulness. Recent work instead learns representations that stay stable across subjects, via Euclidean alignment [54], contrastive learning, and contrastive predictive coding. # 3 METHOD 

ALS detection is formulated as a window-level binary classification problem. Each sample is a short, overlapping temporal window of multichannel EEG aligned with a corresponding ET segment, supporting both unimodal (EEG-only) and multimodal (EEG+ET) learning. The proposed pipeline consists of three main stages: (i) data preprocessing, cov-ering signal cleaning, epoching, ET feature extraction, and class balancing; (ii) a DL framework that encodes EEG and ET signals into modality-specific representa-tions; and (iii) development of Siam-ALS, a Siamese architecture that fuses these representations into a compact, subject-invariant embedding for explainable ALS classification. 

3.1 Data Preparation 

All EEG and ET recordings undergo a unified preprocessing pipeline tailored to the proposed window-based learning framework and multimodal architecture. Raw EEG signals are band-pass filtered to suppress low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise, re-referenced to a common average, and normalized on a per-channel basis to facilitate stable representation learning. ET signals, when available, are temporally aligned with EEG using event markers and resampled to ensure synchronization across modalities. Both EEG and ET streams are segmented into short, fixed-length overlapping windows, which constitute the fundamental input units of the proposed model. Each window inherits the subject-level diagnostic label (ALS or control), enabling weakly supervised learning at the window level. To prevent subject leakage and ensure reliable generalization, preprocessing, window extraction, and normalization are performed independently within each cross-validation fold using subject-level splits. Table 1 summarizes the two datasets used in this study, described in detail below. 

3.1.1 Data Sources and Sampling 

EEGET-ALS 

Our primary dataset, EEGET-ALS [31], contains synchronized 32-channel EEG and ET recordings from ALS patients and controls during a gaze-controlled spelling task. We use only full 32-channel sessions with valid ET streams, balance classes by downsampling, and split subjects at the subject level to avoid leakage. 7BigP3BCI 

As a secondary, EEG-only dataset [55], BigP3BCI provides P300-speller EEG record-ings from ALS and healthy participants, used for cross-dataset generalization. We infer ALS/Non-ALS labels from file metadata, exclude ambiguous cases, and build class-balanced, subject-level stratified folds as with EEGET-ALS. 

3.1.2 Data Processing 

EEG Preprocessing and Epoch Construction 

For both corpora, continuous EEG was band-pass filtered (0.5–40 Hz) with notch filtering to attenuate slow drifts, line noise, and high-frequency noise, re-referenced to a common average, and resampled to 128 Hz for EEGET-ALS and 124 Hz for BigP3BCI. In EEGET-ALS, signals were segmented into 32 × 128 epochs (channels 

× time samples) via a 1-s sliding window with 50% overlap, time-locked to spelling-related events; epochs below the target length or flagged as corrupted in the metadata were discarded. In BigP3BCI, all recordings were first restricted to a common channel subset present across the selected EDF files, and 1-s windows (124 samples) were then extracted with 50% overlap, excluding recordings too short to yield a complete win-dow. Each BigP3BCI window was zero-padded to 128 samples to match the encoder input (Section 4.8) and inherited its subject’s ALS/non-ALS label. For both datasets, channel-wise standardization was applied strictly within each cross-validation fold: mean and standard deviation were estimated on the training partition only and sub-sequently used to normalize the training, validation, and test epochs, preventing information leakage across splits. Fig. 1(a)–(b) illustrates this pipeline on a represen-tative 10-s window, showing raw EEG overlaid with synchronized gaze trajectories, followed by the corresponding band-pass filtered and resampled signal. 

Eye-Tracking Feature Extraction (EEGET-ALS Only) 

EEGET-ALS provides ET streams sampled at approximately 30 Hz, comprising gaze coordinates and per-sample validity flags. Each EEG epoch was temporally aligned with its corresponding ET segment and summarized into a nine-dimensional feature vector encoding gaze position, velocity, path length, and signal validity (Table 2). Consistent with the EEG pipeline, ET features were z -score normalized per fold using training-set statistics only, and epochs with insufficient or invalid ET coverage were excluded from analysis. Fig. 1(c) shows the resulting standardized horizontal and vertical gaze trajectories after removal of invalid samples. 

Class Balancing 

To mitigate bias arising from class imbalance, ALS and non-ALS windows were bal-anced by random downsampling of the majority class, applied independently within each fold after preprocessing and preserving the subject-level splits. Training, vali-dation, and test partitions are therefore class-balanced, yielding a window-level ALS prevalence of 50%. Consequently, prevalence-dependent metrics (PPV, NPV) reflect this balanced setting rather than clinical prevalence. 3.2 Proposed Siam-ALS Architecture 

Siam-ALS is a Siamese dual-branch network that learns a joint, subject-invariant embedding of EEG and ET signals through pairwise metric learning, rather than direct per-sample classification. As illustrated in Fig. 3, two weight-sharing branches, indexed by samples i and j, each process an input pair through identical modality-specific encoders and a shared fusion encoder, producing fused embeddings that are compared via a similarity head during training. This design is deliberately chosen for two reasons: (i) pairwise supervision increases the effective number of training instances available from a small cohort, mitigating overfitting under scarce ALS data, and (ii) constraining both branches to identical weights enforces a single, subject-invariant mapping ϕ(·), rather than allowing the network to memorize subject-specific shortcuts. 9Fig. 1 : Multimodal synchronization and preprocessing of EEG and ET signals over a 10-second interval. 3.2.1 Modality-Specific Encoders 

Each branch independently encodes its EEG and ET inputs prior to fusion. The EEG encoder follows an EEGNet-style design, combining standard and depthwise convolutions with batch normalization, average pooling, and dropout to capture spa-tiotemporal cortical patterns while remaining parameter-efficient. The ET encoder is a lightweight two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with an intermediate dropout layer; it is intentionally shallower than the EEG encoder, reflecting the lower dimen-sionality and intrinsic simplicity of the nine-dimensional ET feature vector relative to raw multichannel EEG. 3.3 Explainability Framework 

Although Siam-ALS provides strong predictive performance, accuracy alone does not reveal why the model makes a particular decision or whether the learned pat-terns correspond to meaningful ALS-related characteristics. Therefore, we develop an explainability framework for the frozen-trained model to investigate the features con-tributing to ALS prediction. The framework consists of two stages: first, a systematic comparison of six XAI methods at the fused-embedding level; and second, a signal-level analysis to trace important learned representations back to their corresponding physiological signals. 

Embedding-Level Explainability. The explainability analysis is performed on the 64-dimensional fused embedding zi generated by the Siam-ALS fusion encoder. This compact representation enables efficient interpretation of the learned multimodal fea-tures compared with directly analyzing high-dimensional raw EEG and ET inputs. Since the final classifier operates on this embedding space, explanations are gener-ated with respect to the embedding-level prediction, allowing the contribution of each latent dimension to be quantified. 

Comparison of XAI Methods. To evaluate different explanation strategies, we inves-tigate six widely used XAI methods, including SHAP, LIME, Integrated Gradients, Saliency, Occlusion, and Permutation Importance. Each method is used to rank the 64 embedding dimensions by their contribution to the model’s output. The reliability of each explanation method is evaluated using fidelity, defined as the AUROC retained when a new classifier is trained using only the top-ranked embedding dimensions iden-tified by the corresponding XAI method. This evaluation measures how effectively each method captures the most informative features learned by Siam-ALS. 

Feature Attribution and Physiological Mapping. After identifying the most influ-ential embedding dimensions, further analysis is performed to investigate their relationship with the original physiological signals. Embedding-level attribution indi-cates which learned multimodal representations contribute to ALS predictions, while gradient-based saliency analysis traces individual embedding dimensions back to the original EEG input space. This produces channel- and temporal-importance maps that highlight the EEG regions contributing to the learned features. 

Two-Level Interpretation Strategy. The proposed explainability framework pro-vides interpretation at two complementary levels. The first level identifies important latent features within the learned EEG–ET representation, while the second level con-nects these features to the underlying physiological signals. This two-level strategy allows the model predictions to be examined beyond accuracy and provides insights 13 into whether Siam-ALS relies on meaningful EEG and ET patterns associated with ALS-related changes rather than dataset-specific artifacts. 

3.4 Baseline Model Families. 

To rigorously contextualize Siam-ALS, we benchmark it against a broad set of base-lines spanning classical ML, deep sequence models, tree ensembles, and non-Siamese multimodal fusion, all trained and evaluated under the identical preprocessing, cross-validation, and evaluation protocol described in Section 3.1.

4.2 Dataset Statistics and Splits 

In this study, we use two complementary public datasets, EEGET-ALS [31] and BigP3BCI [55]. The EEGET-ALS corpus provides synchronized EEG and ET record-ings from ALS and control participants performing a gaze-assisted spelling task. The BigP3BCI corpus contains raw EEG data from ALS and non-ALS subjects who par-ticipated in a P300-based spelling paradigm. We exclude subjects with incomplete or unusable recordings. For both datasets, EEG is segmented into 1-s windows with 50% overlap; for EEGET-ALS, windows are extracted within the labelled task inter-vals (Thinking, Acting, Typing, and Resting). In EEGET-ALS, each EEG window is paired with a 9-dimensional ET feature vector that summarizes gaze position, veloc-ity, path length, and data validity. All experiments use subject-level cross-validation to avoid leakage: in EEGET-ALS, subjects are partitioned into four folds with approx-imately balanced class proportions, and in each run, two folds are used for training, one for validation, and one for testing. All windows from a given subject appear in exactly one split. For BigP3BCI, we apply the same 4-fold subject-level protocol to a balanced subset of all 48 ALS subjects and 48 non-ALS subjects selected from the 279 available [SELECTION METHOD, e.g., at random with a fixed seed], used exclu-sively for the independent cross-dataset generalization experiments in Section 4.8. The EEG encoder is trained from scratch on BigP3BCI using the identical architecture validated on EEGET-ALS, with no shared weights, checkpoints, or pretraining trans-fer between the two datasets. 4.3 Leave-One-Subject-Out Validation 

To address the limited number of ALS subjects ( n=6) in EEGET-ALS, we addition-ally report leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation, where each of the six ALS subjects is held out as the sole test-set ALS subject in turn, with all other ALS sub-jects and a class-balanced set of control subjects used for training. This yields six independent test folds, each anchored to a single, distinct patient, directly exposing between-subject variance rather than aggregating it into a single 4-fold summary as in Section 4.2. Table 6 reports per-subject AUROC, accuracy, and F1 for the full Siam-ALS model under this protocol. Performance remains high for five of the six ALS subjects (AUROC 0.981–0.996), with a mean AUROC of 0.983 ± 0.015 across all six. This is lower than the 4-fold estimate in Table 7 (AUROC 0.998), even though LOSO trains on more ALS subjects (five, versus roughly three under the 4-fold protocol, in which two of four folds are used for training). The difference therefore does not stem from reduced training data. It more plausibly reflects how the test set is formed: each LOSO fold is evaluated on a single ALS patient, so subject-specific recording char-acteristics cannot be averaged out by other ALS subjects in the same test fold, and the per-fold estimate directly reflects individual patient variability. The two protocols thus answer different questions and are not directly comparable. The 4-fold result estimates pooled window-level performance, whereas LOSO shows how performance varies from patient to patient. Subject S4 shows the lowest AUROC (0.954), which we attribute to a shorter, noisier ET calibration session for this participant. Excluding S4, the mean AUROC across the remaining five subjects is 0.988 ± 0.006 , which narrows but does not close the gap to the 4-fold estimate. Because the drop is concentrated in a single subject rather than spread across all folds, the 4-fold results do not appear to depend on one favorable train/test partition. The S4 result does, however, indicate sensitivity to session quality, which we identify as a limitation for future data collection.

4.8 Cross-Dataset Generalization on BigP3BCI dataset 

To assess whether the learned representation generalizes beyond a single acquisition setup, we evaluate EEG-only Siamese variants (G1–G3, Table 11) on the independent BigP3BCI corpus, which differs from EEGET-ALS in recording hardware, stimulus paradigm (P300 speller vs. gaze-controlled speller), and patient population. The iden-tical EEG encoder architecture is used without modification (32 channels, matching the EEGET-ALS input); because BigP3BCI windows (124 samples at 124 Hz) are four samples shorter than the 128-sample input the encoder expects, each window is zero-padded to 128 samples prior to encoding. Trained from scratch on BigP3BCI, the base Siamese model (G1) reaches an AUROC of 0.954 and a window-level accu-racy of 0.890, showing that the architecture remains effective on a second dataset with a different paradigm and population. Adding the embedding-level logistic-regression classifier (G2) raises AUROC to 0.983 and accuracy to 0.937, with sensitivity and specificity improving from 0.882/0.897 to 0.931/0.942. A deeper projection head (G3) yields only a marginal further gain (AUROC 0.984, accuracy 0.940, sensitivity 0.935, specificity 0.945). 4.9 Explainability Analysis 

As shown in Sections 4.5–4.7, all twelve baseline models spanning classical ML, DL, and multimodal fusion converge to near-ceiling AUROC (0.988–0.998), and none of the seven directly tested baselines differs significantly from Siam-ALS after multiple-comparison correction, with three of them (EEGNet+ET Late Fusion, CNN, and EEGNet+ET Middle Fusion) not significant even at the uncorrected level (Table 10). None of these twelve baselines, however, provide any mechanism to explain why a given sample is classified as ALS. This section demonstrates, through six indepen-dent attribution methods, including SHAP, LIME, Integrated Gradients, Saliency, Occlusion, and Permutation Importance, applied identically to the frozen embedding-level classifier, two complementary levels of explanation (embedding and signal), and a direct structural comparison against all twelve baselines, that the primary advan-tage of Siam-ALS is not marginal accuracy improvement but that its architecture is the only one in this comparison that supports rigorous, validated, and clinically ver-ifiable explainability. Table 13 quantifies this directly, contrasting the exact Shapley attribution cost across representations and showing that only Siam-ALS’s compact 64-dimensional embedding admits tractable, exact attribution.

# 5 Conclusion 

This study introduced Siam-ALS, an explainable Siamese DL framework that jointly learns from EEG and ET signals for early ALS detection. By integrating comple-mentary information from EEG-derived neural activity and ET-derived oculomotor behavior, Siam-ALS achieved strong performance on the EEGET-ALS dataset, reach-ing 98.8% accuracy and an AUROC of 0.998 under subject-level evaluation. The framework demonstrated competitive performance against a wide range of classical ML and DL models while providing interpretable insights into ALS-related patterns. Beyond accuracy, this study highlights the role of explainability in medical AI. The six XAI methods demonstrated that the learned 64-dimensional embedding captures 30 meaningful ALS-related patterns, with SHAP achieving the highest, though statisti-cally comparable, fidelity in explaining the EEG and ET features that influenced the model’s predictions. Ablation studies showed that multimodal fusion with a dedicated fusion encoder improves on EEG-only and concatenation variants, while cross-dataset evaluation on BigP3BCI demonstrated the potential of the EEG branch to generalize across different BCI settings. However, this study is limited by the small number of ALS participants in EEGET-ALS, which may affect statistical power and generalization. Future work will focus on larger multi-center datasets and additional BCI paradigms to further validate the framework. Overall, Siam-ALS provides an interpretable and multimodal approach toward reliable AI-based ALS detection.
