# Neuromorphic Decoding and Dynamical Image Reconstruction from Populations of Spiking Retinal Ganglion Cells 

## Ning Zhang ∗ and Arto Nurmikko †

School of Engineering, Brown University, Providence, RI 02912 USA 

Keywords: neural decoding; spiking neural network; retinal ganglion cells; neuromorphic computing; dynamic vision reconstruction; object tracking; energy efficiency; vision restoration. 

Abstract 

Objective. Decoding visual stimuli from the spiking activity of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) informs both our understanding of the retinal code and the design of visual prostheses. Natural vision is dynamic, yet state-of-the-art decoders mostly reconstruct single frames with artificial neural networks that process dense, continuous-valued signals rather than the retina’s sparse, asynchronous spikes. We asked whether dynamic scenes can be reconstructed and tracked entirely within the spike domain, and at what computational cost. 

Approach. We built an end-to-end spiking neural network (SNN) whose neurons integrate the recorded RGC spikes over time. Populations of up to 1836 RGCs were recorded ex vivo from mouse retina on high-density microelectrode arrays while flashed images, moving bars, drifting gratings and randomly moving handwritten digits were presented. For moving digits, one shared spiking encoder fed two readouts, one reconstructing the image and one decoding the digit’s position. Compute energy was estimated from counted synaptic operations. 

Main results. The SNN decoded what and where at once. It reconstructed the stimuli with a structural similarity (SSIM) of up to 0.93, refining the reconstruction as spikes accumulated, and tracked a randomly moving target with a mean error of 1.33 pixels on a 32 × 32 grid. Its fidelity nearly matched that of the best of 17 larger spiking and artificial decoders (5.9 million to 1.7 billion parameters, versus 5.4 million), and at a mean firing rate of 14.2% it needed an estimated 

∼70-fold less compute energy than a dense artificial network of the same architecture. 

Significance. Dynamic visual scenes can be decoded from retinal population activity in the spike domain, with a compact network and at a compute cost compatible with implantable and wearable neural interfaces, supporting spike-native decoders for visual prostheses. 

# 1 Introduction 

The visual system is an existence proof that spiking, event-driven computation can transform incident light into perception at remarkable energy efficiency and low latency. In the vertebrate eye, the retina maps and compresses a photoreceptor image into parallel spike trains computed by a network of some ∼40 functionally distinct retinal ganglion cell (RGC) types [1, 2]. Reconstructing the visual stimulus directly from recorded spiking activities of a large enough RGC subset is a dual scientific and engineering opportunity. First, a successful computational decoding model can improve our understanding of how the retina transforms and compresses visual information prior to

Second, a successful computational paradigm can help advance efforts to restore vision by guiding the stimulation strategies of next-generation retinal prostheses [3, 4]. Two properties of biological vision make the RGC population decoding problem well suited to a direct neuromorphic computational approach. First, natural vision is overwhelmingly dynamic :the retinal image changes continuously, with eye movements and with motion in the scene itself. A useful decoder must be able to generate a time-varying reconstruction of a time-varying scene rather than reproduce a single frozen snapshot. Second, the retinal code is fundamentally sparse and asynchronous : information is carried by discrete spikes across a heterogeneous cellular population, not by dense, clocked, continuous-valued activations. A decoder that performs two simultaneous functions on retinal population data (reconstructing a visual target and tracking its motion) while running entirely in the spike domain represents, we suggest, a more direct reflection of the underlying biology, and it offers potential savings in computational energy. Currently, the dominant paradigm for neural decoding of RGC population signals is an artificial neural network (ANN) model operating on synchronous, continuous-valued signals. Linear, Bayesian and nonlinear decoders have shown that natural images can be recovered from RGC populations [4–6], and deep networks, from the Spike-Image Decoder to diffusion-prior systems developed for functional imaging, have pushed reconstruction quality substantially higher [7–11]. These decoders can indeed achieve high fidelity, but in our view they are not naturally matched to the biological substrate in ways that matter for energy-efficient neural engineering. First, such decoders are largely energy-blind :they are trained, and their outcomes usually reported, without regard to the computing cost, which matters in any implantable or wearable interface [12–14]. Second, they are representationally mismatched : the ANNs process dense analog tensors rather than the sparse asynchronous spikes that carry information in the nervous system. This mismatch can limit both biological interpretability and downstream deployment on future event-driven neuromorphic hardware [15, 16]. Third, and perhaps most consequential in the inherently nonstationary moving visual world, they have largely addressed only single, non-moving images. Even powerful linear decoders, which reconstruct the stimulus as a weighted sum of the population’s spike counts using one fixed spatial filter per cell, recover images from primate RGC data that are “more similar to the smoothed image than to the original image” [6]: fine detail is already lost before the target begins to move. Once the target moves, the degradation compounds: with one fixed spatial filter per cell and no mechanism to integrate evidence over time, the recovered image is further blurred by the target’s own motion. The dimension that matters most for natural vision is thus the one such decoders handle least well. In this paper we address these issues by introducing a novel, adaptable, biomimetic decoder built on a spiking neural network (SNN). The model is applied to multielectrode recordings from ex vivo mouse retina with data from up to 1836 spiking RGCs (ON, OFF and ON–OFF cells). Our model is capable of dynamical image reconstruction and simultaneous target tracking; it refines the reconstruction through its own temporal dynamics, and it does so efficiently. By way of background, directly trained deep SNNs have recently become computationally practical through surrogate-gradient learning [17–19] and spiking transformers now approach ANN accuracy [20], with spike-driven variants retaining sparse, addition-only computation [21, 22]. Work on spiking image reconstruction and tracking, however, has centered primarily on robotic vision applications using data from event-driven optical cameras [23, 24], where computing efficiency is typically assessed post hoc. We have recently applied an SNN model to reconstruct and track moving targets from event-camera streams when imaging through optically dense scattering media [25]. By contrast, the substrate in this paper is the biological retina. Its RGC spiking populations, recorded by dense microelectrode arrays, are receptive-field mapped and separated by contrast polarity, and they carry the trial-to-trial variability inherent to living tissue, as opposed to the deterministic 2protocol of man-made cameras. The end-to-end spiking decoder introduced in this paper is a compact spiking network algorithm capable of reconstructing images of a dynamical target while tracking its position with the same architecture. Its computational strategy consumes a fraction of the energy of an equivalent ANN model, a direct reflection, we believe, of the benefit of the SNN model’s mimicking the computational principles of the retina itself. The overall flow of the ‘spike-native’ SNN approach is shown schematically in Fig. 1. We show below (1) how the approach has allowed us to demonstrate what is, to our knowledge, the first end-to-end spiking neural population decoder that reconstructs dynamic, moving visual targets, as well as flashed images, from real RGC population spikes. In a way that seems similar to how the brain decodes and interprets retinal data, the reconstruction is seen to sharpen in real time through temporal integration in the spike-native , high-fidelity, compact network model. We also show (2) how the SNN engine, as a single shared spiking encoder, tracks the moving target’s position using a learned spiking readout that outperforms a calibrated population-vector (center-of-mass) readout [26] roughly two-fold, showing that spiking-based computation extracts position information that a classical readout leaves behind; (3) that the SNN engine, being spike-native, is energy efficient ( ∼70 × lower compute energy than the ANN-equivalent); and (4) we suggest that the model can be compatible with emerging neuromorphic hardware [15, 16]. While this work focuses on decoding retinal cellular populations, the approach can potentially extend to the large-scale decoding of neural data required by brain-computer interfaces that deploy dense microelectrode arrays and probes [27–30] or distributed microchip sensors [31]. The model’s energy efficiency suggests that it could scale to decoding cortical dynamics from thousands of neurons. 

# 2 Results 

All results are obtained with the spiking neural network model applied to spike trains of mouse RGCs recorded by a microelectrode array (MEA) (Fig. 2a and Methods). High-quality single units were identified through spike sorting and quality control (pairwise correlations in Fig. 2b), and each unit was annotated with receptive-field location information for the ON, OFF and ON–OFF principal cell types (Fig. 2c). We map per-cell firing rates onto a 32 × 32 grid at each cell’s receptive-field (RF) center using Gaussian kernels (Fig. 2d), and separate cells into ON, OFF and ON–OFF channels, giving an input tensor of shape [ T × C × 32 × 32 ] (Methods). The SNN decoder module is based on a U-Net-like architecture [32] (Fig. 3) built from leaky or non-leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF/IF) neurons. Its encoder processes the input along its own native time axis, so that evidence about the stimulus accumulates across time bins rather than being pooled in a preprocessing step. Two modules read the same shared features: an SNN reconstruction module that outputs the image, and an SNN tracking module that outputs the target’s ( x, y ) position. We evaluated two recording regimes: (a) recordings from 1836 spiking RGCs responding to 4000 flashed natural and synthetic images and (b) recordings from 1206 RGCs responding to 3000 clips of randomly moving handwritten digits, moving bars, and drifting gratings (bars and gratings from a separate recording of 760 RGCs). All reported numbers are computed on held-out data (Methods). 

## 2.1 Image reconstruction – flashed stimulus 

The end-to-end SNN engine was applied to reconstruct flashed stimuli (each image projected as a 400 ms flash followed by 400 ms of darkness) from the full recorded population of 1836 RGCs. The reconstructions reach reasonably high fidelity in structural similarity: masked-SSIM ∼0.81 on natural images and ∼0.88 on synthetic patterns (Fig. 4). ## 2.2 Reconstruction fidelity versus model size 

To place the image reconstruction results in context, we benchmarked a suite of state-of-the-art reconstruction architectures applied to the same held-out 1836-cell natural-image decoding task: spiking decoders (Meta-SpikeFormer [22], QKFormer [35], and STAtten, a spatio-temporal-attention spiking transformer [36]) and state-of-the-art artificial restoration and decoder networks (SwinIR [37], Restormer [38], Real-ESRGAN [39], NAFNet [40], and the Spike-Image Decoder family [7]), together with ten further spiking and artificial decoders, refiners and generative models (Fig. 5a). Each decoder was fed the identical receptive-field-mapped RGC input and scored with masked-SSIM. Table 1 lists performance against model size for our model, the three spiking decoders and four of the artificial networks, and Fig. 5a plots it for all models. Two observations can be made. First, reconstruction fidelity appears to “saturate”: across 18 ANN and SNN architectures spanning more than two orders of magnitude in size, the masked-SSIM values fall in a narrow range, suggesting that the fidelity is limited by the information conveyed in the neural data rather than by a given model’s capacity. This is consistent with the flat spread we observe across attention variants, and with the loss of fine detail reported for linear decoding of primate RGC populations [6]. ## 2.3 Image reconstruction – moving objects, simple geometry 

As the first test of our SNN model on dynamical scenes, we asked the spike-integrating encoder to reconstruct simple classic patterns of moving geometry [41, 42]. The model achieved high fidelity: SSIM 0.93 for moving bars (four held-out trials, windows with the bar inside the recorded patch) and 0.80 for drifting gratings during the drift (0.86 including the blank interval after each drift; Fig. 6). Fidelity is independent of motion direction but falls with the gratings’ temporal frequency, from 0.93 at 1 Hz to 0.51 at 15 Hz (Fig. 6d, Video S1). The reconstruction quality for these simple patterns, which move along predictable, deterministic linear paths, showed that the encoder’s temporal integration was able to follow a continuously changing visual scene, extending the results for the spatially static, temporally flashed images of Sec. 2.1. 

## 2.4 Image reconstruction – randomly moving targets 

We then applied the model to the harder case of randomly moving visual targets, here handwritten MNIST digits [43]. The objective was to reconstruct a target that moves unpredictably across the visual field, a regime that single-frame decoders cannot address. With video clips of randomly moving handwritten digits projected onto the ex vivo mouse retina, our SNN model reached a reconstruction fidelity of ∼0.80 SSIM, and the same shared-encoder model also tracked the target. The reconstructions are consistent across the held-out test set (Fig. 8), as summarized per digit class in Fig. 7e. This is the main result of this paper. The reconstruction is refined over time by the network’s own dynamics : at early timesteps the estimate is diffuse, and the moving digit emerges as the membrane potentials of the spiking network accumulate evidence during motion, with no explicit re-centering of the target (Fig. 7a–c, Video S2). ## 2.5 Object tracking 

The SNN tracking module, which recovers the target’s position, is embedded in the same shared encoder that reconstructs images of randomly moving targets (here handwritten digits). We asked how the model performs with reduced, sparse information. Figure 9c plots the performance of the tracking module against different levels of pixel content. A simple spike-count centroid readout reaches a reasonable mean position error of 2.14 pixels (px). As an alternative, a full-resolution spiking regression readout using the entire feature map lowers the error to 1.33 px on held-out clips (Fig. 9a,b), well below the calibrated center-of-mass baseline ( 2.91 px ) and well below chance (predict-center, 9.10 px ). Over the course of a clip the regression readout’s error is below that of the COM baseline except for a brief transient about 250 ms after motion onset; from ∼570 ms into the clip it stays below the baseline for the rest of the motion (1.2 px versus 3.2 px averaged over 700–1850 ms; Fig. 9d). The calibrated center-of-mass is a linear readout of the population centroid. That a learned spiking readout halves its error shows that the spiking-based computation accesses position information that the classical readout does not capture. Image reconstruction and object tracking are achieved by the same encoder module from the same spiking RGC population data; the tracking capability is thus a property of one system rather than of two separate SNN models. 

## 2.6 Energy efficiency 

From measured synaptic operations, our spike-driven decoder performs ∼5.0 × 10 9 SynOps per sample (based on the moving-digit reconstruction model), versus ∼6.8 × 10 10 dense MACs for the ANN-equivalent, giving ∼70 × lower compute energy at a 14.2% mean firing rate (Fig. 5b). As detailed in Methods (Sec. 4.6), this reduction in energy cost is the product of two terms: ∼13.6 ×

reduction from spike sparsity and ∼5.1 × reduction from the accumulate-versus-MAC cost convention (i.e. 13 .6 × 5.1 ≈ 70). This reduction applies to the actual computing cost only. The ∼5.1 × cost term is fixed by the 45 nm CMOS semiconductor process node convention. The sparsity term, and hence the overall energy cost per sample, depends on the number of spike-driven synaptic operations, which the training objective can control. Exposing the firing rate as a differentiable penalty term (Sec. 4.6) and training with λ = 0 .1 removes 21% of the synaptic operations and raises the ratio to ∼88 ×, at a cost in reconstruction fidelity (validation SSIM 0.80 → 0.73; Fig. 5c). The penalty lowers firing where fan-out is largest, in the reconstruction decoder, and raises it in the small tracking head, so the mean firing rate is unchanged even as synaptic operations fall (Fig. 5d). The spike count, not the mean rate, is therefore the relevant knob. Video S4 shows this for one test clip, layer by layer: the spiking activity of each layer group in the λ = 0 and λ = 0 .1 models, and the compute energy accumulating over the clip.

14 3 Discussion 

The main goal in our work was to develop a neural decoding model, entirely in the spiking domain, especially for the purpose of reconstructing images of dynamical visual targets, recorded ex vivo from a sizable population of mouse retinal ganglion cells (up to 1836 cells), and to do so computationally efficiently. An SNN model was built in which a single spike-native encoder (one architecture, trained separately for each stimulus set) reconstructs dynamic targets (flashed images, moving bars and drifting gratings, and randomly moving targets). In the case of spatially stationary, flashed images we show high-fidelity reconstruction across a range of natural and synthetic images. The case of reconstruction and tracking of randomly moving targets (here simple handwritten digits moving in the x–y plane) represents a particular challenge to any neural decoder. In our one-module SNN model the reconstruction is temporally refined by computational integration while the model simultaneously tracks the target movement. This is accomplished at a fraction of the ANN-equivalent computational energy. The two experimental target scenarios, flashed stationary targets and randomly moving targets, stress the SNN model in different ways. The flashed stimuli are spatially rich, comprising natural scenes and synthetic patterns reconstructed at up to 512 px resolution. The moving targets in this paper are spatially simpler 32 × 32 digits; however, because their position is unknown and changes randomly, the SNN must integrate evidence over time to recover them. Reconstructing a target as it moves , while sharpening the image as evidence accrues, is something a spike-native, temporally integrating network can do automatically, whereas a single-frame decoder fundamentally cannot. By contrast, recovering fine spatial structure from a briefly flashed, spatially constant natural scene poses a different problem, one that single-frame decoders can also meet, albeit at higher computational energy as shown above. Our SNN decoder offers three advantages for a future visual neural interface. First, the same decoder architecture is able to reconstruct moving as well as spatially stationary flashed stimuli recorded from large retinal spiking neural populations at high fidelity (masked-SSIM ∼0.81 natural, 

∼0.88 synthetic), and does so as a compact SNN ( ∼5.4M parameters). Second, the same encoder is capable of tracking a moving target, approximately halving the error of a calibrated center-of-mass baseline model. Third, the decoder is computationally efficient : it uses some 70-fold less compute energy than an ANN-equivalent, with spiking activity as a trainable ‘knob’. These capabilities can be understood by analogy: the decoder is effectively a biomimetic computer that mimics the operating principles of the biological retina, using sparse, asynchronous spikes as the visual pathway does from the retina to image formation and perception in the brain. The efficiency and event-driven structure of the decoder in this paper could suggest how a practical visual neural interface might be built in hardware. Because of the spike sparsity and addition-only synaptic operations, the network should be able to map efficiently onto asynchronous neuromorphic processors such as Intel Loihi and SynSense Speck-class event-driven systems-on-chip [15, 16] and other emerging silicon hardware whose energy scales with spiking activity rather than being locked to a global clock. On such hardware the roughly 14-fold estimated reduction in synaptic operations would translate into a comparable reduction in switching activity. The always-on, low-latency operation these devices provide also matches the demands of real-time, mobile, wearable visual prostheses where the compute-energy budget, not necessarily an algorithm’s accuracy, can be the decisive consideration. Admittedly, we have focused on only one (critical) piece of a complete on-chip RGC prosthesis pipeline; mapping the full model to a specific device and measuring its wall-plug power and latency is a substantial engineering effort in its own right. The ‘energy advantage’ in this paper includes several elements. At a 14.2% mean firing rate the decoder uses ∼70 × less compute energy than the ANN-equivalent, as shown in Fig. 5b, but 15 this factor is activity dependent . The overall efficiency can be further factored into two parts of different robustness: a ∼14 × reduction in operation count from spike sparsity, which is independent of the silicon hardware process node, and a ∼5× reduction in per-operation cost (accumulate versus multiply–accumulate) under a 45 nm CMOS process convention. The ∼14 × sparsity term is thus the conservative, node-independent core of the claim. As noted, the calculations in this paper are for compute-only costs. On real hardware, processes such as memory transfer, which per-operation accounting shows can dominate the energy budget [12], will reduce the realized energy advantage, and our estimates do not speak to full system-level power consumption. This work has limitations. First, the results are bounded by the multielectrode ex vivo recordings; improving the image reconstruction fidelity would require richer or multi-session data rather than a larger decoder model. The dynamic stimuli in the form of moving handwritten digits are relatively simple, and how our model handles complex natural scenes containing multiple moving objects 

remains an open question and a direction for future work. The computing energy estimates in this paper do not sweep the full accuracy–energy Pareto front. We have also evaluated richer spiking neuron dynamics (adaptive-threshold neurons with spike-frequency adaptation), which appear not to improve reconstruction over non-adaptive integrate-and-fire units. This justifies the use of the simpler LIF/IF neurons and is consistent with a particular neuron model not being the bottleneck. Future work could pursue several directions: adding confidence-gated early exit, so that computation halts within a clip once the reconstruction and track have converged and fixed per-clip compute becomes adaptive per-sample compute; extending to multi-trial and multi-session ex vivo or in vivo recordings for calibrated uncertainty; and mapping the model onto emerging neuromorphic hardware to measure on-chip power and latency. 

# 4 Methods 

## 4.1 Experimental animals 

Animal care and experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [46] and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Medical College of Fudan University. The mice were raised and bred in controlled environmental conditions, including a room temperature of 23 ± 2°C, a relative humidity of 60-65%, and a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. The wild-type (C57BL/6J) mice were obtained from the Slac Laboratory Animal Co. in Shanghai, China. 

## 4.2 Benchtop retinal electrophysiology and data preparation 

Microelectrode array (MEA) recordings were conducted with the MaxOne HD-MEA system (Maxwell Biosystems, Switzerland). The HD-MEA is a CMOS-based recording platform comprising 26400 electrodes with an electrode pitch of 17.5 μm. Up to 1024 electrodes can be simultaneously selected for extracellular recording, enabling high-resolution mapping of neuronal activity across large cell populations [47]. Adult C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (1–2% at 0.5–1.0 L ·min -1 ). Enucleation was performed on one eye, which was quickly placed in oxygenated Ringer’s solution (in mM: NaCl 124, KCl 2.5, CaCl 2 2, MgCl 2 2, NaH 2PO 4 1.25, NaHCO 3 26, and glucose 22; pH 7.35; oxygenated with 95% O 2 and 5% CO 2). A small piece of retina (2 mm × 2 mm in size and 200 μmin thickness) was excised and placed on the MEA, with the inner limiting membrane in contact with the MEA, to record the activity of retinal ganglion cells. A chamber surrounding the MEA was perfused with oxygenated Ringer’s solution at a rate of 1 mL ·min -1 . Stimuli were delivered using a 405 nm DLP projector (PRO4500, Wintech, China). A stereomicroscope (SZ61, OLYMPUS, Japan) 16 was used to focus the stimuli onto a 2 mm × 2 mm region of the photoreceptor layer of the retina. A total of 4000 distinct images were presented across 5 trials, with each image displayed for 400 ms, followed by 400 ms of darkness. Data were acquired at a sampling rate of 20 kHz. Raw data were initially sorted into different units using SpyKING CIRCUS [48] and then manually reviewed. High-quality single units were scored and selected based on mean pairwise correlation coefficient across trials (threshold: 0.2). Finally, the units were averaged across the 5 trials. Receptive-field mapping was performed by dividing the stimulus area (2 mm × 2 mm) into a 20 × 20 grid of small squares, which were sequentially illuminated in a randomized order. Each square was presented for 500 ms, followed by 500 ms of darkness. Spatial response heatmaps were generated separately for the ON window (during the 500-ms presentation) and the OFF window (during the subsequent 500-ms dark period) to determine the receptive field and ON/OFF response type. 

## 4.3 Retinal recordings and datasets 

All decoder inputs are population spike trains of mouse RGCs recorded on multielectrode arrays. The flashed-image regime comprises 1836 recorded RGCs responding to 4000 flashed images (2000 synthetic and 2000 natural), binned at 10 ms over an 80-bin (0 .8 s) response window (400 ms of image presentation followed by 400 ms of blank), with six repeat trials per image. The dynamic regime comprises 1206 RGCs responding to 3000 clips of randomly moving handwritten digits, binned at 10 ms over a 200-bin (2 s) window; each clip presents a single digit executing an independent, 

completely random x–y trajectory (1.6 s of motion followed by 0.4 s of blank), so the target moves unpredictably across the field. From these spikes the network jointly decodes two things: it tracks 

the digit’s time-varying position and, from the same shared encoder, reconstructs the target. We also decode other dynamic stimuli: eight-direction moving bars and 40 drifting gratings (a separate recording of 760 RGCs). Both are decoded from a causal sliding window over the recording, 10 bins (100 ms) long for the bars and 3 bins (30 ms) for the gratings. Each of these recordings comprises ten repeat trials; for the bars, five trials were used for training, one for validation and four were held out for testing, and the reported bar metrics are computed over windows in which the bar lies inside the recorded 32 × 32 patch. Held-out evaluation uses a fixed random split, and every reported number is computed on data disjoint from training. 

## 4.4 From spikes to a spatial input tensor 

A recorded population is an unordered set of cells at irregular positions on the retina, whereas a convolutional network requires its input on a regular spatial grid. How that mismatch is bridged determines what spatial structure the network is able to exploit at all: an arbitrary ordering of cells into channels would discard the retinotopic organization that the population already carries. Because RGCs tile the retina, each cell’s spikes report activity at a specific visual-field location. We therefore place each cell’s firing rate at its measured receptive-field center and spread it with an isotropic two-dimensional Gaussian kernel of standard deviation 0 .5 grid pixels ( Gaussian splatting [49]). Each kernel is normalized to unit mass per neuron, so every cell contributes equally regardless of where its center falls relative to the pixel lattice (Fig. 1c). Where receptive fields overlap, contributions are summed rather than averaged, so that neighboring cells reinforce one another and sub-pixel differences in receptive-field position are preserved instead of being collapsed onto the nearest grid point (a hard snap; compare the two panels of Fig. 2d). This yields an input tensor of shape [ T × C × 32 × 32 ] where T is the number of time bins and C is the number of polarity channels. Describing retinal receptive fields as two-dimensional Gaussians is long established in retinal physiology [50, 51]; here that description is used in the opposite direction, as the rasterization 17 step that turns an irregularly sampled population into a decoder input. Cells are separated into ON, OFF, and ON–OFF channels ( C = 3) for the dynamic-digit recording, reflecting the retina’s parallel encoding of contrast polarity [44, 45]. Polarity labels were not available for the flashed-image recording, which was decoded without polarity separation. This receptive-field-mapped, polarity-separated representation preserves the retinotopic and functional structure of the population while presenting the network with a spatially patterned “spiking movie”. 

## 4.5 Spiking Neural Network 

4.5.1 Shared SNN encoder 

The core of the SNN is a spiking convolutional U-Net-like structure [32] (Fig. 3) built from leaky or non-leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF/IF) neurons, implemented in SpikingJelly [19] with a CUDA/cupy backend for training and evaluation. 384 channels and optional residual connections. Each stage widens the region of visual space to which a unit responds, so the deepest features summarize the field as a whole while the shallowest retain the retinotopic detail of the input. This division of labor matters because the input is a coarse and noisy 32 × 32 rate map, not a photographic image: the 4 × 4 bottleneck alone cannot support stroke-level reconstruction, and the skip connections that carry shallow features forward to the reconstruction module return that detail to the output. In depth the network is a feedforward feature hierarchy; in time it is a stateful integrator, because every unit carries its membrane potential from one time bin to the next. The same weights are applied at each of the T steps, so across a clip the computation is fixed and only the accumulated state changes. Evidence about the stimulus therefore accumulates as the clip proceeds, and for a moving target the reconstruction can sharpen without any explicit re-centering of the target on each frame. This motion invariance comes from the network’s temporal memory, not from a preprocessing step. Each unit emits a binary value, so graded stimulus intensity cannot be carried by activation amplitude as it would be in an artificial network; it must instead be represented by which units fire and at which time steps, distributed across the population and across the T bins. This is why the reconstruction is read from an accumulated membrane potential rather than from a spike count. In exchange, computation stays event-driven and sparse (Sec. 4.6), which suits the model to event-driven neuromorphic hardware and keeps the learned mapping in the same units the retina itself uses. Apart from the input stage, which reads the RF-mapped input (the optional attention front-end and the first convolution), every layer receives spikes (binary or, where a residual block adds its two spike branches, counts of at most two), so its synaptic operations are accumulates. The remaining analog operations decode the membranes of the non-firing readout neurons. 18 4.5.2 SNN reconstruction module 

The SNN reconstruction module mirrors the encoder with three nearest-neighbor upsampling stages, which keep the upsampled tensor a spike tensor, and skip connections that reintroduce fine spatial detail. Its output layer is a single high-threshold (5000) analog-membrane readout neuron whose accumulated membrane potential, passed through a sigmoid, is the reconstructed image [ 1 × H × H ] at the target resolution H (32 for the dynamic experiments; up to 512 for the flashed natural/synthetic experiments). Training minimizes a combined loss [52] L = LBCE + LSSIM + LIoU 

(equal weights), where LSSIM uses a window size of 3 (matching the small target structures) and 

LIoU encourages correct support. Optimization uses AdamW [53] at a base learning rate of 10 −3

with weight decay 10 −4 and a reduce-on-plateau schedule; mixed-precision (fp16) autocast is used throughout. The same end-to-end spiking model (trained separately for each dataset) is used for the flashed natural/synthetic and the dynamic reconstructions. The high-resolution flashed-image results are obtained by training it at the larger target resolution on the full 1836-cell population, with the four additional ×2 upsampling stages this requires interleaved with spiking convolutions.

4.5.4 Task coupling (ablation only) 

We optionally couple the two modules so that the tracked position modulates the reconstruction features, either by a soft Gaussian gate centered on the estimated position ( condition ) or by a grid-sample warp that re-registers features toward the center ( register ). The coupling is bidirectional 

for condition : the reconstruction loss back-propagates through the gate into the tracking head, so in principle the two tasks train one another. For register , the position estimate is detached from the warp grid, so the tracker still learns true position while the warp uses those coordinates. Coupling is evaluated purely as an ablation (Sec. 2.7) and is not part of the primary model; as reported there, it does not improve reconstruction, and we include it for completeness rather than as a contribution. 

4.5.5 Attention front-end and temporal-fusion readout 

Two further components sharpen reconstruction, and both are ablated in Sec. 2.7. First, a multi-dimensional attention front-end (MA-SNN [33]) re-weights activity in the input spike tensor before the encoder, jointly along the channel (ON/OFF/ON–OFF polarity), spatial, and temporal axes: the three axes along which retinal information is organized. This lets the otherwise purely local convolutional encoder exploit non-local spatial, cross-polarity, and temporal structure it would miss. Second, rather than reading the reconstruction from the output membrane at the final timestep alone, a temporal-fusion readout learns a softmax weighting over the per-timestep output membranes and reconstructs from their weighted sum. It is initialized, through a positional bias, to reproduce the last-timestep readout exactly, and then learns to redistribute weight toward the most informative moments of the clip. Because a randomly moving target is best resolved at different times during its trajectory, fusing evidence across time rather than sampling a single frame is the component that carries dynamic reconstruction past the last-timestep baseline (Sec. 2.7).

## 4.7 Metrics and evaluation protocol 

Reconstruction is quantified by the structural similarity index (SSIM), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and mean squared error (MSE); for the high-resolution flashed-image decodes we additionally report a foreground-masked SSIM (“masked-SSIM,” computed only over the object support to avoid rewarding the large black background) and the LPIPS perceptual distance [54] (a learned feature-space image-similarity metric; lower is better). SSIM is reported with a Gaussian window of 11 for the 512-px flashed-image decodes, following the standard convention [55], and with a window of 3 for the 32-px moving-stimulus reconstructions (bars, gratings and randomly moving digits), where the target occupies only a few pixels. The training loss uses a window of 3 throughout. The flashed natural and synthetic numbers are reported as masked-SSIM, the foreground-only convention appropriate to the large black background. Tracking is quantified by the mean Euclidean position error in 32-grid pixels over the valid response window. All evaluation is on held-out test clips. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We have presented an end-to-end spiking neural network that reconstructs visual stimuli directly from the population activity of retinal ganglion cells, and that tracks a moving target from the same 21 shared encoder. The model reconstructs flashed natural and synthetic images at masked-SSIM 0 .81 and 0 .88 from 1836 cells, and moving stimuli at SSIM 0 .93 for bars, 0 .80 for drifting gratings and 

∼0.80 for randomly moving digits. The reconstruction is refined over time as the spiking membranes accumulate evidence during motion. A learned spiking readout locates the moving target to 1 .33 px, halving the error of a calibrated center-of-mass baseline. Reconstruction fidelity is comparable across 18 architectures spanning more than two orders of magnitude in size, suggesting a data-limited rather than a capacity-limited regime. Our network reaches that fidelity with ∼5.4 M parameters and, being sparse and event-driven, at a small fraction of the compute energy of an equivalent dense network. Decoding dynamic vision in the spike domain therefore appears feasible at a computational cost compatible with the implantable and wearable interfaces for which such decoding is ultimately intended.
