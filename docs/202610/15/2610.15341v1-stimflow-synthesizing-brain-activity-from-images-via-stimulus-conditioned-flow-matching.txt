Abstract. The inherent variability of fMRI responses across trials en-courages a generative view of the stimulus-to-response mapping. Prior generative encoders often operate in compressed latent spaces and condi-tion predominantly on semantic features, which provide limited low-level cues for early visual cortex. We propose StimFlow , which synthesizes fMRI directly in native voxel space using conditional flow match-ing with a linear path. This design aligns training and inference dis-tributions, enabling competitive performance with a single sample and reducing reliance on averaging. To match the visual hierarchy, StimFlow combines structural DINOv2 features with semantic CLIP features, which prove complementary along the cortical processing stream. With a single sample, StimFlow achieves a per-voxel Pearson correlation of 

0.371 , matching baselines that average five draws. Generation in voxel space allows direct ROI analysis and concept localization without a de-coder. Furthermore, it recovers over half of full-data performance from just one hour of scans, supporting efficient cross-subject adaptation. 

Keywords: Conditional flow matching · fMRI synthesis · Brain encod-ing · Native-space modeling · Cross-subject generalization 

## 1 Introduction 

Understanding how the human brain encodes natural visual stimuli is a central goal in computational neuroscience and brain–computer interfaces (BCI) [10,23, 24]. Owing to physiological noise, cognitive-state fluctuations, and hemodynamic variability, the stimulus-to-response mapping is inherently stochastic [1]; even the more modest goal of approximating the conditional mean benefits from a generative formulation whose training and inference distributions are aligned, rather than from a deterministic regressor or a diffusion prior whose higher-curvature paths necessitate multi-sample averaging for stability. Recent generative encoders pursue this goal but share two limitations. First, they synthesize in a compressed, CLIP-aligned latent space and are evaluated mainly through a decoded image; because the signal is recovered through an arXiv:2610.15341v1 [cs.CV] 11 Oct 2026 2 B. V. Hung et al. 

autoencoder rather than generated in voxel space, fidelity at the voxel and ROI level remains largely unexamined. Second, they condition exclusively on language-supervised CLIP features [28], which favour high-level semantics and retain little of the low-level structure (edges, textures, spatial frequency) driving early visual areas (V1–V3), so a single semantic stream is poorly matched to the full functional hierarchy of the visual cortex. The two representative encoders also differ in how they reach the data dis-tribution: MindSimulator [22] places a diffusion prior over fMRI autoencoder latents and relies on multi-trial averaging and post-selection to obtain stable predictions, whereas SynBrain [2] uses a one-step mapper that is stable but acts as a deterministic predictor. We seek a formulation whose training and inference paths are aligned by construction, so that a single sample ( T =1 ) already yields a faithful estimate of the conditional mean without post-hoc averaging. We propose StimFlow , which addresses both axes. StimFlow casts fMRI synthesis as Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) [17,18] along a conditional linear path with independent coupling, generating directly in native voxel space with a 1D Diffusion Transformer (DiT-1D) [27]; because the training interpolation starts exactly at the Gaussian distribution inference begins from, training and inference are aligned by construction. To match the cortical hierarchy, it pairs a structural stream (DINOv2 [26]) with a semantic stream (CLIP), which we find functionally complementary: structural features lead in early-to-mid visual areas, semantic features in higher ventral and lateral streams. A shared trunk with lightweight per-subject adapters further enables few-shot adaptation to novel subjects. On the Natural Scenes Dataset [1], a single StimFlow sample reaches a per-voxel Pearson correlation of 0.371 , matching or exceeding baselines that average five draws at the voxel level. More importantly, because generation stays in voxel space, fidelity can be read directly per ROI without a separate decoder, and synthesized concept-selectivity maps align closely with the ground truth (r = 0 .94 , top-10% Dice = 0 .81 ), supporting data-driven cortical analysis. In summary, our contributions are: 

– We formulate fMRI synthesis as conditional flow matching in native voxel space , enabling direct per-ROI readout and concept localization with-out a decoder; a single sample matches or exceeds multi-sample baselines on NSD [1] at both the voxel and semantic level. 

– We show that CLIP-only conditioning is suboptimal : a structural stream (DINOv2) matches or exceeds CLIP alone, and the two are functionally com-plementary along the cortical hierarchy, motivating dual-stream condition-ing. 

– We adapt the DiT architecture to 1D voxel sequences and equip it with per-subject adapters , supporting few-shot adaptation to novel subjects. StimFlow: Stimulus-conditioned Flow Matching for fMRI Synthesis 3

## 2 Related Work 

Brain visual encoding. Visual encoding models predict voxel-level fMRI re-sponses from images and have long been used to probe the functional orga-nization of visual cortex [24]. Early work fit explicit feature spaces (Gabor-wavelet, population receptive-field models) by linear regression [10, 15, 23]; the deep-learning era replaced these with hierarchical CNN activations predicting ventral-stream responses [8, 9, 35], and recent encoders use self-supervised (DI-NOv2 [26]) or language-supervised (CLIP [28]) features. Yet these encoders re-main deterministic, mapping each image to a single response and thus unable to express the trial-to-trial variability intrinsic to fMRI [1]. 

Brain decoding and reconstruction. The inverse problem (reconstructing the seen image from fMRI) has progressed from early movie reconstruction [25] to diffusion-prior methods [29, 30, 34] that map fMRI into CLIP space and condi-tion an image generator, with MindEye2 enabling strong low-data cross-subject transfer. Decoding is complementary to our goal of synthesizing responses from stimuli; we reuse a frozen MindEye2 [30] only to decode synthesized responses and check their semantic content (Sec. 4). 

Generative fMRI synthesis. Closest to us, a small line of work models the stimulus-to-response mapping as a conditional distribution. MindSimulator [22] learns a diffusion prior over fMRI-autoencoder latents and SynBrain [2] a one-step semantic-to-neural VAE mapper; both operate in a compressed, CLIP-conditioned latent space, sharing the two limitations we target (no direct per-ROI readout, a semantic-only bias) and a stability trade-off: the diffusion prior requires multi-sample averaging for stable results, while the one-step mapper is stable but deterministic. StimFlow instead synthesizes in native voxel space with a complementary DINOv2 stream and sidesteps the trade-off via flow matching; we compare against both in Sec. 4. 

Visual representations and the semantic bias of CLIP. Which feature space best matches visual cortex remains open. CLIP’s language supervision makes it predictive of high-level, category-selective regions (FFA [14], PPA [6]) but discards low-level structure driving early areas V1–V3, whereas self-supervised transformers such as DINOv2 [5, 26] retain fine spatial structure; supervised and self-supervised models are known to correlate differently with cortical hi-erarchies [3, 11, 16]. This motivates the multi-stream conditioning we analyse per-ROI in Sec. 4.3, where the two representations prove functionally comple-mentary rather than interchangeable. 

Flow matching and diffusion transformers. Flow matching [17] regresses a ve-locity field onto a probability path; a conditional linear path with independent noise–data coupling yields straight conditional trajectories whose lower curva-ture allows stable integration with fewer ODE steps than typical diffusion sam-plers [18, 32]. The Diffusion Transformer (DiT) replaces the U-Net with a trans-4 B. V. Hung et al. 

former conditioned via AdaLN [7, 27]. Within neuroimaging, flow and rectified-flow models have so far been applied to medical image synthesis [36] and to brain-connectivity matrices on Riemannian manifolds [4], but to our knowledge not yet to stimulus-conditioned, voxel-space fMRI encoding. StimFlow adapts DiT to 1D fMRI sequences under conditional flow matching to model visual-to-neural mapping in native space, and is among the first to bring CFM to this setting. 

## 3 Method 

Two properties of fMRI data motivate our design. First, fMRI voxels are in-dexed by anatomical location rather than a regular 2D pixel grid, so flattening them into a 1D token sequence imposes far less artificial structure, making a 1D Transformer the appropriate backbone. Second, we want the training and inference distributions to match exactly: conditional flow matching achieves this because the interpolation starts at the same Gaussian distribution inference begins from, and the same ODE is used at both stages. In practice this train– inference alignment yields lower-curvature trajectories than a VP-SDE diffusion path [18], allowing stable integration with fewer steps.

3.3 Stimulus Conditioning 

The visual cortex spans a hierarchy from oriented edges in V1 to semantic cate-gories in areas such as the fusiform face area (FFA). A single CLIP embedding, as used by prior generative encoders, is biased toward the semantic end and underserves early areas, so StimFlow conditions on two complementary streams. 

Global semantic context (CLIP). A pooled 1280 -d embedding from a frozen SDXL-CLIP encoder, projected by a two-layer MLP and summed with the timestep embedding, forms the global signal c = et + eCLIP that modulates ev-ery transformer block through Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN). Because CLIP provides a single scene-level summary, AdaLN, which uniformly scales and shifts each layer’s activations, is a natural fit, setting the overall semantic context without attending to specific voxel positions. 

Structural context (DINOv2). We extract multi-layer grid features from a frozen DINOv2-ViT-G/14 backbone [5, 26]. Unlike CLIP, these self-supervised features retain spatial structure and fine-grained properties (texture, contour, part–whole relations), spanning low-level structure to emerging semantics across layers. Af-ter linear projection and RMSNorm, the token sequence enters each block via multi-head cross-attention, so each voxel patch attends to the most relevant spa-tial features. Cross-attention is preferred over concatenation or global pooling because the feature map is spatially structured and different cortical regions need different subsets of it, which a single global projection would collapse.

3.5 Multi-Subject Shared Trunk with Per-Subject Adapters 

To leverage multiple subjects in one model, StimFlow uses a shared–private 

design: all transformer blocks, timestep/CLIP embedders, and context projectors are shared, forming a common trunk that learns subject-invariant stimulus-to-velocity dynamics. The only per-subject parts are two lightweight adapters at the trunk boundaries: a patch embedder (Conv1d) at the input and a readout head (AdaLN + linear) at the output. Since all subjects are zero-padded to a uniform L = 2 14 = 16384 , the patch grid is identical regardless of voxel count; mini-batches are subject-homogeneous (each forward pass uses a single adapter pair) and a scalar subject index selects the adapters. This keeps the per-subject parameter count tiny while the shared trunk benefits from all subjects’ data, and, unlike methods that map different subjects’ fMRI into a common latent bottleneck, keeps synthesis in each subject’s native voxel space. 

3.6 ROI-Ordered Native Voxel Representation 

Before patchification, voxels are sorted once by functional ROI rather than ar-bitrary index (e.g. V1–V3, hV4, EBA, FFA, PPA), a static reordering that uses no ROI labels at inference. Because generation stays in native voxel space, per-ROI fidelity can be read directly off the synthesized signal by indexing the rel-evant voxels, without any region-specific decoder or re-mapping step (Secs. 4.3 and 4.4). 

3.7 Training and Inference 

Training. We optimise the flow-matching loss (Eq. (2)) with AdamW [21] ( β1 =0.9, β2 = 0 .95 , weight decay 0.01 ) using a cosine learning-rate schedule [20] from 

10 −4 to 10 −5 over 50 epochs, with 500 warm-up steps (see Fig. 1a). Gradients are clipped to unit norm, and all training is conducted in bf16 mixed precision. Effective batch size is 32 × 8 = 256 (via gradient accumulation). 

Inference. At test time, we solve the learned ODE from t = 0 (noise) to t = 1 

(data) with the Euler method using Nstep = 50 steps (see Fig. 1b); each noise sample x0 ∼ N (0, I) yields a different plausible response. We report a single-sample setting ( T =1 ) as our primary operating point and an optional multi-sample average ( T =N ) approximating the conditional mean. Unlike diffusion-prior encoders that rely on multi-trial averaging and post-selection, StimFlow already attains competitive results at T =1 (Tab. 1); averaging is a deliberate variance-reduction option, applied identically to every method for fairness. 

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Dataset. We use the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) [1], focusing on the four subjects (1, 2, 5, 7) who completed all scanning sessions. Following the standard protocol, each subject has roughly 9,000 unique training images and a shared set of 1,000 test images presented up to three times. We operate on z-scored single-trial betas restricted to the nsdgeneral ROI. 8 B. V. Hung et al. 

Baselines. We compare against two deterministic regression encoders (per-voxel ridge regression and a transformer encoder trained in our pipeline) and two recent generative fMRI synthesizers, MindSimulator [22] and SynBrain [2]. As MindSimulator has no public implementation, we report its published numbers; SynBrain and both regression baselines are run in our pipeline. Every method we run uses the same z-scored single-trial betas, the normalization standard in re-cent NSD work [22,30], and the identical per-voxel, across-image Pearson metric on the same test split. Re-running SynBrain under this standardized z-scoring yields lower voxel-level Pearson than its original paper, whose preprocessing re-tains the per-voxel mean. 

Metrics. We evaluate at two levels. Voxel-level metrics (MSE and Pearson cor-relation) are computed per voxel across the test images (the standard encoding metric) and averaged over trials; the Pearson correlation is used consistently in all tables and per-ROI analyses. Semantic-level metrics are obtained by decod-ing the synthesized fMRI with a frozen MindEye2 [30] model and comparing the decoded image to the stimulus (Inception, CLIP, EfficientNet, SwAV). 

Implementation. StimFlow and its ablation variants (backbone replacements, single-stream variants) are trained with the flow-matching objective and schedule of Sec. 3.7. SynBrain is reproduced from the authors’ released code with its original VAE + S2N objective and hyperparameters; MindSimulator numbers are from the published paper.

## 5 Conclusion 

Three findings emerge from our experiments. First, the primary practical bene-fit of the generative formulation is train–inference alignment : because the flow-matching path begins at the same Gaussian distribution used at test time, a sin-gle sample already matches or exceeds multi-sample baselines, reducing the need for post-hoc averaging or selection. The model does not yet capture meaningful trial-to-trial variability: averaging further improves every metric, indicating that the inter-sample variance is estimation noise rather than neural signal. Single-sample fidelity remains high precisely because training and inference share the same starting distribution. Second, generating in native voxel space, rather than in a compressed latent code, unlocks analyses that prior generative encoders cannot perform directly: per-ROI fidelity readout, concept-selective region lo-calization, and cross-subject adaptation via lightweight adapters, all without an auxiliary decoder. Third, the structural (DINOv2) and semantic (CLIP) condi-tioning streams show a consistent complementary trend along the cortical hier-archy ( ρ = −0.86 , p < 0.05 ), and their pairing yields gains over either stream alone while matching or slightly exceeding generic backbone replacements; the aggregate margin is modest, but the per-ROI mechanistic rationale provides a principled basis for the dual-stream design. 

Limitations and future work. StimFlow approximates the conditional mean of 

p(v | x); it does not yet model the structured component of trial-to-trial vari-ability (e.g. cognitive-state or attention-dependent fluctuations), which remains an open direction. Performance is bounded by frozen backbones ( ∼72 –80% noise ceiling), and validation is limited to four NSD subjects; broader evaluation across datasets, tasks, and populations is needed before generated responses could sup-plement, let alone substitute for, empirical data in applied or clinical settings. ## S7 The SynBrain Baseline: Why Its Reported Per-Image Pearson Is Inflated 

This section explains why SynBrain’s originally reported Pearson ( 0.687 ) is far above the comparable value ( 0.340 ). The effect is specific to the per-image, across-voxel Pearson (the metric SynBrain reports) combined with its normal-ization, and is therefore analyzed here in that metric; it is distinct from the per-voxel, across-image encoding Pearson used for all results in the main text. 

Two normalization regimes. The main text z-scores the single-trial betas: each voxel is centred and scaled to zero mean and unit variance using training-set statistics, following the standard NSD encoding/decoding protocol [1, 22, 30]. The publicly released SynBrain pipeline instead divides every beta by a fixed constant ( 2000 ) applied uniformly to all voxels, without removing the per-voxel mean. We denote the two regimes mean-retained and z-scored .

SynBrain’s metric, and why the normalization matters. SynBrain scores fidelity with the per-image, across-voxel Pearson correlation: for test image i it correlates the synthesized voxel pattern ˆvi ∈ RV against the measured pattern vi across the 

V voxels, then averages over images. Crucially, this correlation is invariant to any positive rescaling, PCC( aˆvi, vi) = PCC(ˆ vi, vi) for a > 0, so the constant division by 2000 by itself cannot change this metric . The regimes differ in two ways — z-scoring removes the per-voxel mean and additionally rescales each voxel by its own standard deviation — but, as the mean-only control below isolates, it is the retained per-voxel mean , not the rescaling, that drives the inflation: under across-voxel scoring with the mean kept, a model only has to reproduce the static per-voxel mean pattern to attain a high score, so the reported Pearson is largely an artifact of the normalization rather than evidence of stimulus-specific fidelity. 

Why mean retention inflates per-image Pearson. Write each measured pattern as vi = ¯ m + ri, where ¯m is the static per-voxel mean (shared by every image and reflecting baseline and anatomical offsets) and ri is the stimulus-driven residual. Under the mean-retained regime ¯m is present in both the target and any reasonable prediction, and because it is identical across images it is trivially reproduced. The across-voxel correlation is then dominated by agreement on ¯mStimFlow: Stimulus-conditioned Flow Matching for fMRI Synthesis 21 

rather than on the stimulus-specific ri, inflating per-image Pearson. Z-scoring removes ¯m, leaving ri alone, so the metric measures stimulus-driven fidelity. 

A mean-only control isolates the effect. Consider a predictor that ignores the stimulus and outputs the constant per-voxel training mean ¯m for every image. Under the mean-retained regime this trivial predictor already attains a high per-image Pearson of 0.83 on subject 1, exceeding SynBrain’s reported 0.687 (Fig. S2, “mean-only”), despite carrying no stimulus information; under z-scoring its cor-relation collapses to chance, because the prediction equals the train mean and loses all across-voxel variance once the mean is removed. Since the static mean alone already yields 0.83 in the mean-retained regime — itself above SynBrain’s reported 0.687 — a score reported in that regime reflects mostly this mean rather than stimulus-specific fidelity; removing the mean by z-scoring strips the artifact and gives the comparable 0.340 .

MSE moves oppositely. Unlike Pearson, mean-squared error is scale-dependent: the constant division shrinks the signal’s dynamic range, so the mean-retained regime also reports a much smaller MSE ( 0.139 vs. 0.398 after z-scoring). The two metrics improve together only because both follow from the same normalization, not because the synthesized signal is closer to the target. 

Convergent evidence for the comparable value. Under z-scoring, our re-run of SynBrain ( 0.340 ) closely matches the independently reported MindSimulator value ( 0.346 ); only SynBrain’s mean-retained number ( 0.687 ) stands apart. Two independently developed methods converging to ≈ 0.34 under the standard nor-malization, with the single outlier accounted for by mean retention, supports treating 0.340 as the comparable voxel-level score. 

Reproduction protocol. The effect is reproduced as follows, using the released SynBrain checkpoint and the shared 1,000 -image test set restricted to the nsdgeneral 

ROI: (i) obtain SynBrain’s synthesized betas ˆvi and the measured betas vi;(ii) compute per-image across-voxel Pearson under the mean-retained regime (constant division, no mean removal) and under z-scoring (per-voxel train mean/std); (iii) add the mean-only control by replacing ˆvi with the broadcast per-voxel train mean; (iv) report the mean over images. The mean-only control in Fig. S2 is com-puted on the measured betas of subject 1, for which the mean-retained signal is available; the analysis script is released with our code. 

Implication for evaluation. Semantic-level metrics alone can mask voxel-level er-ror: a robust frozen decoder can recover the correct image even from a coarsely synthesized response, so high decoded-image scores do not guarantee faithful voxel patterns. A voxel-level Pearson computed under standard z-scoring is therefore a necessary check, and we report both levels throughout. 22 B. V. Hung et al.
