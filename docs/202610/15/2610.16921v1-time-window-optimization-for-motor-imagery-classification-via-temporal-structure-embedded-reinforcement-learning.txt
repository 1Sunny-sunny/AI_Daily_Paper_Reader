# Time Window Optimization for Motor Imagery Classification via Temporal Structure Embedded Reinforcement Learning 

Huan Luo, Na Lu, Xiaopeng Wang, and Ziyang Shang 

Abstract — Online motor imagery-based brain-computer interfaces (MI-BCIs) usually processes continuous EEG signal with fixed-length sliding windows, which is inflexible, human intervened, and misaligned with neural events. Time window optimization has shown promising improvement as a substitute solution. However, the existing optimiza-tion methods typically evaluate windows isolatedly and fail to model temporal dependencies across consecutive windows, which may produce fragmented and physiologi-cally implausible sequences. To address this limitation, we propose a novel Temporal Structure Embedded Reinforce-ment Learning (TSE-RL) framework. This framework em-beds prior temporal-structure knowledge of MI events into a stable RL formulation, reformulating window optimization as a constrained Markov Decision Process with ordinal re-lation enforcement of neural event. First, the evaluation and action selection upon each window has been changed from simply “retain/discard” to semantic alignment between win-dow and neural events as Discard /Onset /sustain /Offset .Second, action masking is imposed by a finite-state ma-chine (FSM) which enforces physiologically plausible de-cision orders with constraint of neural activity progress in term of planning /execution /recovery , enhancing both rationality, interpretability and classification performance. Third, a Transformer-based history encoder is designed to capture long-range inter-window dependencies in parallel, improving training stability and efficiency compared with existing recurrent architectures. Besides, TSE-RL adopts a plug-and-play design that can be integrated with diverse MI classifiers. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets demonstrate that TSE-RL consistently improves decoding performance in both cross-session and cross-subject settings. Visualization analyses further suggest that the learned policy of window optimization aligns with neurophysiological principles. 

Index Terms — Brain computer interface, electroen-cephalography, motor imagery, reinforcement learning

# BRAIN computer interface (BCI) technology establishes a direct communication and control pathway between the brain and external devices by decoding neural activ-ity [1]. It has broad potential in diverse fields such as neurorehabilitation, entertainment [2], and human-machine interaction [3]. Among non-invasive acquisition modalities, electroencephalography (EEG) is widely adopted due to its high temporal resolution and relatively low cost [4]. In motor imagery (MI) BCIs, users voluntarily imag-ine limb movements to elicit event-related desynchroniza-tion/synchronization (ERD/ERS) patterns [5], [6], thereby enabling device control without external stimuli. Recent years have witnessed significant advances in MI-EEG signal decoding techniques, most of which analyze a predefined post-cue segment (typically 0–4 s) for feature ex-traction and classification [7]. Representative approaches range from Common Spatial Patterns (CSP) [8], which learns spatial filters by maximizing inter-class variance, to diverse deep learning methods. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) like EEGNet [9] and M-ShallowConvNet [10] efficiently cap-ture spatio-temporal patterns with depthwise separable convo-lutions or shallow designs. FBCNet [11] performs frequency-band fusion via multi-view convolutions, while ATCNet [12] integrates attention mechanisms with temporal convolutional networks. More recently, Transformer-based models have been explored in EEGConformer [13] and TSLANet [14]. And CSPNet [15] embeds CSP principles within a neural net-work framework. Riemannian geometry-based methods such as manifold attention network (mAtt) [16] characterize EEG patterns on the Riemannian manifold of symmetric positive-definite (SPD) matrices. Although these methods perform well in offline evaluations, their performance in online scenarios are constrained by using a single, shared temporal segment to represent MI activity across trials. For instance, online systems often relies on a sliding window of fixed length (around 1 s) moving along the continuous EEG signal. Some windows may cover only a fraction of an MI event, some may contain excessive pre-event baseline activity, and some may be contaminated by burst artifacts, the majority of which are misaligned with actual neural events. Meanwhile, fixed-length windows restrict the timeliness of the system response. Online decoding thus can benefit from time window optimization, where windows are adaptively accumulated or discarded to form a segment that arXiv:2610.16921v1 [cs.NE] 12 Oct 2026 2 PREPRINT 

better matches informative neural patterns, providing better balance between timeliness and accuracy. Researchers have proposed various time window optimiza-tion strategies. Correlation-based Time Window Selection (CTWS) [17] adjusts the onset of window by correlating sliding windows with reference signals. Approaches based on Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) [18] and Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA) [19] employ meta-learning and heuristic searches to identify optimal configurations in time-frequency parameter space. However, these techniques typically evaluate and select candidate windows independently, treating each as an isolated “good/bad” decision based on the quality of the window , with configurations searched offline and applied uniformly across trials [20], [21]. This limitation can result in fragmented or physiologically implausible window sequences that fail to capture the coherent evolution of MI events. In contrast, our method optimizes the time window sequence, conditioning each decision on cross-window history and on the constraint of MI phases. MI events exhibit contralateral desynchronization of the μ (8–13 Hz) and β (13–30 Hz) rhythms during MI execution, and a subsequent rebound of synchronization of the β-rhythm after the event ends [22]. Such an evolution suggests distinct MI phases from onset to sustained imagery and finally to offset. Reinforcement Learning (RL) provides an ideal framework to overcome this limitation, as it inherently models sequential decision-making processes with uncertainty. By formulating the problem of time window optimization in MI-EEG as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), an agent makes adaptive decisions at each time step based on the current state and historical context (e.g., whether to retain a window and what temporal role it plays in the MI event) to maximize cumulative rewards through interactions with the environment. It also avoids brute-force search over window combinations through exploration–exploitation. In addition, Safe Reinforce-ment Learning [23] also inspires us to incorporate neural event ordinal information, where temporal-structure patterns must be embedded to constrain the action space. Notably, preliminary studies have demonstrated the potential of RL in improving BCI adaptivity. Aung et al. [24] use RL to decide whether to classify or skip the current time point, and Zhang et al. [25] use RL to adaptively focus on features at varying scales. In both, RL improves the adaptivity of the BCI decoding: the action optimizes one component inside the decoding pipeline, while the analyzed window remains fixed and predefined. Our work instead optimizes the window itself, taking each incoming window as the decision unit, assigning it an explicit semantic role, and constraining the action space by the structure of MI events. Therefore, we propose a Temporal Structure Embedded Reinforcement Learning (TSE-RL) framework for adaptive time window optimization in MI-EEG decoding. The core innovation of this framework is to embed prior temporal-structure knowledge of MI events into a stable RL framework, transforming window optimization from isolated “good/bad” judgments to semantic alignment of complete neural events. Specifically, TSE-RL incorporates three mechanisms to make this idea practical and reliable. The first mechanism transforms the action selection upon each time window from simply “retain/discard” to semantic alignment between window and neural events as Discard /Onset /sustain /Offset . This seman-tic alignment mechanism imposes physiological patterns on window optimization of EEG signal. The second mechanism adopts Finite State Machine (FSM) for constrained action masking which enables safe reinforcement learning in re-stricted action space. Without proper constraints, the RL agent may generate physiologically implausible window sequences that violate the known temporal structure of MI events (e.g., selecting an Offset before an Onset ). The third mechanism concerns Transformer-based history encoding. The decisions of window optimization require integrating historical con-text, while traditional RL methods often employing Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to capture temporal dependencies. When processing long sequences, RNNs are prone to gradient vanishing or explosion, resulting in training instability [26]. The main contributions of this paper include:  

> •

We propose a temporal window optimization method that embeds MI event temporal structure knowledge into a re-inforcement learning framework. The action space based on EEG time window evaluation is semantically aligned with neural event for interpretability and performance improvement. FSM-based action masking is designed to ensure physiological plausible decision sequence with constraint of neural activity progress in term of plan-ning /execution /recovery . 

> •

We design a Transformer-based history encoder to replace training-unstable recurrent networks. This encoder takes historical window sequences as input and leverages self-attention mechanisms to learn temporal dependencies across windows in parallel, improving both training sta-bility and computational efficiency.  

> •

TSE-RL is plug-and-play and can be integrated with various mainstream MI classifiers. Simulated online ex-periments on multiple public datasets demonstrate that it significantly improves decoding performance in both cross-session and cross-subject settings. 

II. M ETHODS 

We formulate time-window optimization for online MI-EEG decoding as an constrained sequential decision-making problem. In contrast to fixed-length window decoding and traditional window optimization, TSE-RL learns to assign each candidate window a semantic role ( Discard , Onset , Sustain ,or Offset ). As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), TSE-RL encodes the MI event’s temporal structure as a Finite State Machine (FSM) that governs the valid action space of an RL agent via action masking. This mechanism ensures the learned policy adheres to physiological plausibility, elevating window optimization from isolated “good/bad” judgments to semantic alignment of complete neural events. Moreover, TSE-RL employs a Transformer-based history encoder to stably integrate histori-cal information in parallel, preventing the training instability and sequential overhead commonly observed in recurrent RL. B. Temporal structure embedding via FSM-constrained action masking and Transformer-based history encoding 

As mentioned in Introduction, applying RL to MI-EEG time window optimization faces two significant challenges. First, the agent must respect safety constraints so that selected window sequences remain physiologically meaningful. Sec-ond, the agent has to learn from long sequences of noisy EEG observations in a stable and data-efficient way. The proposed TSE-RL framework addresses these issues through a temporal structure embedding mechanism that combines an FSM-constrained action mask with a Transformer-based history encoder. This design enforces the decision of window optimization alignment with MI neural patterns, while provid-ing a stable architecture for learning. MI events exhibit contralateral desynchronization of the μ

(8–13 Hz) and β (13–30 Hz) rhythms during MI execution, and exhibit a rebound of synchronization of the β-rhythm after the event ends [22]. Conventional isolated window optimization methods ignore this temporal pattern, leading to discontin-uous or semantically incomplete window sequences. This consequently reduces classification accuracy and diminishes interpretability. Reward shaping can, in principle, discourage such sequences by penalizing non-contiguous or incomplete window selections. However, this soft constraint can only indirectly influence the policy through policy gradients. During the early stages of training, the policy that is not aligned with physiological patterns can still be explored. Even during inference, a converged policy employing stochastic sampling cannot entirely prevent the occurrence of invalid action se-quences. To ensure that the selected time window consistently consti-tutes a valid MI event, we embed the temporal structure of MI events into the decision process using FSM-constrained action masking. We employ FSM to model the legal phase transitions of events, restricting valid actions Aϕn based on the current phase ϕn. The probability of all invalid actions is set to zero before the action sampling. This hard constraint reduces the policy search space, forcing the agent to follow physiologically plausible trajectories. Huang et al. [27] showed that invalid-action masking yields more effective gradient updates, with benefits increasing as the number of invalid actions grows. Masking scales well when invalid actions are numerous, while penalty-based methods struggle to explore the task. Further-more, Stolz et al. [28] found that focusing exploration on state-specific valid actions significantly improves learning efficiency and the predictability of the agent in safety-critical tasks. Therefore, in EEG decoding, we propose FSM-constrained action masking combined with the reward function described in Section II-A to guide the agent to generate continuous and complete MI windows. The second challenge is to integrate information over the histories of extended observation when the agent determines actions. Traditional RL methods employ RNNs, but they suffer from instability, particularly in MI-EEG tasks with trials spanning dozens of windows over several seconds [26]. Each additional window in the sequence elongates the gradient path, causing gradient to decay or explode exponentially. Beyond in-stability, RNNs require careful management of variable-length sequences and hidden states during training, complicating the implementation of batching strategies. These challenges can hinder the deployment of recurrent RL approaches in MI-EEG scenarios. We thereby adopts a non-recurrent Transformer-based his-tory encoder. Instead of propagating hidden states, it processes the recent history Hn (as feature vectors {fn−k, . . . , f n−1})in parallel using self-attention mechanisms. This generates a fixed-dimensional context vector hn that captures temporal dependencies across history observations. When concatenated with the current window feature fn and trial progress ρn, this forms the full input for the policy network. By eliminating recursive propagation of hidden state, the design circumvents gradient instability while leveraging parallel computation for efficiency. The Transformer’s multi-head attention effectively captures long-range temporal dependencies. This architecture has demonstrated superior performance in partial-observation RL tasks [29].

D. Learning framework 

To address the low sample efficiency and training instability of end-to-end RL from random initialization, the TSE-RL framework is trained in three stages that progressively shift from supervised learning to policy optimization. 

Stage 1: Supervised pretraining. In the first stage, we train the feature extractor F and discriminator D as a standard classifier on full time windows. From each training trial, we extract time windows Xn using the window length L and stride ∆, and assign each window the corresponding trial label 

ytrue . The backbone then produces pn = D F (Xn) for each window, and we minimize the cross-entropy loss

At the end of this stage, F has learned discriminative spatio-temporal representations of EEG windows, and D has learned the class distribution whose probabilities pn will be reused in the reward design. 

Stage 2: Policy learning. After the convergence of super-vised pretraining, the parameters of F and D are frozen. Then we train the history encoder G and the FSM-constrained policy network Π by reinforcement learning. We adopt Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [30] algorithm. In each trial, the agent interacts with the environment as described in Section II-A: the frozen extractor F converts the current and historical windows into features, the frozen discriminator D provides window-level probabilities pn for computing rdisc and rterm ,the history encoder G produces the context vector hn, and the policy network Π maps zn = [ fn; hn; ρn] to an action distribution that is masked by the current phase ϕn.During this stage, PPO gradients update only the parameters of G and Π. Consequently, the mapping from raw windows to features and probabilities does not change while the agent is learning decision policy. This separation prevents represen-tation drift from interfering with policy optimization, making training more stable and data-efficient. The FSM-based action mask is active throughout, restricting the exploration to phys-iologically plausible action. 

Stage 3: Joint fine-tuning. Once the policy has reached stable performance, we perform a final joint fine-tuning. We unfreeze the last few layers of the feature extractor F while keeping the discriminator D fixed, and continue PPO training with a smaller learning rate. Policy gradients are now allowed to slightly adjust the high-level feature representations, which allows mild adaptation of the feature representation to the learned policy while preserving the stability gained in Stage 2. III. E XPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

A. Datasets and preprocessing 

TSE-RL is evaluated on four public MI-EEG datasets from MOABB [31], namely BNCI2014001, BNCI2014002, BNCI2015001 and OpenBMI [32]. Table I summarizes their statistics. BNCI2014001 is derived from BCI Competition IV-2a [33] and is the only four-class dataset, while the other three are two-class. Two experimental protocols are considered. For within-subject cross-session experiments, the T session of BNCI2014001 is used for training, while the E session is used for testing. For BNCI2014002, the first five runs are used for training, with the remaining three runs used for testing. In the case of BNCI2015001, the first session is used for training, while the second session is used for testing, and the same split is applied to OpenBMI. For cross-subject experiments, a leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) protocol is adopted in which, for each fold, a single subject serves as the test set and all remaining subjects are merged to form the training set. All datasets share the same preprocessing steps. Raw EEG signals are band-pass filtered (8–32 Hz), then multiplied by a factor of 10 6 to scale to microvolts ( μV). For each recording block used in training or testing (session or run, depending on the dataset), all trials are concatenated along the time axis, in their original recording order, to form a continuous EEG dataflow that simulates online decoding. Sliding windows with length L = 1 .0 s and stride ∆ = 0 .1 s are applied to this EEG dataflow, producing a sequence of time windows {Xn} as defined in Section II-A. At each time step, the TSE-RL agent interacts with this unlabeled window sequence and decides whether to discard the current window or to retain it as the Onset, Sustain, or Offset phase. The true trial boundaries and labels are used only to compute rewards during training and to evaluate accuracy during testing, and remain inaccessible to the agent. Decisions are causal in that the agent observes only the current window Xn and the finite history Hn at step n,while no future sample is available. At test time the trials are decoded one by one with a batch size of one, and no batch-normalization statistics are computed across trials, which rules out any leakage of information between trials.

Hyperparameters of these backbones follow the recommendations in the original papers or the official code repositories. For each backbone B, the network is decomposed as B(Xn) = D(F (Xn)) following the principle in Section II-C. Concretely, the split between F and D is defined as follows:  

> •

EEGNet [9]: F contains the initial temporal convolu-tion and depthwise spatial convolution blocks (including batch normalization, ELU, and average pooling), which produce a compact spatio–temporal feature map. D com-prises the final separable convolution, global average pooling, dropout, and the dense classification layer that maps the pooled feature to class logits.  

> •

M-ShallowConvNet [10]: F contains the temporal con-volution and depthwise spatial convolution layers to-gether with the first batch normalization. D includes the squaring non-linearity, mean pooling, log transformation, dropout, and the final fully connected classifier.  

> •

FBCNet [11]: F is the FBCNet feature extractor that ap-plies multi-band temporal convolutions and spatial filters for each filter bank branch and outputs concatenated fea-ture maps across bands. D is the FBCNet discriminator that performs covariance-based pooling over the spatially filtered signals and projects the pooled representation to class logits through fully connected layers.  

> •

ATCNet [12]: F consists of the temporal, depthwise and spatial convolution layers, and the stacked attentional temporal convolution blocks that form the main encoder. 

D contains the final global average pooling, dropout, and classification layers that operate on the encoder output.  

> •

EEGConformer [13]: F is the temporal and depthwise convolution layers. D includes the remaining convolution layers, Conformer encoder, and the output linear classi-fier.  

> •

TSLANet [14]: F comprises the temporal segmenta-tion and patch embedding, and positional encoding. D

consists of the stacked temporal–spectral local attention blocks, final pooling over the sequence dimension and the dense classification head.  

> •

CSPNet [15]: F includes the filter-bank temporal convo-lutions and CSP-like spatial projection layers that produce multi-band spatial features. D aggregates these features via pooling and passes them through the final fully connected classifier.  

> •

mAtt [16]: F is the multi-head Riemannian feature extractor that computes, for each window, covariance matrices, projects them to a low-dimensional subspace, and applies the matrix logarithm to obtain a stack of normalized log-SPD matrices without flattening. D vec-torizes the per-head SPD features, applies an attention mechanism to weight and aggregate the heads, and feeds the aggregated representation into an MLP classifier to produce class logits.

The agent learns a policy to perform time-window optimization by selectively discarding or retaining windows and marking them as Onset, Sustain, or Offset. The agent then aggregates the probabilities of retained windows for classification. All exper-iments are independently repeated ten times, and performance is reported as mean accuracy and standard deviation across the ten runs. Statistical significance of performance improvement between TSE-RL and its corresponding Baseline-full counter-part is assessed using a paired test over the ten runs. 

Hyperparameters. Unless otherwise stated, we use shared hyperparameters across datasets and backbones. The history encoder G operates on the most recent k = 5 windows and is implemented as a standard Transformer encoder with one layer, four attention heads, hidden size dh = 128 , dropout rate 

0.1. The encoded sequence is temporally mean-pooled and linearly projected to obtain a vector hn ∈ Rdh , d h = 64 . The FSM-constrained policy network receives the concatenated vector zn = [ fn; hn; ρn] and is realized as an actor–critic multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 64 units. The reward and discount factor follow Section II-A. Training follows the three-stage scheme. In supervised pre-training, each backbone B is trained end-to-end on full win-dows using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 10 −3, batch size 64 , and 300 epochs. After convergence, B is split into F

and D, and their parameters are frozen. In policy learning, only the history encoder G and the FSM-constrained policy network are optimized by PPO with learning rate 3 × 10 −4,PPO clipping parameter 0.2, generalized advantage estimation parameter λ = 0 .95 , and a total of 4 × 10 4 environment steps. In joint fine-tuning, F are unfrozen and updated together with 

G and the policy network using a smaller learning rate of 

3 × 10 −5 for 2 × 10 4 additional environment steps. 

Configuration. Experiments were implemented in Python 3.10 and PyTorch 2.3.1 and ran on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA TITAN V GPU and an Intel Xeon Gold 6230 CPU (2.10 GHz). 

C. Within-subject cross-session classification in simulated online experiment 

Tables II and III report within-subject cross-session accu-racies. In all four datasets, TSE-RL consistently improves the performance of the Baseline-full models for most back-bones, with some gains being statistically significant. On BNCI2014001 (Table II), TSE-RL yields improvements for EEGNet, FBCNet, ATCNet, M-ShallowConvNet, and CSPNet, with average accuracies increasing by about 2–3 percentage points. Only TSLANet exhibits a slight degradation, and on this dataset it has both the lowest accuracy and the largest standard deviation among the eight backbones. Since the re-wards are computed from the window-level confidences, such a backbone provides a noisy learning signal for the policy. On BNCI2014002, BNCI2015001 and OpenBMI (Table III), TSE-RL improves every backbone, including TSLANet, and the benefit persists on the much larger OpenBMI dataset. 

D. Cross-subject classification in simulated online experiment 

Table IV summarizes cross-subject performance on BNCI2014001. Strong cross-subject variability makes this set-ting considerably more challenging, and Baseline-full models drop substantially relative to the within-subject case. Nev-ertheless, TSE-RL still improves average accuracy for most backbones, with statistically significant gains for EEGNet, FBCNet, ATCNet, mAtt, and CSPNet. TSE-RL is therefore compatible with a wide range of MI backbones across both cross-session and cross-subject decoding. Rehabilitation ap-plications involve a further shift, as the ERD/ERS of stroke patients differs markedly from that of young healthy subjects. On the stroke dataset of Liu et al. [34], TSE-RL improves all eight backbones (Section 5 of the Supplementary Material).

F. Computational efficiency analysis 

Online MI decoding requires repeated inference on sliding windows, so the computational cost for each window is crit-ical for practical deployment. We compare the computational efficiency of different backbones. A single EEG window of size 22 × 250 (1.0 s at 250 Hz) from BNCI2014001 is forwarded with batch size 1. Table V reports inference time, forward FLOPs, and parameter count. Baseline measures the backbone classifier only, consisting of a feature extractor and a discriminator (FE+Disc). TSE-RL measures the full window optimization pipeline, which adds a history encoder and a policy network on top of FE+Disc. The increase in FLOPs and parameter count from Baseline to TSE-RL primarily results from the additional history encoder and policy network. They process multiple historical windows with intermediate high-dimensional features. Despite this additional computation, the per-window inference time remains low across all backbones. Note that mAtt exhibits longer inference time due to its reliance on eigendecomposition operations, which are less suited to parallel computations on GPUs. G. Ablation study 

To validate the core contributions of TSE-RL, an ablation study is conducted on the EEGNet backbone. The results are reported in Table VI across settings and datasets. The comparison starts from a supervised baseline that disables RL, the history encoder, and the FSM-based action mask, which corresponds to EEGNet (Baseline-full) operating on all windows without window optimization. RL is then enabled while keeping both the history encoder and the action mask disabled, yielding a minimal policy that uses only the current-window feature and the trial-progress indicator. Two inter-mediate variants are further evaluated by adding either the FSM-based action mask or the history encoder. And the full model then enables RL, the history encoder, and the action mask simultaneously. Overall, Table VI shows that RL alone improves performance over the supervised baseline, indicating the benefit of learning an time-window optimization policy. Adding either the FSM-based action mask or the history encoder provides further gains, and enabling both consistently achieves the highest accuracy across all datasets and settings. 

H. Sensitivity analysis 

To assess the robustness of TSE-RL to hyperparameters, a sensitivity analysis is performed with respect to the window length L, stride ∆, and history length k on the EEGNet backbone. The default configuration in the main experiments is L = 1 .0 s, ∆ = 0 .1 s, and k = 5 . Fig. 4 varies these hyperparameters within practical ranges ( L ∈ [0 .6, 1.4] s, ∆ ∈

[0 .04 , 0.4] s, and k ∈ [3 , 9] ) and reports accuracy along with the retained-window ratio (retained windows divided by all candidate windows). This ratio is reported because changing L

or ∆ alters the number of candidate windows per trial, making raw window counts incomparable. Overall, Fig. 4 reveals that TSE-RL demonstrates considerable stability around the default parameter configuration, with only moderate performance vari-ation under perturbations of L, ∆, and k.

To address this limitation, we proposed Temporal Structure Embedded Reinforcement Learning, which reformulates win-dow optimization as a constrained Markov Decision Pro-cess in which an FSM-constrained action mask keeps the selected windows consistent with the temporal structure of MI events while assigning each of them an explicit temporal role. A Transformer-based history encoder captures long-range inter-window dependencies with better training stability than recurrent designs. experiments show consistent improvements in cross-session and cross-subject settings at modest overhead compatible with real-time constraints. Visualization analyses further show that the learned policy aligns decision windows with contralateral mu-beta ERD/ERS, confirming that it exploits neurophysiolog-ical patterns. These results establish TSE-RL as an effective approach for online time window optimization. Its behavior under closed-loop feedback remains to be examined, since the simulated online experiment reproduces the non-stationary environmental noise and the fatigue-induced neural pattern shifts of real recordings but leaves the BCI output disconnected from the user. Closed-loop validation on data acquired will be studied in future work.
