ABSTRACT 

Effective unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) requires a representation that captures transferable structure from both the labeled source domain and the unla-beled target domain. We introduce Joint Contrastive-Adversarial Representation Learning (JCAR), a two-stage framework that learns a common representation from the union of unlabeled source and target samples by combining contrastive learning with adversarial domain alignment, and then freezes the representation to fit a source-supervised predictor. We establish a non-asymptotic excess-risk bound for this procedure that couples representation and domain-discriminator estimation while separating downstream prediction, with explicit neural-network approximation errors. Under the stated assumptions and common Hölder smooth-ness β ≥ 1, the bound identifies a sufficiently large unlabeled-sample regime in which the downstream rate is eO(m−β/ (2 β+d)), where m counts labeled source samples and logarithmic factors are suppressed. The labeled-sample exponent is governed by the latent dimension d rather than the ambient dimension p, which enters the pretraining cost. Experiments on Office-31, Office-Home, and Domain-Net demonstrate clear transfer gains across diverse domain shifts, with particularly strong performance when representations are learned from scratch. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Domain adaptation (DA) (Pan & Yang, 2010) aims to transfer a predictor from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain. UDA methods use target samples in different ways: for example, statistical matching aligns feature distributions (Gretton et al., 2012), reweighting corrects distribution shift (Kato & Teshima, 2021), and adversarial methods train a feature extractor against a domain discriminator (Long et al., 2018). In many such methods, however, task-discriminative supervision is available only in the source domain, while target samples enter primarily through an alignment objective. This asymmetry motivates us to ask whether both domains can participate directly in unsupervised representation construction before the source-supervised predictor is fitted. Figure 1a contrasts the conventional source-supervised adaptation pipeline with the joint-domain representation-learning pipeline in Figure 1b. To address this question, we introduce Joint Contrastive-Adversarial Representation Learning (JCAR). In Stage 1, contrastive learning (Chen et al., 2020) allows unlabeled source and target samples to jointly shape the representation, while an adversarial domain objective explicitly reduces the distinguishability of their feature distributions. In Stage 2, the learned feature extractor is frozen and a predictor is trained on labeled source samples. This design exploits target-domain structure without target labels and cleanly separates task-agnostic representation learning from downstream prediction. Our non-asymptotic analysis jointly controls representation learning and domain discrimination while separating their cost from downstream prediction. It explicitly balances neural-network approximation and statistical errors, linking the coupled complexity of contrastive–adversarial pretraining to downstream label efficiency. Under the theorem’s assumptions, sufficiently many unlabeled samples yield a downstream polynomial rate governed by the representation dimension d, while the ambient dimension p enters the pretraining cost. Remark 4. Theorem 3 separates the joint cost of learning representations and domain alignment from the labeled-sample cost of downstream prediction. The coupled pretraining rate reflects the combined smoothness and capacity of the feature extractor and discriminator: domain discrimination acts on learned features, so its statistical complexity depends on both components. This quantifies their interaction within pretraining, while the frozen representation enables a separate analysis of predictor fitting. The resulting bound assigns the ambient-dimensional learning cost to the unlabeled stage and the low-dimensional prediction cost to the labeled stage. In the theorem’s large-unlabeled-sample regime with common smoothness β ≥ 1, the bound reduces to eO(m−β/ (2 β+d)), where the tilde suppresses logarithmic factors. Thus sufficiently accurate pretraining preserves a downstream label-sample exponent governed by d rather than p, explicitly linking unlabeled representation learning to low-dimensional prediction efficiency. 

Remark 5. The analysis explicitly controls neural approximation errors, allowing the ideal repre-sentation, discriminator, and predictor to lie outside the chosen network classes. Increasing norm budgets improves the approximation bounds but enlarges the statistical bound; the coupled rate results from balancing these effects jointly for the feature extractor and discriminator. Under the maximum-row-sum product constraint of Definition C.3, the pretraining complexity bound has no explicit width penalty at fixed norm budgets and depths. This provides a quantitative basis for coordinating approximation capacity and statistical complexity in contrastive–adversarial learning. 

Discussion. JCAR integrates joint-domain contrastive feature learning with explicit adversarial alignment, then freezes the representation for source-supervised prediction. Whereas Shen et al. (2022) explain transfer through augmentation-graph connectivity for spectral contrastive learning and a linear head, our theory quantifies the estimation and neural-approximation costs of the contrastive– adversarial objective. Its distinctive contribution is to connect the coupled complexity of feature learning and domain discrimination to downstream label efficiency, with separate roles for unlabeled and labeled sample sizes. 5.2 EVALUATION 

Tables 1 and 2 compare randomly initialized models, with ResNet-based and ViT-based methods grouped separately. JCAR delivers clear gains in several challenging from-scratch transfers. On Office-31, our ResNet variant obtains the best result in its architecture group on four of six transfers, including gains of 1.9, 3.3, and 3.4 percentage points over the strongest competing ResNet result on A→D, D →A, and W →A, respectively. Our ViT variant improves over ViT-B on four of six Office-31 transfers. On Office-Home, it improves over ViT-B on five of six transfers, with a 5.1-point gain on Cl →Pr. These results demonstrate the value of joint-domain contrastive-adversarial pretraining, especially for the ViT backbone. The ResNet variant is less effective on Office-Home and on the D→W and W →D Office-31 transfers, showing that the gains depend on the backbone and domain pair. Tables 5–9 in the Appendix provide the complementary pretrained evaluation. 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced JCAR, a two-stage UDA framework combining joint-domain contrastive learning with adversarial alignment, followed by source-supervised prediction on frozen features. Our non-asymptotic analysis balances neural approximation and statistical errors, captures the coupled cost of feature learning and domain discrimination, and separates it from downstream prediction. Under the stated assumptions, sufficiently accurate unlabeled pretraining yields a downstream polynomial rate governed by the latent dimension rather than the ambient dimension. This establishes a quantitative link between contrastive–adversarial pretraining and label-sample efficiency. The experiments substantiate the practical value of this design, with strong from-scratch improvements across multiple Office-31 and Office-Home transfers, particularly for the ViT backbone. The current results also identify productive directions for extension: domain-specific augmentation design, stronger compatibility with supervised initialization, and component-wise ablations of contrastive learning, adversarial alignment, and stage-wise freezing. The formulations above highlight two differences between JCAR and standard single-stage adversarial UDA: (A). Source-Supervised vs. Joint-Domain Contrastive Feature Learning. In standard DANN-style training, task-discriminative supervision for Gf is supplied by labeled source examples, while unlabeled target examples contribute through the domain objective. Consequently, source labels remain the primary driver of feature construction even though target data par-ticipates in alignment. JCAR instead applies the same self-supervised contrastive objective to the combined unlabeled dataset ( DS ∪ DT ), so both domains contribute symmetrically and directly to feature construction. The adversarial term complements this instance-level objective by aligning the two feature distributions, yielding a representation shaped jointly by within-sample consistency and cross-domain agreement. (B). Separating Representation Learning from Downstream Fitting. Standard adversarial UDA jointly optimizes Ly and Ld in one minimax loop. JCAR separates joint-domain represen-tation pretraining from fitting the downstream label predictor, isolating the task-agnostic representation objective from source-label supervision. JCAR completely decouples these two objectives: Stage 1 (Task-agnostic representation learning): We learn the representa-tion using LinfoNCE and Ld without task labels. The representation is therefore constructed from the geometry of both domains and their domain-level alignment, independently of the source classification objective. Stage 2 (Flexible task learning): After pretraining, ˆGf is frozen and the downstream predictor Gy is trained on top of it. This decoupling prevents downstream fitting from overwriting the learned common representation and permits the representation to be reused across downstream tasks. A.1.3 DISTINCTIONS FROM SHEN ET AL . (2022) Both JCAR and Shen et al. (2022) use contrastive pretraining followed by downstream classification. JCAR incorporates an explicit adversarial alignment objective and analyzes its joint statistical and approximation costs with feature learning: (A). Representation Learning and Transfer Mechanisms. Under their augmentation-connectivity model, Shen et al. (2022) show that contrastive representations can support target prediction while retaining domain information. JCAR instead includes domain distinguishability in the pretraining objective, making feature alignment an explicit optimization target alongside contrastive learning. (B). Mathematical Formulation and Explicit Alignment. The contrastive objective in Shen et al. (2022) has no explicit adversarial alignment term. JCAR couples instance-level contrastive learning with domain discrimination through a minimax objective; its analysis therefore controls the composite class of discriminators acting on learned features. 14 Preprint. At the implementation level, JCAR combines symmet-ric source–target feature processing with a progressive gradient-reversal schedule: Symmet-ric Feature Processing: The same feature extractor and contrastive objective are applied to source and target mini-batch examples, allowing both domains to contribute uniformly to the representation rather than assigning target samples only an alignment role. Progressive Gradient Reversal (Warm-Start GRL): We use a warm-start schedule that gradually increases the adaptation factor λ(t):

λ(t) = 2 · (hi − lo )1 + e−α·t/T − (hi − lo ) + lo This schedule introduces the adversarial signal gradually while contrastive features are being formed, avoiding an abrupt full-strength alignment objective at the start of training. (D). Generalization and Theoretical Guarantees. Whereas Shen et al. (2022) derive target-error guarantees for spectral contrastive learning through augmentation-graph connectivity, our analysis explicitly tracks neural-network estimation and approximation errors for a contrastive–adversarial objective. Theorem 3 jointly balances feature-extractor and discrimi-nator complexity, while treating downstream predictor fitting separately. Under its assump-tions, common smoothness β ≥ 1, and sufficiently many unlabeled samples, the bound reduces to eO(m−β/ (2 β+d)). This quantifies how unlabeled data support high-dimensional pretraining while labeled data govern low-dimensional prediction. Together, joint-domain contrastive learning, explicit adversarial alignment, and frozen-feature predic-tion define JCAR’s methodological contribution. The non-asymptotic analysis connects this unlabeled representation-learning stage to downstream statistical efficiency. 

B RELATED WORK 

Distribution shifts between training and test data can degrade predictive performance (Quiñonero-Candela et al., 2022). Domain adaptation addresses such shifts to improve performance on down-stream domains. Huang et al. (2006); Sugiyama et al. (2007) used reweighting techniques that estimate density ratios between domains through nonparametric methods such as kernel estimators. These methods, however, can be sensitive to kernel selection and difficult to apply to high-dimensional un-structured data (Sugiyama et al., 2007; 2008; Yu & Szepesvári, 2012; Cao et al., 2019). Furthermore, Maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) (Gretton et al., 2012; Sejdinovic et al., 2013) is widely used to align distributions. Long et al. (2015; 2016b;a) studied feature transferability in deep convolutional networks and used MMD in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space to align deeper-layer representations across unlabeled source and target data. Long et al. (2017; 2018) extended this approach to joint and conditional distribution matching, respectively. Motivated by spectral analyses of feature representa-tions, Chen et al. (2019) penalized dominant singular values to strengthen less dominant directions and improve feature discriminability. Domain-adversarial methods provide another approach to distribution alignment (Tzeng et al., 2017; Long et al., 2018). Deep domain-adaptation methods embed adaptation modules in neural networks to learn transferable representations while matching feature distributions across domains (Long et al., 2014; Tzeng et al., 2015; Ganin & Lempitsky, 2015; Ganin et al., 2016; Long et al., 2016b; Tzeng et al., 2017). Cao et al. (2018) relaxed the shared-label-space assumption by allowing the target label space to be a subset of the source label space; their method suppresses outlier source classes and aligns distributions within the shared label space. Adversarial alignment can nevertheless impair transfer when suppressing domain-specific variation distorts useful feature structure. Liu et al. (2019) instead generated examples that are adversarial to both the category classifier and the domain discriminator without altering the feature representations. When data distributions embody complex multimodal structures, adversarial adaptation methods may fail to capture these structures for a discriminative alignment of distributions, potentially leading to model mismatch. This risk cannot be tackled by aligning distributions of features and classes via separate domain discriminators as Hoffman et al. (2016); Chen et al. (2017), because there is no guarantee that two distributions are sufficiently similar (Song et al., 2009; Arora et al., 2017) even when the discriminator is fully confused. DNNs have achieved significant success, and they are widely believed to be able to learn more transferable features (Zhao et al., 2019) since they disentangle explanatory factors of variations. Ben-David et al. (2010); Pan et al. (2010); Zhang et al. (2013); Wang & Schneider (2014) studied feature representations for transfer between source and 15 Preprint. Under review at ICLR 2027 target domains. Several recent efforts have focused on learning domain-invariant features in the context of neural networks (Ajakan et al., 2014; Ghifary et al., 2014; Tzeng et al., 2014). Yosinski et al. (2014) showed that learned deep representations capture factors shared across populations and can transfer from the original tasks to related tasks. DNNs can also learn more transferable representations than traditional hand-crafted features (Bengio et al., 2013). DNNs can reduce domain discrepancy (Glorot et al., 2011), but residual dataset shift can increase target-domain risk (Mansour et al., 2009; Ben-David et al., 2010). Yao et al. (2020) noticed that DNNs may suffer from the problem of long-tail data distribution in the target domain. Furthermore, not all knowledge of the pretrained models can be directly applied to the target task due to the discrepancy of various domains.

N EXPERIMENT DETAILS 

Datasets and Processes. We use Office-31 (Saenko et al., 2010), Office-Home (Venkateswara et al., 2017), and DomainNet (Peng et al., 2019). For the reported protocol, each domain is randomly partitioned into an 80% training split and a 20% held-out test split. Representation pretraining uses images from the source and target training splits without target class labels, and downstream predictor fitting uses labels only from the source training split. The target test split is reserved for final evaluation. We apply the same split-based protocol to every compared method, enabling controlled comparisons across learning objectives and architectures. Table 3: The dataset information for domain adaptation experiments. Data Domain Class Total sample Office-31 3 31 4,625 Office-Home 4 65 15,588 DomainNet 6 345 586,753 We then combine the training data from both the source and target domains and apply a series of augmentations for contrastive learning. The augmentation procedures follow the same setup as (Chen et al., 2020), including: • Randomly cropping the image to a size of 224 × 224 pixels. 25 Preprint. Under review at ICLR 2027 • Randomly applying a horizontal flip to the image with a probability of 0.5.• Randomly applying color jitter to the image with a probability of 0.8.• Converting the image to grayscale with a probability of 0.2.• Applying a custom Gaussian blur with a specified kernel size. All of the above augmentations are applied to two randomized copies of the original image x to generate a positive pair consisting of two augmented views, denoted by x+(1) and x+(2) , respectively. Other images within the same training mini-batch serve as negative samples. 

Model Architectures. We use ResNet (He et al., 2016) and ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020) backbones. The domain discriminator and downstream predictor are fully connected networks with ReLU activations, and a shallow perceptron connects the backbone to the discriminator. During Stage 1, a mini-batch contains unlabeled source and target training examples. Their features are used by both the contrastive loss and the domain discriminator. The discriminator minimizes its domain-classification loss, while a gradient-reversal layer sends the reversed domain gradient to the feature extractor; the contrastive gradient is propagated normally. During Stage 2, the feature extractor is frozen and only the downstream source-supervised predictor is fitted. DANN also uses labeled source and unlabeled target examples, but couples source classification with adversarial alignment; our Stage 1 replaces source classification with joint-domain self-supervised contrastive learning.
