# Predicting Cognitive Tasks from Single-Channel EEG through Task-Semantic Alignment 

Ryota Kanai Araya, Inc. 10 October 2026 

Abstract 

Studies of data scaling in electroencephalography (EEG) and related noninvasive recordings suggest that simple wearable devices could eventually reveal more about cognitive states. However, publicly available EEG data are difficult to combine. Different laboratories record different tasks, participants, and devices. Task-agnostic self-supervised learning can exploit such data, but representations learned only with EEG signals have not consistently produced feature representations that are directly effective for decoding the cognitive state. We therefore aimed to incorporate task information by embedding experimental instructions, stimuli and response demands in a common semantic space, and trained an EEG encoder to align signals from a single anterior–posterior differential channel with those task vectors. This framework supports zero-shot prediction without target-condition calibration and few-shot prediction using labeled samples. On a reserved ten-class evaluation, the highest observed zero-shot balanced accuracy was 30.13% (chance 10%). In a separate developmental few-shot experiment, balanced accuracy reached 34.47% using 16 labeled EEG examples per class from another participant, and 74.99% using examples from the test participant’s own task recording. Our experiments suggest that training models on datasets with multiple tasks per participant appeared useful, whereas adding datasets containing only one or a few tasks sometimes reduced established performance despite broader task coverage. These exploratory results suggest that EEG datasets that repeat varied cognitive tasks and everyday activities within the same individuals across days, devices and independent studies would be particularly useful for training an EEG foundation model for predicting cognitive states. 

Keywords: electroencephalography; task-semantic alignment; zero-shot decoding; few-shot calibration; data integration; cognitive tasks. 

# 1. Introduction 

The amount of neural data available for machine learning is increasing, and recent decoding studies in electroencephalography (EEG) show measurable benefits from larger training sets. Sato and colleagues recorded 175 hours of speech-production EEG from one person: in their zero-shot speech-segment retrieval task, Top-1 accuracy increased from approximately 2.5% with about ten training hours to 48% with the larger dataset [1]. A subsequent report extended the recording and calibration analysis [2]. Inoue and colleagues showed that heterogeneous electrode configurations can contribute to speech decoding [3], then examined training-data expansion for a wearable, around-ear EEG system [4]. Meta’s research has similarly combined public electroencephalography 1arXiv:2610.15104v1 [q-bio.NC] 10 Oct 2026 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

and magnetoencephalography recordings to decode perceived speech [5], and studied typed-sentence decoding using EEG and magnetoencephalography [6]. A recent magnetoencephalography-only extension reports further gains with recording volume [7]. Related scaling trends have been documented for decoding visual stimuli from brain activity [8]. These findings motivate the possibility that once powerful foundation models are established for EEG, simpler, consumer-oriented devices may also become more useful for decoding the cognitive states from EEG. The principal obstacle to increasing task-labeled EEG data is heterogeneity. Publicly available EEG datasets were acquired for different scientific questions, with different participants, electrodes, reference choices and environments. Their labels also describe different things: a stimulus type, a response rule, a memory operation or a particular period of a task. Consequently, pooling waveforms and treating all study-specific labels as a single categorical target does not produce a coherent learning problem. The Cognitive Atlas distinguishes experimental tasks from the psychological constructs they are intended to engage; text–neuroimaging resources describe related cross-study associations [9–11]. In the present study, we use task semantics to make these differences explicit. Each source task is described using its instructions, sensory material, required response and evaluated phase. Asentence encoder was used for mapping the detailed and structured descriptions to a shared task vector. An EEG encoder is then trained to align the corresponding signal segments with these vectors. Given a bank of candidate descriptions, the same encoder can rank tasks without fitting a new classifier for every source. We call this zero-shot prediction when no labeled EEG from the target evaluation condition is used to calibrate the readout. This does not necessarily imply that the participant, study and cognitive operation are all unfamiliar. We examine those forms of novelty separately. Few-shot prediction uses a small set of labeled EEG examples to adapt the task readout. Our few-shot experiments test utilized data from other participants or from the test participant’s own recording to adapt the task readout for specific test datasets or participants. Self-supervised learning (SSL) offers a complementary route to integrating EEG without relying on task annotations. BENDR, LaBraM, CBraMod and REVE demonstrate the value of pretraining under different downstream protocols [12–15]. SingLEM encodes each channel separately and combines the resulting features in downstream classification [16]. However, the advantage of frozen representations under simple linear probing varies across datasets and adaptation methods [17, 18]. Work on speech decoding from magnetoencephalography demonstrates that self-supervised representations can also transfer across people and recording contexts [19]. Information retained by a signal-level objective need not be the information required to separate cognitive tasks; recent work also documents spectral and participant-related biases in some reconstruction-based models [20]. This is a motivation to test explicit task supervision, rather than evidence that self-supervised approaches are ineffective. Our initial masked-latent SSL pilots used approximately 585 hours of unique EEG recordings. A supervised ten-task evaluation changed from 34.41% to 35.41% balanced accuracy after pretraining, whereas a separate six-task evaluation changed from 20.86% to 20.09%. These pilot results are summarized in Supplement S0. Since we did not obtain clear benefits from pretraining with SSL, we trained the task-semantic models from fresh initialization, while recognizing that signal-derived and task-derived objectives could also be combined [21]. Language provides a practical interface for this supervision. Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP) learned image representations from natural-language supervision [22]. EEG-CLIP uses clinical descriptions; NeuroLM, LEAF and EEG-PRIME use language alignment or instruction to support different EEG decoding settings [23–26]. transfer across tasks and people [27]. Our focus is the integration of diverse cognitive-task protocols under a physical one-channel constraint. Table 1 distinguishes this problem from these related approaches. For this research, the key question is what kind of additional training data improves transfer. More EEG hours, more people, new task types and repetitions of the same task in different laboratories are distinct resources. A multi-task experiment can provide several task contrasts from the same participants under approximately shared acquisition conditions. By contrast, a study devoted to a single task may expand the vocabulary of operations represented during training, but task identity can also become inseparable from that study’s participants and recording setup. We therefore tested both an expanded task vocabulary and mixtures anchored in multi-task datasets. This approach asks whether cross-study task-semantic learning can improve zero-shot performance, and whether the representation is useful when a few labeled target-context examples are available. The experiments investigate these hypotheses without fitting a universal EEG scaling exponent. A common output space also creates an interpretive problem. If each study contributes a unique label, source recognition may be sufficient for correct classification. Within a study, task blocks can differ in stimuli, responses and recording time [28]. Decoding a label consequently need not identify a selective cognitive process [29, 30]. Candidate-set composition, model selection and repeated inspection of evaluation data further affect the conclusion [31–36]. We examine both the gains obtained by pooling tasks and the distinctions those gains support. We report developmental scaling and calibration results, a model panel fixed before scoring, and subsequent diagnostic analyses.

# 2. Study scope and evaluation structure 

2.1 The task space is the common interface 

Each source task has a free-form rich description and a field-structured canonical description . The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence encoder maps each to 384 coordinates [37–39]. The primary centroid readout unit-normalizes the two vectors, sums them and normalizes the result. A learned projection maps the EEG representation into the same space; prediction selects the candidate with greatest cosine similarity. Methods 5.2–5.5 give the transformations and objectives. The task register links source-native labels through verified functional task (VFT) identifiers. These are protocol-based equivalence assignments, not validated latent cognitive factors. They determine which EEG examples share a target during training. The continuous task vectors additionally define relations among targets and permit a new candidate bank for inference. Dataset identifiers serve as provenance fields rather than intended input to the text encoder. Figure 1 displays the 44 primary-training task prototypes represented in the Bridge3 mixture, defined in Section 2.2, from its frozen bank using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). The original-space cosine similarities include 0.918 for motor execution and motor imagery, 0.960 for verbal memory manipulation and retention, and 0.884 for active and passive auditory oddball tasks. These values describe text representations: proximity does not establish neural similarity or successful EEG discrimination. The full cosine matrix, nearest-neighbor table and projection sensitivity analysis are in Supplement S8. The coordinates are used only for display.

The experiments below used several training configurations, each designed to address a different question about how to build a generalizable EEG model. They differ either in the composition of the training data, the amount of training, or the method used to align EEG representations with semantic descriptions of cognitive tasks. We first investigated whether adding datasets covering a wider range of cognitive tasks would improve generalization to unseen studies and tasks. The ST19-MATCHED configuration (abbreviated as ST19 in figures) combined an existing core of heterogeneous EEG recordings, including studies in which the same participants performed multiple tasks, with 19 additional datasets, many containing only one or a few tasks. This configuration comprised 38 dataset identifiers in total. We then extended this approach to ST40 , which included 40 additional datasets relative to the original core, giving 59 dataset identifiers. The motivation was to increase the diversity of cognitive tasks represented during training. However, adding datasets in which each study contributes only a small number of tasks can also strengthen the association between task identity and study-specific recording characteristics. The labels ST19 and ST40 refer to the number of added datasets. A separate Full-Mixture (FM) experiment addressed a different question of whether performance would continue to improve with more optimization when the available training data remained unchanged. Instead of progressively adding datasets, this experiment used a fixed mixture of tasks and studies and extended the training duration. This allowed us to examine the effect of additional optimization steps without simultaneously changing the composition of the training data. The Bridge3 configuration was designed to explore a more selective alternative to broad dataset expansion. Starting from the ST19-MATCHED mixture, which had shown promising performance during development, we added three datasets chosen to extend cognitive-task coverage while limiting disruption to the original training distribution. During training, approximately 95% of primary EEG samples were drawn from the ST19-MATCHED mixture and 5% from the three additional datasets. Bridge3 therefore tested whether a small, targeted expansion could retain the benefits of the original multi-task-rich mixture while introducing additional task types. Finally, we compared two approaches to learning EEG representations using the Bridge3 data. In the fixed-text approach, semantic task vectors derived from task descriptions remained fixed, and the EEG encoder was trained to map EEG segments into this predefined task space. In the CLIP-X approach, the same Bridge3 primary-data mixture and native four-second auxiliary data were used, but the semantic representations were also adapted during training. Specifically, CLIP-X introduced a residual text adapter, a learned similarity scale, and regularization terms encouraging preservation of task-space geometry and consistency across studies. Both approaches used bidirectional, multi-positive contrastive alignment between EEG and task representations; CLIP-X was therefore a broader modification of the learning procedure, rather than simply the introduction of a contrastive objective. The suffix X denotes the additional cross-study consistency term in the original implementation. 2.3 Development, reserved evaluation and source structure 

We began by developing and comparing models using two publicly available EEG datasets that contain several experimental task conditions. The first, EEG-Asymmetries (ds007172) [40, 41], includes different perceptual and cognitive tasks. The second, ERP CORE [42], contains recordings from several established experimental paradigms. Together, these datasets formed our initial development benchmark, which we call D1 . Using datasets with multiple task conditions was particularly useful because it allowed us to compare EEG recorded during different tasks within the same data source, rather than relying entirely on differences between studies. Early evaluations distinguished five conditions from EEG-Asymmetries and six from ERP CORE. In later experiments, we combined two closely related face-target conditions into one category, giving ten task classes in total: four from EEG-Asymmetries and six from ERP CORE. Although these two datasets provided a useful starting point, they covered only a limited range of cognitive tasks. To test whether the models could generalize to a broader variety of activities, we assembled a second development benchmark from six additional studies. Each study contributed one evaluated task, including activities not represented in the original benchmark. We call this extension D1-ST , where ST refers to the single-task sources used in this evaluation. Including these studies allowed us to test more diverse cognitive tasks, but also introduced an important difficulty: because each task came from a different source, task identity could be confounded with differences in participants, equipment, or recording conditions. We therefore used two versions of this benchmark. 

BROAD6 included all six tasks, whereas CLEAN4 was restricted to the four tasks that satisfied stricter criteria for selecting valid EEG intervals. The tasks and selection criteria are described in Table S2, and their source references are provided in Table S16. Both of these benchmarks were used during model development. We compared different architectures, training datasets, and learning procedures on them, and used the results to guide subsequent experiments, including the selection of training data for Bridge3. To obtain an evaluation that was not used for these development decisions, we then assembled a separate benchmark, which we call D2 . It contained ten cognitive-task classes drawn from seven dataset identifiers. The final evaluation comprised 87,058 nonoverlapping eight-second EEG segments and 10,597 eligible 64-second groups. Participant counts reported across the seven sources sum to 290, although this should not be interpreted as 290 verified unique individuals, because identities cannot always be matched across independently distributed datasets. Table 2 summarizes the datasets and task conditions included in this benchmark. Across these benchmarks, we evaluated whether a model could identify a cognitive task without being calibrated on labeled EEG examples from the particular condition being tested. We refer to this as 

calibration-free or zero-shot (ZS) prediction. The model receives an EEG recording and descriptions of K candidate tasks, and predicts which description best matches the recording. Labeled EEG examples from the target condition are not used to fit a new output classifier. However, zero-shot prediction can involve different degrees of novelty. We distinguish three cases: ZS-Study , in which the recording comes from an unseen study, but the task is represented in training; ZS-Task , in which the task is new but other tasks from the same study were included in the training; and ZS-Both ,in which neither the study nor an equivalent task was included in the training, according to our predefined task-equivalence rules. These categories describe the novelty of studies and tasks, not necessarily of individual participants. Indeed, two task classes in D2 include participants whose EEG was used during training on other tasks. 7Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

The evaluation procedure also differed between the development benchmarks and D2. For D2, we fixed the set of candidate models, task classes, and evaluation procedure before examining model scores. Some participant eligibility criteria were subsequently amended without access to those scores. After the D2 results became available, we conducted further analyses to understand the observed performance patterns. These included combining task classes across benchmarks, examining discrimination between tasks recorded within the same study, merging semantically related task labels, harmonizing evaluation metrics, and revisiting statistical comparisons. Because these analyses were formulated after D2 scoring, we regard them as exploratory rather than independent confirmation of model performance. The recordings used in these experiments were obtained from public EEG repositories. Dataset identifiers beginning with ds refer to OpenNeuro, while those beginning with nm refer to native records distributed through the Neuroelectromagnetic Data Archive and Tools Resource (NEMAR). The prefix on identifies OpenNeuro datasets mirrored through NEMAR. Importantly, these identifiers indicate data distributions rather than necessarily independent studies. Some records are converted or mirrored versions of previously released datasets, and may contain recordings from the same participants. We therefore tracked the provenance of the dataset and source relationships when assembling the training and evaluation sets. # 3. Results 

3.1 Models that performed well during development did not necessarily perform well on new evaluation data 

Our first question was whether models that performed well during development would also perform well when evaluated on different cognitive tasks and EEG datasets. To examine this, we compared nine model configurations on two benchmarks, each requiring the model to choose among ten possible cognitive tasks. The first benchmark, D1, had been used during model development, whereas the second, D2, was reserved for evaluating a predefined set of candidate models. The two benchmarks contained different tasks and recording sources. In both cases, the model was given an eight-second segment of single-channel EEG and had to identify the most likely task from ten candidate descriptions, without using labeled EEG from the evaluated condition to train a new classifier. The nine configurations differed in three main ways. First, they were trained on different mixtures of EEG datasets, allowing us to examine whether including more studies and cognitive tasks improved generalization. Second, we compared models using only the raw EEG waveform (RAW) with models combining raw EEG and time–frequency representations (LATE). Third, we compared models that mapped EEG into a fixed semantic task space with models that also adapted the task representations during training (CLIP-X). These comparisons allowed us to examine whether the benefits of different training strategies were consistent across the development and reserved benchmarks. 9Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

To compare performance on the same basis, we recomputed the D1 results after D2 scoring using nonoverlapping EEG windows and the same weighting procedure used for D2. We measured performance using balanced accuracy (BA), which gives equal weight to each task class and, within each class, to each participant (Methods 5.6). Because both benchmarks involved ten task classes, chance-level balanced accuracy was 10% in each case. Figure 3 compares the mean performance of the nine model configurations on D1 and D2. Surprisingly, models that performed well on D1 were not necessarily the best models on D2. The correlation between their rankings was very weak (Spearman ρ = 0 .083). This finding applies to the nine configurations examined here and does not establish that development benchmarks are generally unreliable. One example concerns the composition of the training data. The ST19 training mixture combined multiple-task EEG datasets with additional sources, whereas ST40 expanded the collection to cover a wider variety of studies and cognitive tasks, including more sources containing only one or a few tasks. We expected that broader task coverage might improve generalization. However, on D2, the RAW model trained with ST19 for 64,000 optimizer updates achieved 22.66% balanced accuracy, compared with 21.88% for the corresponding ST40 model (Table 3). Thus, expanding the training collection did not improve performance in this comparison. Because several aspects of the training mixture changed together, this result does not establish that adding single-task datasets was itself responsible for the difference. A second example concerns how the EEG signal was represented. Within the Bridge3 configuration, we compared a model using only raw EEG with one that additionally incorporated time–frequency features (i.e. LATE model). Both used fixed semantic task vectors. In the development benchmark D1, the LATE model performed worse than RAW (17.11% versus 23.11% balanced accuracy). However, in D2, the result was reversed: LATE achieved 30.13%, compared to 23.98% for RAW. Thus, the apparent benefit of incorporating spectral information strongly depended on which tasks and datasets were used for evaluation. Finally, we examined whether allowing the semantic task representations to adapt during training improved performance. Compared with the fixed-text Bridge3 LATE model, CLIP-X LATE improved balanced accuracy on D1 by 3.77 percentage points but reduced it on D2 by 18.34 points. The paired participant-bootstrap 95% confidence interval for the difference in D2 was −20 .50 to −16 .19 percentage points, conditional on the sources evaluated and the trained models. The standard deviation of the difference across the three training seeds was 6.64 points. Thus, an approach that appeared beneficial on the development benchmark could substantially underperform on a different collection of tasks. Together, these results show that the performance of the model was sensitive to the choice of evaluation data. Increasing the diversity of training datasets, adding spectral features, or adapting semantic task representations did not produce improvements that were consistent across D1 and D2. This motivated us to examine which tasks the models could recognize reliably and how much of their apparent task-prediction ability might reflect differences between studies and recording conditions. Table 3 and Figure 4 report all nine conditions. Fixed-text Bridge3 LATE had the highest observed mean: 30.13 ± 1.58% BA, 25.65% macro-F1 (the mean of per-class precision–recall F1 scores), and 76.57% Top-5 accuracy. Its participant-bootstrap interval for BA was 28.28–32.11%. The jointly adapted RAW and LATE models varied more across training seeds; in each architecture, two of three seeds were below the 10% BA baseline. This comparison changes a training package. In addition to text adaptation, the implementations differ in similarity scale, regularization and prototype-bank construction. 3.2 The leading model recognized some tasks much better than others 

The previous analysis showed that the relative performance of different models changed substantially between the development and reserved benchmarks. We next asked a different question: how well could the best-performing model on D2 recognize each of the ten cognitive tasks? This distinction matters because a model could achieve above-chance accuracy by recognizing a few tasks particularly well, even if it performed poorly on most others. We examined the fixed-text Bridge3 LATE model, which achieved the highest overall balanced accuracy on D2 (30.13%, compared with 10% chance). Balanced accuracy gives equal weight to the ten task classes, so each class contributes one-tenth of its recall to the final score. Figure 5 shows these contributions separately. The results were highly uneven. The model recognized the go/no-go recognition task with 97.22% recall, which alone contributed 9.72 percentage points to the overall balanced accuracy. Together, go/no-go recognition, auditory oddball, and rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) language processing contributed 20.98 of the 30.13 percentage points, accounting for 69.63% of the total score. In contrast, the remaining seven classes contributed only 9.15 percentage points, corresponding to an average recall of approximately 13.1%, only slightly above the 10% chance level. One important qualification concerns the go/no-go recognition task, which accounted for the largest contribution. Although this task itself had been excluded from training, its participants 12 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

had previously contributed EEG recordings from other tasks. The same was true of the surprise-recognition class. Thus, these two classes tested generalization to new tasks, but not necessarily to entirely new participants. To examine how strongly the overall result depended on these classes, we recalculated mean recall after excluding them from the average. Performance across the remaining eight classes was 21.75%. Importantly, the model still had to choose among the original ten candidate task descriptions for every prediction. This was therefore not an eight-class classification problem, and the chance-level baseline remained 10%. We also examined which task labels the model tended to predict, regardless of whether those predictions were correct. This revealed another important pattern. When each true class was given equal weight, the model assigned 32.88% of predictions to auditory oddball, far more than the 10% expected if predictions were distributed uniformly across the ten labels. However, only 11.59% of predictions assigned to auditory oddball were correct. RSVP language processing was also selected frequently, accounting for 19.27% of predictions, although its precision was higher, at 38.64% (Table S8). In other words, the model did not simply fail to recognize some tasks; it frequently classified them as a small set of preferred alternatives. The confusion patterns make this more concrete. 3.3 Distinguishing cognitive tasks within the same study remains difficult 

The previous analysis showed that the model could recognize some tasks much better than others. However, many of the evaluated tasks came from different studies, raising an important question of whether the model recognized differences in cognitive activity, or partly distinguished the studies in which the EEG had been recorded. Differences in recording equipment, participants, and experimental environments could provide clues to task identity when each task comes from a different source. To investigate this possibility, we examined datasets in D2 that contained more than one task condition. We restricted the candidate labels to tasks recorded within the same dataset, so identifying the data source alone would no longer be sufficient to make a correct prediction. This analysis used the existing trained models without further training or calibration. We examined two comparisons: thinking versus breath-counting meditation, and three driving conditions that differed in their experimental demands. Unlike the original ten-class evaluation, these were two-class and three-class classification problems, with chance levels of 50% and 33.33%, respectively. Because these comparisons were formulated after examining the D2 results, they should be regarded as exploratory. We first evaluated the fixed-text Bridge3 LATE model, which had achieved the highest overall balanced accuracy in the ten-class D2 evaluation. For thinking versus meditation, its balanced accuracy was 50.34%, almost identical to the 50% chance level (Figure 6). For the three driving conditions, balanced accuracy was 34.43%, compared with 33.33% chance (Figure 7). Thus, the model’s above-chance performance in the broader ten-class evaluation did not translate into reliable discrimination between these particular conditions recorded within the same source. Across all nine models, balanced accuracy ranged from 50.05% to 52.73% for thinking versus meditation, and from 32.90% to 35.21% for the driving conditions. One model, FM LATE, showed a small above-chance effect for driving, with a participant-bootstrap interval of 33.63–37.01%. We therefore do not regard this isolated result as strong evidence of generalizable within-study task discrimination. There are at least two possible explanations for these near-chance results. A second possibility is that the task conditions examined here are themselves difficult to distinguish from short single-channel EEG recordings. Their differences in attention or mental state may therefore produce EEG changes that are subtle, variable across individuals, or difficult to detect from a single anterior–posterior differential channel. Similarly, the three driving conditions may differ in the level of driver engagement or assistance while sharing much of the same visual, motor, and attentional context. Such differences could be meaningful at the behavioral level without producing consistently distinguishable EEG patterns in eight-second windows. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the present analysis cannot determine their relative contributions. Longer recordings, additional electrodes, or measurements targeting specific task-related processes might reveal differences that this evaluation could not detect. It is also important not to generalize these findings to all within-study task comparisons. However, such performance may partly reflect differences in stimuli, responses, eye movements, or experimental blocks, rather than cognitive processes alone. For example, ERP CORE paradigms were recorded in separate blocks [28, 42]. Together, these results illustrate two challenges for learning cognitive representations from het-erogeneous EEG datasets. When different studies contribute different tasks, a model may exploit source-specific information. When several tasks are recorded within the same study, their neural signatures may nevertheless be too similar or too weakly measured to distinguish reliably. Building models that generalize across studies will require training data that address both problems: record-ings of multiple well-characterized tasks within participants, together with repeated measurements of comparable tasks across independent studies. 

3.4 Adding more training studies broadens task coverage, but improvements are uneven 

A central motivation of this study was to take advantage of the large amount of EEG data already available from different research groups. Most of these datasets were collected for different scientific purposes and contain different cognitive tasks. Our task-semantic alignment approach was designed to make it possible to learn from these heterogeneous recordings within a common framework. We therefore asked a simple question: does adding more studies and a wider variety of tasks to the training data improve the model’s ability to recognize cognitive tasks in recordings not used for training? We first investigated this question in an early experiment conducted during model development. Starting with a fixed collection of EEG datasets, we progressively added 5, 10, 15, and finally all 19 additional datasets that had passed our data-quality and eligibility checks. We refer to these five training mixtures as SC0 , SC5 , SC10 , SC15 , and SCALL , respectively. These names indicate the 16 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

number of datasets added to the original collection, rather than the total number of independent studies used for training. To make the comparison interpretable, we evaluated every mixture on the same development benchmark, D1, using its ten cognitive-task classes and nonoverlapping eight-second EEG segments. We repeated training with three random seeds and examined performance after 16,000, 32,000, and 64,000 optimization updates. This allowed us to examine whether adding training datasets was beneficial across different stages of learning. The overall result was encouraging, but not straightforward (Figure 8). After 16,000 updates, mean balanced accuracy increased from 18.38% with the original training mixture to 24.89% with all 19 additional datasets, an improvement of 6.50 percentage points calculated from the unrounded values. The largest mixture also outperformed the original mixture at later checkpoints: 23.18% versus 19.37% after 32,000 updates, and 24.32% versus 18.01% after 64,000 updates. Thus, the expanded training collection produced consistently higher development scores than the original collection at these three checkpoints. However, adding datasets did not produce a steady improvement at every stage. In particular, increasing the number of added datasets from 10 to 15 reduced balanced accuracy by 2.27, 1.98, and 3.41 percentage points at the three checkpoints, respectively. Performance then recovered when all 19 datasets were included. These fluctuations suggest that the effect of adding data depends not only on how much is added, but potentially also on which studies and tasks are included. Importantly, this experiment changed several properties of the training data simultaneously, including task coverage, participants, and sampling exposure. It therefore does not establish which of these factors caused the observed changes. These results raised a more fundamental question about what it means to increase the diversity of EEG training data. Adding a new dataset can introduce a cognitive task that the model has never encountered before. This expands the range of activities represented in training. However, if that task appears in only one study, the model may also learn characteristics specific to that study, its participants, or its recording equipment. In contrast, when comparable tasks are recorded in multiple studies, the model has an opportunity to learn which EEG features are shared across different recording conditions. Similarly, datasets containing multiple tasks provide information about how EEG changes between activities while some aspects of the recording context are held relatively constant. Therefore, we examined the structure of the later training collections, rather than considering only their total number of datasets (Table 4). For this analysis, we distinguished dataset identifiers, independent study families, and registered cognitive-task categories (VFTs). These counts capture different aspects of the available training information. The ST19-MATCHED mixture contained 38 dataset identifiers representing 32 study families and 41 VFTs. The larger ST40 mixture contained 59 dataset identifiers, 53 families, and 59 VFTs. 3.5 Few-shot calibration improves task prediction, particularly with examples from the same participant 

So far, we have evaluated how well the models could identify cognitive tasks without using labeled EEG examples from the target condition. Although some tasks could be recognized above chance, performance was often limited and varied considerably across tasks. However, poor zero-shot prediction does not necessarily mean that the learned EEG representations contain no useful information about cognitive activity. Some of that information might become easier to extract if a small number of labeled EEG examples were available. This possibility is also important for practical applications. For example, a person using a simple EEG device might be willing to perform a few known activities to calibrate the system. We therefore 19 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

investigated a few-shot setting, in which the model was given a small number of labeled EEG examples to help identify subsequent recordings. We asked two questions: could examples from another participant improve prediction, and would examples from the person being evaluated provide an additional benefit? We examined these questions using an earlier model trained on the fully expanded dataset mixture (SCALL), after 16,000 optimizer updates. The analysis used one training seed and the ten-task D1 development benchmark, rather than the reserved D2 benchmark. Each prediction was made from a 64-second EEG recording, while calibration examples consisted of labeled eight-second EEG windows. We varied the number of calibration examples, referred to as support windows , available for each task class. We compared two ways of selecting these examples. In the cross-participant condition, the support examples came from one participant in each target study, and predictions were evaluated on different participants. This tested whether a small amount of labeled EEG from the relevant study could improve prediction for other people. In the same-participant condition, support examples came from the person being evaluated. Support and query recordings were selected to avoid any overlap in their raw EEG samples, although they still came from the same task recording. The results showed a clear difference between these two settings (Figure 9). Without labeled support examples, the model achieved 26.50% balanced accuracy on the ten-task benchmark, compared with a 10% chance level. With cross-participant support, balanced accuracy was 30.22%, 32.35%, 34.47%, and 35.91% with 1, 4, 16, and 64 labeled windows per available class, respectively. Thus, examples from another participant were associated with a modest improvement. The results were substantially higher when the support examples came from the same participant. Balanced accuracy reached 47.24% with just one labeled window per available class, increased to 65.84% with four windows, and reached 74.99% with sixteen. These results suggest that a small amount of person-specific calibration can make task distinctions much easier to read out from the learned EEG representation. The large benefit associated with same-participant support is encouraging, but its interpretation requires care. These calibration and evaluation examples came from the same task recording, even though overlapping EEG samples were excluded. The model could therefore benefit from participant-specific patterns, recording conditions, or other features shared between support and query data, rather than solely from cognitive-task information. Furthermore, increasing the number of support windows reduced the pool of eligible queries, so the reported differences do not constitute a controlled comparison on an identical set of test recordings. This was also a developmental analysis using only one trained seed. Its 26.50% zero-support result differs from the corresponding three-seed mean of 28.98% for 64-second queries. The findings should therefore not be interpreted as independent validation of a personalized model, or as evidence that similar performance would be obtained across recording sessions or days. Nevertheless, these results suggest a promising direction for practical EEG applications. A model trained across many studies may provide a shared representation of cognitive activity, while a small amount of labeled EEG from an individual could help adapt predictions to that person’s signals. This possibility is especially relevant to low-burden wearable EEG, where perfect zero-shot decoding may be difficult to achieve. 3.6 Grouping related cognitive tasks reveals information beyond exact task labels 

The previous analyses evaluated whether the models could identify the exact cognitive task associated with an EEG recording. However, tasks used in different experiments are not always completely distinct in terms of the cognitive processes they involve. Two studies may use different instructions, stimuli, or response procedures while engaging similar forms of attention, memory, or decision-making. A model might therefore confuse two closely related tasks while still capturing something meaningful about their shared cognitive demands. We asked whether this possibility could be examined by grouping semantically related tasks into broader categories. Rather than requiring the model to distinguish every individual task, we evaluated whether it could identify the broader type of cognitive activity. We refer to this process as semantic coarsening , because it replaces fine-grained task labels with coarser categories based on their meaning. For this exploratory analysis, we combined the task labels from D1 and D2 into a single evaluation bank containing 20 candidate tasks. The best model on this combined bank, fixed-text Bridge3 LATE, 21 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

achieved 14.59% balanced accuracy using eight-second EEG segments, compared with 5% chance. We then constructed three alternative task groupings containing 15, 13, or 8 broader categories, denoted F15, F13, and F8, respectively. When predictions were made using the corresponding merged semantic task prototypes, the leading balanced accuracies were 15.57%, 19.49%, and 29.30%. These results suggest that the model can more readily distinguish broader task categories than individual tasks. However, this improvement must be interpreted carefully. Reducing the number of categories also makes the classification problem easier: uniform chance increases from 5% for 20 classes to 6.67%, 7.69%, and 12.5% for F15, F13, and F8, respectively. Furthermore, some distinctions that were difficult in the earlier analyses disappear when tasks are grouped together. In particular, F8 no longer requires the model to distinguish thinking from meditation or the individual driving conditions. Higher accuracy on these broader categories therefore does not necessarily indicate improved recognition of fine-grained cognitive states. We next asked a more specific question: do the improvements depend on grouping tasks according to their cognitive meaning, or could similar improvements arise from combining labels arbitrarily? This distinction is especially important because task identity is strongly associated with recording source in our datasets. If a grouping simply combines tasks from particular studies, its apparent benefit might reflect study-specific signals rather than cognitive similarity. To address this issue, we compared the semantic groupings with randomized groupings that preserved the same relationship to recording sources. We kept each model’s original 20-class predictions unchanged and reassigned task labels only within their source datasets. This procedure preserved both the number of original task labels in each group and the exact number contributed by each source. It therefore allowed us to ask whether the chosen semantic groupings agreed with the model’s existing predictions better than alternative groupings with the same source composition. We generated 10,000 randomized groupings for each partition and evaluated this control across all nine models (Table S12). For fixed-text Bridge3 LATE, the results favored the semantic groupings (Figure 10). With the 13-category grouping (F13), assigning the original predictions to their broader semantic categories yielded 19.89% balanced accuracy. The mean across source-conditioned random groupings was 15.71%, a difference of 4.18 percentage points. Only 0.25% of the randomized groupings reached or exceeded the observed score, under the counting convention described in Methods 5.7. The same pattern appeared at the other levels of grouping. For F15, the observed accuracy was 16.26%, compared with 14.07% for the source-conditioned random groupings, a difference of 2.19 points. For F8, the corresponding values were 28.14% and 24.23%, a difference of 3.91 points. Thus, grouping tasks according to their semantic relationships provided a modest advantage over randomized groupings that preserved the same source composition. These results should be distinguished from the merged-prototype evaluation described above. In that evaluation, the model selected among newly constructed representations of the broader task categories. In the randomization analysis, the original 20-class predictions were held fixed and only their assigned category labels were changed. The two procedures therefore answer different questions and need not produce identical accuracy values. These findings provide tentative evidence that some of the model’s errors are structured by re-lationships between task descriptions, rather than being entirely arbitrary. # 4. Discussion 

4.1 What task-semantic alignment achieved, and what remains unresolved 

The main goal of this study was to find a way to learn from EEG recordings collected for different cognitive experiments. These recordings are abundant, but their task labels are difficult to combine because each study uses different instructions, stimuli, and response requirements. We approached this problem by representing the tasks themselves in a shared semantic space and training an EEG encoder to predict those representations. This provides a common prediction framework in which recordings from different studies can contribute to learning without requiring every experiment to use the same task labels. The results show both the promise and the limitations of this approach. The best model achieved 30.13% balanced accuracy when choosing among ten cognitive tasks in the reserved evaluation, compared with 10% chance. This demonstrates above-chance task prediction from a single anterior– posterior differential EEG channel without fitting a new classifier using labeled examples from the evaluated condition. However, the result should not be interpreted as reliable recognition of ten cognitive states. Performance differed substantially across tasks, nearly 70% of the total balanced-accuracy score came from three classes, and two evaluated classes included participants whose EEG had been used during training on other tasks. The models also struggled to distinguish the thinking and meditation conditions and the three driving conditions when predictions were restricted to tasks recorded within the same source. A second important finding was that the model’s apparent quality depended strongly on the evaluation data. Models that performed well on the development benchmark were not necessarily the best on the reserved benchmark. In addition, grouping related task labels revealed that some prediction errors were consistent with semantic relationships between tasks, even after controlling for the distribution of those labels across recording sources. This suggests that the representation may contain information about broader task similarities, but it does not establish that the model has learned neural representations of cognitive operations that are invariant across people and studies. The few-shot results offer a complementary perspective. When labeled examples were provided from the person being evaluated, task prediction improved markedly. This suggests that the learned representation may contain information that is difficult to use through a fixed, description-based readout but easier to access with participant-specific calibration. Nevertheless, the strongest calibration results used support and query examples from the same task recording and therefore cannot establish generalization to a new session or day. Taken together, these findings distinguish three different achievements: learning an EEG representa-tion from heterogeneous task-labeled data, predicting tasks without target-condition calibration, and adapting predictions using a small amount of labeled EEG. The first provides a useful way to integrate existing recordings; the second remains limited and uneven; and the third is promising but requires more independent validation. Understanding how to improve each of them is a more useful goal than interpreting any single accuracy score as evidence of general cognitive-state decoding. 

4.2 Why multi-task contrasts and broader task coverage are complementary 

One of the central questions raised by our results is what kind of EEG data should be added when building a larger model. More recordings are not necessarily more informative in the same way. A new dataset may provide additional participants, new recording equipment, or an entirely new 24 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

cognitive task. Alternatively, it may provide repeated measurements of tasks already represented in the training data. These additions create different opportunities for learning, and they may also introduce different sources of bias. Studies in which the same participants perform multiple tasks are particularly valuable for learning task distinctions. Because several activities are recorded from the same individuals, often using the same device and experimental environment, the model can compare EEG patterns across tasks while some participant- and study-specific factors remain relatively stable. This creates an opportunity to learn what changes with the cognitive task rather than simply learning who was recorded or where the data came from. Such comparisons are not perfectly controlled: different tasks may still involve different stimuli, eye movements, motor responses, or recording blocks. Moreover, a model trained only on a small number of multi-task studies may encounter too narrow a range of cognitive operations and recording contexts. Datasets containing only one or a few tasks offer a different benefit. They can introduce previously unseen activities and broaden the range of task descriptions with which the EEG encoder is trained to align. They also increase the diversity of participants and recording conditions. This could be important for zero-shot prediction of tasks that were not represented in the original training collection. The difficulty is that, when a particular task appears in only one study, its identity is closely associated with the characteristics of that study. The model may learn to distinguish recording sources instead of the cognitive operations they contain. At a fixed training budget, adding many such recordings can also reduce the relative exposure to the within-participant task contrasts already present in the training mixture. Our experiments illustrate this tension. In the historical dataset-addition experiment, the largest training mixture outperformed the original mixture on the development benchmark, showing that additional heterogeneous recordings could be useful (Figure 8). However, the gains were not monotonic: some intermediate additions reduced performance. Similarly, the broader ST40 training mixture did not improve reserved-benchmark accuracy over ST19 in the corresponding RAW comparison (Table 3). These results do not demonstrate that single-task datasets are harmful, because task composition, participant coverage, sampling exposure, and other factors changed together. Rather, they show that expanding the training collection does not guarantee better transfer. The structure of the training data provides a clue to why this may happen. From ST19-MATCHED to ST40, the number of represented task categories increased from 41 to 59. Yet the number of task categories appearing in at least two registered study families remained at 16, and the number of families containing multiple task categories remained at 19. The model therefore gained access to more kinds of tasks without an increase in these two counts of repeated task observations. This does not mean that the amount of data available for each repeated task was unchanged. It does mean that broader coverage did not automatically produce broader replication of comparable tasks across studies (Table 4). The ideal strategy may therefore combine the strengths of both types of data. We need recordings in which the same people perform several tasks, allowing meaningful comparisons within individuals, as well as recordings in which comparable tasks are repeated across independent groups, devices, and environments. In such a design, the model would encounter both differences between tasks under relatively similar conditions and similarities between tasks across different conditions. The importance of separating task information from recording-source information is also illustrated by two additional analyses. Under the label structure of D2, a hypothetical classifier that knew only the source dataset could achieve as much as 70% class-balanced accuracy, despite having no ability to distinguish conditions within a source. This is a property of the evaluation set, not the performance of our EEG models. Separately, a supervised classifier trained to identify recording sources from simple EEG features achieved 78.83% balanced accuracy across nine sources, compared with 11.11% chance (Methods 5.10). This probe was trained after the reserved benchmark had been examined, and it does not show that the task models used the same features. Nevertheless, it demonstrates that source-related information can be strongly expressed in the recordings. A decisive future comparison would vary the amount of within-person task contrast and the degree of task repetition across studies while holding total EEG duration, model training budget, and other relevant factors as constant as possible. Evaluation should include both unseen studies and discrimination between tasks recorded within the same source. The goal would be to determine whether repeated evidence about the same cognitive operation across contexts improves generalization more reliably than adding new task labels alone. 

4.3 What the model learns depends on its task descriptions and EEG inputs 

Our approach assumes that descriptions of experimental tasks provide useful targets for learning EEG representations. While this is a practical assumption, the meaning assigned to a task depends on how the task was described. Two experiments may use similar instructions while differing in sensory stimulation or response demands. Conversely, two experiments engaging similar cognitive operations may be described using different terminology. The semantic vectors are therefore a structured form of supervision, not direct measurements of the cognitive processes taking place in the brain. This limitation seems to be indeed present in our study. For example, one resting-state task specifies a manual response in its canonical description but no response in its richer description. We retained the descriptions and vectors used in the original experiments so that the reported results would remain faithful to the evaluated models. Revising the annotations after observing the reserved-benchmark results and then selecting models on that basis would require a new development and evaluation cycle. 26 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

The semantic-grouping analysis provides a useful but limited indication that the chosen task descriptions capture relationships relevant to EEG prediction. Some broader semantic groupings agreed with the model’s errors more than randomized groupings with the same recording-source composition (Section 3.6). However, similar task descriptions can also reflect similar stimuli, response rules, or experimental procedures. We therefore cannot conclude from this analysis alone that the model has learned a shared neural representation of abstract cognitive functions. A stronger test would use tasks with similar intended cognitive demands but different sensory and motor implementations, evaluated in independent studies. The choice of learning architecture introduces a related uncertainty. In our experiments, both the fixed-text model and CLIP-X already used bidirectional contrastive alignment between EEG and task representations. CLIP-X additionally changed several components, including the adaptation of text vectors, the similarity scale, regularization, and candidate-task-bank construction. Its lower performance on the reserved benchmark cannot therefore be attributed solely to making the task representations trainable. Controlled comparisons that change one component at a time would be needed to identify why these approaches generalized differently. Related language-conditioned EEG models have also reported sensitivity to the supplied linguistic context [25, 26]. The EEG representation itself may impose further limits. The RAW encoder summarizes locally processed waveform features over an eight-second window using temporal averages and variation, rather than explicitly modeling the full sequence of events. Adding spectral representations provides complementary information, but may also expose differences in recording bandwidth, noise, or preprocessing. Resampling recordings to a common frequency does not remove these differences. Because the original source-level processing histories are not fully reconstructed (Table S3), we cannot completely separate task-related signals from acquisition-related effects or claim exact end-to-end reproduction of every training run. Finally, the motivation for using task-semantic supervision should not be mistaken for a conclusion that self-supervised learning is ineffective for EEG. Our earlier signal-only pretraining experiments produced only small and inconsistent benefits under the protocols we tested (Supplement S0). Other architectures, larger training collections, different adaptation methods, or combinations of self-supervised and task-supervised objectives may behave differently [12–18, 21]. The present work does not include a protocol-matched comparison with public EEG foundation models or compact supervised baselines such as EEGNet [50]. Those comparisons are necessary to establish how much of the observed performance is attributable specifically to the proposed semantic-alignment approach. 

4.4 From laboratory EEG to everyday cognitive-state prediction 

The long-term motivation for using a single EEG channel is practical. If a useful representation of cognitive activity could be obtained from a small number of electrodes, it might support devices that are easier to wear and use outside the laboratory. Our experiments therefore used one anterior– posterior differential signal formed from two physical electrode locations. This deliberately restricted input is different from attempting to maximize accuracy using all available laboratory electrodes. However, the present results do not establish that a consumer wearable can already recognize everyday cognitive states. The underlying recordings came from research datasets, and the evaluated labels referred to experimentally defined tasks. We did not test a new wearable device during normal daily activities. Furthermore, using one channel may discard neural information useful for distinguishing subtle mental states. A direct comparison between the same anterior–posterior channel, sparse multi-electrode configurations, and conventional higher-density EEG would help 27 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

measure that trade-off. Such comparisons should use the same participants, task periods, and evaluation splits so that the benefit of additional electrodes can be assessed alongside the burden of wearing them. The distinction between zero-shot and few-shot prediction is especially relevant for practical deployment. A zero-shot model could, in principle, recognize activities without requiring each user to perform an initial calibration procedure. Our present zero-shot results show that this remains difficult. Few-shot calibration offers a more realistic alternative if a person is willing to provide a small number of labeled examples. The strong same-participant developmental result is encouraging in that respect, but its support and query examples came from the same recording. We do not yet know whether similar gains would persist after the device is removed and reapplied, or when the user performs the activity on another day. A practical personalized system would need to be evaluated on later, independent sessions using the same held-out query recordings across different calibration budgets. Everyday recordings also introduce problems that are largely absent from our present evaluation. People move between activities, perform several things at once, change their goals, and encounter situations outside the model’s predefined task vocabulary. Electrode contact, motion artifacts, and recording conditions change throughout the day. A useful system would therefore need to recognize transitions, represent uncertainty, and sometimes decline to assign one of the known task labels. Its predictions would also need to be interpreted as uncertain estimates of broad activities or task demands, not direct access to a person’s moment-to-moment thoughts or clinical state. Several limitations of the current evidence reinforce this distinction between a laboratory result and a practical device. The few-shot analysis used one training seed and a changing set of eligible queries; the within-source comparisons involved only two particular sources; and the semantic-partition and source-identification analyses were exploratory. The reported participant-bootstrap intervals are conditional on the available studies and trained models, and intervals that include chance do not establish that neural information is absent. These limitations define the next questions to test rather than ruling out the possibility of useful low-burden cognitive-state prediction. 

4.5 Designing data that separate cognitive activity from recording context 

The most important implication of these findings may concern future EEG data collection. If the aim is to predict cognitive activity across people and situations, simply increasing the total number of EEG windows is unlikely to be sufficient. The training data must also make it possible to distinguish an activity from the participant, recording environment, and experimental procedure associated with it. The results on source confounding motivate repeating comparable tasks across independent studies, while the few-shot results motivate collecting repeated sessions from the same individuals. Several existing resources already contain useful elements of this design. ERP CORE provides multiple experimental paradigms [42], and the Healthy Brain Network includes a common EEG task battery [51]. The Zurich Cognitive Language Processing Corpus combines reading-related EEG with eye tracking [52]. The Brain, Body, and Behavior Dataset studies learning and attention during educational videos [53], while the Everyday Activity Science and Engineering Table Setting Dataset combines EEG with synchronized behavioral annotations [54]. These datasets show that multi-task and naturalistic EEG collection is already feasible. What is still needed for the present goal is a benchmark that combines repeated tasks within individuals, comparable operations across 28 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

independent recording environments, and evaluations designed specifically to separate cognitive information from recording context. A practical collection could begin with a small set of everyday activities performed by every participant: checking messages, reading, watching videos, writing, planning, and taking quiet breaks. Each activity would be repeated on different days, with the device removed and reapplied between sessions. The same core activities would be collected at multiple sites, while optional modules could provide additional task diversity. Controlled computer-based sessions could establish reliable task boundaries and experimental contrasts, followed by more naturalistic recordings at home or at work. Stimuli, task order, response schedules, and time of day should be varied so that the model cannot identify activities simply from a particular block or recording routine. Importantly, an everyday activity is not equivalent to a single cognitive operation. Checking messages might involve finding a fact, prioritizing requests, remembering information, or composing a reply. Watching a video might involve passive viewing, learning material for a later test, or monitoring for an event. We therefore propose varying the goal while keeping some aspects of the activity or stimulus comparable. Conversely, the same cognitive goal should be tested across different applications and sensory modalities. For example, retaining information could be studied while reading a message, reading an article, or watching a video. This would allow us to ask whether the model recognizes the cognitive demand rather than the application or stimulus format. Table 6 provides examples of these activity–goal contrasts.

Collecting the EEG signal is only part of the challenge. The labels must also specify what was observed and what was inferred. We would distinguish the activity being performed, the instruction or goal given to the participant, the sensory events and responses occurring during it, and any evidence that the intended cognitive operation was actually engaged. The temporal structure of everyday cognition also requires more detailed annotations than a single label assigned to every eight-second EEG window. Opening a message or pressing a button is an event with a particular onset, whereas maintaining information or sustaining attention may extend over an interval. Different activities can overlap, and their boundaries are often uncertain. The dataset should therefore preserve continuous EEG alongside accurately synchronized event markers and interval annotations. Our eight-second prediction task can remain a common benchmark, but shorter events and longer-lasting states should be evaluated at appropriate timescales. When testing EEG-only decoding from continuous recordings, the model must not be given the true task boundaries or behavioral logs as hidden inputs. The intended wearable signal should be recorded using the device that would actually be deployed. A subset of sessions could include simultaneous higher-density EEG to determine what information is lost when using fewer electrodes. Eye movements, muscle activity, and motion measurements would be useful for understanding alternative explanations for model predictions. However, these measurements should be used for annotation or separately reported control analyses unless the experiment explicitly evaluates a multimodal system. They must not silently become additional inputs to a model claimed to operate on EEG alone. Privacy is another central consideration for everyday activity recordings. Real messages, documents, and screen contents may reveal sensitive information about participants or third parties. Controlled sessions with study-provided materials would create a shareable reference dataset, while naturalistic sessions would require explicit consent, careful minimization of retained content, and participant review. In many cases, event timing and audited semantic annotations may be scientifically useful without releasing private message content or identifying details. The derived annotations themselves would still require privacy assessment. Finally, the evaluation must be designed to answer the questions that the present study could not resolve. Separate tests should assess unfamiliar participants, later sessions from known participants, independent study sites, novel stimuli or applications, and new combinations of activities and goals. Task and participant exposure during all forms of training, including self-supervised pretraining, should be documented. Few-shot calibration should use examples from earlier sessions or other people while keeping the future-session test set fixed. Continuous evaluation should include transitions, overlapping activities, and unknown states, not only balanced accuracy for clearly separated task blocks. The final test sets should be fixed before model selection rather than repeatedly reused during development. The decisive experiment would compare training collections that differ in the amount of within-person task diversity and cross-study task repetition while matching total EEG duration, sampling, and optimization as closely as possible. Greater diversity may improve transfer, but it may also introduce noise or new shortcuts. The relevant measure of progress is therefore performance on prespecified independent tests, not simply the number of tasks represented during training or the accuracy of distinguishing one familiar application from another. This would test the hypothesis suggested by our exploratory results: for cognitive EEG foundation models, the most useful form of scale may be repeated evidence about the same cognitive operations across people and contexts, rather than additional recordings from isolated task–study combinations. 30 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

# 5. Methods 

The methods below describe how recordings collected for different experiments were converted into a common prediction problem. We first explain how we defined the EEG input and tracked which studies and tasks had been used in training. We then describe how task descriptions and EEG segments were represented, how the two types of representations were aligned during training, and how predictions were evaluated. Finally, we detail the additional analyses used to investigate possible recording-source effects and the limits of the learned task representations. 

5.1 Defining the EEG input and tracking study and task exposure 

To compare recordings from studies using different EEG systems, we restricted the primary input to one physically measured anterior–posterior differential signal, referred to as AP2E. We formed this signal by subtracting a recorded posterior channel from a recorded anterior channel, or by using an admitted pair close to these electrode locations. The model received 4,000 samples per primary example, corresponding to eight seconds at 500 Hz. Importantly, the signal was derived from two actual electrodes; a virtual channel reconstructed by interpolation was not treated as equivalent. Source-specific preprocessing and interval selection followed the archived preprocessing records. The processing details recoverable for this release are summarized in Table S3, and should not be taken as a complete reconstruction of every source pipeline. Because the central question concerns generalization to new studies and tasks, we also tracked what each model might already have encountered during training. The composition of the reserved D2 evaluation set was revised during quality assurance without consulting model scores, and was then fixed before scoring. Two D2 task holdouts, from ds002680 and ds005189 , included participants who had contributed training EEG for other tasks. The driving conditions have different novelty classifications across models because ST40 and Full-Mixture had been exposed to driving-related tasks. Under the strict study-and-task novelty definition shared across the evaluated models, the common ZS-Both subset consists of the thinking and meditation conditions from one source. Unless we explicitly state that candidate labels were restricted, subgroup recall was computed while retaining the original ten possible task predictions. We checked the source identifiers used in the D1 and D1-ST development evaluations against the primary training indices of ST19-MATCHED, ST40 and Bridge3, as well as the common auxiliary source M3CV ( nm000166 ) [55]. None of these exact evaluation identifiers appeared in those indices (Table S2). This is a check of recorded identifiers, not proof that all participants, files or related study families were independent: such overlaps may be hidden by aliases or different processing branches. We therefore defined study and task novelty with respect to each model’s frozen training register, while recording participant exposure separately. We also used those training registers to describe how well tasks were represented within and across studies. A study-family–task edge was counted whenever an accepted eight-second training-index entry linked a registered study family to a verified functional task (VFT) category. We counted each such relationship once, regardless of how many EEG windows it contained. The VFT categories group source-native tasks according to registered functional equivalence; they may still combine conditions with different stimuli, responses or task phases. 5.3 Extracting EEG features and constructing the training mixtures 

We compared two ways of representing exactly the same one-channel EEG input. The RAW model learns features directly from the voltage waveform, whereas LATE adds a parallel representation of how signal power changes across frequencies and time. The purpose of LATE was to test whether spectral information could complement waveform features; it did not receive additional EEG electrodes. The RAW encoder consists of three one-dimensional convolutional layers with 32, 64 and 128 output channels, kernel lengths of 51, 15 and 9 samples, and strides of 5, 2 and 2. The layers use Gaussian 32 Cognitive-task prediction from EEG Preprint 

error linear unit (GELU) nonlinearities, and the input amplitude is divided by a fixed factor of 20. After convolution, we summarize the feature sequence by its mean and standard deviation over time, producing 256 features. A learned 256-to-256 projection followed by normalization creates the shared EEG representation. A separate bias-free 256-to-384 projection W maps it to the semantic task space. The convolutional stack contains 106,304 parameters, the 256-to-256 projection 65,792, and W 98,304, for 270,400 parameters along the common RAW inference path. Before the global temporal pooling, the nominal receptive field is 51 + 14 × 5 + 8 × 10 = 201 input samples. For LATE, we compute three short-time Fourier representations of the same EEG segment. We use periodic Hann windows of 256, 512 and 1,024 samples, with respective hops of 64, 128 and 256 samples; transforms are neither centered nor padded. Their configured frequency range is 0.5–100 Hz. The one-sided power spectral density (PSD) is transformed to log 10 (PSD + 10 −12 ), without fitting a separate spectral normalization to each dataset. Each resolution is processed by its own two-layer two-dimensional convolutional branch: first 1-to-8 channels with a (5 , 3) frequency-by-time kernel and (2 , 1) padding, then 8-to-16 channels with a (3 , 3) kernel and (1 , 1) padding. Both layers have stride (1 , 1), include biases, and are followed by GELU. Averaging over frequency and then pooling the temporal mean and population standard deviation produces 32 features per resolution; the variance calculation adds 10 −6 before the square root. The three spectral branches therefore supply 96 features and contain 3,888 parameters in total. We kept these protocols separate rather than combining windows across recording gaps, tasks or unrelated four-second auxiliary epochs. To describe uncertainty from the evaluated participants, we used 5,000 bootstrap resamples with random seed 20261010, introduced after D2 scoring. Resampling was stratified within the fixed source datasets, and a participant sampled for one class was carried jointly across all classes they contributed. The same resamples were used for all models, enabling paired differences. For the reported three-seed model mean, we averaged a participant’s recalls across the three fixed trained seeds before bootstrap resampling. As a result, the intervals quantify variability among the observed participants under the selected sources and trained models; standard deviations across training seeds are reported separately. Neither measure accounts for newly sampled studies, selection of the model panel, or new optimization seeds. Some supplementary comparisons were also specified after seeing D2 results. These include restricting candidate labels to conditions recorded within one source and correlating the rankings of nine model configurations between development and reserved benchmarks. Because those nine conditions were related and were not independent repetitions, we did not assign a p-value to their rank correlation. We likewise did not introduce an equivalence margin or claim multiplicity-adjusted superiority retrospectively. 5.8 Measuring few-shot calibration with labeled EEG examples 

Our main task-prediction tests required no labeled examples from the evaluated target condition. A separate historical developmental analysis asked whether the same representation would be easier to use after seeing a few labeled EEG windows. It used the full-addition model after 16,000 training updates, with training seed 42, and evaluated the original ten-class D1 task bank using 64-second queries. The labeled support sets contained 1, 4, 16 or 64 eight-second windows for each hierarchy class available in the relevant support study. Task classes absent from a support study retained their frozen text prototypes. The archived report describes adaptation using EEG centroids computed for the target study; it was not an additional self-supervised pretraining experiment. We distinguished who supplied the support recordings. In the cross-participant setting, one participant from each D1 study provided support, while the other participants supplied queries. In the same-participant setting, support examples came from the participant being evaluated. We excluded every eight-second or 64-second query sharing any raw EEG sample with a support window, although same-participant examples could still come from the same task recording. This protects against exact sample overlap but does not make the test independent of participant- or session-specific recording features. Figure 9 presents the archived fine-10 results. The 64-support same-participant point was omitted because too few eligible queries remained. Increasing support can also change which queries are eligible, so the different support budgets are not necessarily compared on identical query sets. Only one model seed was evaluated; uncertainty across training seeds was not estimated for this calibration analysis. The supplied analysis materials document the reported values and support definitions.

5.10 Measuring how easily recording sources can be identified 

Finally, we asked a diagnostic question complementary to the source-only oracle: is information about the recording source readily available in simple features of these EEG recordings? We trained a separate supervised classifier to identify the source dataset, rather than the cognitive task. This analysis was performed after D2 scores were examined and does not reveal which features the frozen task-prediction models actually used. Its aim was to measure the strength of a possible recording-source signal under an explicitly source-supervised probe. The probe distinguished nine datasets from the combined D1/D2 bank. We extracted 22 prespecified signal features from 16,960 eight-second windows belonging to 429 source-namespaced participant groups. We used at most 16 uniformly spaced eligible windows per participant and task class. Participant groups were defined within sources; matching participant numbers in different datasets were not assumed to indicate the same individual. For each EEG window, we subtracted its mean and estimated spectral power using Welch’s method at 500 Hz, with 1,000-sample segments, 500-sample overlap and a 1,024-point transform. We integrated power in nine bands: 0.5–2, 2–4, 4–8, 8–13, 13–20, 20–30, 30–45, 45–70 and 70–100 Hz. The feature vector contained nine base-10 log absolute band powers, nine log powers relative to the sum over these bands, and four base-10 log amplitude statistics: standard deviation, median absolute deviation, the 99.5th percentile of absolute amplitude and standard deviation of the first differences. Before taking logarithms, band powers were floored at 10 −10 and amplitude statistics at 10 −5. The archived D1 feature extractor restored its normalized stored signal by multiplying by 20, whereas D2 features used its processed signal directly. These differences, along with the original source-specific processing histories, are relevant when interpreting the source classifier. To avoid testing on windows from a participant group seen during classifier fitting, we used five-fold stratified group cross-validation with random seed 20261009. Each group belonged entirely to one fold. Within each training fold, we fitted a StandardScaler and then logistic regression with 

C = 1, class-balanced weights and a maximum of 1,200 iterations. The scaler was applied to the held-out fold without refitting. Source-class balanced accuracy was calculated from all out-of-fold predictions by giving equal weight to the nine source recalls. It reached 78.83%, compared with 11.11% chance for nine sources. Because this analysis explicitly used source labels in training and tested held-out participants within sources rather than held-out sources, the result is evidence that source information is detectable, not evidence that the EEG task decoder relies on the same features.

# 7. Conclusion 

Task-semantic alignment provides a common prediction interface for heterogeneous single-channel EEG recordings, supporting both zero-shot candidate ranking and few-shot calibration. Multi-task recordings offer useful within-person contrasts, while task-sparse studies can expand the range of candidate cognitive operations. Their balance remains unresolved: historical additions sometimes helped and sometimes harmed transfer, and greater task coverage did not ensure discrimination within a new study. The next test is whether independent task replication and multi-task contrasts improve generalization at a fixed data and training budget. S0. Developmental SSL pilots that motivated the semantic strategy 

The historical pilot figures below are transcribed from the dated project research summary of 28 September 2026, which records completed downstream evaluations. They are included to explain the research trajectory, not as a new external-model benchmark. The original pretraining pool comprised five named source families: PEERS/Courier [58–61], SparrKULee [62, 63], THINGS-EEG1 [64, 65], EEG Moments [66, 67] and Language ERP [68, 69], with 219 person groups and an audited union of 584.7 exposed recording hours. The pilot used a width-128, four-layer, four-head causal encoder, eight-second inputs and batch size 128. A masked-latent objective trained a student to predict an exponential-moving-average teacher representation using Smooth L1, approximately 50% span masking, 5% warm-up and cosine learning-rate decay. A fixed sequence of 4,096 batch draws was repeated four times to reach 16,384 updates. Its cumulative presentation was 4,660.34 sample-hours. S7. Analysis provenance and reproducibility boundaries 

The statistical and interpretive additions to the archived reserved evaluation comprise joint-participant uncertainty from saved D2 scores, nine-condition rank and weighting accounting, class concentration and standardized precision, and source-incidence-preserving partition diagnostics from saved twenty-class confusion matrices. Training indices and loss code were inspected without modifying model files. All these additions are post-score; the original D2 predictions and historical per-class bootstrap summaries were not overwritten. The text-only geometry analysis uses the frozen task bank and does not read EEG samples or model prediction arrays. Its complete cosine matrix and plotting inputs are included. The historical data-addition and calibration figures are redrawn from the same archived numeric values, without new training or inference. The record distinguishes three kinds of reproducibility. The included aggregate tables support checking numerical identities, rank disagreement and summary plots. The mathematical utilities and synthetic tests support inspection of metric and partition definitions. Full reproduction of the trained models and participant resampling additionally requires the source-specific data versions, accepted-window masks, participant linkage, exact per-checkpoint prototype banks and original score arrays. The present release supplies the first two levels, not complete end-to-end reproduction. Outstanding limitations include unresolved entries in the physical-input record, incomplete checkpoint-specific consolidation of bank membership and realized exposures, cross-source partic-ipant/family duplication, and incomplete external-evaluation accounting for the 240-checkpoint randomized training sweep. These gaps limit both exact reproduction and claims of independent cross-study validation.
