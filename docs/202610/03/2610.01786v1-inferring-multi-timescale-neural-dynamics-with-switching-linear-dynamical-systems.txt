## ABSTRACT 

Neural activity often exhibits multiple timescales that can vary with behavioral states and task conditions. Identifying these timescales from neural recordings is important for better understanding neural computation and function. However, traditional approaches based on autocorrelation fitting are difficult to scale to high-dimensional population recordings and can become unreliable when neu-ral dynamics change with behavior. State-space models have been a powerful framework for modeling high-dimensional neural population activity through la-tent dynamical systems, but standard formulations and inference methods do not explicitly account for multiple timescales and therefore do not guarantee accurate recovery of the underlying temporal structure. Motivated by these questions, we introduce the Multi-Timescale Switching Linear Dynamical System (MTS-SLDS), a framework for identifying regime-specific latent timescales from continuous or spiking neural observations. MTS-SLDS combines a multi-lag moment initial-ization, which captures temporal structure across multiple observation lags, with 

regime-conditioned Laplace-EM inference, which reduces mixing of dynamical statistics across uncertain regimes. Characteristic timescales can then be extracted directly from the eigenvalues of the learned latent transition matrices. In synthetic experiments with Gaussian and Poisson observations, MTS-SLDS accurately re-covers timescales and switching structure over a range of signal levels. We further apply this framework on neural spike recordings from two behavioral tasks. During a fixation task with neural activity recorded from V4, the inferred timescales agree with estimates from a dedicated Bayesian autocorrelation method. In Area 2 motor cortex recordings during a reaching task, we find that MTS-SLDS identifies behav-iorally aligned dynamical regimes and provides more interpretable regime-specific timescale estimates. These results establish MTS-SLDS as a reliable framework for investigating how neural timescales behave within and across behavioral states, and for studying their role in neural population dynamics. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Neuroscience experiments have revealed that activity from the brain evolves over multiple timescales, which reflects how neural circuits integrate information and maintain temporally extended represen-tations. Neural recordings exhibit a hierarchy of temporal correlations across cortical areas, with generally shorter timescales in sensory regions and longer timescales in association cortex (Murray et al., 2014; Soltani et al., 2021; Spitmaan et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2025). Multiple timescales also co-exist within individual populations and change with attention and working-memory demands (Zeraati et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2020; Zeraati et al., 2026). These observations connect temporal organization to neural computation: fast dynamics support sensitivity to changing inputs, whereas slower dynamics support integration and persistence (Chaudhuri et al., 2015). Recovering this temporal structure can therefore reveal computational differences that average firing rates or trajectory visualizations alone may obscure. 1arXiv:2610.01786v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 Under review Current approaches to recovering neural timescales are largely based on statistical summaries: the autocorrelation function of a recorded signal, or of a population average, is fitted along with one or more exponential decays, and the fitted decay constants are reported as timescales (Murray et al., 2014; Zeraati et al., 2022). These estimates depend on observation noise, on which projection of the population is measured, and on the assumed form of the decay; importantly, they carry no description of the dynamics that produced the correlations. Latent variable models of neural activity offer a different route (Paninski & Cunningham, 2018). They posit that the recorded population is driven by a lower-dimensional latent state, xt, that evolves according to its own dynamics, and is observed through a measurement process, such as a Gaussian readout of continuous signals or Poisson spike counts. Because this is a generative model rather than a summary statistic, it provides a richer dynamical description of the population, supports model-based prediction and held-out validation, and separates the dynamics of interest from the noise through which they are observed. In a linear dynamical system, the latent state evolves across successive time bins of width ∆t as 

xt = Ax t−1 + wt, and in a switching linear dynamical system (SLDS) a discrete regime zt selects among transition matrices A1, . . . , A K so that the dynamics can change with behavioral state. The eigenvalues of each recovered Ak then directly inform us of the timescales: a stable eigenvalue λ

decays by a factor |λ| per bin, which corresponds to a relaxation time of −∆t/ log |λ|. However, neural recordings are usually limited in duration and trial count, and with limited data the recovered dynamics are not uniquely determined. Two transition matrices with different eigenvalues can yield similar likelihoods, because the likelihood is most sensitive to the predicted observations and only indirectly to the decay rates behind them. Different fitted models can therefore reconstruct similar latent trajectories while assigning different decay rates or different switching structure to the underlying dynamics. This identifiability problem makes it difficult to interpret the eigenvalues of a fitted model as timescales, which matters when the scientific objective is to interpret the learned system rather than only predict its observations. Here, we introduce the Multi-Timescale Switching Linear Dynamical System (MTS-SLDS), a learning framework for recovering regime-specific latent timescales from multivariate Gaussian or Poisson observations. MTS-SLDS addresses the problem with two components. Firstly, a multi-lag moment initialization estimates the latent dynamics from observation moments at several lags, using moment relations specific to the Gaussian or Poisson observation model. Secondly, regime-conditioned Laplace EM (RC-L-EM) refines the model while retaining regime-specific state moments for Ak updates, so that uncertain regime assignments do not mix dynamical statistics across regimes. We evaluate MTS-SLDS against a standard SLDS in stationary and switching simulations with Gaussian and Poisson observations, across observation noise levels and mean spike counts per bin. Timescale recovery is measured directly against the ground-truth spectrum alongside latent-state reconstruction and regime identification, and the two can diverge: models with similar reconstruction accuracy can differ substantially in the timescales they recover. We then apply the framework to two different neural recordings. In V4 during sustained fixation, a single-regime model yields timescales consistent with autocorrelation-based estimates, with higher held-out likelihood and closer simulated autocorrelations than the SLDS baseline. In somatosensory area 2 during reaching, the inferred regimes align with movement onset and the regime-specific timescales agree with behavioral decay times. MTS-SLDS thus offers a principled route to characterizing how neural timescales differ across behavioral states, with the recovered eigenvalues carrying a direct biological interpretation. 1.1 RELATED WORK 

Timescale estimation Exponential function fits to neural recording autocorrelation have been widely used to discover cortical temporal organization (Murray et al., 2014; Soltani et al., 2021; Spitmaan et al., 2020; Cavanagh et al., 2016). Frequency-domain approaches provide complemen-tary estimates from aperiodic spectral knees while separating oscillatory peaks from the aperiodic background (Donoghue et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2020). Adaptive approximate Bayesian computation (aABC) improves on direct fitting by comparing simulated and observed summary statistics, address-ing finite-sample bias and quantifying uncertainty within candidate generative models (Zeraati et al., 2022). When applied to population-summed counts, both approaches characterize a scalar projection whose weighting of latent modes can obscure some temporal components. Autoregressive models can represent multiple decay rates through their poles (L¨ utkepohl et al., 2005), while multistep regression can address subsampling bias under appropriate model assumptions (Wilting & Priesemann, 2018). 2Under review A stationary autoregressive fit alone, however, does not explicitly separate latent variability from observation noise or infer changes between dynamical regimes. 

Spectral and switching state-space methods Subspace identification connects temporal obser-vation structure to state-space realizations (Overschee & Moor, 1996). Spectral learning connects observation moments to latent dynamics, including moment conversion for Poisson observations (Buesing et al., 2012). More recent methods identify dynamics shared between generalized-linear processes and explicitly evaluate dynamical eigenvalue recovery (Oganesian et al., 2024). SLDSs and recurrent extensions represent complex neural activity through locally linear regimes (Linderman et al., 2017; Zoltowski et al., 2020). Their learning procedures differ in how they approximate uncertainty: factorized variational Laplace EM uses shared continuous-state moments weighted by regime responsibilities, whereas switching filtering approaches retain regime-conditioned estimates. Related methods support heterogeneous neural observations and Poisson cubature filtering, includ-ing sPCF (Song et al., 2022; Song & Shanechi, 2023). These developments establish important foundations, but neither model flexibility nor a particular posterior approximation alone ensures spectral recovery. MTS-SLDS builds on them by combining regime-local multi-lag initialization with regime-conditioned learning and explicitly testing whether the inferred dynamics recover the underlying timescales.

## 3 SYNTHETIC DATA 

We evaluate timescale identification under known ground truth, progressing from stationary dynamics to switching dynamics with multiple regimes. For each experiment, we generate 128-channel Gaussian or Poisson observations from 10-dimensional latent (switching) linear dynamical systems, using 5-ms bins and 30 training and 20 held-out trials of 4 s each. We prescribe timescales through the transition eigenvalues, |λk,j | = exp( −∆t/τ k,j ). The stationary experiment ( K = 1 ) contains timescales of 20 and 100 ms, each represented by five latent modes. In the switching experiment (K = 2 ), we alternate timescales between spectra {10 , 40 , 100 } ms and {20 , 80 , 300 } ms, with 

3/3/4 modes per timescale in each regime. A symmetric Markov chain governs the regime switching, with self-transition probability 0.9975 , corresponding to an expected dwell time of 2 s. We compare MTS-SLDS with the standard SLDS (implementation in (Linderman Lab and con-tributors, n.d.)), using each method’s default initialization and inference procedure. In stationary comparisons, we additionally include two-exponential autocorrelation fitting (ACF-exp) for both observation models and adaptive approximate Bayesian computation (aABC) for Poisson observations specifically. These two methods use population-summed spike counts in the Poisson comparison. ## 4 NEURAL DATA APPLICATIONS AND ANALYSES 

4.1 INTERPRETABLE TEMPORAL STRUCTURE IN V4 POPULATION ACTIVITY 

We first examine whether the inferred temporal structure of MTS-SLDS provides a reproducible and predictive description of neural population activity. We analyze a 16-channel visual area 4 (V4) spike recordings from a macaque doing simple fixation task Engel et al. (2016); Gieselmann & Thiele (2023). We focus on the 3-s sustained fixation period and use 2-ms spike count bins (Fig. 3A). We fit a single regime ( K = 1 ) state-space model with Poisson emission on the data to characterize dynamics within this sustained behavioral epoch. We evaluate the model with different latent dimensionality 

H ∈ { 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 } using five-fold cross-validation and multiple seeds (see details in Appendix G. We find that both methods’ prediction saturate at around H = 8 and increasing dimension yields little additional predictive gain; thus we focus subsequent analyses on H = 8 .MTS-SLDS provides higher predictive likelihood on all held-out trials, improving log-likelihood by 52 .4 ± 6.9 (mean ± SE) per trial. The example trial illustrates how modest differences in one-step spike rate predictions accumulate into a likelihood advantage of MTS-SLDS over SLDS (Fig. 3C and Fig. 9). In addition, this predictive advantage accompanies better reproduction of the observed temporal correlations. When simulating spike counts from each fitted model and applying the same pooled-autocorrelation estimation to simulated and recorded counts, we can see MTS-SLDS reproduces the observed autocorrelation more closely than SLDS (Fig. 3D). Both methods yielded three reproducible timescales (Fig. 3E). MTS-SLDS’s inferred timescales center on 26 .4,

49 .5, and 120 .2 ms, while SLDS produce 23 .5, 36 .1, and 110 .9 ms. This agreement supports a multiscale description of population dynamics. On the other hand, autocorrelation-based methods provide coarser descriptions due to pooled activity (see Appendix E for pooling effect). ACF-exp leads to timescales of 3.0 and 55 .4 ms, while two-component aABC leads to posterior medians of 28 .3 and 108 .1 ms. Adding a third aABC component shifted the estimates and introduced a poorly constrained component near 540 ms. The fitted three-component model also matched the pooled autocorrelation less closely than the two-component model, which implies the two-component fit therefore provided a more parsimonious approximation of this pooled statistic. One possible interpretation is that its fast component summarizes contributions from the two faster latent timescale bands as a single effective decay. A separate analysis using the sPCF-EM method (Song & Shanechi, 2023) also yields no clear distinction of timescales and no relation with the behavioral timescale ordering (Fig. 12), which further supports MTS-SLDS as an effective approach for regime-specific timescale estimates. ## 5 DISCUSSION 

Here, we introduce MTS-SLDS, a framework for recovering regime-specific latent timescales from neural population recordings. It combines a multi-lag moment initialization, which constrains decay rates using temporal dependencies across several observation lags, with regime-conditioned Laplace EM, which keeps the dynamical statistics of each regime separate during learning. The result is a fitted switching model whose transition eigenvalues can be read as timescales: in simulations, MTS-SLDS recovers the prescribed spectra and switching structure across noise levels and spike counts where a standard SLDS does not, and in neural recordings it yields regime-specific timescales that can be compared across behavioral states. Ablations (Appendix F.1) indicate that both components contribute: regime-conditioned inference recovers much of the regime information from the baseline’s own parameters, and multi-lag initialization accounts for the remaining gap in timescale error. We note several limitations to this model. MTS-SLDS estimates effective timescales under the assumed model and cannot separate intrinsic circuit dynamics from temporally structured inputs (Chaudhuri et al., 2015; van Meegen & van Albada, 2021); a fitted transition matrix may absorb persis-tent drive from unobserved regions or behavioral variables. Weakly observed modes, closely spaced 9Under review decay rates, short regime visits, and limited recording duration can restrict recoverability. Multi-lag initialization relies on approximate stationarity within candidate regimes, while regime-conditioned learning still uses Gaussian, Laplace, and mixture-reduction approximations. Neither component guarantees globally optimal or unbiased estimates. Future work should quantify uncertainty in the recovered spectrum and its sensitivity to latent dimension, regime number, and observation model.

## E SPATIAL POOLING OBSCURES TIMESCALES 

In neural data analysis, some methods pool spike counts across channels to reduce sparsity in short time bins of neural recordings. This operation can increase the number of events per bin in the accumulated one-dimensional time series, and thus it can facilitate autocorrelation analysis when individual channels are sparse. However, it also changes how strongly each latent dynamical mode contributes to the observed temporal structure. A pooled signal may therefore reveal fewer timescales than are present in the underlying population. Therefore, applying estimating methods on the pooled signal may underestimate the underlying timescale number and values. In contrast, using state-space model on the multivariate neural is not affected and can give more reliable timescale estimates. Here, we explain this effect through the geometry of spatial pooling and illustrate its consequences for timescale recovery and model-order selection.

## G V4 DATA ANALYSIS DETAILS 

We analyzed extracellular recordings from the 16 channels V4 data during 81 fixation trials, using a continuous 3-s epoch per trial and 2-ms bins (1500 bins per trial). We treated these epochs as ongoing population activity rather than an event-locked response and fitted all state-space models with one dynamical regime ( K = 1 ). A fixed trial-index split (seed 20260914) reserved 10 trials for final testing; the remaining 71 trials formed the development set. We fitted Poisson MTS-SLDS and the ssm reference (SSM-SLDS) using five-fold cross-validation, three initialization seeds, and latent dimensions H ∈ { 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 }. We averaged predictive gains across seeds within each trial before computing uncertainty, avoiding repeated counting of the same trial. The main comparison uses matched H = 8 , not independently selected dimensions. Both methods were evaluated through the same Laplace-approximate filter using their fitted param-eters. We measured one-step predictive log-likelihood relative to a constant-rate Poisson baseline estimated from the corresponding training data. The reserved test trials were not used for fitting or model selection, and supplementary diagnostics used archived parameters without refitting. For H = 8 , we grouped timescales across the 15 seed–fold fits by single linkage with a 0.12 gap in log-timescale space. We retained bands present in every fit and centered above one sampling bin. MTS-SLDS yielded band centers of 26.4, 49.5, and 120.2 ms; SSM-SLDS yielded 23.5, 36.1, 26 Under review and 110.9 ms. We reported one center per band rather than counting closely spaced eigenvalues as separately resolved processes. This criterion is conservative: every MTS-SLDS fit contained an additional mode between 69.7 and 101.9 ms, but its variability prevented any single sub-band from attaining full occupancy. We also compared pooled autocorrelations after summing channels, subtracting the trial-average time course and grand mean, and normalizing at zero lag. Forward simulations from the state-space models underwent the same processing. aABC fitted this statistic over 0–100 ms; its agreement within that interval therefore measures fit to its objective, not independent predictive performance. The state-space models had smaller ACF errors over 100–500 ms than the two- and three-timescale aABC fits, with the latter using our three-component extension. Direct exponential fits provided a complementary diagnostic: increasing their order introduced near-bin components and a boundary-limited 1-ms component across the tested fitting windows (Fig. 10b). At matched H = 8 , MTS-SLDS improved predictive likelihood by 52.37 nats per trial, with positive differences on all 10 reserved trials. This corresponds to approximately 0.0022 nats per channel–bin observation. The advantage remained 44.14 nats per trial when compared with SSM-SLDS at its development-optimal H = 4 . A trial-level likelihood decomposition attributed the improvement primarily to predicted firing rates, rather than covariance corrections. Figure 9 shows population-averaged and single-channel predictions, cumulative likelihood differ-ences, and per-channel gains for one reserved trial. The single-channel trace is a selected best-channel example, not a representative-channel summary. Together, these analyses support multiple repro-ducible effective timescale bands and improved held-out prediction, while leaving the exact number and separation of decay processes dependent on model dimension and spectral resolution. In the main text, we select latent dimension through the prediction performance. Now we want to study how the timescales look like with specifically lower latent dimension, as according to (Zeraati et al., 2023) it is reported that two timescales beat three timescales for this dataset. In MTS-SLDS, we can force the model to learn two timescales by setting H = 2 . Setting H = 2 restricts the model to at most two distinct spectral decay times, but it does not prescribe their values. These low-dimensional fits yielded effective components in the tens-of-milliseconds and approximately 100-ms ranges, and they reside in estimates of the two-timescale aABC fit, while the later method showed broader posterior uncertainty (Fig. 10a). Meanwhile, ACF-exp fits tends to give much faster timescales when using two and three exponentials (Fig. 10b).
