Abstract 

Explaining a time-series model requires both locating the ev-idence and extracting meaningful knowledge from it. Yet a single timestep is not a semantic unit: the meaningful pat-terns live across many samples, not in any one of them. Per-timestep saliency is therefore noisy, high-dimensional, and unstable. Existing methods address this by introducing fixed-size windows and treating them as independent players in a cooperative game. Evaluating a coalition of such players re-quires defining a “baseline” region, which prior work tackles with fixed counterfactuals (e.g., a flat line, noise, or a spec-tral surrogate). This choice frequently dominates the resulting explanation, obscuring the model’s true decisions. To address these issues, we propose GRAIL, a region-based time-series explanation framework that uses domain-specific intervals, such as the P-wave and the QRS complex of an electrocardiogram (ECG) beat, as players. We describe each region with human-readable features and generate counter-factual baselines with an on-manifold conditional generator. Such a choice of players also admits non-temporal features: static covariates enter as singleton players attributed with the temporal regions on one scale, so a patient’s tabular evidence (e.g., age, sex) is weighed directly against the signal. We at-tribute the predictions to the features via Owen values with common-random-number variance reduction. Experimental results on both synthetic and real data show that the proposed framework produces faithful and robust explanations, evalu-ated under matched on- and off-manifold operators, offering meaningful knowledge to domain experts. 

# 1 Introduction 

Deep learning models are increasingly deployed on high-stakes time series, from clinical data to industrial sensors and financial data. Wherever they are used, deploying them hinges on a question that’s hard to answer for time series: which part of the signal drove a decision, in terms a domain expert can act on.

Our approach. We propose GRAIL (Generative Region Attribution with Interpretable Labels), a framework that explains time-series predictions in domain-aligned units. GRAIL produces feature-based, additive explanations via a cooperative game: each region and each covariate is a player ,and its value is its contribution to the prediction across all coalitions, so the contributions sum to the model’s output. Scoring a coalition requires saying what the signal looks like when a region is absent , but there is no canonical “off” state for a stretch of a time series such as an ECG: prior work fills it with a fixed counterfactual, a flat line, noise, or a spectrally matched surrogate, an arbitrary choice that fre-quently dominates the resulting explanation (Sundararajan and Najmi 2020). The choice of the absent state is thus not an implementation detail; it is the explanation. In this work, we stop choosing from a fixed menu and instead build the counterfactual: an interpretable codec encodes each region as named features, and a conditional generator regenerates an absent region on the data manifold , so every coalition remains a plausible real signal. GRAIL attributes the model’s log-odds to regions and co-variates with Owen values (Owen 1972) (a two-level Shapley value (Shapley 1953), features nested in regions) estimated by a common-random-number sweep, and always reports the residual ρ, so the accounting is exactly complete. Figure 2 shows the pipeline of GRAIL, which also amortizes into a fast single-pass surrogate for deployment. 

Contributions. 

1. A region-and-covariate cooperative game. We cast time-series attribution as a cooperative game whose play-ers are semantic temporal regions and non-temporal co-variates, credited on one common scale, so reliance on metadata is read off alongside the waveform. To our knowledge, no prior time-series explainer accommodates both (Section 3.1). 2. An exact, on-manifold attribution. GRAIL plays the game by generating each absent region on the data man-ifold rather than from a fixed filler, and attributes with exactly-complete Owen values (CRN-estimated). This is the faithful but slow reference; a model-aware segmenter recovers regions absent a prior (Section 3.2). 3. A fast surrogate for deployment. GRAIL-fast amortizes the game into one forward pass, ∼10 3× cheaper and still exactly complete, though its on-manifold faithfulness holds only on real data (Section 3.3). 4. A comprehensive benchmark. An at-scale comparison over four families of benchmarks, including real ECG, scoring every method under matched on- and off-manifold operators with paired significance tests against concept-and group-level Shapley baselines, with kernel, generator, reference, and segmenter ablations (Section 4). 

# 2 Related Work 

Attribution and time-series explainers. Attribution via Shapley values (Shapley 1953; Lundberg and Lee 2017) de-pends on how an “absent” feature is modeled (Sundarara-jan and Najmi 2020; Janzing, Minorics, and Blöbaum 2020; Covert, Lundberg, and Lee 2021). Most time-series explain-ers give per-timestep saliency (Theissler et al. 2022); those at a coarser grain group timesteps into windows or segments (WindowSHAP (Nayebi et al. 2023), TimeSHAP (Bento et al. 2021), TS-MULE (Schlegel et al. 2021), LIMESeg-ment (Sivill and Flach 2022), Dynamask (Crabbé and Van Der Schaar 2021)), but all fill the absent state with a fixed, off-manifold filler. Instead, we make the removal operator a conditional generator that stays on-manifold and lift the flat Shapley game to a two-level Owen value (Owen 1972) over features nested within regions. 

Closest comparators and evaluation. Nearest to us are methods that highlight time series players: C-SHAP (Jutte et al. 2025) uses signal concepts, while GroupSegment-SHAP (Kim and Park 2026). We also benchmark these ap-proaches. They redefine the player but keep an off-manifold mean-replacement operator, whereas we hold the player structure fixed and change the operator, and neither ad-mits non-temporal covariates. CausalConceptTS (Lopez Al-caraz and Strodthoff 2025) shares our generative premise but imputes over full recordings, and ManifoldShap (Taufiq, Blöbaum, and Minorics 2023) restricts the interventional background to an on-manifold region rather than generating it. Our per-region generation keeps an exact Owen sweep af-fordable. Because perturbation faithfulness is confounded by the removal operator (Hooker et al. 2019; Jethani et al. 2021), we score every method under both a matched on-manifold and a neutral off-manifold operator, and amortize the exact game for deployment (Jethani et al. 2022). Appendix A ex-pands each baseline and the hierarchical-credit connection. 

# 3 The GRAIL framework 

GRAIL represents time-series attribution as a cooperative game and solves it on the data manifold. We define the game and its players, semantic regions together with non-temporal covariates in Section 3.1; play it exactly, generating each ab-sent region’s counterfactual on-manifold and attributing with exactly-complete Owen values in Section 3.2; and amortize the exact game into a fast single-pass surrogate for deploy-ment in Section 3.3.

Intervention and reference semantics. The interven-tional default draws absent features from the marginal p0, iso-lating what the model uses, while the conditional draw takes them from p(θ ¯S | θS ), crediting shared information. This is referred to as the interventional-versus-observational dis-tinction (Janzing, Minorics, and Blöbaum 2020). A marginal reference is assumption-free, whereas a class-conditional ref-erence draws the absent value from a labeled cohort: the normal cohort (default), so each ϕi reads as its deviation from normal; the sample’s own class; or a foil, giving the contrastive “why this class rather than another.” Conditional draws use a Gaussian copula that stays deterministic in u (so CRN coupling survives) and collapses exactly to the marginal when the conditioning set is empty, so any difference is at-tributable to conditioning alone; App. F compares the four references. 

3.2 Playing the Game On-manifold 

Playing the game means evaluating coalition values, each of which requires regenerating the absent regions on the data manifold. The codec’s generator makes “feature absent →

regenerate region” well-defined. Our default generator is a Gaussian process (GP) poste-rior: a distribution over plausible region shapes given the retained features and the region’s boundary. A present fea-ture conditions the GP on its value, an absent feature is set to the reference, and the region is sampled from that posterior. This keeps every coalition on the data manifold. For exam-ple, a spectrally and autocorrelation-matched GP sample of an ECG resembles the original form rather than a flat or noisy filler. The generator is swappable. Replacing it changes only the region sampler, not the game, estimator, or residual. A learned conditional VAE (Kingma and Welling 2014; Sohn, Lee, and Yan 2015) keeps the analytic encoder but replaces the GP sampler, improving off-manifold behavior at a small on-manifold cost, and swapping only the GP kernel (RBF, Matérn, or spectral) alters the counterfactual’s texture for ro-bustness (App. D). The exact GP game is our on-manifold reference: its closed-form posterior is what keeps an exact Owen sweep affordable, since a learned generator pays a per-coalition sampling cost it cannot amortize. 

Owen values and exact completeness. We attribute fea-ture importance with Owen values, the average marginal con-tribution of a region-feature under permutations that preserve the block (region) structure. Since features are nested in re-gions, their Owen values roll up consistently. Region discovery with GRAIL-auto When no region prior is available, the segmenter must find regions itself. The proposed segmenter model adopts three sources of in-formation: a prior, when available; Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) change points (Killick, Fearnhead, and Eckley 2012); and a model-aware segmenter. The signal-only default is PELT change, which reacts only to the signal. Our model-aware segmenter instead learns a selector (trained REAL-X-style (Jethani et al. 2021) with the generator as imputer) that reads regions from a thresholded mask. A raw selector might place boundaries within meaningful regions, where the codec regenerates from ambiguous fluctuations. We move each boundary at inference to the nearest clean-cut point, minimizing a cleanliness cost c(t) (the distance to a PELT boundary or to an edge of the generator’s informativeness profile), without retraining. 

3.3 Amortizing for Deployment with GRAIL-fast 

The exact GP game of Section 3.2 is faithful but expensive: each coalition value invokes the generator and the model, and an exact Owen sweep evaluates many coalitions per in-stance. Because the game is a value function rather than a solver, its region-Owen attribution can be amortized. We train a single-forward-pass network gψ against the game with the FastSHAP objective (Jethani et al. 2022) and enforce the additive-efficiency constraint P 

> i

ϕi = f (x) − v(∅) by pro-jection, so completeness holds exactly at inference rather than only in expectation. The resulting GRAIL-fast runs ∼10 3×

faster than the exact sweep while staying exactly complete. The speedup comes at a cost in faithfulness. Because the surrogate is trained against a cross-sample removal operator, it is faithful to that operator: faithful off-manifold and, on real structured data, competitive with the exact game on-manifold. Its on-manifold faithfulness holds only where the signal carries sufficient structure for the surrogate to learn; on low-signal synthetic data it degrades, a limitation we report explicitly (App. J). We therefore recommend GRAIL-fast for deployment on real data, and retain the exact GP game as our on-manifold reference throughout. 

# 4 Experimental Evaluation 

We evaluate GRAIL on six datasets across three domains: two synthetic families with ground-truth region masks (Synth and Mixed, which add static covariates); two clin-ical ECG families with cardiologist P/QRS/T annotations (ECG, from MIT-BIH (Moody and Mark 2001) and QT (La-guna et al. 1997); and multivariate LUDB (Kalyakulina et al. 2021), carrying patient age and sex; and two industrial-sensor families without region annotation (FordA, Wafer). All tar-gets are InceptionTime (Ismail Fawaz et al. 2020) classifiers. We invoke GRAIL in three configurations: (i) provided regions; (ii) discovered regions with GRAIL-auto; and 

(iii) amortized deployment with GRAIL-fast, against six time series-specific windowing and perturbation baselines (WindowSHAP (Nayebi et al. 2023), TimeSHAP (Bento et al. 2021), TS-MULE (Schlegel et al. 2021), LIMESeg-ment (Sivill and Flach 2022), Dynamask (Crabbé and Van Der Schaar 2021)), the closest concept- and group-level Shapley players (C-SHAP (Jutte et al. 2025), GS-SHAP (Kim and Park 2026)), and non-time series gradient and sampling attributions (Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan, Taly, and Yan 2017), gradient ×input (Simonyan, Vedaldi, and Zisser-man 2014), KernelSHAP (Villani, Lockhart, and Magazzeni 2022)). Since perturbation faithfulness depends on how evidence is removed, we score each method under two matched op-erators: a neutral off-manifold operator and an on-manifold regeneration operator. We additionally report localization, robustness, cost, and completeness; every claim is gated on a paired Wilcoxon test with family-wide Benjamini–Hochberg 

q < 0.05 . Setup details are in Appendix E. 

In summary, the exact game is the most faithful method on-manifold on every family and exactly complete (Sec-tion 4.1); reporting both operators exposes the teaching-to-the-test trap, where the exact game still wins on real data under the comparators’ own operator (Section 4.2); signal and static covariates are attributed on one common scale, so a model’s reliance on metadata is read directly off the explanation (Section 4.3). The method is robust to its de-sign choices and discovers regions unaided (Section 4.4), while direct diagnostics show the generator’s uncertainty is miscalibrated even where its attributions are most faithful (Section 4.5); and the amortized surrogate recovers all of this at lower cost on real data (Section 4.6). 4.1 Explanation Faithfulness and Completeness 

On-manifold, where every method is scored under the same generative removal it is asked to explain, the exact game is the most faithful method on every family (Table 2); here faith-fulness is the rank correlation between a player’s attributed importance and the model’s response when that player is removed by on-manifold regeneration (Bhatt, Weller, and Moura 2020). Provided regions (GRAIL) leads on Synth ( 0.872 ), Mixed ( 0.921 ), ECG ( 0.828 ), LuDB ( 0.657 ), FordA ( 0.864 ), and discovered regions (GRAIL-auto) leads on Wafer ( 0.774 ). For each dataset, GRAIL outperforms all twelve baselines. The margin is significant against all twelve baselines jointly (Benjamini–Hochberg q < 0.05 over the 

144 tests). This exact game is the reference standard against which the amortized surrogate is measured in Section 4.6. Since in the exact game ρ=0 , each attribution is an Owen value estimated by a common-random-number (CRN) sweep over a permutation budget P . Therefore, the estimate must be tight within the provided budget. Figure 3 depicts P on a fixed Synth game. In this game, the per-player standard error falls at the Monte-Carlo-optimal rate. At the operating budget of ¯P =128 , the region ranking already matches the largest-P run at Spearman correlation 0.98 , with a noise floor of about 14% of mean |ϕ|. So “exact” names the estimand, the true two-level Shapley value rather than a windowing or gradient surrogate, and the residual is estimation variance that shrinks at the optimal rate with budget, not a modeling approximation. Completeness is exact by construction. GRAIL adds a residual player that closes the gap ρ between the summed attributions and the model output, so v(N )= f (x) and the mean absolute residual |ρ| is 0.000 on every family, for both the exact game and the amortized surrogate. 4.3 Temporal Signal and Covariates on One Scale 

The cooperative game admits static covariates as singleton players (Section 3.1), so signal regions and metadata share a single scale, and a model’s reliance on it is read off rather than inferred. The reason is structural: Owen block values only focus on whole-block coalitions, where no ab-sent feature has a retained sibling to condition on. Thus, fea-ture correlation cannot change which regions matter. Within-region redistribution remains invariant. Faithfulness does not change for all six datasets, and only trades localization pre-cision against recall (Appendix G). 

4.5 Generator Fit and Calibration 

The counterfactual generator is a swappable module: a GP posterior by default, or a cVAE or a DDPM in its place, and all three leave the accounting exactly complete. Expressive-ness is not free, though. The more learned the generator, the more its regenerations resemble the counterfactuals a splice-based operator would produce, blurring the on-manifold/off-manifold distinction the two-operator protocol relies on, so a more powerful generator can raise measured faithfulness while weakening the very contrast that makes on-manifold faithfulness meaningful. Because of this, we judge the generator by direct diagnos-tics rather than by the downstream faithfulness it produces. A probability-integral-transform check, whether held-out ob-servations fall in their predicted quantiles at the right rate, gives Kolmogorov-Smirnov distances of 0.23 to 0.40 from uniform: the generator’s predictive uncertainty is miscali-brated exactly on the families where its attributions are most faithful. Passing downstream faithfulness is therefore weaker evidence of a good generative model than it appears, which is why we report the fit directly (Appendix H).

# 5 Conclusion 

Meaningful time-series explanation begins with the choice of the right unit, the region, and the non-temporal covari-ates a model uses alongside it. Having fixed the players, an explanation is governed by the definition of a "baseline" re-gion, defined by a counterfactual. Instead of choosing a fixed filler, the proposed method generates an absent region on the data manifold. Then it attributes the logit via Owen val-ues estimated with common random numbers, and always reports the residual. Since all players share a single scale, the model’s overreliance on static data rather than time series can be assessed. Experiments show that the exact game is the most faithful on-manifold on every family and exactly complete. On real ECG data, GRAIL almost reaches the same off-manifold performance as other baselines. Finally, we attempt to ease the deployment process by introducing an amortized surrogate that reduces runtime on real data; a faithfulness-aware segmenter that enables region discovery without ground truth; and a game that supports multi-lead signals by recovering the clinically expected leads. 

Limitations. The most important question we want to ad-dress is whether these explanations are useful to domain experts. Therefore, our next step is conducting a user study. Since the amortized surrogate is trained on a single model, the model-randomization sanity check requires retraining the surrogate. Although the method achieves high performance on non-stationary data, we need to explore additional signals that a GP cannot represent. The exact generator saturates the logit, which the residual ρ detects, and the swappable cVAE and DDPM generators mitigate this effect. Automated region discovery is improved but still unsolved. Further lessons that generalize beyond GRAIL, an expert-rule agreement analy-sis, and the scope of our reimplementations, diffusion arm, and multivariate claims are developed in Appendix K.

Shapley attribution and the removal operator. Shapley values, originally proposed by Shapley (1953), form the foun-dation of the modern attribution framework SHAP (Lund-berg and Lee 2017). The framework satisfies four basic ax-ioms: efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity. The main modeling decisions of Shapley attributions are the “absence” and sampling of features (Štrumbelj and Kononenko 2014). Building on top of the framework, Sundararajan and Najmi (2020) and Kumar et al. (2020) introduce attribution ambi-guities; Janzing, Minorics, and Blöbaum (2020) represent the observational-vs-interventional choice as causal; Covert, Lundberg, and Lee (2021) parameterize removal-based ex-planations with a masking operator. To adapt the removal mechanism for time series, we implement feature removal with a conditional generator to stay on-manifold. Further, we extend the value function from a traditional Shapley game to a two-level Owen value (Owen 1972) over nested features with corresponding time-series regions. 

Time-series explainers. Time-series-specific explanation methods treat time steps as players to produce attribu-tions (Theissler et al. 2022). However, as Example 1 sug-gests, timestep saliency might not be sufficiently expressive. To test this, we compare GRAIL against five representa-tive methods that operate at, or can be aggregated to, the region level: WindowSHAP (Nayebi et al. 2023) explains fixed-sized windows, TimeSHAP (Bento et al. 2021) per-turbs sequence events with pruning, TS-MULE (Schlegel et al. 2021) runs LIME over segmented series, LIMESeg-ment (Sivill and Flach 2022) builds realistic time-series per-turbations, and Dynamask (Crabbé and Van Der Schaar 2021) learns a soft temporal perturbation mask. We lever-age a region-based approach to learn generators that sample regions on the manifold. 

Concept- and group-level players. Alternatively, other methods approach the saliency problem by defining play-ers differently. C-SHAP (Jutte et al. 2025) decomposes a series into high-level concepts and computes exact Shapley values over them, trading localization for interpretability: its players are global properties and carry no position. For the multivariate methods, GroupSegment-SHAP (Kim and Park 2026) builds players as (feature group × time interval) cells, grouping variables by Hilbert–Schmidt independence (Gret-ton et al. 2005) and segmenting time by maximum mean dis-crepancy (Gretton et al. 2012). While these methods adopt a similar two-level construction, they redefine the player and keep an off-manifold removal operator (mean replacement). Oppositely, we hold the player structure fixed and change the operator. Our harness scores both axes at once, so Table 2 can separate them. 

Hierarchical credit assignment. Nesting players is ex-actly what Owen values (Owen 1972) formalizes: a coalition structure partitions players into blocks, credit is allocated be-tween blocks and then within them, and the result reduces to Shapley when the partition is trivial. We adopt that structure directly—features nested in regions, and regions nested in channels for multivariate signals—rather than building a be-spoke coarse-to-fine procedure, which buys the consistency and efficiency guarantees for free. 

Generative imputation for counterfactual concepts. We build on the notion that counterfactuals are generated rather than spliced. Thus, our generator operates only on a single region conditioned on its retained features and boundary, al-lowing us to keep exact Owen value computation affordable. We include a region-scale conditional DDPM (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020) as a third generator family (Section B) to test whether the choice of generator, rather than the decision to generate at all, is what matters. 

Generators and backbones. Our default generator is a Gaussian process (Rasmussen and Williams 2006a) with a spectral-mixture kernel (Wilson and Adams 2013) and a beat-template mean for ECG; a learned conditional VAE (Kingma and Welling 2014; Sohn, Lee, and Yan 2015) and a condi-tional DDPM (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020) are the alter-natives we explored behind the same game. Segmentation change points are identified via PELT (Killick, Fearnhead, and Eckley 2012). As a predictive baseline, we leverage In-ceptionTime (Ismail Fawaz et al. 2020). 

Amortized and learned explanation. Solving an exact game for every instance is computationally expensive, which motivated training learnable networks to predict explana-tions. FastSHAP (Jethani et al. 2022) amortizes Shap-ley values with an efficiency-constrained regression loss, L2X (Chen et al. 2018) and REAL-X (Jethani et al. 2021) learn instance-wise feature selectors. We leverage the Fast-SHAP objective while modifying the model to account for time-series specifics. This allows the deployment of the ex-pensive exact method. 

On- vs off-manifold evaluation. Perturbation-based faith-fulness is defined by corresponding methods’ feature re-moval mechanisms (Petsiuk, Das, and Saenko 2018; Fong and Vedaldi 2017). Scoring an explainer only with an off-manifold operator rewards methods that measure impor-tance in that same space, i.e., the “teaching-to-the-test” trap (Hooker et al. 2019; Jethani et al. 2021). We report faithfulness under both a neutral off-manifold operator and a matched on-manifold one, applied identically to every method.

# G Where a region’s credit goes 

Within-region redistribution (§B) preserves the region roll-up by construction, so it can only move localization —never faithfulness. Table 7 confirms this holds exactly in practice and shows what the choice does buy. The invariant is exact: six arms spanning two perturbation families (structural occlusion and finite-difference gradients) and three uniform-floor settings return the same faithfulness to three decimals. Only rank-align , which by design reorders regions, changes it—and it changes it for the worse (0.620 → 0.561 , 0.582 → 0.534 ) while leaving localization untouched, so we leave it off by default. What the profile does control is a genuine trade, and its direction flips with the region regime. With ground-truth re-gions, the flat broadcast is best on area metrics (AUPRC 

0.634 ) and worst at pointing ( 0.525 ): when the annotated span is the ground truth, spreading credit uniformly max-imizes overlap and any sharpening can only lose it. With discovered regions the smoothed occlusion profile wins both (0.543 AUPRC, 0.600 pointing), because imprecise bound-aries leave real slack for the profile to recover. The honest summary is that within-region redistribution earns its keep exactly when the segmentation does not already hand you the answer. 

On real data, the trade is a precision/recall axis, not a single winner. Because the arms cannot move faithfulness, the sweep can be run the cheap way—one Owen pass per instance, all six profiles scored off the same attribution— which is what makes it affordable on real ECG. Table 8 repeats it on ecg and ludb, and the synthetic story survives only in part. The clean regime flip we saw on synth is really a precision-versus-recall split that the synthetic data was too small to separate. Across all four real cells, the occlusion profiles win the precision family—AUP and pointing—and the flat broadcast wins the recall family—AUR and, where the region support is handed over, AUPRC and IoU. This is what the two profiles are for: uniform credit maximizes overlap with a span, a peaked profile maximizes agreement with a point. What the region regime controls is how much of the recall advantage flat retains. With ground-truth support on ecg it is large (AUPRC 0.688 vs 0.594 ); with discovered regions it collapses to nothing ( 0.379 vs 0.375 ); and on ludb’s discov-ered regions it reverses (0.193 vs 0.249 ), matching synth. The mechanism is the same one the synthetic sweep suggested: when the annotated span is the region, coverage comes free, and there is nothing for a profile to recover, and when bound-aries are approximate there is slack the profile can claim. The real data just shows this is a matter of degree rather than a switch. The default ( occ-6 +f ) is defensible on this evidence without being the winner of every column. It is never worst in any cell; it is best or tied-best at pointing in the two discovered cells—the regime that matters at deployment, where nobody hands you the regions—and it is runner-up on AUPRC in all four. The pure-gradient profiles are the clear losers on real data (worst or near-worst in seven of eight columns), which is worth stating: a finite-difference sensitivity inside a region is a poor guide to where that region’s coalitional 

credit belongs. We report the ludb cells as directional only— at n=16 the between-seed SEMs on AUPRC reach ±0.10 .

# H Swapping the generator, and asking whether it fits 

Section 3.2 argues the generator is a component rather than a commitment, and that its adequacy can be checked directly instead of inferred from task metrics. Both claims are cheap to test, and neither is flattering in the way we expected. 

Swappability holds, and there is a gradient behind it. 

All three generators leave the game exactly complete, so the interface claim is met: the counterfactual moves and the ac-counting does not. What is more informative is the ordering. As the generator becomes more learned and less analytic, on-manifold faithfulness falls ( 0.662 → 0.610 → 0.430 ) while 

off -manifold faithfulness rises monotonically ( −0.092 →

0.335 → 0.439 ). The learned generators drift towards pro-ducing counterfactuals a splice-based operator recognizes, which is precisely the property the analytic GP was chosen to avoid. Part of the DDPM’s on-manifold deficit is its 8×

smaller permutation budget and we do not read its 0.430 

as a converged number; the off-manifold ordering , which is budget-insensitive in direction, is the part we rely on. 

The generator fits real ECG better than synthetic noise. 

Per-sample log-density is highest on ecg with annotated re-gions ( −12 .4) and lowest on synth ( −17 .8 to −21 .5), and the regeneration distances agree ( 0.36 vs. 2.24 ). This inverts the intuition that synthetic data is the easy case: synth’s in-formative regions sit on i.i.d. background, which a smooth boundary-conditioned GP is a poor model of, whereas a QRS complex is exactly the kind of correlated excursion it represents well. It also supplies a mechanism for a result we previously only observed: the exact game’s large synth ad-vantage on-manifold is measured against a generator that fits synth worst .

Segmentation quality is visible in the generator, not just downstream. On ecg, moving from annotated to discov-ered regions costs 54 nats per sample ( −12 .4 → − 67 .0)— a far larger swing than anything in the synth column, and larger than the corresponding faithfulness gap would sug-gest. Change-point boundaries place region edges where the GP’s boundary conditioning is badly wrong. This makes the log-density useful as a segmentation diagnostic that needs no ground-truth mask, which is exactly the setting where boundary F1 is unavailable. 

The uncertainty is not honest, and we should say so. PIT KS distances of 0.23 –0.40 are large: if the generator’s pos-terior were calibrated, the whitened residual L−1(xr − μr )

would be standard normal and this number would be near zero. It is not. The generator’s mean is evidently good enough to drive the most faithful attributions in this paper while its 

error bars are miscalibrated—which is the whole point of reporting a diagnostic that task performance cannot see. Two consequences follow. Any use of GRAIL that reads the gener-ator’s variance (confidence intervals on a counterfactual, say) is not currently supported by this evidence. And the down-stream faithfulness metrics are demonstrably insensitive to error-bar calibration, so passing them is weaker evidence of a good generative model than it appears.

# J Amortized deployment for fast attribution 

The exact GP game is faithful and exactly complete but slow. Because it is a value function , the same attribution can be recovered by a learned network trained against it. This sec-tion emphasizes amortized deployment and the trade-off with faithfulness. 

J.1 Amortizing the game for deployment 

The exact estimator’s cost is |N |+1 generator-and-model evaluations per permutation. We amortize it into a network that reproduces the same region attribution in one forward pass. We report two heads, because the difference between them is the finding of this appendix. 

GRAIL-fast: a FastSHAP density. The first head is a convolutional map gψ : RT → RT emitting a per-timestep attribution density (pooled per region at explain time, so it is segmentation-agnostic), trained with the Fast-SHAP objective (Jethani et al. 2022): sampled coalitions with the additive-efficiency constraint enforced by projection given the measured v(∅) and v(N ). A convolutional den-sity is translation-equivariant and cannot express position-dependent attribution, so we add a positional input chan-nel, without which the network converges to its target mean and learns nothing. Its training coalitions are realized with a cross-sample removal operator, and it is correspond-ingly strong off-manifold and only real-data -competitive on-manifold—a deficit we first read as a training-operator mis-match and then measured (§J.4).

J.2 Runtime and robustness 

The exact game’s main practical cost is runtime, and both heads pay it down (Table 13). GRAIL-fast runs in 9–35 ms against 2.7–12 .5 s—a 290 –710 × speed-up—at a baseline-level effective-player count ( 6–14 vs. GRAIL’s 27 –53 ), while remaining exactly complete. GRAIL-fast is a further 3–5×

faster ( 0.7–3.2 × 10 3 overall), and the gap is the price of the model-evidence channel: GRAIL-fast runs an occlusion sweep over the signal before its forward pass, so it spends classifier calls that GRAIL-fast does not. Section J.3 high-lights this difference. Robustness was measured on the GRAIL-fast head: both it and GRAIL are jitter-stable ( ≥ 0.87 ), and max-sensitivity improves under amortization on the real families (ecg 0.76 → 0.37 , ludb 0.22 → 0.05 ) but worsens on synthetic (0.68 → 1.00 , 0.85 → 1.07 ). We do not carry that result over to GRAIL-fast: its occlusion channel is a function of the classifier, not of the signal alone, so its perturbation response need not match GRAIL-fast’s. 

J.3 On-manifold faithfulness tradeoff 

On-manifold faithfulness is where the exact game is un-beatable and where a surrogate performs worse (Table 14). GRAIL remains the best across all four families. But the dif-ference depends almost entirely on the head. GRAIL-fast beats the best baseline on all four datasets, including synth. What remains is a real but much narrower gap to the ex-act game. On ecg it is 0.052 (0.776 vs. 0.828 )—significant at q=8 × 10 −6 but small enough that the ordering, not the level, is what a 250 × cheaper explainer gives up. On ludb the gap ( 0.124 ) is not significant ( q=0 .12 , n=20 ): at this sam-ple size, the deployed head is statistically indistinguishable from the game it was distilled from. Synth and mixed keep a clear, significant gap ( 0.316 and 0.228 , q < 10 −5), so the exact GP game remains the on-manifold reference and the recommendation is unchanged in kind—only in degree. J.4 Is the surrogate’s negative a training-operator mismatch? 

There is an obvious suspect for the synthetic negatives, and it is worth naming before defending anything: GRAIL-fast regresses coalition values built by cross-sample splicing —absent spans filled from another signal—and is then scored under the on-manifold operator. Fitting one removal operator and grading against another is a semantic mismatch, and it would explain the sign. We tested it directly rather than arguing about it. We added a second distillation target that evaluates coalitions through the on-manifold GP game itself—the exact value function 

v(S) of §3.1, the same one GRAIL is computed against— and left the FastSHAP machinery downstream untouched, so the training operator is the only thing that moves. Both heads were trained on identical corpora ( 200 signals, 24 

coalitions each) and evaluated on identical instances and seeds (Table 15). The mismatch is not the explanation. Matching the train-ing operator to the evaluation operator moves faithfulness by 

−0.012 on synth and +0 .011 on mixed. Both are far in-side a single between-seed SEM, and on synth in the wrong direction. The negative survives its most obvious confound. What the on-manifold target does buy is localization on synth (0.415 → 0.452 ), i.e. a better-placed explanation that is no more faithful: the head learns where the informative region is without learning how the model responds when that region is regenerated on-manifold. We therefore retract the operator-mismatch reading and state that the residual explanation is the narrower claim sup-ported by the data. A FastSHAP-style head is a single amor-tized map from signal to attribution; the on-manifold value function it is asked to imitate is a function of the region gen-erator as well as the classifier, and on low-signal synthetic data the classifier’s response to a regenerated region is close to noise. There is little stable structure for a single forward pass to capture, regardless of which operator produced the targets. On real data—where regenerating a QRS actually changes the decision in a repeatable way—the same archi-tecture and the same training budget succeed (ecg 0.556 , ludb 

0.381 ). This is a sharper statement than the one it replaces, and it was cheap to obtain: one extra target operator, four benchmark runs. 

# K Extended Discussion 

GRAIL is the method to reach for when the question is which regions the model’s decision is most sensitive to, on-distribution and with a complete accounting: it is the most faithful on-manifold method on every family and ex-actly complete. GRAIL-fast is the method to deploy: on real, structured data it recovers this at a small fraction of the cost, at a faithfulness price that is modest on real data and disqualifying on low-signal synthetic data. 

Three lessons that generalize. First, the costs of an exact on-manifold game are the solver’s, not the game’s: amor-tizing against the same value function pays down runtime without changing what is being explained (Section 4.6). Sec-ond, faithfulness is operator-relative; reporting both on- and off-manifold operators for every method is the only way to avoid teaching-to-the-test, and it is precisely what exposes the surrogate’s on-manifold limit (Section 4.2). Third, local-ization against a fixed anatomical prior is only as meaningful as that prior is to the model: the LUDB LVH case shows a faithful explanation can correctly point away from the anno-tated region when the model relies on static or un-annotated evidence, measurably so, since admitting age as a player takes top rank from the annotated QRS on a quarter of instances while leaving faithfulness unchanged (Section 4.3). 

Limits that are structural. Three of our limits are struc-tural, and each was reached by testing a claim we had already made in print. The generator’s mean is good enough to drive the most faithful attributions here while its error bars are not calibrated, so faithfulness metrics cannot certify a gen-erative model (Section 4.5). Matching an amortized head’s training operator to its evaluation operator does not rescue it on low-signal data, so the surrogate’s deficit is not a seman-tic mismatch. And a conditional reference, however strongly its features co-vary, cannot alter a region-level attribution at all, because Owen block values only ever see whole-block coalitions (Section 4.4). Testing claims we had already com-mitted to in print is the habit we would most like to see carried forward.
