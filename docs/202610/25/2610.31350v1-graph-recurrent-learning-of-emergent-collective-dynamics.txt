Graph-Recurrent Learning of Emergent Collective Dynamics 

Ali Rayat, 1 Acacia Lee, 1 and Gia-Wei Chern 1

> 1

Department of Physics, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, Virginia 22904, USA 

(Dated: October 23, 2026) Collective ordering in correlated electron systems emerges from microscopic dynamics, yet the evolution of observable textures need not admit a closed, memoryless description. We introduce a graph-recurrent neural network (GRNN) framework that learns such emergent dynamics directly from trajectories without specifying microscopic equations of motion. The framework integrates gated recurrent memory with graph message passing: local hidden states encode information beyond the instantaneous observables, while spatial coupling allows this information to propagate between sites. The observable and hidden fields evolve jointly through a shared local rule, providing a scalable representation of dynamics with spatial interactions and temporal memory. We demonstrate this framework on the emergent dynamics of charge-density-wave order in the semiclassical Holstein model, successfully reproducing temporal and spatial correlations and dynamical scaling during domain coarsening. By learning finite-time evolution directly, the GRNN offers a route to efficient, data-driven descriptions of collective dynamics with unresolved microscopic degrees of freedom. 

Pattern formation is a pervasive feature of correlated electron materials, where competing interactions gener-ate magnetic textures, phase-separated clusters, and elec-tronic stripes [1–5]. The evolution of these patterns poses a further challenge. Domains grow and defects move and annihilate across widely separated length and time scales, often exhibiting dynamical scaling during phase ordering [6, 7]. At the same time, their motion can depend on the electronic degrees of freedom underlying the competing orders. Phenomenological descriptions, including time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau and phase-field models, reach large scales efficiently but may omit these material-specific effects [8–10]. Microscopic simula-tions retain the electronic degrees of freedom, but must recalculate their response as the configuration evolves, restricting accessible sizes and times [11, 12]. Machine learning (ML) offers a route to efficient, transferable dy-namical models that connect microscopic physics with large-scale pattern formation. A prominent example is the development of ML force fields for quantum-based molecular dynamics [13–19]. By replacing repeated electronic calculations with learned force predictions, these models have extended simulations to larger systems and longer times. This strategy has also been extended to condensed-matter lattice Hamilto-nians, where learned forces drive the collective dynam-ics [20–23]. Its scalability draws on locality: the force at a site is inferred from its surrounding configuration, allowing the same predictor to be applied throughout a large lattice. Incorporating the relevant microscopic symmetries ensures consistent predictions for physically equivalent local environments. Such models have en-abled large-scale studies of electronic phase separation and charge-density-wave (CDW), orbital, and magnetic ordering kinetics [24–29]. An alternative to force-field modeling is to learn a finite-time evolution map directly from order-parameter trajectories, relating a configuration to one at a later time. Such a model advances the field without evalu-ating microscopic forces and may permit larger effective time steps. In principle, it can also be trained on ex-perimental trajectories without knowledge of the under-lying Hamiltonian. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) provide a natural framework for learning these time se-quences, using an evolving hidden state to retain infor-mation from previous observations [30–32]. Architec-tures ranging from reservoir computing to long short-term memory (LSTM) networks have been used to pre-dict chaotic classical dynamics, molecular trajectories, and quantum evolution [33–42]. The need for temporal context also has a physical ba-sis. Although microscopic dynamics are Markovian when expressed in terms of a complete state, eliminating elec-tronic and other unobserved degrees of freedom can make the reduced dynamics non-Markovian [43–45]. Past ob-servations may therefore contain information absent from the present one; under suitable conditions, Takens’ theo-rem shows how delayed observations can reconstruct the underlying dynamical state [46]. This motivates mod-els that take a short history as input, including recent transformer approaches [47, 48]. An RNN instead compresses past observations into a recursively updated hidden state. The joint update of visible variables and hidden states is Markovian, while the visible dynamics alone can retain memory. From a coarse-graining perspective, the hidden states may en-code the influence of unresolved microscopic degrees of freedom without identifying them individually [49–51]. Some recurrent models have addressed spatially extended dynamics [36, 52, 53], but interacting lattice systems pose a further design question: how can local recurrent mem-ory be coupled through symmetry-aware GNN message passing to predict evolving textures across large lattices? Here we introduce a graph-recurrent neural network (GRNN) that learns the evolution of spatially distributed observables directly from trajectories, without specifying their microscopic equations of motion. We formulate it as a message-passing extension of a gated recurrent unit (GRU), whose gates regulate how a hidden state incor-porates new information and retains its past [35, 54]. Thus, even beyond the adiabatic approximation, retaining only ϕ produces effective non-Markovian dynamics; for example, states with the same displacements but different momenta can have the same ϕ and evolve differently. The GRNN ad-dresses this missing information through recurrent states that encode the influence of unresolved lattice motion, coupled through graph messages to account for spatial interactions. A central test of this learned description is whether it reproduces the temporal correlations of the emergent field. For the half-filled model, the numerical parameters and reference-trajectory protocol are summa-rized in Appendix B. The dataset comprises 30 independent trajectories, di-vided into 16 for training, 4 for validation, and 10 for testing, with complete trajectories kept separate across these subsets. We use three graph layers with feature di-mensions 1 → 128 → 96 → 64 and a readout MLP with four hidden layers and GELU activations. The bounded readout, specified in Appendix D, uses ϵ = 10 −5 and a trainable α initialized to 0 .1. Hidden states are initial-ized to zero and conditioned on reference configurations during an initial burn-in period. Training minimizes the trajectory mean squared error using truncated backprop-agation through time and AdamW, with cosine annealing and gradient clipping. After burn-in, reference inputs are used with probability 0 .2 during training; evaluation is fully autoregressive. The correlation benchmarks below use the held-out test trajectories. We first examine the normalized single-site autocor-relation function, A(τ ) = ⟨ϕi(t0)ϕi(t0 + τ )⟩/⟨ϕi(t0)2⟩,where ⟨· · · ⟩ denotes an average over lattice sites i and reference times t0 for which both configurations are avail-able. Here and below, t, t0, and τ denote physical times. This quantity probes the temporal persistence of local CDW order and provides a direct test of the time se-quences learned by the recurrent model. As shown in Fig. 2, the GRNN closely reproduces the KPM refer-ence results over the displayed time-lag range. While this agreement establishes the accuracy of local tempo-ral correlations, characterizing collective phase ordering also requires examining how correlations develop across the lattice. We therefore turn to the normalized equal-time spa-tial correlation function C(R, t ), which probes the orga-nization of the CDW field into growing domains. Fig-ure 3 compares the KPM reference dynamics and GRNN predictions at several stages of the evolution, with the separation x rescaled by the instantaneous correlation length L(t), so that the horizontal axis is R = x/L (t). The results demonstrate two complementary features. First, the GRNN reproduces the spatial correlation pro-files of the microscopic dynamics, including their decay and weak anticorrelations at larger scaled separations. Second, the curves at different times collapse onto a com-mon profile in both panels, consistent with dynamical scaling: the evolving domain structure is statistically self-similar when lengths are measured in units of L(t). Be-yond capturing local temporal behavior, the GRNN thus reproduces the collective spatial correlations and scaling structure characteristic of CDW coarsening. Finally, we apply the trained GRNN to a 200 × 200 lattice, demonstrating its ability to simulate extended CDW textures, as shown in Fig. 4. The evolving domain patterns are qualitatively consistent with those obtained in previous ML force-field simulations using descriptor-based and graph neural network models [26, 59]. These established features of CDW phase ordering provide a physical context for the large-scale demonstration, whose primary purpose here is to illustrate the scalability of the learned graph-recurrent dynamics. Combined with the quantitative temporal and spatial correlation bench-marks above, these results support the GRNN as an ef-fective reduced description of collective ordering. The dynamical-scaling analysis gives an algebraic growth law for the characteristic domain size, L(t) ∼ tα,with α < 1/2, slower than the Allen–Cahn law for a nonconserved scalar order parameter [8]. This sub-5Allen–Cahn growth is consistent with previous ML force-field simulations of CDW coarsening [26]. Its physi-cal origin is a kinetic constraint imposed by microscopic electron-number conservation. Although staggered CDW order is nonconserved, nearly binary site occupations tie local domain-wall displacement to charge rearrange-ment; consequently, a wall segment generally cannot ad-vance independently but must move cooperatively with compatible segments. This suppresses the independent curvature-driven motion underlying Allen–Cahn growth, while thermally induced partial occupations at the walls can relax the constraint and allow a crossover toward the Allen–Cahn regime [68, 69]. In summary, we have introduced a GRNN framework that combines local recurrent memory with graph mes-sage passing to learn the evolution of spatially distributed observables. Its hidden states encode information absent from the instantaneous physical field, while spatial cou-pling allows this information to influence the dynamics of neighboring sites. Learning finite-time evolution maps already allows the system to advance over intervals larger than the microscopic integration step; extending the pre-diction interval further could provide additional gains in computational efficiency. Because training requires tra-jectories of the selected observables rather than explicit microscopic equations, the framework is also suited to data-driven modeling from experimental measurements, where the underlying Hamiltonian or the relevant micro-scopic degrees of freedom may be incompletely known. A particularly compelling direction is the extension to nonadiabatic dynamics. Although the present adia-batic setting already involves an emergent field with un-resolved lattice motion, the electrons remain equilibrated to the instantaneous lattice configuration. In nonadi-abatic or time-dependent Hartree–Fock dynamics, the electronic sector has its own evolution, and its feedback on the order parameter can depend on electronic exci-tations and coherences that are not determined by the instantaneous order. Learning only the order-parameter evolution would therefore require the recurrent states to encode the influence of this much larger unresolved sec-tor. Such applications may demand larger hidden-state dimensions or more expressive memory architectures, al-though the required capacity is governed by the informa-tion relevant to collective evolution rather than the total number of microscopic variables. Extending the GRNN in this direction would provide a route to reduced dynam-ical models that retain electronic memory while avoiding explicit propagation of the full electronic state.
