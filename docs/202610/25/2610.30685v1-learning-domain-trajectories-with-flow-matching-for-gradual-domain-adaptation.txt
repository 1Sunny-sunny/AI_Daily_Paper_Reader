## Abstract 

Large distribution shifts remain a primary obstacle for domain generalization and unsupervised adaptation. Although gradual domain adaptation alleviates this difficulty by introducing ordered intermediate domains, most existing approaches still operate in a discrete, step-wise manner, which can accumulate errors and often relies on unstable Min–Max optimization. We propose FlowGDA , a flow-matching framework that models gradual adaptation as continuous transport on a statistical manifold in a representation space. FlowGDA learns time-dependent velocity fields that induce smooth domain trajectories, using a simulation-free regression objective rather than adversarial training. To prevent semantic drift, we couple left-to-right transport between consecutive domains with a source-anchored transport and a semantic consistency loss, and we further stabilize learning via a two-stage sequential optimization scheme. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method outperforms state-of-the-art techniques, validating flow matching as an effective alternative for domain adaptation.

Despite its promise, existing GDA methods typically treat domain evolution as a discrete sequence of adaptation steps. Such stepwise procedures may accumulate errors and fail to capture the smooth geometric structure of distribution shifts [23]. Many methods also rely on adversarial training [10, 31] or complex optimal-transport optimization [32, 25, 37, 38], which can be unstable or computationally demanding in high-dimensional representation spaces [12, 22]. ## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Gradual Domain Adaptation 

Gradual domain adaptation addresses large domain gaps by exploiting ordered intermediate domains. Early theory showed that self-training over intermediate domains can outperform direct adaptation when consecutive shifts are small, while later analyses established tighter bounds and characterized optimal adaptation paths [33, 42]. Algorithmically, existing methods construct or exploit intermediate domains through optimal transport, manifold-constrained robust optimization [23], graph-based adaptation [17], and domain osmosis [34, 35]. However, most approaches still treat the domain path as a discrete sequence, which may accumulate errors and underuse the smooth structure of gradual distribution evolution. Our work instead models this evolution as a continuous flow in representation space. 

2.2 Optimal Transport for Domain Adaptation 

Optimal transport provides a geometric framework for comparing and aligning probability distribu-tions [32, 25, 39]. It has been widely used in domain adaptation to learn transport plans that map source samples to target samples while preserving class structure [8]. Wasserstein-based methods further connect optimal transport with adversarial learning, including Wasserstein GANs [1, 13] and domain adaptation methods such as WDGRL [28]. Although these methods offer meaningful distributional geometry, they often require costly transport computation or adversarial optimization with difficult Lipschitz constraints, especially in high-dimensional feature spaces [22, 11]. FlowGDA keeps the geometric transport perspective while replacing adversarial alignment with simulation-free velocity-field regression.

## 4 Methodology 

FlowGDA formulates gradual domain adapta-tion as continuous transport in representation space.As shown in Fig. 2, discrete stepwise adaptation may accumulate local misalignment along the domain path. FlowGDA instead models domain evolution as a smooth trajectory on a statistical manifold, governed by a time-dependent velocity field uθ (z, t ).This velocity field is learned through flow matching, avoiding adversarial optimization and trajectory simulation. Fig. 3 summarizes the framework, including dual flow-matching losses, semantic consistency regularization, and two-stage sequential optimization over consecutive domain pairs.

4.3 Dual Flow Architecture 

For each adaptation step, FlowGDA uses two velocity fields. The first velocity field, denoted by 

uθlr , transports representations from the current domain Dl to the next domain Dr. The second velocity field, denoted by uθsl , transports source representations from the source domain Ds to the current domain Dl. Here, θlr and θsl are the trainable parameters of the left-to-right and source-to-left velocity networks, respectively. The left-to-right flow captures the local distribution shift between neighboring domains, while the source-to-left flow provides a semantic anchor from the labeled source domain. ## 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. Following the standard gradual domain adaptation (GDA) protocol, FlowGDA is eval-uated on four benchmarks covering complementary domain trajectories. Rotated MNIST captures smooth geometric shift through increasing rotation angles; Color-Shift MNIST captures appearance shift through gradual changes in color statistics; Portraits captures real-world temporal drift across 6decades; and Cover Type evaluates tabular covariate shift in structured feature space. For each bench-mark, domains form an ordered chain D0 = Ds, D1, . . . , DT = Dt according to the natural shift parameter. Only the source domain is labeled, while intermediate and target domains are unlabeled during adaptation. All methods are trained sequentially along the chain. 

Baselines. FlowGDA is compared with direct unsupervised domain adaptation baselines, including DANN [10], DeepCORAL [30], and CDAN [20], as well as gradual adaptation methods including GST [16], GOAT [14], AST [29], and CNF [27]. 

Architectures and optimization. All methods are implemented in PyTorch. Image benchmarks use a three-layer CNN encoder followed by a two-layer fully connected classifier, while Cover Type uses a three-layer MLP. Velocity fields are implemented as 4-layer residual MLPs with hidden dimension 512. AdamW [21] is used with learning rates 10 −4 for velocity networks and 10 −5 for the encoder. The classification weight is set to λcls = 0 .1. Models are trained for 10 epochs per domain pair with batch size 64 on NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPUs. 

5.2 Results 

FlowGDA achieves the best performance across all four benchmarks. Compared with the strongest reported gradual baseline, FlowGDA improves target accuracy by +6.4 points on Rotated MNIST, +7.4 on Color-Shift MNIST, and +2.8 on Portraits; on Cover Type, it outperforms GST by +2.6 points. Direct UDA baselines perform substantially worse under large shifts, confirming the impor-tance of intermediate domains. These results show that modeling gradual adaptation as continuous representation transport provides consistent gains over both direct alignment and discrete gradual adaptation. On Rotated MNIST, the effect of intermediate-domain choice is further evaluated by fixing the source at 0◦ and the target at π/ 3, while varying a single intermediate domain D1. As shown in Fig. 4(a), intermediate angles near the midpoint of the source-target path, around π/ 6, yield the highest and most stable accuracy, whereas very small or overly large intermediate shifts lead to lower accuracy and higher variance. Fig. 4(b) further shows that direct source-to-target adaptation performs worst, indicating that intermediate domains are essential for large shifts, while their quality further affects stability and final performance. 

5.3 Ablation Study 

To better understand how different components contribute across different types of domain shifts, we conduct a dataset-wise ablation study by removing one component at a time from FlowGDA. Table 2 reports the target accuracy on each benchmark, with the accuracy drop relative to the full model shown in parentheses. The effect of each component is not uniform across datasets. Removing the semantic consistency loss Lcls causes the largest degradation on average, especially on Portraits and Cover Type, where preserving class semantics under complex temporal or tabular covariate shifts is more challenging. The single-flow variant also suffers larger drops on the real-world and tabular datasets, indicating that source-anchored transport provides useful regularization beyond local left-to-right adaptation. Manifold-aware conditioning improves over time-only velocity parameterization across all datasets, while the two-stage optimization strategy consistently yields more stable final performance than joint optimization.

6.4 Why Flow Matching is More Stable than Adversarial Alignment 

Adversarial domain alignment solves a min–max problem, whose convergence depends on discrimina-tor optimality and Lipschitz enforcement, often leading to oscillatory dynamics in high-dimensional representation spaces. In contrast, flow matching reduces transport learning to a single-level regres-sion objective, producing unbiased gradients and avoiding saddle-point instability. 

Remark 6.11 (Optimization Landscape) . Flow matching optimizes min θ E∥uθ (z, t ) −

u⋆(z, t )∥2, a standard supervised regression objective, whereas adversarial alignment optimizes 

min ϕ max D J (ϕ, D ), which can exhibit multiple equilibria and sensitivity to critic capacity. This difference explains the empirically observed stability and faster convergence of our method. 97 Conclusions 

This work introduced FlowGDA, a flow-matching framework for gradual domain adaptation. By mod-eling domain evolution as continuous transport on a statistical manifold, FlowGDA learns velocity fields that move representations across domains through a simulation-free regression objective, avoid-ing adversarial optimization. Experiments on multiple benchmarks show that FlowGDA consistently outperforms existing gradual adaptation methods, demonstrating the effectiveness of continuous representation transport for large domain shifts.

## Limitations 

FlowGDA relies on ordered intermediate domains that form a meaningful gradual path between the source and target distributions. When such domains are missing, unordered, or contain abrupt semantic changes, the learned transport may no longer approximate a smooth domain trajectory. The method also assumes that class semantics are largely preserved along the path, so severe label shift or class-appearance changes can weaken the source-anchored consistency signal. In addition, although training is simulation-free with respect to ODE integration, FlowGDA introduces extra velocity networks and sequential optimization over domain pairs, which may increase implementation complexity and training cost compared with simpler direct adaptation baselines. 12 A Theoretical Proofs

B.1 Effect of Intermediate-Domain Quality 

We first analyze whether the quality of the intermediate domain affects FlowGDA. On Rotated MNIST, we fix the source domain as unrotated digits ( 0◦) and the target domain as digits rotated by 

π/ 3. We then use exactly one intermediate domain D1 and vary its rotation angle. For each setting, FlowGDA is trained with the same configuration and evaluated on the target domain over five random seeds. Fig. 5 shows that the best performance is obtained when the intermediate domain lies near the midpoint of the source–target trajectory, around π/ 6. Intermediate domains that are too close to the source or too close to the target lead to slightly lower accuracy and larger variance. This supports the intuition behind gradual adaptation: the intermediate domain should reduce the local transport difficulty while still lying on the underlying geometric trajectory of the shift. Direct source-to-target adaptation without an intermediate domain performs worse, indicating that the existence of an intermediate bridge is more important than the exact optimality of its location. 

B.2 Effect of the Number of Intermediate Domains 

We study how the number of intermediate domains affects FlowGDA. Table 3 reports target accuracy when using different numbers of intermediate domains. The setting with zero intermediate domains corresponds to direct source-to-target adaptation. As the number of intermediate domains increases, performance improves consistently across all datasets, with larger gains on Portraits and Cover Type. This is consistent with our theoretical analysis in Section 6: more intermediate domains reduce the discrepancy between consecutive domains, making each local transport problem easier. The improvement gradually saturates when the trajectory becomes sufficiently fine-grained. The results show two important trends. First, direct adaptation is consistently weaker than gradual adaptation, confirming the need for intermediate domains under large shifts. Second, the marginal gain from adding more domains decreases after a moderate number of intermediate domains. This suggests that FlowGDA benefits from finer trajectories, but does not require arbitrarily dense domain sequences once local shifts become sufficiently small. 

B.3 Robustness to Domain Ordering 

Gradual domain adaptation assumes that domains are ordered so that consecutive domains are closer than the source and target. To evaluate the importance of this assumption, we compare four orderings: the original ordered trajectory, a local shuffle that only swaps neighboring domains, a random permutation, and a reversed order. Table 4 reports the results. FlowGDA is robust to mild local perturbations, but performance drops when the domain order is random or reversed. This confirms that the method benefits from a meaningful gradual trajectory. The drop is most pronounced on Portraits and Cover Type, where the shifts are less controlled and the representation trajectory is more complex. These results also suggest that when domain order is noisy in practice, estimating a reasonable ordering or neighborhood graph before adaptation may further improve performance.

B.5 Hyperparameter Sensitivity 

Finally, we study the sensitivity of FlowGDA to key hyperparameters on the Portraits dataset. Table 6 reports the effects of the semantic consistency weight λcls , the hidden dimension of the velocity network, the number of curriculum levels for p, and batch size. FlowGDA is stable across reasonable ranges of these hyperparameters. The best performance is obtained with λcls = 0 .1, hidden dimension 512, four curriculum levels, and batch size 128. The semantic consistency weight shows a non-monotonic trend. Without Lcls , performance drops because transported representations may lose label information. However, an overly large λcls also hurts performance because it over-constrains the transport and limits adaptation to the target domain. The velocity-network width shows saturation after 512 hidden units, suggesting that excessive capacity is unnecessary. For the curriculum schedule, a moderate number of levels works best: too few levels make adaptation abrupt, while too many levels add optimization overhead without clear benefit.
