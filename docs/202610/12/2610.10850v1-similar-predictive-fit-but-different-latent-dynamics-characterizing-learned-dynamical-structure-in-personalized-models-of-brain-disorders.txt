Abstract 

As AI models move toward clinical decision-making and personalized treatment, understanding what amodel learns becomes important beyond predictive accuracy alone. This is particularly relevant in clinical populations, where patients are heterogeneous and latent representations learned by large-scale models remain difficult to interpret. We investigate whether personalized latent dynamics can reveal clinically associated differences even when predictive fit is similar. We employ a lightweight CNN–Transformer EEG foundation model, pretrained on the large-scale Temple University EEG Corpus (TUEG), to extract segment-level EEG representations. Using the Temple University Epilepsy Corpus (TUEP) as a clinical testbed, these representations are mapped to a shared latent-state space, and sparse multinomial logistic transition distributions (mLTD) are fit independently to each subject to obtain personalized latent transition-dependency graphs Wn. Group analyses use n=198 labeled subjects (99 epilepsy / 99 non-epilepsy). At k=4 , epilepsy subjects exhibit substantially denser learned dependency structure than non-epilepsy subjects (mean nonzero dependencies: 10.01 vs. 5.67; Mann–Whitney p=1 .1 × 10 −7), with the same group-level pattern observed at k=6 (19.90 vs. 13.46; p=5 .2 × 10 −5). Graph-derived features retain moderate subject-level group discrimination under stratified 5-fold subject-wise cross-validation (AUROC 0.68 at k=4 and 0.65 at k=6 ). In contrast, within-subject held-out log-likelihood is nearly identical between groups at k=4 (−0.992 vs. −0.991 ; p=0 .95 ), and next-state prediction is similarly matched (AUROC 0.855 vs. 0.861; p=0 .54 ). Therefore, similar predictive fit does not imply similar learned dynamics: the two groups are comparably predictable under their personalized models while differing substantially in the internal dynamical structure learned by those models. This distinction is important even before individual latent states acquire clinical interpretation, because predictive equivalence alone is insufficient to establish that personalized models have learned equivalent internal dynamics. These findings motivate complementary evaluation of predictive validity and learned dynamical structure before extending such models toward intervention-aware patient world modeling. 

Keywords: patient world models; personalized dynamical models; EEG foundation models; latent dynamics; epilepsy; transition-dependency graphs. 

# 1 Introduction 

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being developed for clinical applications, including personalized treatment and intervention-related decision-making [Rajpurkar et al., 2022, Vasey et al., 2022]. In neurological disorders, machine-learning approaches are also being explored for personalized and adaptive interventions, including closed-loop brain stimulation [Chandrabhatla et al., 2023]. Patient world models extend this 1arXiv:2610.10850v1 [cs.LG] 7 Oct 2026 direction by seeking to represent how an individual’s state evolves over time and, ultimately, how that trajectory may change under intervention. Nevertheless, the development and evaluation of AI models still focuses heavily on predictive performance, while providing more limited understanding of what information these models learn internally. This issue becomes increasingly important as models are pretrained on large-scale heterogeneous data and transferred across tasks, populations, or disorders, as in foundation models [Rajpurkar et al., 2022]. In clinical applications, limited understanding of learned representations may introduce uncertainty into their interpretation and use for decision-making. This issue is particularly relevant because patient populations are heterogeneous, disease-related alterations may differ substantially across individuals, and learned latent states often lack definitive state-level clinical ground truth. A model may therefore achieve good predictive performance without clearly revealing what patient-specific information it has learned. This challenge is particularly relevant for neurological disorders, where brain activity is inherently dynamic and disease-related alterations may be expressed not only in static neural patterns, but also in how neural states evolve over time. Epilepsy provides a particularly clear example: it has long been conceptualized as a dynamical disorder involving transitions among different brain states, and contemporary work increasingly emphasizes the time-evolving organization of epileptic brain networks [Da Silva et al., 2003, Br¨ ohl et al., 2024]. EEG directly measures electrical brain activity with high temporal resolution, making it well suited for investigating such temporal organization. For personalized patient models, it is therefore important not only to identify latent brain states, but also to characterize how these states evolve over time and how previous states are related to future states. However, comparable predictive performance does not necessarily indicate that personalized models have learned similar temporal organization. Even when subject-specific models achieve comparable predictive fit, their learned patterns of temporal dependencies may differ substantially across individuals or clinical groups. Predictive performance alone may therefore overlook clinically associated differences in the dynamical structure learned by these personalized models. This leads to a simple but important question: 

If personalized models achieve similar predictive performance, does this necessarily mean that they have learned similar temporal dynamics? 

We therefore test the hypothesis that similar predictive fit does not necessarily imply similar learned latent dynamical structure. 

Motivating progression. Preliminary analyses from the same research program provide two earlier levels of evidence motivating the present study (Appendix A). First, our lightweight EEG foundation model was pretrained on approximately 18,800 hours of the large-scale Temple University EEG Corpus (TUEG) and demonstrated competitive frozen cross-disorder transfer on Temple University Hospital Abnormal EEG Corpus (TUAB) and Temple University Epilepsy Corpus (TUEP), with an elbow near k=4 for latent-state discovery. Second, comparison with an independently pretrained encoder (LUNA-Base) showed that absolute state-occupancy patterns were strongly encoder-dependent, whereas occupancy-informed alignment yielded descriptive concordance of epilepsy-associated occupancy changes ( r∆=0 .96 ; exploratory). Building on these findings, we move beyond representation and state occupancy to subject-specific temporal dynamics. State occupancy describes where and how often a subject visits latent states, but not how the subject moves between them .

Present study. We investigate this question using scalp EEG, with epilepsy as a clinical testbed. Using the TUEP [Obeid and Picone, 2016], frozen representations from our EEG foundation model are mapped to a shared latent-state space, and the temporal dependencies among these states are modeled independently for each subject using multinomial logistic transition distributions (mLTD) [Tank et al., 2021]. For each subject, 2mLTD estimates a sparse latent transition-dependency graph Wn that summarizes predictive temporal depen-dencies among latent states. We then evaluate the resulting personalized models from two complementary perspectives. First, predictive validity asks whether an individual’s model can predict that same subject’s held-out trajectory. Second, characterization of learned dynamical structure asks how the model organizes temporal dependencies within that individual and whether this organization contains clinically associated information. Our goal is not to classify epilepsy, but to use epilepsy versus non-epilepsy as a clinical contrast to examine whether similar predictive fit can be associated with different learned dynamical structures. 

Contributions. We (i) separate within-subject predictive validity from characterization of learned dynam-ical structure; (ii) demonstrate a fit–structure dissociation that is robust across k=4 and k=6 latent-state resolutions; and (iii) show that personalized dependency structure contains moderate epilepsy-associated information. These results motivate structural characterization before extending personalized dynamical models toward intervention-conditioned patient world modeling. The present study is observational and presents this pipeline as an intermediate step for evaluating personalized dynamical models before their extension toward intervention-aware clinical applications. 

# 2 Methods 

2.1 Experimental pipeline 

Two EEG datasets, TUEG and TUEP, were used for model pretraining and personalized latent-dynamics analysis, respectively, following previous EEG foundation-model studies [Zhou et al., 2026, Jiang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025]. EEG recordings were band-pass filtered between 0.3 and 75 Hz, notch filtered at 60 Hz, mapped to the standard 19-channel international 10–20 montage, resampled to 200 Hz, and divided into non-overlapping 30-s segments. Signal amplitudes were normalized to approximately ±100 μV and subsequently scaled to [−1, 1] . Our lightweight EEG encoder was pretrained on 2,259,998 TUEG segments, corresponding to approximately 18,800 hours of EEG, using self-supervised reconstruction. The encoder was frozen throughout all TUEP analyses; no task-specific fine-tuning was performed. We obtained mLTD fits for 200 TUEP subjects. Group analyses use n=198 labeled subjects (99 epilepsy / 99 non-epilepsy); two recordings with missing group labels were excluded from all between-group comparisons. As illustrated in Figure 1, each 30-s EEG segment was mapped by the frozen encoder to a 128-dimensional representation. A single global k-means model was then applied across subjects to obtain a shared latent-state space. We use k=4 as the primary latent-state resolution, motivated by the elbow observed in our preliminary analysis of the TUEP representation space (Appendix A). We additionally evaluate k=6 as a finer state-space resolution to assess whether the main findings are robust to the choice of k. Because changing k changes the discretization of the latent space, individual states and dependencies are not assumed to correspond directly between the two resolutions. # 3 Results 

3.1 Similar predictive fit but different learned dynamical structure 

Our central question is whether comparable within-subject predictive performance implies similar learned latent dynamics (Fig. 2A–B; Table 1). 

Predictive fit is comparable. At k=4 , held-out CV log-likelihood was nearly identical in epilepsy and non-epilepsy ( −0.992 vs. −0.991 ; p=0 .95 ), as was within-subject next-state AUROC ( 0.855 vs. 0.861 ;

p=0 .54 ). Held-out CV log-likelihood likewise did not differ at k=6 (−1.399 vs. −1.370 ; p=0 .18 ). Thus, the personalized models achieved comparable out-of-sample predictive fit across groups, while the learned dependency structures nevertheless differed substantially. 

Learned structure differs. Despite comparable predictive fit, epilepsy subjects retained substantially more nonzero dependencies than non-epilepsy subjects at k=4 (10.01 vs. 5.67; p=1 .1 × 10 −7), corresponding to normalized dependency densities of 0.626 and 0.354 , respectively. Thus, the epilepsy models retained approximately 62.6% of all possible state-to-state predictive dependencies, compared with 35.4% in the non-epilepsy models, indicating a denser predictive dependency structure in the learned latent dynamics. The same group-level densification was observed at k=6 (19.90 vs. 13.46; p=5 .2 × 10 −5; normalized densities: 0.553 vs. 0.374 ), showing that the structural difference persisted after accounting for the larger number of possible dependencies at k=6 . # 4 Discussion 

4.1 Similar predictive fit can conceal different learned dynamics 

The central finding is that epilepsy and non-epilepsy subjects are similarly predictable under their personalized mLTD models but exhibit substantially different learned dependency structures. At k=4 , epilepsy subjects retained approximately 62.6% of the possible directed dependencies, compared with 35.4% in non-epilepsy subjects. At k=6 , the corresponding normalized dependency densities were 55.3% and 37.4%, respectively. Thus, although increasing k increases the absolute number of possible dependencies, the epilepsy group remained relatively denser after normalization by the size of the latent-state space. This group-level structural difference was observed despite comparable held-out predictive fit at both resolutions. This fit–structure dissociation highlights two distinct aspects of personalized model evaluation. Held-out likelihood and next-state AUROC ask whether a model predicts an individual’s unseen latent-state transitions. Properties of Wn, in contrast, characterize how the model organizes temporal dependencies among those states. Comparable predictive performance therefore does not imply that personalized models have learned equivalent dynamical organization. Features derived from Wn additionally provided moderate epilepsy versus non-epilepsy discrimination, suggesting that clinically associated information is present in the organization of learned latent dynamics even when overall predictive fit is similar. These associations should not, however, be interpreted as diagnostic performance or as evidence that the learned dependencies represent physiological causal interactions. 

Why this matters. This analysis addresses a fundamental model-evaluation question: whether comparable predictive performance is sufficient to conclude that personalized models have learned comparable internal dynamics. Our results show that it is not. Two clinical groups can be similarly predictable while their personalized models organize temporal dependencies differently. Establishing this distinction is important before assigning physiological meaning to latent structure or extending such models toward intervention-aware reasoning. Clinical interpretation of individual states and dependencies therefore represents a subsequent validation step rather than a prerequisite for characterizing learned dynamical structure. 

4.2 Implications for patient world models 

The present results do not establish Wn as a complete patient world model. Rather, they identify an intermediate evaluation problem before personalized dynamical models are extended toward intervention-aware reasoning. A patient world model should ultimately characterize both how an individual’s state evolves and how that trajectory changes under clinically relevant interventions. Our results show why predictive fit alone is insufficient: similar predictability can coexist with substantially different learned latent dynamics. 8A natural extension is to condition dynamics on a measured intervention or perturbation ut,

# 5 Limitations and Responsible Use 

Limitations. The learned k-means states are unsupervised and do not have established clinical or physiolog-ical semantics. The present analysis is observational and case–control; differences in Wn therefore represent predictive temporal dependencies rather than causal disease mechanisms or treatment effects. The higher null-model prevalence among non-epilepsy subjects at k=4 may partly contribute to the observed density difference, although non-null sensitivity analyses indicate that clinically associated structural information is not solely attributable to all-zero graphs. The personalized analysis also uses representations from a single frozen EEG foundation model, and cross-encoder concordance of subject-specific Wn remains to be established. Finally, prospective, test–retest, and longitudinal analyses will be required to establish the stability and clinical generalizability of the learned dynamical structure. 

Responsible use. The learned dependency graphs should be interpreted as research descriptors of model-learned latent dynamics rather than direct representations of underlying neurophysiology. Our framework provides a potential approach for evaluating whether clinically associated dynamical structure is present in a learned latent space before such representations are incorporated into downstream clinical AI or intervention-aware models. The resulting dependencies should not be interpreted as diagnostic biomarkers, causal brain graphs, or direct physiological connectivity maps. Accordingly, they should not be used for autonomous diagnosis, treatment selection, or intervention planning without prospective clinical validation. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We investigated whether personalized latent dynamical models can reveal clinically associated information beyond predictive fit alone. On TUEP, epilepsy and non-epilepsy subjects showed comparable within-subject predictive performance but substantially different learned dependency structures, with denser latent transition-dependency graphs in epilepsy. Features derived from these graphs additionally retained moderate information for clinical group discrimination. The central result is therefore not that mLTD provides a strong epilepsy classifier, but that similar predictive fit does not imply similar learned latent dynamics. These findings motivate evaluating predictive validity and learned dynamical structure separately. Characterizing whether personalized latent dynamics are predictively supported, stable, and clinically informative may provide an important intermediate step before such models are extended toward intervention-aware patient world modeling. 9References
