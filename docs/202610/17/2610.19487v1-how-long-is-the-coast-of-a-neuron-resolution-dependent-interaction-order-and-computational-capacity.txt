Mandlebrot’s coastline paradox reframes measurement as a joint property of an object and its ruler. Here we ask how observational resolution shapes the apparent structure of neural computation. We introduce a scale-indexed decomposition that separates the predictive information captured by direct dependencies from the residual outside that model family. Exact constructions show that projection can convert higher-order dependence into pairwise structure. Projection can increase the direct fraction of surviving information even as total predictive information decreases. A schematic branch-to-soma cascade grounds these effects in local coincidence detection, saturating integration and somatic mixing. Resolving organized nonlinear subunits improves approximation and raises effective representational rank. In a nested hierarchy, these gains occur without increasing information about the target. This distinction separates information availability from access by a restricted readout. A point-neuron or direct population model therefore establishes an effective description at a specified scale. It does not uniquely identify the finer computation that generated the observed relation. Which resolution is adequate depends on the observable and perturbation of interest. b

Keywords: neural computation, coarse-graining, maximum entropy, dendrites, higher-order interactions 

I. INTRODUCTION 

The question How long is the coast of Britain? did more than introduce a memorable fractal 1. It exposed a dependence that ordinary measurement language tends to hide: the answer belongs jointly to an object and to a ruler. A coarse ruler crosses bays and omits bound-aries that a fine ruler must follow. The resulting lengths disagree without either measurement being erroneous. The point neuron is neuroscience’s coarse ruler. From early threshold-unit models onward, a large branch of theoretical neuroscience has represented a cell by a small number of state variables and an input–output rule 2–6 .This abstraction underlies theories of recurrent dynam-ics, chaos, balanced excitation and inhibition, and popu-lation computation, and has proved extraordinarily pro-ductive despite suppressing the spatial structure of the cell. A related statistical tradition asks how much of pop-ulation activity can be captured by low-order dependen-cies alone. In small groups of cells, pairwise maximum-entropy models reproduce surprisingly rich collective ac-tivity from first- and second-order statistics 7. That ade-quacy is conditional: it is expected in small, sparsely ac-tive populations whatever the underlying interactions 8,it gives way to explicit higher-order terms in larger pop-ulations under natural stimulation 9, and it changes with which cells the model retains 10 .Against this background, a recent maximum-entropy analysis reports striking support for the coarse descrip-

tion at the level of the single cell: direct, equal-time de-pendencies on relatively few recorded neurons explain more than 90% of output entropy in mouse record-ings and 62% in C. elegans 11 . The analysis is careful about what is measured. Its inferred weights are func-tional dependencies among recorded cells, not necessar-ily synapses, and unexplained variability can combine higher-order dependence, temporal dependence, unob-served inputs, and stochasticity. We propose a scale-theoretic fromalism. The success of a direct model at the resolution of a population record-ing characterizes an effective relation among the variables that survive measurement and projection. It does not imply that the cellular computation realizing that rela-tion is itself direct, additive, or single-stage. This dis-tinction matters because a neuron is not, at finer resolu-tion, a single computational degree of freedom. Dendrites contain spatially distributed nonlinear subunits 12–14 , and the input–output transformation of a detailed neuron can require a multilayer artificial network for accurate func-tional emulation 15,16 . Thus, the population-level and dendritic descriptions are not competing accounts of the same object; they are descriptions at different obser-vational scales, connected by a many-to-one projection. The question is therefore not whether the reduced model is useful, but whether predictive adequacy at one scale warrants identifying its effective dependencies with the computation that produces them at another. The coastline analogy is deliberately epistemic. We do not claim that every dendrite possesses an exact frac-tal dimension over an unlimited range. Neuronal ar-bors do exhibit scale-dependent geometric organization and constrained branching 17–19 , but geometry is not the premise of the argument. What matters is that obser-vation can collapse many underlying degrees of freedom arXiv:2610.19487v1 [q-bio.NC] 14 Oct 2026 How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? 2into the same measured variable while nonlinear compu-tation persists below that resolution. We instead ask how much of a neuron’s computa-tion is resolved at a given observational scale, which distinctions are erased by projection, and which reap-pear as the ruler is refined. We approach this prob-lem through an information-theoretic lens together with constructive counterexamples in which the efficiency of neural-network architectures depends on the representa-tion available at each scale. These examples show that the same coarse input–output relation can be compati-ble with markedly different internal computations, even when the reduced description is highly predictive. The coastline problem for a neuron is therefore not whether computation is visible or hidden, but how its apparent structure changes with the scale at which it is observed.

> B. What Coarse-Graining Guarantees

Suppose that a coarser observable is obtained from the variables at resolution ε through Z = T (Xε). The data-processing inequality gives 

I(Y ; Z) ≤ I(Y ; Xε). (12) Thus coarse-graining cannot increase the total predictive information available about Y . In the notation of the previous section, Iavail can only decrease or remain un-changed as information is discarded by the projection. It is important, however, to separate this absolute in-formation loss from changes in the structure of the surviv-ing dependence. Equation (12) does not, by itself, order either the higher-order residual Ihigher or the normalized direct fraction F . The reason is that the optimal direct model is defined relative to the observable coordinates themselves, and that admissible family changes when Xε

is replaced by Z. A blanket claim that higher-order infor-mation must decrease under every coarse-graining would therefore be false. A stronger statement becomes possible under an addi-tional closure condition. Form the joint direct surrogate 

Q⋆ε (y, x) = Pε(x)Q⋆ε (y | x), (13) which preserves the true distribution of the observed in-puts while replacing the conditional response by its op-timal direct approximation. Project both the true joint distribution Pε and the surrogate in Eq. (13) through the same map T . If the conditional distribution induced by the projected surrogate remains within the direct fam-ily defined at the coarse scale, then the family is closed under that projection. Under this condition, two inequalities apply in se-quence. First, because the direct model is refitted after coarse-graining, the optimal coarse direct model can per-form at least as well as the projected fine-scale surrogate. Second, KL divergence contracts when both distributions are passed through the same stochastic map. Together these facts give

> C. Interaction Order Changes Under Projection

Coarse-graining can change not only how much infor-mation is retained, but also the order at which the sur-viving dependence appears. A dependency that is irre-ducibly higher-order in one set of observables can become an ordinary pairwise dependency after projection. This distinction is important for mechanistic inference: ob-serving pairwise structure at a coarse scale does not es-tablish that the process generating it is pairwise at finer scales. A three-spin construction makes this ambiguity exact. Let si ∈ {− 1, +1 }, then the joint probability distribution is 

PK (s) = exp( Ks 1s2s3)8 cosh K . (16) The distribution in Eq. (16) contains only a three-body coupling, with K setting the coupling strength. Because the interaction depends on the triad product s1s2s3,marginalizing out any single spin cancels the coupling term and leaves the remaining pair completely uncor-related. Therefore, every one- and two-spin marginal is uniform, and the pairwise maximum-entropy distribution 

eP (2) is uniform over the eight possible states. Its entropy is exactly 3 bits. The connected information of order three is therefore exactly the difference between the en-tropy of three independent spins and the true entropy of the coupled system 20,21 

I(3) = H[ eP (2) ] − H[PK ] = 3 − H[PK ]. (17) How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? 4At K = 1 .8, exact enumeration gives I(3) = 0 .823 bits. Thus the departure from the pairwise maximum-entropy description is entirely third-order in the fine variables. The interaction order changes when the same distribu-tion is viewed through a different ruler. To demonstrate how this interaction order shifts under coarse-graining, we write bi = ( si + 1) /2 and retain only x = b1 and a compressed variable z = b2 ∧ b3. Because x and z de-pend on disjoint fine coordinates, they are independent at K = 0 (zero coupling, where Eq. (16) is uniform). This provides a clean baseline: any mutual information observed at nonzero coupling reflects the coarse-grained effect of the triadic interaction, rather than a spurious dependence introduced by overlapping projections. At K = 1 .8, however, the ordinary mutual informa-tion between these same projected variables is 0.261 bits (Fig. 1). Part of the third-order dependence quantified by Eq. (17) has therefore become an ordinary pairwise dependency after projection. No new information has been created; the many-to-one map has changed the or-der at which part of the existing statistical structure is represented. The dependence on the ruler is even clearer under a second projection. If instead z = 1[b2 = b3], the pro-jected pairwise information is 0.823 bits, equal to the full value of I(3) in Eq. (17). This occurs because the equality variable encodes the product s2s3. The fine-scale inter-action s1s2s3 is thereby represented at the coarse scale as a dependency between two observables. Under this pro-jection, the entire third-order dependence survives, but its apparent interaction order has changed from three to two. The same fine distribution can thus exhibit no pairwise dependence in its original coordinates, a weaker pairwise dependence under one projection, and the full depen-dence as a pairwise relation under another. Interaction order is therefore not invariant under a many-to-one mea-surement map. It is a property of the statistical structure relative to the observables through which that structure is resolved. A strong pairwise dependency at coarse res-olution cannot, by itself, identify a pairwise mechanism at finer resolution. The ruler can change the apparent order of the computation.  

> D. The Branch-To-Soma Cascade

Having established that interaction order can change under mathematical projection, we now translate the same logic into a neuron-like architecture. The purpose is not to construct a compartmental model of a particu-lar dendrite. It is to show, with an exactly enumerable example, how familiar biophysical operations—local co-incidence detection, saturating integration, and somatic mixing—can act as successive coarse-graining maps. We therefore follow the same signal through three observa-tional rulers. Individual synaptic contacts, two dendritic branches, and a single somatic variable. The underlying input–output law is fixed throughout. What changes is the set of distinctions available to the observer, and un-der coarse-graining a neuron with genuinely synergistic substructure can masquerade as an additive unit.  

> 1. The fine-scale ground truth: NMDA coincidence detection

We begin at the finest resolution of the construction. Six independent binary inputs, x ∈ { 0, 1}6, are divided evenly between two three-input subunits. Think of a neu-ron receiving six independent binary inputs, distributed evenly across two dendritic branches. In this construct, the output probability is governed strictly by third-order interactions of the two input branches: 

P (Y = 1 | x) = 0 .06 + 0 .40( x1x2x3 + x4x5x6). (18) Thus the baseline firing probability is 0 .06, one active triplet raises it to 0 .46, and simultaneous activation of both triplets gives the maximum probability 0 .86. All 26 = 64 input configurations can therefore be enumerated exactly. The two products in Eq. (18) are deliberately sharp abstractions of local dendritic coincidence detection. NMDA-dependent dendritic events provide the biological motivation: because NMDA conductance is strongly volt-age dependent through extracellular Mg 2+ block, suffi-ciently coordinated local excitation can recruit a regener-ative response that isolated inputs do not produce 12,13,22 .The number three is not intended as a literal universal threshold for an NMDA event. Its role is mathemati-cal: x1x2x3 is nonzero only when all three members of the local group are active, providing the simplest exact representation of a third-order coincidence. At this contact-level ruler, the generative law is there-fore explicitly organized around third-order products. This does not mean that individual contacts carry no predictive information. Marginalizing over the other in-puts produces direct one-coordinate effects that an ad-ditive model can capture. What it cannot reconstruct is the full dependence on the joint configuration. For the coordinate-invariant categorical-additive family defined in Eq. (1), the direct fraction is consequently only 55 .0%. In other words, slightly more than half of the available information can be assigned to direct single-coordinate effects, while the remainder requires their joint configu-ration. > 2. Intermediate projection: sublinear summation and saturation

We next change the ruler to an intermediate macro-scopic one. Suppose that individual contacts are no longer resolved and that the observer instead has access to one effective variable from each dendritic branch. We define those branch observables by the saturated sums 

B1 = min( x1 + x2 + x3, 2) , B2 = min( x4 + x5 + x6, 2) ,

(20) so that each branch can now occupy only the states 0, 1, or 2. The hard ceiling in Eq. (20) is a stylized represen-tation of saturating or sublinear local integration, not a quantitative membrane equation. Real dendritic re-sponses can become sublinear through several conduc-tance and driving-force mechanisms 12 . As a local den-dritic membrane depolarizes toward the reversal poten-tial of its excitatory ion channels, the electrochemical driving force decreases, and voltage summation plateaus. However, active dendrites can also exhibit strongly supra-linear regimes 13,14 . What matters for the present con-struction is the many-to-one character of the map. Ex-plicitly, distinct microscopic input configurations become indistinguishable once only B1 and B2 are retained. The state collapse can be seen directly. If exactly two contacts on the first branch are active, then B1 =min(2 , 2) = 2. If all three are active, B1 = min(3 , 2) = 2 as well. The fine-scale states (1 , 1, 0) and (1 , 1, 1) are therefore assigned the same observed branch value even though Eq. (18) treats them differently. The triplet con-tribution is zero in the first case and nonzero in the sec-ond. The projection has removed precisely the distinc-tion needed to determine whether the local third-order gate was completed. That lost distinction reduces absolute predictive in-formation. The available information falls from Iavail =0.156742 bits at the six-contact ruler to 0 .028607 bits at the two-branch ruler. But the higher-order residual falls much more sharply, from Ihigher = 0 .0706 to 0 .0011 bits. Consequently, the information that remains after pro-jection is overwhelmingly describable by direct effects of the two observed branch variables. For the categorical-additive family, F rises from 55 .0% to 96 .1%. This is the important direction of the effect. The branch projection has not made the microscopic triplet interaction disappear from the generative mechanism. It has erased much of the observer’s ability to distinguish the microscopic states on which that interaction depends. The coarse conditional distribution therefore looks sub-stantially more direct than the fine one.  

> 3. Somatic projection: aggregation and the denominator trap

The final ruler retains only a single somatic observ-able, Z = ( B1 + B2)/2. The nine possible branch pairs (B1, B 2) are thereby compressed into only five somatic values, Z ∈ { 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0}. Relative to the original 64 synaptic configurations, this is an extreme contraction of the observable state space. The symmetric average performs a second kind of coarse-graining in addition to the within-branch satura-tion of Eq. (20). It removes branch identity and the spa-tial origin of the input. For example, ( B1, B 2) = (2 , 0) gives Z = (2 + 0) /2 = 1, while ( B1, B 2) = (0 , 2) gives exactly the same somatic value. The observer can there-fore no longer determine which branch generated a given contribution to the soma. The absolute information measures make clear how much has been discarded. Across the full cascade, Iavail 

falls from 0 .156742 to 0 .028607 to 0 .023526 bits. The cat-egorical higher-order residual simultaneously falls from 0.0706 to 0 .0011 to 0 .0000 bits (Fig. 2). The direct-information fraction for the categorical-additive family is therefore 55 .0% −→ 96 .1% −→ 100 .0% . (21) That is, the same underlying system moves from 55 .0% direct at contact resolution, to 96 .1% at branch resolu-tion, to 100 .0% at somatic resolution. The last number requires careful interpretation. At the somatic ruler there is only one observed coordinate, 

Z. The categorical direct family of Eq. (1) allows an arbitrary one-coordinate function u(Z), so there can be no residual interaction among observed coordinates (i.e. there is only one coordinate left). Thus Ihigher = 0 and 

Idir = Iavail exactly. The 100% result does not mean that the six-contact mechanism in Eq. (18) has become micro-scopically additive. It means that, after the projection has removed the relevant internal distinctions, all of the information still accessible at the somatic ruler is rep-resentable as a direct dependence on the single retained variable. This is the denominator trap in its sharpest form. The fraction F = Idir /I avail reaches unity while the denomi-nator itself contracts by 85%. The complementary total-entropy ratio makes that contraction visible: R is only 13 .6% at the contact scale, 4 .3% at the branch scale, and 3 .7% at the soma. A coarse model can therefore be perfect relative to the information remaining at its own resolution while retaining only a small fraction of the pre-dictive structure that existed before projection. High F

and low R are not contradictory statements; they answer different questions. These numerical ratios are not in-tended as a model of the recordings in Ref. 11 ; the cascade is a constructive example showing how projection can in-crease apparent directness while the predictive informa-tion retained by the coarse representation decreases. If the numerical labels 0, 1, and 2 assigned to a branch state were forced to en-ter a straight-line predictor, an apparent residual could arise simply because the chosen coordinate happened to be nonlinearly encoded. > 4. Control: asymmetric mixing via electrotonic attenuation

The symmetric average is intentionally a strong many-to-one projection, so the result should not be mistaken for a claim that every somatic mixing rule produces the same conversion. To test the dependence on the projection itself, we replace the symmetric mixture by 

Z = 0 .6B1 + 0 .4B2. Unequal weighting is biologically natural as an effective description because dendritic sig-nals undergo location- and state-dependent electrotonic transfer before reaching the soma 12 . A branch physically closer to the soma will experience less electrotonic at-tenuation than a distal branch. The particular 0 .6/0.4values are not intended as a fit to a specific morphology; they provide a controlled perturbation of the projection geometry. The asymmetry removes the degeneracy generated by symmetric averaging. The branch states (2 , 0) and (0 , 2), which both produced Z = 1 under equal mixing, now produce 0 .6(2) + 0 .4(0) = 1 .2 and 0 .6(0) + 0 .4(2) = 0 .8, respectively. The nine branch states now map to nine distinct somatic values, so branch identity is recoverable from the scalar somatic coordinate. For this control, the relevant comparison is the linear-moment family. The fully categorical family is uninfor-mative for distinguishing two one-dimensional scalar pro-jections, because an arbitrary function of a single scalar variable is direct by construction. The linear family in-stead asks whether the remaining input–output depen-dence is simple in the particular numerical coordinate produced by the projection. At the fine quantization used in Fig. S1, changing the somatic mixture from 0 .5/0.5 to 0.6/0.4 lowers the linear-family direct fraction F from 98 .9% to 78 .4%. The control therefore exposes the role of the map it-self. The microscopic law in Eq. (18) has not changed, nor have the six input variables. Only the projection from branch states to the measured somatic coordinate has changed. Yet the apparent directness of the result-ing description changes substantially. Directness is con-sequently not an intrinsic label that can be attached to the neuron independently of resolution. It belongs jointly to the underlying distribution, the coordinates retained by the observer, the model family used to describe those coordinates, and the biophysical projection that connects one ruler to the next.  

> E. From One Weight To A Field Of Contacts

The branch-to-soma cascade showed how spatial ag-gregation can make a neuron appear increasingly direct even when its fine-scale input–output law contains ex-plicit higher-order interactions. We now apply the same argument to a more elementary object in network mod-els: the synaptic weight. The question is what a single edge wij represents when the biological connection that it summarizes is resolved at progressively finer spatial scales. At point-neuron resolution, one connection is naturally represented by one effective coefficient. At contact res-olution, however, the same anatomical relation can con-tain multiple spatially embedded synaptic events whose influence depends on where they arrive and on the local state into which they arrive. The purpose of this section is therefore not to deny the usefulness of a scalar weight. It is to identify what has already been integrated out when a spatially distributed, state-dependent biological connection is represented by one number.  

> 1. The scalar-weight abstraction

In a standard point-neuron description, the influence from presynaptic cell j to postsynaptic cell i is repre-sented by a scalar coefficient wij . Depending on the model, that coefficient may multiply an instantaneous activity variable, a filtered spike train, or another effec-tive presynaptic signal. In each case, however, the spatial structure of the connection inside neuron i has been re-moved from the representation. The model retains one edge between two neuronal nodes rather than the indi-vidual sites at which that edge is physically realized. Synaptic efficacy at the soma depends on electrotonic distance, local membrane conductances, neighboring excitation and inhibition, and the nonlinear state of the dendritic compartment through which the signal propagates 12,13,15 . Thus two contacts associated with the same presynaptic neuron need not participate in the same local computation merely because a coarse graph assigns them to the same edge. > 2. Context-dependent influence and higher-order structure

Consider one resolved contact k from neuron j onto a dendritic branch of neuron i. Its contribution cannot in general be specified from presynaptic activity alone. If the local branch is relatively hyperpolarized and away from a conductance or voltage-saturation regime, acti-vation of the contact can produce one incremental re-sponse. If coincident activity elsewhere has already al-tered the local voltage, recruited active conductances, or changed the balance of excitation and inhibition, acti-vation of the same contact can produce a substantially different response 12–14 (e.g. the same spike from cell j at synapse k may generate zero additional depolarization). The relevant point is therefore not simply that synap-tic strength varies. It is that the effect assigned to one contact can depend on the state created by other con-tacts. Let Nε denote this fine-scale context: local mem-brane voltage, active conductance state, nearby synaptic activity, and other unresolved variables relevant at ruler

This distinction gives a precise information-theoretic meaning to local dendritic context. A contact whose ef-fective contribution is invariant across the surrounding state can remain informationally direct even if its abso-lute magnitude differs across cells or locations. A contact whose contribution changes according to the configura-tion of other resolved variables instead participates in a joint dependence. The latter is the fine-scale structure that disappears when the local context is removed from the observer’s coordinate system. This is the microscopic version of the branch example in Section V. At the coarse ruler, neuron j may appear to exert one direct influence on neuron i. At the fine ruler, that influence can be distributed across contacts whose effects depend jointly on their dendritic location and on the activity surrounding them. The anatomical connection remains monosynaptic; the informational de-composition need not remain additive.

> 4. Anatomical vs. informational directness

The distinction exposes a conceptual trap that recurs throughout computational neuroscience and connection-ist network models: anatomical directness and informa-tional directness answer different questions. Anatomical directness asks whether cell j forms a direct connection onto cell i. Informational directness asks whether the dependence of the output on the variables resolved by the observer can be represented without irreducible joint terms. The distinction can be stated formally. Let Aij = 1 de-note the existence of an anatomical connection from neu-ron j to neuron i, so that at contact resolution Aij = 1 whenever nij (ε) > 0. Informational directness at ruler ε,by contrast, is the condition that the resolved conditional law lies within the direct family of Eq. (1), equivalently that the higher-order residual vanishes. There is there-fore no implication 

Aij = 1 ̸ ⇒ Ihigher (ε) = 0 . (26) The left-hand side of Eq. (26) is a statement about adja-cency. The right-hand side is a statement about factor-ization of the conditional response. A monosynaptic anatomical connection therefore need not correspond to an additive computational interaction at finer resolution. Multiple contacts from the same axon can occupy different dendritic locations, participate in different local nonlinear regimes, and interact with dif-ferent subsets of the surrounding input 12,13 . When those variables are resolved, the response can contain higher-order structure. When they are collapsed through the somatic projection of Eq. (24), that structure can be hidden inside one effective edge. Anatomical directness consequently does not imply informational additivity. A minimal counterfactual makes the distinction ex-plicit. The existence of a contact field does not prove that every biological con-nection exhibits large higher-order information in every operating regime. At contact resolution, a single neuron-to-neuron edge can unfold into the contact field of Eq. (22), dis-tributed across spatially distinct local domains. At neu-ronal resolution, that field is compressed back into the effective weight of Eq. (24). The topology of the compu-tational description therefore changes with the level at which nodes and edges are defined.

> F. Resolution Expands An Approximation Space

Having established that projecting a spatially dis-tributed contact field onto a scalar weight can remove or re-express higher-order interaction structure, we now invert that perspective. Instead of asking what structure becomes inaccessible when fine variables are projected away, we ask what becomes representable when those variables are progressively resolved. Here we map the complex physical geometry of a neu-ron onto the mathematics of approximation theory de-veloped above. A model can only construct functions from the variables and nonlinear features made available at its chosen ruler. A point-neuron description that ex-poses only a few input coordinates therefore operates in a smaller approximation space than a description that additionally resolves nonlinear combinations generated within distinct subunits. Refining the ruler by un-pooling distinct dendritic subunits does not change the target function. It enlarges the feature space in which that same target can be approximated. This is the computational counterpart of the coastline argument. A coarse ruler does not prove that the under-lying object has few degrees of freedom. Rather, it re-stricts the degrees of freedom available to the description. Conversely, resolving additional subunits can expose in-teraction terms that were absent from the coarse repre-sentation. The following constructions test that claim in two stages. We first compare point, shuffled-contact, and spatially organized models at fixed contact count. We then construct a genuinely nested hierarchy in which the same architecture is viewed at progressively finer res-olutions. This refinement also connects directly to the information-theoretic formalism developed above. The distinction between direct and higher-order information is defined relative to the coordinates made available to the model. For example, the interaction x1x2 is a joint dependence when the resolved coordinates are (x1, x 2, . . . ), but becomes a one-coordinate dependence if the refined description explicitly contains the subunit variable z12 = x1x2. Refinement can therefore move structure that appears in Ihigher at a coarse ruler into Idir 

at a finer one, without changing the underlying input– output law.

All models are evaluated on held-out samples rather than on their training error. The point of the experiment is therefore not whether a sufficiently flexible model can interpolate the target, but how efficiently different rep-resentations approximate it out of sample.

III. DISCUSSION A. Effective descriptions need a scale 

The adequacy of a low-order statistical model in ex-plaining observed neural activity is a joint property of the underlying generative mechanism and the ruler through which it is observed. Pairwise maximum-entropy models can explain collective activity in small, sparsely active population of neurons 7, but this measure permits accu-rate fits even when the underlying interactions are not pairwise 8. Common input can be a source of higher-order correlations 24 , and larger neural populations can have explicit higher-order terms 9. The finding that di-rect, pairwise dependencies explain much of the output entropy of recorded neurons 11 therefore establishes an ef-fective description at an observation scale rather identify-ing the underlying fine-scale computational mechanism. It does not provide a window to the computation im-plemented by intracellular voltages, channel states and contact locations absent from that description. Our prior work illustrate why both the observable and the measure scale matter. In human cortex, pair-wise models that exclude inhibitory neurons substan-tially overestimate excitatory synchrony 10 . In multielec-trode recordings from cats, monkeys and humans, neu-ral avalanche statistics differ between spikes and field-potential, cautioning against interpreting apparent scal-ing as a unique criticality signature of the underlying generative mechanism 25 . Spike avalanches show no clear power-law scaling, and the apparent scaling of local-field-potential negative peaks is shared by positive peaks un-related to firing. Temporal coarse-graining of excita-tory and inhibitory ensemble activity reveals coordina-tion that persists across scales, while surrogates that ran-domize the precise timing of spikes disrupt that struc-How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? 15 ture 26 . These observations motivate examining which relations survive a change of ruler, rather than assum-ing that either universal simplification or universal loss of organization follow coarse-graining.  

> B. Projection and mechanistic non-identifiability

Our information-theoretic formalism separates two consequences of coarse-graining. It allows us to discern information loss from changes in apparent interaction or-der. Data processing inequality guarantees that under no projection the available predictive information could in-crease; however, it does not constrain the interaction or-der at which the surviving dependence may manifest. A strictly third-order dependency may disguise as pairwise interaction under projection, with partial or complete in-formation preservation depending on the map (Fig. 1). Along the branch-to-soma cascade, the direct fraction F

increases while available information decreases and the total-entropy fraction R falls (Fig. 2). This denominator trap concerns the fraction of surviving information un-der coarse-graining captured by the direct family, not the total-entropy fraction R. Residual Ihigher contraction is mathematically guaranteed under the closure condition of Eq. (14); without it, no description of universal in-teraction order is meaningful (Fig. S1). This traces the boundary of effective theories for neural systems: inte-grating out degrees of freedom renormalizes the orders of interactions as well as their strengths. Eliminating a me-diating variable generates higher-order couplings among those that remain, while merging variables into a collec-tive coordinate can instead absorb an interaction into the limiting definition of that coordinate. Apparent interac-tion order is therefore a property of the retained coordi-nates and descriptive measure, rather than an invariant of the generative mechanism. A recent work shows that fitting a maximum-entropy model while constraining only a selected set of sum-mary statistics is equivalent to projecting the empiri-cal sampling distribution onto that restricted family 27 .Therefore, any higher-order or nonlinear interactions that covaries with the constrained statistics cannot be represented explicitly; instead, it will be absorbed into the low-order parameters. This misattribution— what they call “feature leakage”—would be particularly pro-nounced in sparse, unevenly sampled binary data. When input sampling is sparse and biased, rare input com-binations and biased sampling would cause different nonlinear/higher-order mechanisms to project onto the low-order statistics, so they look like mere shifts in mean firing or pairwise covariance. To isolate this effect, they use a procedure that keeps each neuron’s conditional re-sponse distribution (the probability of each output given a specific input pattern) unchanged and only alter the input-state weights used in the fit, so any change in at-tribution must come from the sampling geometry. An example would be replacing empirical sampling weights with product-marginal or balanced weights. Because the conditional responses are unchanged, it would fol-low that that any change in the fitted attribution must stem from the altered sampling geometry. In their ex-amination of hipocampal recordings, the median frac-tion of explanatory power attributed to first-order terms falls from 0.75 (empirical) to 0.68 (product-marginal) and 0.46 (balanced). One can then conclude that the ap-parent low-order explanatory power depends on which input states were over- or under-sampled rather than the system harboring genuinely low-order generative structure 27 .Our construction unifies two apparently conflicting ob-servations — that a low-order fit can be predictively ad-equate under the observed state distribution 11 and that the same fit can fail to identify the underlying condi-tional mechanism 27 — by showing they probe different arguments of the same object. The “direct fraction” is a function of three variables: i) the distribution over input states, ii) the measurement map that defines the observed coordinates, and iii) the model family used for fitting. One analysis computes the direct fraction at the empiri-cal value of all three (distribution, observed coordinates, and chosen model), concluding predictive adequacy un-der those conditions 11 . The other keeps the coordi-nates and model fixed but perturbs the sampling distri-bution, finding the attribution unstable and thereby re-vealing feature leakage 27 . We instead vary the measure-How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? 16 ment map and the fitted family at fixed mechanism and show that interaction order itself need not be preserved .Leakage is the sampling-geometry counterpart of the state-space many-to-one measurement map. A concen-trated input distribution folds higher-order interactions into effective low-order parameters in parameter space, just as a many-to-one measurement map converts inter-action order in state space (Figs. 1, 2). The scale-indexed decomposition provides the coordinate system in which both perspectives are visible simultaneously: changing the distribution, the observed coordinates, or the fitted family lawfully changes the direct fraction. Predictive ad-equacy and mechanistic non-identifiability are therefore two sides of one moon; the scale-indexed decomposition brings the dark side of that moon into view.

Cable theory provides the bound when the hypothesis is justified. If the dendritic tree were pas-sive, linearity and superposition would translate to each synaptic contribution being routed through a fixed trans-fer impedance. Ac-tive dendritic conductances or nonlinear synaptic inter-actions violate superposition. This departure from pas-sivity can invalidate the weighted-sum sufficiency even if a low-order fit appears to provide adequate statistical predictivity. Neuronal biophysics determines how spatially dis-tributed inputs invoke local dendritic responses and turn into somatic signals. Biophysical properties such as local voltage, NMDA-receptor occupancy, and calcium dynamics are distinct state variables with different input dependencies and time constants. The point is to reveal what is lost when an observer makes fewer distinctions than the fine response law mandates. The spatial ruler also has a biophysical root. The appropriate spatial ruler (the number of effectively inde-pendent subunits and the weights of the somatic mixture) therefore depends on the network state as well as the ge-ometry of the excitable units. Read in the other direction, the same physics ex-plains why neuronal geometry and spatial organiza-tion of contacts is computationally load-bearing. Scat-tering a multi-contact connection across electrically semi-independent compartments need not be anatomical redundancy; it can enlarge the set of nonlinear features available to the cell. Synaptic convergence onto a nonlin-ear compartment invokes local coincident activity 13,14,16 .In contrast, scattered input across compartments pre-serves distinct response and limits shared saturation 12 .Therefore, how the inputs share compartments determine what nonlinear combination of the inputs can be eco-nomically computed by the neuron 15 . Multicontact seg-regation therefore enables the retention of higher-order cross-terms as distinct features that a single summa-tion node would otherwise erase. This is the biophys-ical counterpart of the approximation-space argument: organized subunits expose useful features (Fig. 3), and resolving additional subunits can enlarge the computa-tional space accessible to a restricted readout (Fig. 4). Those transformations are determined by morphology, conductances and contact placement rather than by con-tact count alone. Compressing each neuron’s compu-tation to a node and each connection to a scalar edge removes contact-to-subunit assignments unless they are compensated through additional state or effective param-eters. Cell-level connectivity therefore constrains the cir-cuit but does not fully determine the computation per-formed at its nodes.  

> D. Computational scale in neuro-inspired network design

Our approximation results show why simply match-ing neuron counts and weight estimates is insufficient for principled neuro-inspired design. In our compari-son, three aligned subunits can reach the accuracy that forty fixed sigmoid random features (Fig. 3). Shuffling the contacts degrades the network performance despite preserving contact count and subunit count. The gain therefore depends on which inputs share a nonlinear op-eration. The nested hierarchy makes this effect explicit;

expands the readout’s approximation space. The re-sult is prediction improvement and an enhanced effective rank as resolution exposes useful higher-order interac-tions (Fig. 4). These results identify a concrete architec-tural choice. One options is to harness useful interactions by by adding variables inside each unit so those compu-tations remain local. Alternatively, one can keep units simple and rely on a larger network to reconstruct the same interactions. A biological neuron and an artificial unit need not occupy the same computational scale. The same compression issue arises when one tries to abstract the connectome wiring and map it into a network design. Equation (29) collapses distinct presynaptic-to-subunit relations into one cell-level edge. The compression factor χ(ε) counts that multiplicity, and Eq. (35) shows that apparent sparsity depends on the chosen graph. A biological connection composed of multiple synaptic contacts therefore carries organization detail that a cell-level adjacency does not specify. Ad-jacency alone does not determine functional influence. Equation (24) makes the effective weight a function of the contact field and local state. And in Eq. (28), the same anatomical edge contributes α+γn as dendritic con-text changes. Morphology, contact placement and mem-brane state thus sit beneath the graph and shape com-putation. Connectome-constrained models reproduce fly visual tuning 35 and sensorimotor responses 36 . Their suc-cess supports the tested cell-level descriptions, but it does not recover the within-cell organization lost via projec-tion. Functional connectomics can provide a practical route from biological measurements to network design if the ex-tracted priors reflect scale-dependent measurements. Us-ing MICrONS (Machine Intelligence from Cortical Net-works) data 37 , we have shown how activity-derived rela-tionships and cortical geometry can guide recurrent net-works that outperform randomly initialized baselines 38 .We used functional relationships to initialize recurrent weights, neuronal positions to define spatial costs, and anatomical connectivity to constrain network communi-cability. These constraints improve task performance. Functional initialization provides the largest performance gain, while measured geometry adds a further bene-fit without increasing model size 38 . Our scale-indexed decomposition framework extends this design question to dendritic structure, where contact-to-subunit assign-ments determine which nonlinear features become acces-sible. The biophysical models provide concrete implemen-tations of the interactions our theory highlights. The voltage-dependent NMDA factor in Eq. (44) couples each contact to local depolarization, enabling context-dependent integration. In detailed model of pyramidal neurons, optimizing synaptic strengths and locations can enable reasonable XOR and ten-bit parity performance 39 .Removing NMDA conductances or dendritic structure impairs that capability. These are in-silico model demon-strations, not evidence of in-vivo computation. How-ever, they illustrate that computational power depends on internal organization suppressed by single-node de-scriptions. A bilinear reduction captures part of that or-ganization through location- and timing-dependent pair-wise coefficients 40 . Seen through the lens of our frame-work, such approach enlarges the effective family within How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? 18 its tested subthreshold regime. For neuro-inspired design, the question is therefore ex-plicit: which contact groups, nonlinear features and state dependencies should each model unit retain? The same question applies when reducing circuit motifs for compo-sition. We have shown that coupled models of canonical circuit motifs (divisive competition and winner-take-all normalization) can generate joint computational oper-ations absent from the uncoupled motifs 41 . A reduc-tion adequate for an isolated motif must therefore be reassessed when that motif interacts with others, an-swering does the composition preserve the state dependen-cies through which the coupling acts? Our scale-indexed decomposition and computational results provide con-trolled comparisons that answer the question of what to retain, beyond merely matching cell counts or copying cell-level connectivity. 

E. How long is, then, the coast of a neuron? 

In analogy with Mandelbrot’s coastline paradox 1, the question is well posed only after specifying i) the ruler, ii) the target observable, iii) the class of perturbations, and iv) the domain of conditions. Our scale-dependent con-struction makes mechanistic non-identifiability explicit and shows why the amount of information lost at a given scale in a biological regime must be determined empiri-cally. One should hold the output fixed and then ask how much extra predictive information finer-grained variables contribute. That comparison is a direct test of observa-tional sufficiency (Eq. (43)). Doing so isolates the con-tribution of higher-order interactions and finer variables from the residual that any fitted model family leaves un-explained. One can then apply perturbations (such as branch-targeted inhibition, shifts in spatial input cluster-ing, or changes to the temporal input regime) to probe whether an observational description that was adequate continues to be so under new conditions. The continued success across such tests supports the validity of reduc-tion and corase-graining within the tested domain. Such empirical support, however, does not uniquely identify the microscopic implementation. A coarse variable can be simple to record, easy to fit, and straightforward to simulate. It may even ac-count for most of the in-distribution output entropy. Yet that convenience can be misleading. The same somatic or point description that suffices to forecast spikes in a fixed population regime may fail to capture phenomena that depend on subcellular detail. Branch voltages, for example, may be essential to explain coincidence detec-tion or distal–proximal coupling. Likewise, contact- or spine-resolved variables are often required for questions about plasticity. Conversely, resolving more microscopic detail can in-crease estimation error without improving the prediction of interest 23 . Interaction order, directness, sparsity, and capacity are joint properties of the neuron and the cho-sen scale of description. Integrating out hidden degrees of freedom renormalizes apparent interaction strengths and can change which orders of interaction appear dominant. The appropriate scale is therefore question-dependent. Neither tractability nor goodness of fit alone establishes that a coarse-graining preserves the quantities an infer-ence about computation requires. The point-neuron is then a ruler. It is a useful, predictive abstraction. How-ever, it becomes misleading when its readings are re-ported as properties of the coast.
