## Abstract 

A fundamental bottleneck for translating thoughts to speech is the high physical cost of motor input forcing a strict tradeoff be-tween communication speed and expressivity. Generative AI offers a promising solution by expanding highly abbreviated text input into fluent sentences, significantly reducing the motor burden on the user. However, sparse inputs are inherently ambiguous; without sufficient context, AI models frequently misinterpret these abbrevi-ations and generate outputs that fail to align with the user’s true cognitive intent. We propose Cognitive Steering , a framework that can be leveraged to reconstruct user intent by grounding Large Language Models (LLMs) in multimodal context and applying AI control methods. We demonstrate that aligning frontier models with selected environmental referents, emotional states, and commu-nication motives on simulated abbreviation benchmarks achieves up to ∼88% top-5 expansion accuracy, doubling no-context base-lines. We also reveal that different types of context require distinct AI control techniques: while physical objects in a scene are best provided through explicit text prompts, abstract factors like emo-tion may be more effectively managed by directly altering the AI’s internal activations. These findings suggest a hybrid architecture for future interfaces that leverage Cognitive Steering to help users communicate both quickly and accurately, recruiting multiple AI control methods simultaneously to perfectly capture their intended communication goals.

## 1 Introduction 

Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) systems for users with severe speech and motor impairment, such as those with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), suffer from a fundamental bottleneck: they require limited high-cost motor input to express high-bandwidth cognitive intent. Although humans can speak ∼150 words per minute [1], eye-gaze typing interfaces restrict transmis-sion to roughly ten words per minute [18]. Generative AI offers a transformative opportunity to bridge this gap by expanding low-bandwidth inputs into fluent natural language (Fig. 1A) [13]. Recent systems allow users to transmit highly compressed signals—extreme abbreviations like “h-w-t-g-o-a-p” which are expanded into full sentences (“he wanted to go out and play”) [7]. We view the user’s abbreviated input not as incomplete data, but as a compressed signal—a “blurred pattern”— that the AI must use to reconstruct the user’s intent [17]. Large Language Models (LLMs), much like human cognition, possess a capacity to reconstruct user intent from such minimal inputs based on contextual cues and grounding [20]. In human communication, this reconstructive capacity is largely governed by pragmatics, i.e., the ability to derive intended meaning beyond literal syntax and semantics by relying on shared context, environmental constraints, and conversational maxims [11, 28]. However, relying on LLMs introduces a critical Cognitive Align-ment (CA) challenge: without sufficient context, models frequently misinterpret ambiguous user intent [30]. For example, a user enter-ing an abbreviation like “h-w-t-g-o-a-p” may intend “he wanted to go out and play”, yet in a different context, the same compressed input could be expanded into the unintended but plausible alter-native, e.g., “how we think governs our actual perception”. Hence, while abbreviation expansion significantly reduces motor load, it in-troduces a problem: Cognitive Misalignment (CM). We functionally define CM as the difference between the user’s intended message and the AI’s interpretation of the user’s intent. This may happen for many reasons, e.g., lack of theory of mind or misinterpreted user intent [30], leading the AI to unexpected behavior. This misalignment between human and AI may be best addressed by treating the LLM not as a digital replica of human cognition, but as a distinct form of intelligence [5, 12]. While cognitive science has extensively mapped the pragmatic cues humans rely on to resolve ambiguity [9], we lack a comparable understanding of the requirements that enable LLMs to perform similar feats [37]. There is no guarantee of an isomorphism between the signals that ground human understanding and those that appropriately influence the model’s behavior. Therefore, rather than imposing an anthropocentric understanding onto these systems, we must empirically interrogate them with minimal assumptions [25]. This challenge necessitates the analysis presented in this study. In order to build a capable and robust Cognitive Communication Interface (CCI) for humans and AIs (Fig. 1B), we must identify the signals required to achieve CA so the model’s behavior outputs what the user intended without costly interventions. Here, we an-alyze how different contextual information sources, particularly those from commercially available eye-tracking interfaces [3], can be leveraged to reconstruct abbreviated user inputs across both synthetically generated and real-world datasets, by exploiting prag-matic elements of the context for CA (Fig. 1C). We demonstrate that achieving better CA may require distinct technical interventions at test-time inference. While discrete semantic grounding bene-fits from explicit prompting, high-level intent, like those shaped through latent factors like emotion, can be effectively managed through targeted model interventions of its internal activations at test-time, i.e., activation steering [33], which has been shown to shift the recruitment of latent model concepts [4]. We believe these results will help inform the future engineering and design decisions needed for next-gen CCIs that are bidirectionally aligned between humans and AIs [26]. 

## 2 Methods. 

Our Generated Dataset. Evaluating whether LLMs can resolve ambiguities as a consequence of Cognitive Misalignment requires the use of benchmarks that systematically vary the amounts of pragmatically relevant contexts under which utterances are pro-duced. Here, we focus on investigating the impact of four pragmatic factors (Fig. 1C): (1) Motive of Communication (MOC) : why the user is speaking and what functional goal are they aiming to achieve? For example, is the user requesting, sharing, or informing [32]. Multi-System Cognitive Steering for Generative Communication BiAlign ’26, April 15, 2026, Barcelona, Spain 

(2) Explicitness : how concretely has the user expressed them-selves? The gradient of underdetermination theory [23] de-fines three levels, namely elliptical (i.e., fragmentary, high-context productions such as single words or gapped construc-tions), underspecified (i.e., syntactically complete sentences using pronouns or deictic expressions such as “that one” or “over there”), and explicit (i.e., full noun phrases with disam-biguating attributes). (3) Referents : what are the concrete entities (objects, people, locations) in the physical world that a speaker is talking about. (4) Emotions : what is the speaker’s latent affective state (e.g., frustration, sadness, neutrality), which acts as a global mod-ulator on the utterance. Our data generation pipeline proceeds through four sequential stages designed to construct each evaluation datum with complete and separate control over these variables. First, a scenario configura-tion is sampled from a structured ontology of over 235,000 permuta-tions of environmental, embodied, and social factors. These factors include real-world domain (e.g., medical, office), visual complex-ity (how cluttered a scene is), attentional states (e.g., joint, disen-gaged, face-to-face), ego posture (from where the first-person view is taken from), and interlocutor dynamics (the relation between user and interlocutor, epistemic and social). This scenario configu-ration constrains what a speaker could plausibly say and why with considerable diversity across the generated set. Second, referential grounding uses a prompted LLM to populate each generated sce-nario with a target object (the semantic anchor of the utterance) and a controlled set of distractor objects in the scene. Third, visual scene generation translates the seeded scenario into structured prompts for a text-to-image model ( Gemini nano-banana ), producing ego-centric scene images that preserve the camera perspective, object composition, and atmospheric cues implied by the scenario factors. Finally, utterance generation independently and uniformly samples values for the three other pragmatic factors—MOC, Explicitness, and Emotion—then prompts an LLM to produce a naturalistic spo-ken utterance that is grounded in the scene and shaped by these sampled factors. The utterance is then reduced to an abbreviation (as explained in the next paragraph), yielding the compressed input signal that the LLM under evaluation must expand back into the intended sentence. 

Image-Chat dataset. To complement our generated benchmark with an external dataset acquired from human users, we adapt Image-Chat [27], a large-scale dataset of grounded human dialogues about images. In Image-Chat, crowdworkers were each assigned a personality trait (e.g. adventurous, sentimental) and asked to converse about a photograph from the YFCC100m corpus; alternat-ing between Speaker A and Speaker B. We analyze only the first two dialogue interactions. We selected 150 random conversations from the dataset, where both the opening (Speaker A) and response (Speaker B) utterances contain two to seven words, filtering out entries containing slang, multi-sentence turns, grammatical errors, and pejoratives. The original dataset does not include any descrip-tion of the image, so we sent the corresponding YFCC100m image to a multimodal LLM ( Gemini-3-Flash ) to get a scene-description from a first-person point of view, and a list of all distinct visible objects, people, and notable items. 

Abbreviation encoding. We simulate AAC-style compressed input by reducing each word in a target utterance to its first letter. High-frequency common words ( a, the, is, to, it, are, that, not, you, and )are given verbatim in square brackets (e.g. [the] b [is] r h ). The AAC keyboard can include these common words in the interface (Fig. 1B). 

Context layers. To systematically test which context information improves abbreviation expansion, we define a hierarchy of context layers that incrementally enrich the prompt. For the generated dataset, the layers are: L0 (abbreviation only), L1 (+ communicative intent and motive of communication), L2 (+ speaker emotion and explicitness), L3 (+ target and distractor referents), and L4 (+ scene description). An additional ablation condition ( 𝐿 4 \ 𝐿 2) provides referents and scene description but withholds speaker emotions. For the Image-Chat dataset, the layers are: L0 (abbreviation only; for Speaker B, the preceding turn is always included), L1 (+ speaker personality), L2 (+ interlocutor personality), L3 (+ objects visible in the image), and L4 (+ full scene description). We also included the condition 𝐿 4 \ 𝐿 2. 

Frontier model evaluation via structured prompting. For each con-text layer, we construct a structured prompt containing the ab-breviation, a token-by-token constraint specification (indicating which positions are fixed words vs. first-letter hints), and the con-text features corresponding to that layer. The prompt instructs the model ( Gemini-3-Pro ) to return five ranked candidate expan-sions. For the Image-Chat dataset, separate system prompts are used for Speaker A (opening turn, no conversational history) and Speaker B (responding to the preceding turn, which is included in the prompt). In both datasets, we implement a modular template design for prompts that ensures that the only difference between context conditions is the presence or absence of specific context blocks, keeping all other prompt text identical. 

Activation steering. To compare prompting-based alignment with test-time residual intervention, i.e. activation steering [8, 16, 24, 33, 40], we use OLMo-2-13B-Instruct [21]. ## 3 Results. 

Aligned frontier models expand abbreviations with up to 88% ac-curacy (Fig. 2). Frontier LLMs have an exceptional ability to re-construct a sentence given a corrupted version [17]. We find that this capability extends to abbreviation expansion: given only the user’s abbreviation, frontier models can often infer the intended full phrase, even when the abbreviation occurs in the first or second turn of a dialogue. This inference is not purely lexical. Many ab-breviations are compatible with multiple plausible expansions, and disambiguation requires pragmatic grounding (e.g., who/what is be-ing referred to, the speaker’s affect, and the desired communicative intent). To test whether such grounding is necessary, we provide the model with controlled access to context elements (Fig. 1C) via struc-tured prompting (Fig. 3A), and evaluate performance as these ele-ments are included or ablated. Throughout, we use Gemini-3-Pro 

as our frontier model, and defer comparisons to alternative align-ment mechanisms in open-source models to the next subsection. In the Image-Chat dataset (Fig. 2A), the first dialogue line (Speaker A; first row) is the most underdetermined setting: there is no con-versational history, so the model must rely on extra-linguistic cues to resolve ambiguous shorthand. Exploiting the context, the LLM interpreter achieves 73% abbreviation-expansion performance (see L3, top-5 LLM-judged equivalency, from now on we refer to this per-formance metric for the analysis), doubling the performance of the no-context baseline (L0). When supplied with pragmatic context, the frontier LLM reconstructs the intended phrase from the abbreviation with 88% top-5 accuracy, even in the absence of any prior conversational content. Since top-5 corresponds to a realistic AAC interaction loop in which the user selects from candidate expansions, these results imply that context-dependent alignment can materially improve usability: it increases the chance that the intended message appears among a small set of options, while preserving the efficiency benefits of abbreviated input. The efficiency of alignment approaches depends on context type (Fig. 3). . We found that the efficiency of alternative approaches for Cognitive Alignment depends on which dimension of the context is being considered (e.g. emotions vs. referents). Latent steering [2, 16, 19, 33, 34, 36, 39] is a promising approach for alignment, an alternative to prompting, by which a vector is added to the residual activity of a target layer(s) at test-time, in order to in-fluence the model’s behavior. Evaluating steering in proprietary models is not possible as it requires accessing intermediate ac-tivations during inference, so for this analysis we use the open-source OLMo-2-13B-Instruct model [21], evaluated on the gener-ated dataset. This model achieves significantly lower performance than the frontier model (compare with Fig. 2B), but it allows us to compare test-time intervention approaches for different context types. We compare prompting (Fig. 3A) with steering on early or later layers (Steering 1 and 2 in Fig. 3B). We found that aligning the L3 context layer in Fig. 2B benefits from different approaches than aligning to L4 in Fig. 2B (Fig. 3C). Alignment to MOC, Emotions and Explicitness (L3) benefits from steering early layers, which performs similarly to prompting. In contrast, alignment to referents (L4) is best achieved by prompting, or by steering a late layer. Intuitively, MOC, affect, and explicitness largely shape how an utterance should be produced. Steering early layers can act like a subtle prior: it nudges the model into the ap-propriate communicative mode without overriding the semantic or lexical content. In contrast, resolving referents is primarily about 

what the utterance is about: selecting the correct entities and filling in missing arguments from the context. Directly providing refer-ents in the prompt is highly effective because it makes the relevant entities explicit in the model’s immediate context, similarly to deci-sively shifting token-level logits toward the intended entity using late-layer steering.

use in LLMs. By treating user input as a compressed signal to be reconstructed, we demonstrate that LLMs have the potential to en-able high-bandwidth communication from extremely limited motor inputs (e.g., selecting abbreviations). However, this capability is contingent upon solving the problem of Cognitive Misalignment whereby the LLM lacks the necessary context to resolve user in-tention. Our results suggest that solving misalignment requires grounding the LLM in highly specific pragmatic contexts, e.g., visual attention, emotional state, and communicative intent, that human listeners naturally utilize. With appropriate context, like that avail-able from commercially wearable eye-trackers—those with world-cameras and microphones to catch and diarize conversations—we demonstrate that frontier LLMs can reconstruct intended utter-ances from highly compressed abbreviations with up to 88% top-5 accuracy (Fig. 2). In particular, we found that (visual) referents– information readily available via the same eye-tracking hardware used for typing–are the most significant driver of disambiguation (Fig. 2). This suggests a promising direction for future work where eye-gaze interfaces do not merely act as cursors for input, but as semantic sensors and selectors, actively logging attended objects to engineer context [6]. While visual context (referents and scene descriptions) provided the most significant boost in lexical accuracy (Fig. 2), the small but consistent performance drop observed in the emotion-ablated condition ( 𝐿 4 \ 𝐿 2) suggests that semantic grounding (“what acts upon what”) can be paired with pragmatic grounding (“how and why the speaker feels”) to further narrow the search space of plau-sible expansions. This aligns with theories of human pragmatics suggesting that intent recognition is often distinct from reference resolution [32]. For the user, this suggests that sensing the user’s latent mental state may further improve expansion accuracy. Our comparison of structured prompting versus activation steer-ing (Fig. 3) suggests that different dimensions of communication map to different intervention points in the model architecture. Spe-cific objects in a scene benefit from explicit prompting or late-layer steering. Conversely, abstract features like MOC and Emotion were effectively managed via early-layer steering. While observed in a specific open-source model, this supports the view that early layers process high-level style and global constraints, while later layers resolve specific token identities, though this needs verification in more capable frontier models before generalizing to all pretrained transformers. This dissociation suggests a path toward hybrid CCIs: using discrete prompts to ground the model in the physical world (via object detection) while using steering vectors to modulate the “voice” and intent of the output. Furthermore, treating in-context learning (prompting) as evidence accumulation and steering as a shift in prior beliefs provides a unified Bayesian framework for pre-cisely predicting how these combined interventions will influence the model’s final generated output [4]. Such an architecture is re-flected in dual systems theory of human brain function whereby the fast, implicit System 1 and flexible, explicit System 2 divide the al-gorithmic labor of controlling thought and behavior [10]. A deeper understanding of this analogy may open further opportunities for bidirectional alignment between AIs and humans. 

## 5 Limitations and Future Work. 

(1) Due to the closed nature of frontier models ( Gemini-3-Pro ), our activation steering experiments were restricted to open-source mod-els ( OLMo-2-13B-Instruct [21]), which show significantly lower baseline performance. (2) While we investigated controlled ablation of context factors, our evaluation relies on synthetic data and con-versations from crowdworkers, which may not accurately reflect the linguistic patterns, errors, or true cognitive intent of individuals, especially those with severe motor and speech impairments using AAC devices. We need to deploy a real system under realistic con-ditions and to investigate whether simulated model performance Multi-System Cognitive Steering for Generative Communication BiAlign ’26, April 15, 2026, Barcelona, Spain 

reflects this. (3) Our simple first-letter abbreviation method assumes perfect spelling and consistent encoding, which may not hold true for real-world AAC users who often employ idiosyncratic abbrevi-ations, make typographical errors, or may introduce a significant cognitive load. (4) While frontier model results appear very promis-ing, achieving the latency required for fluent conversation remains an engineering challenge given a gesture-based interface, e.g., Fig. 1B. (5) While our results show a benefit to including emotional context, reliably capturing a user’s latent mental state in real-time AAC systems remains a significant technical and practical chal-lenge. (6) We did not include n-gram baselines in our comparisons, which could further contextualize the performance gains of our LLM-based approach. (7) Finally, the introduction of AI that infers intent raises ethical questions regarding agency [14, 15]. Preserving this agency is paramount: future systems must be designed to speak 

for the user, never over them [31]. 

## 6 Conclusion. 

Generative AI transforms AAC into a cognitive bidirectional align-ment task [26], involving a tradeoff between communication effi-ciency and expressivity exists. We conclude that by coupling low-bandwidth input with rich specific context and through applying the appropriate AI control (e.g., steering mechanisms), we can con-struct interfaces deeply attuned to the cognitive reality of the user thus overcoming Cognitive Misalignment and transform minimal input into maximum expression.
