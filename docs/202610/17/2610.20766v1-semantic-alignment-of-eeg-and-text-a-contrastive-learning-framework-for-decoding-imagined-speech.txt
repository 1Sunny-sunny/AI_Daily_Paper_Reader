# Semantic Alignment of EEG and Text: A Contrastive Learning Framework for Decoding Imagined Speech 

Shahryar G. Namdari*, Francesco Iacomi*, Riccardo Barbieri, Senior Member, IEEE , and Luca Mainardi, 

Member, IEEE 

Abstract — Imagined speech decoding from EEG is apromising route toward naturalistic brain–computer inter-faces. Traditional closed-set classifiers treat semantic cate-gories as independent labels and therefore do not explicitly exploit the relationships among possible linguistic intents, limiting the flexibility of their output representation. We adapt a multimodal contrastive learning framework to the imagined-speech setting, aligning EEG trials with language representations by mapping EEG into the embed-ding space of a frozen pretrained text encoder. An EEG encoder is trained with a composite objective that com-bines semantically reweighted EEG–sentence alignment with text-prototype-based class discrimination, encourag-ing semantically structured representations while preserv-ing separability among the 39 intent categories. We evaluate the approach on the Chisco imagined speech dataset with 39 semantic categories across mul-tiple subjects, reporting Top-k accuracy, macro-F1, and balanced pairwise matching accuracy (2v2). We addition-ally examine whether maximum EEG-text cosine similar-ity provides an intrinsic indicator of decoding reliability and probe semantic structure beyond the original cate-gory labels using evaluation-time semantic axes and a qualitative Semantic Gallery Probe (SGP) with previously unseen candidate sentences. The contrastive framework achieved conventional decoding performance comparable to the task-optimized closed-set classifier while mapping EEG into a frozen language embedding space. MaxCosine consistently identified subsets of held-out trials with higher Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy across subjects, evaluation-time semantic axes showed mean agreement above the 50% chance reference across all five examined dimensions, and the SGP qualitatively illustrated direct comparison with previously unseen candidate sentences. Together, these findings show that EEG–language alignment can retain comparable fixed-intent decoding while enabling similarity-based retrieval, intrinsic reliability ranking, and evaluation of broader semantic structure.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Brain–Computer Interfaces (BCIs) aim to establish a direct communication channel between the human brain and external devices [1]. Beyond motor imagery and P300 paradigms, recent work has increasingly explored decoding of imagined speech , the internal articulation of words without vocalization, as a more natural communication pathway, particularly for individuals with severe motor impairments that limit reliable speech output such as Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) and locked-in syndrome [2], [3]. Despite this promise, imagined-speech EEG decoding is still predominantly formulated as a closed-set classification prob-lem, in which semantic categories are treated as independent labels [4]. Such classifiers are effective for fixed-vocabulary intent recognition, but their output representation does not explicitly encode relationships among semantically related intents. A representation aligned with language space offers a complementary objective: preserving discriminability among known categories while organizing EEG trials according to the semantic structure of their linguistic targets. The recent release of the Chinese Imagined Speech Cor-pus (Chisco) [5], with 39 semantic categories and multiple subjects, enables learning objectives that go beyond rigid classification of a limited set of words. In this work, we investigate contrastive EEG–language alignment as an alternative representation for imagined-speech decoding. The proposed framework maps single-trial EEG into the embedding space of a frozen pretrained language model and is trained using a composite objective that combines semantically reweighted EEG–sentence alignment with text-prototype-based class discrimination. This design aims to pre-serve discriminability among the 39 Chisco intent categories while organizing EEG representations within a structured linguistic space. The study addresses three complementary questions. First, we test whether semantic alignment can retain conventional decoding performance relative to a task-optimized closed-set classifier, using Top-k accuracy, macro-F1, and balanced pairwise matching accuracy (2v2). Second, we investigate arXiv:2610.20766v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026 2

whether the maximum cosine similarity between an EEG embedding and the text-derived category prototypes, already computed during category prediction, can serve as an indicator of prediction reliability and support selective decoding without requiring a separate confidence model. Third, we investigate whether the learned EEG representations exhibit semantic structure beyond the original 39 categories by measuring their agreement with text embeddings along five predefined bipolar semantic dimensions (e.g., practical versus social/emotional), introduced only during evaluation. We also use the Semantic Gallery Probe (SGP) to qualitatively examine their similarity to previously unseen candidate sentences. Together, these analyses evaluate not only whether the intended category can be recovered, but also what additional structure becomes available when imagined-speech EEG is represented directly in language space. 

II. R ELATED WORK 

Traditional EEG-based imagined speech decoding has largely emphasized task-specific preprocessing, handcrafted feature extraction, and supervised classification under con-strained vocabularies, with substantial variability in protocols and performance across studies [4]. Recent progress in neural language decoding increasingly moves beyond closed-set classification toward semantic objec-tives [6], [7], where brain activity is mapped into continuous representation spaces learned from large text corpora. In neuroimaging, multiple works have shown that representing words or sentences as vectors in a semantic space enables decoding that generalizes beyond a fixed label set [8]–[10]. In parallel, advances in non-invasive EEG-based language decoding have begun to leverage pretrained language models to support open-vocabulary generation or retrieval, particularly in stimulus-driven paradigms such as natural reading on ZuCo-style corpora [11]–[16]. These trends motivate representation-level alignment which treats labels as related entities. Recent EEG-language work has also explored semantic intent de-coding beyond fixed class vocabularies, using compositional semantic units to reconstruct more flexible linguistic outputs [7]. 

A. Neural-to-Language Semantic Decoding 

A central theme in modern brain-to-language research is to characterize the representational correspondence between neural activity and the latent spaces of large language models. Early demonstrations in fMRI mapped brain activity to distri-butional semantic vectors for words and sentences, enabling identification and generalization in a shared semantic space [8]. More recently, non-invasive decoders have reconstructed continuous language from fMRI by linking brain responses to latent semantic representations and then generating text [9], with subsequent work integrating large language models more directly into the reconstruction stage [10]. While originally developed for fMRI, these methodologies provide a founda-tional template for aligning high-dimensional neural manifolds with linguistic embedded patterns, enabling evaluation based on semantic proximity rather than binary label accuracy. In EEG, open-vocabulary brain-to-text has been explored, mainly under stimulus-driven protocols (e.g., natural reading on ZuCo [11], [12]), where EEG segments are aligned to corresponding text tokens or sentences using sequence-to-sequence models with pretrained language backbones [13]– [15]. While semantic grounding via language models is the-oretically sound, its implementation in non-invasive EEG is constrained by the inherently low signal-to-noise ratio and the non-stationarity of the neural signal. Recent imagined speech datasets make it possible to revisit semantic decoding under internally generated language conditions rather than externally driven perception [5]. However, most neural-to-language systems either focus on stimulus-driven data or rely on end-to-end generation pipelines, leaving open the question of how reliably semantic structure can be recovered from noisy imagined speech EEG using a shared embedding target. 

B. Contrastive Semantic Alignment and Reliability-Aware Evaluation 

Contrastive learning has become a dominant strategy for learning aligned representations from paired but scarce data. This dual-encoder contrastive alignment paradigm has been popularized at scale in multimodal settings such as vision and language (e.g., CLIP [17] and ALIGN [18]), and has also recently been explored for EEG–language alignment [6]. In the modern retrieval-based paradigm, contrastive objectives bridge the modality gap by pulling neural embeddings to-ward their linguistic counterparts while enforcing separability from semantically distant anchors, facilitating meaning-aware retrieval. This approach has been demonstrated using non-invasive EEG and MEG recordings in word-level decoding by contrasting brain signals against embeddings from a pretrained language model [19]. Related contrastive representation learn-ing has also been investigated for robust EEG decoding in language-adjacent settings such as auditory decoding [20], supporting the view that contrastive alignment provides a ro-bust statistical bridge, grounding noisy, transient brain activity in the structured, persistent semantic manifolds of pretrained models. Evaluation in semantic decoding often goes beyond Top-k

accuracy, using pairwise or retrieval-based tests and statistical validation (e.g., permutation testing) to quantify whether neu-ral representations align with semantics beyond chance [8]. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) offers a standard approach for comparing representational structure via similar-ity patterns [21]. Separately, BCI-oriented evaluation increas-ingly emphasizes operational metrics that capture realistic use such as utility-based metrics [22], or including abstention or no-control behavior, rather than accuracy alone [23]. Despite these advances, traditional imagined-speech research often neglects uncertainty quantification, failing to treat reliability as a primary objective alongside representational alignment. Together, these studies motivate language-aligned neu-ral representations, but several questions remain open for imagined-speech EEG. First, it is unclear whether mapping EEG into a pretrained semantic space can preserve conven-tional decoding performance in a realistic multi-class setting 3

when compared with a task-optimized closed-set classifier. Second, the reliability information carried by the resulting sim-ilarity scores has received comparatively little attention. Third, it remains unclear whether such representations preserve se-mantic structure beyond the category labels used during train-ing. We address these questions jointly by comparing closed-set and language-aligned decoding, evaluating MaxCosine as an intrinsic reliability-ranking signal, and probing the learned EEG representations using evaluation-time semantic axes and an unseen semantic gallery.

In this section, we describe the proposed semantic alignment framework for mapping imagined-speech EEG into a frozen language embedding space, the closed-set classifier used for comparison, and the common evaluation protocol.

We used version 1.1.2 of the publicly available Chisco dataset (OpenNeuro: ds005170), which contains EEG record-ings from five participants. This expanded release includes two additional participants (Sub-04 and Sub-05) beyond the three described in the original dataset publication. All subjects were native Chinese speakers, right-handed, and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Prior to data collection, partic-ipants underwent a five-day Integrative Body-Mind Training 

(IBMT) program to enhance concentration. The acquisition paradigm follows a cue-based imagined speech protocol: each trial begins with a fixation period to stabilize attention, followed by the presentation of a visual linguistic stimulus (belonging to a specific semantic category). Participants are then instructed to silently and repeatedly read and imagine the displayed sentence without overt articulation or movement. During the trial, EEG signals were acquired using a Com-pumedics Neuroscan SynAmps-2 system with a 128-channel Quik-Cap (extended 10-20 montage) at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz. While the dataset provides data for both the Reading and Imagined Speech phases, in this work we exclusively focused on the imagined speech epochs, corresponding to the 3.3 s window where subjects mentally pronounced the stimuli. We used the officially released preprocessed signals . As detailed in the original documentation [5], the raw recordings underwent a standardized cleaning pipeline consisting of channel selection (125 retained channels), downsampling to 500 Hz, bad-channel detection and interpolation with re-referencing using the PREP pipeline [24], power-line noise removal via a 50 Hz notch filter, high-pass filtering at 1 Hz, segmentation according to the experimental paradigm, automated rejection of corrupted data spans using the Autoreject algorithm [25], and Independent Component Analysis (ICA) with ICLabel-assisted component [26] classification and manual correction to remove ocular and muscular artifacts. Pre-segmented trials were further formatted through channel pruning and normalization. The last three channels were removed from the original 125-channel configuration (vertical electrooculogram (VEO), horizontal electrooculogram (HEO), and Trigger), yielding 122 EEG channels for analysis. Ad-ditionally, instance-wise Z-score normalization was applied to each trial to promote consistent input distributions across sessions and subjects.

The framework receives preprocessed EEG signals as input (left side of the figure) and learns to project them into a continuous semantic space defined by a frozen language embedding model (right part of the figure). Training is driven by a composite objective that encourages alignment between EEG projections and text representations while preserving class-level separability. > 1) Text Encoder:

The target semantic space toward which all EEG representations are aligned is defined by a pretrained language embedding model, denoted as Etext . For any stimulus sentence S, the encoder produces a fixed-size embedding vector:

> 2) EEG Encoder:

We employ a trainable neural encoder 

Eeeg to project each imagined speech EEG trial X ∈ RC×T

(with C = 122 channels and T = 1651 time samples) into a semantic embedding z ∈ R512 , aligned with the frozen text encoder. The EEG encoder follows a hybrid architecture with three stages: (i) a compact EEGNet-based convolutional backbone [35] for spatiotemporal feature extraction, producing a feature sequence HCNN , (ii) a Transformer encoder [36] for temporal modeling, implemented as a stack of 4 layers with 8 attention heads (key dimension 64), and (iii) a lightweight projection head. In the final stage, the Transformer outputs are aggregated by attention pooling into a single vector, then mapped through a small MLP (256-unit hidden layer with GELU) to a 512-dimensional embedding. The output is ℓ2-normalized prior to computing the alignment losses to place EEG and text embeddings on a shared unit hypersphere, making similarity comparisons scale-invariant and stabilizing contrastive opti-mization.

C. Task-Optimized Closed-Set Classifier 

For comparison, we implemented a conventional closed-set classifier optimized specifically for 39-way semantic-intent classification. The classifier received the same preprocessed imagined-speech EEG trials as the proposed framework and used the same within-subject cross-validation splits, validation partitions, held-out test trials, and random-seed protocol. The classifier used a simplified EEGNet-like convolutional backbone consisting of temporal, depthwise spatial, and sepa-rable convolutional stages. The resulting feature sequence was combined with sinusoidal positional encoding and processed by a single Transformer encoder block with eight attention heads, a model dimension of 64, and a feed-forward dimension of 256. Global average pooling was then applied, followed by a 39-unit softmax classification layer. The model was optimized using weighted categorical cross-entropy with nor-malized inverse-square-root class-frequency weights. Unlike the proposed framework, this classifier does not use a text encoder or map EEG into a language embedding space. Because its architecture was optimized separately for closed-set classification, the comparison is system-level and does not isolate the effect of the training objective under an identical backbone. 

D. Evaluation Metrics and Analyses 

Both decoding approaches are evaluated on the same held-out test folds using Top-k accuracy, with k ∈ { 1, 5}, macro-F1, and balanced pairwise matching accuracy (2v2). Because the systems produce different output representations, 2v2 is computed from EEG–category-prototype similarities for the proposed framework and from class probabilities for the closed-set classifier, as described below. For the proposed framework, we additionally examine whether maximum EEG– text cosine similarity provides an intrinsic score related to prediction reliability, and probe semantic structure beyond the original category labels using evaluation-time semantic axes and the qualitative SGP.  

> 1) Balanced Pairwise Accuracy (2v2):

In many practical BCI applications, the system often needs to discriminate between a small set of competing intents rather than the full vocabulary. We use the standard 2v2 accuracy [8], [40]–[42], but estimate it using a class-balanced sampling strategy. Specifically, we randomly sample two distinct semantic categories and then randomly select one held-out trial from each category, yielding trials i and j with labels yi̸ = yj .Because the closed-set classifier predicts semantic categories rather than individual sentences, the proposed framework is also evaluated at the category level. Accordingly, its EEG embeddings are compared with the frozen category prototypes 

pyi and pyj defined in the language embedding space, rather than with the embeddings of the individual stimulus sentences. This ensures that both systems are evaluated on the same task: determining the correct category assignment for the two EEG trials. We first examine whether validation-derived MaxCosine thresholds transfer consistently to held-out data. Within each fold, we determined thresholds corresponding to target vali-dation coverage levels of 100% , 80% , 60% , 40% , and 20% ,using only the validation-score distribution. Each threshold is then applied unchanged to the corresponding held-out test fold, and the resulting test coverage is compared with the target coverage used to derive the threshold. Close agreement between target and test coverage indicates that the relevant quantiles of the MaxCosine distribution are reasonably stable across validation and test folds, although it does not imply that the full score distributions are identical. Using these validation-derived thresholds, we then examine selective decoding performance by reporting the achieved test coverage and the Top-1 and Top-5 accuracies among the retained trials. For an additional visualization of the relationship between MaxCosine and decoding accuracy, each test MaxCosine score is converted to its percentile rank within the corresponding fold-specific validation distribution and then assigned to one of five non-overlapping quintiles. Finally, we quantify the ability of mi to discriminate correct from incorrect Top-1 predictions using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), with cor-rectness defined as I[ˆ yi = yi]. AUROC is computed separately for each held-out test fold and summarized using the mean ±

standard deviation. Test labels are used only to evaluate correctness after the thresholds and quintile boundaries have been established. Col-lectively, these analyses assess whether MaxCosine provides a useful ranking signal related to prediction reliability. They do not establish probabilistic calibration or operational online performance.  

> 3) Generalization Across Semantic Axes:

As an exploratory post hoc analysis, we examine whether the learned EEG embeddings preserve broader semantic structure beyond the original 39-category taxonomy. To do so, we define five bipolar semantic axes directly in the frozen language embedding space. These axes are introduced only at evaluation time and are therefore not used as labels or supervision during EEG-encoder training. No additional classifiers are trained for this analysis. Each pole of an axis is represented by three manually defined Chinese anchor phrases describing the correspond-ing semantic concept. The phrases are embedded using the same frozen text encoder employed throughout the proposed framework. For each pole, the three resulting embeddings are averaged and ℓ2-normalized to obtain a single anchor vector. > 4) Semantic Gallery Probe (SGP):

Complementing the quantitative semantic-axis analysis, we use SGP to qualita-tively examine whether the learned EEG embeddings extend beyond the exact training sentences. Because the proposed framework maps EEG and text into a shared frozen language space, previously unseen candidate sentences can be compared directly with a held-out EEG embedding without retraining the EEG encoder or modifying its output layer. This capability is not directly available to the closed-set classifier, whose outputs are restricted to the fixed set of 39 semantic categories. We construct a frozen semantic gallery of manually writ-ten, held-out sentences that express the same target concepts using alternative phrasings (e.g., additional paraphrases within “Food”). All gallery sentences are embedded with the frozen text encoder and projected to 2D via PCA for visualization. Given a single EEG test trial, we visualize how it relates to this unseen gallery by coloring each gallery point according to its cosine similarity to the EEG embedding. For qualitative illustration, we report a representative held-out trial with a high MaxCosine score. We inspect whether the EEG embedding assigns relatively higher similarity to candidate sentences in the intended semantic region, including sentences not observed during EEG-encoder training, while assigning lower similarity to unrelated regions. SGP is therefore used as a qualitative demonstration of candidate-set flexibility rather than as a quantitative performance metric. Visualizations are reported in Section V. 

IV. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP 

A. Protocol and Reporting 

We use stratified, sentence-grouped five-fold cross-validation with a fixed random seed, ensuring that all trials associated with the same sentence remain in the same fold. In each fold, one fold is held out for testing, while 15% of the sentence groups in the remaining training pool are reserved for validation. The same splits are used throughout the study. Held-out test data are used only for final model evaluation and are excluded from all model-selection procedures. 

B. Training Configuration 

Both decoding approaches were implemented in TensorFlow and optimized with Adam [43] for a maximum of 100 epochs. Because the proposed framework and the closed-set classifier were optimized for different objectives, they used task-specific training and model-selection configurations. The proposed contrastive framework was trained with a batch size of 64 and an initial learning rate of 3 × 10 −4.The learning rate was multiplied by 0.7 after five epochs without improvement in validation loss, down to a minimum of 1 × 10 −6. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 16 epochs. During training, we applied additive Gaussian noise ( σ = 0 .05 ), channel dropout that randomly removed 

10% of the channels with probability 0.1, and temporal shifts implemented as circular rolls by δ ∈ [−50 , 50] samples. The closed-set classifier was trained with a batch size of 16 and an initial learning rate of 5 × 10 −4. Its learning rate was multiplied by 0.7 after four epochs without improvement in validation loss, down to a minimum of 1 × 10 −6, and early stopping was applied with a patience of nine epochs. No additional EEG augmentation was applied during closed-set classifier training. For both systems, learning-rate scheduling and early stopping were performed independently within each cross-validation fold. The validation Top-5 criterion was used for the proposed contrastive framework because its decoding objective emphasizes ranked retrieval, whereas validation Top-1 was used for the closed-set classifier because it was optimized for exact category classification. These model-selection cri-teria were fixed before evaluation on the held-out test folds. Each selected checkpoint was subsequently evaluated on the corresponding test fold, and both systems were evaluated using the same held-out test samples. 

C. Chance Baselines and Sampling Procedures 

Top-k accuracy and macro-F1 are compared against a strati-fied random baseline that reflects the class distribution of each fold-specific training set. Chance performance is estimated using 10 ,000 Monte Carlo samples per fold. Balanced pairwise accuracy (2v2) is estimated separately for each held-out test fold using 20 ,000 class-balanced pairs. The same sampling procedure is applied to both systems.

VI. D ISCUSSION 

The present results show that contrastive EEG–language alignment can preserve conventional imagined-speech decod-ing performance while changing the form of the learned representation. Across the five subjects, the proposed frame-work achieved Top-k, macro-F1, and pairwise discriminability comparable to the task-optimized closed-set classifier, while mapping single-trial EEG into a frozen language embedding space. This shared space additionally supports similarity-based retrieval, provides MaxCosine directly at inference time as an intrinsic reliability-ranking signal, and enables the representa-tion to be evaluated along semantic dimensions that were not used as training labels. The conventional decoding results address the first study question by showing that the language-aligned representa-tion retains the fixed-intent information required for cate-gory discrimination. Top-k and macro-F1 performance were closely matched between the two systems, while balanced 2v2 accuracy remained clearly above chance, with subject-level means of approximately 0.69–0.73. These findings establish an important performance anchor: moving from a conventional class output to a frozen language embedding space preserved comparable decoding performance while providing a represen-tation that can support additional similarity-based analyses. The second study question concerns whether the similarity structure itself carries useful information about prediction reliability. Across all five subjects, validation-derived Max-Cosine thresholds transferred closely from validation to held-out test data, and retaining the highest-scoring trials increased both Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy for every participant. At the approximately 20% operating point, Top-1 accuracy increased from 8.3–10.0% at full coverage to 15.6–19.2%, while Top-5 accuracy increased from 29.2–32.7% to 42.9–50.5%. Fold-wise AUROC values also remained above chance across sub-jects. Because MaxCosine is obtained directly from the EEG– prototype similarities used for decoding, these results show that the same retrieval score can provide an intrinsic reliability-ranking signal without requiring an additional confidence model. The third study question examines whether the learned representation contains semantic organization beyond the orig-inal 39-category decision. Mean balanced accuracy exceeded the 50% chance reference across all five evaluation-time semantic axes, with the clearest correspondence for Practical– Emotional, Goal-directed–Leisure, and Physical–Cognitive. The SGP provides a complementary qualitative view by showing that a held-out EEG embedding can be compared directly with alternative candidate sentences that were not used during EEG-encoder training. Together, these analyses indicate that the frozen language space provides structure beyond a conventional class output and may support ranked retrieval, candidate-set expansion, and future user-in-the-loop confirmation in communication-oriented BCI systems [22], [23], [44], [45]. Several considerations define the scope of these findings. Although both systems use the same EEG data, preprocessing, cross-validation splits, and held-out test trials, they employ 12 

task-specific architectures and different checkpoint-selection criteria. Their comparison is therefore system-level and does not isolate the effect of contrastive learning under an identical backbone. Moreover, MaxCosine is derived from the same similarity space used for category decoding and is retained as a raw score. The present analysis demonstrates reliability ranking but does not establish probabilistic calibration. All experiments were conducted within-subject on a single dataset of healthy participants. The results therefore establish subject-specific feasibility but do not yet determine robustness across sessions, recording conditions, participant populations, or long-term use [44]. Although the model operates in a language embedding space, training supervision and the principal decod-ing metrics remain tied to a fixed set of 39 intents and specific textual anchors. The semantic-axis analysis extends evaluation to dimensions introduced only after training; however, the axes are manually defined and the reference assignments are derived from sentence embeddings within the same dataset. The present results therefore support semantic correspondence beyond the original category taxonomy for the evaluated di-mensions. Establishing unrestricted open-vocabulary general-ization will require systematic testing with broader paraphrase sets, alternative formulations, and independently constructed semantic targets. More broadly, imagined-speech EEG remains a low-SNR and highly variable paradigm, with substantial sensitivity to artifacts, limited dataset scale, and unresolved challenges in reproducibility and deployment [4]. In addition, the selective decoding analysis is performed only offline, not in a full asynchronous control loop with an explicit idle state, online timing constraints, or interactive confirmation. These limitations also define the next steps. A first pri-ority is to improve representation quality through stronger preprocessing, better artifact control, and training strategies that make better use of the available signal structure. The Reading condition in Chisco may provide a useful supervisory signal for pre-training before adaptation to imagined speech, particularly when combined with EEG foundation models trained on larger and more diverse datasets. A second priority is to extend semantically aligned EEG representations beyond the fixed-intent setting studied here. A natural next step is to test progressively more flexible retrieval and generation pipelines, beginning with larger candidate sets and systematic paraphrase evaluation before moving toward open-vocabulary decoding. Progress will also depend on broader datasets, more subjects and sessions, and adaptation strategies that combine shared modeling with lightweight user-specific calibration. Future studies should also evaluate semantic alignment in in-teractive BCI settings, where MaxCosine or related similarity scores could be tested as online criteria for abstention, shortlist presentation, or confirmation-based control. 

VII. C ONCLUSION 

This work investigated whether imagined-speech EEG can be aligned with a frozen language embedding space without sacrificing conventional fixed-intent decoding performance. Across five subjects in the Chisco dataset, the proposed con-trastive framework achieved Top-k, macro-F1, and balanced pairwise performance comparable to a task-optimized closed-set classifier while representing single-trial EEG directly in a structured linguistic space. The resulting representation provided capabilities beyond the conventional class output. Validation-derived MaxCosine thresholds consistently identified subsets of held-out trials with higher Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy, demonstrating that the sim-ilarity produced during retrieval carries useful information for reliability ranking. In addition, EEG embeddings showed mean agreement above the 50% chance reference across all five semantic axes introduced only at evaluation time, while the Semantic Gallery Probe qualitatively demonstrated candidate-set flexibility through direct comparison with alternative sen-tences not used during EEG-encoder training. Taken together, these results position contrastive EEG– language alignment as a promising representation framework for imagined-speech decoding: one that retains comparable fixed-vocabulary decoding while supporting ranked retrieval, intrinsic reliability ranking, and evaluation of broader semantic structure. Extending these findings across subjects, sessions, datasets, and interactive BCI settings will be important for determining how far these representational advantages can translate into more flexible communication systems.
