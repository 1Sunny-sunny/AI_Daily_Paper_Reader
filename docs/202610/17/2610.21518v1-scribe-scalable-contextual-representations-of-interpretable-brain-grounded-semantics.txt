ABSTRACT 

Understanding how the human brain constructs meaning from real-world language remains a fundamental challenge, as semantics depend on context that varies in natural settings. To enable the study of real-world linguistic meaning in the brain, we introduce SCRIBE, a framework grounded in a neuroscience-informed se-mantic space that leverages an ensemble of open-source large language models to generate interpretable, word-level semantic representations of arbitrary text. In a behavioral study with 921 participants, we show that human semantic fea-ture judgments vary systematically with context, and that SCRIBE captures these shifts, reaching 75 –81% of the estimated human noise ceiling. Using brain activ-ity recorded while participants listen to naturalistic stories, we further demonstrate that SCRIBE predicts neural responses in language-selective brain regions, sig-nificantly outperforming non-contextual semantic representations. Variance parti-tioning reveals that SCRIBE explains unique brain-predictive information beyond syntactic features, suggesting that its representations capture aspects of linguistic meaning not accounted for by the measured syntactic features. Together, these findings establish SCRIBE as a scalable and interpretable foundation for studying natural language semantics in the brain. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Understanding how the human brain constructs meaning from language is a central goal in cognitive neuroscience and artificial intelligence. Semantic representations, which capture the meanings of words and concepts, are inherently dynamic and context-dependent. For example, the word “knife” may evoke harm and negative affect in a sentence such as “he cut himself with a knife”, but a more neutral, functional meaning in “he chopped vegetables with a knife”. This context depen-dence makes semantics difficult to study in the brain, as the effectively unbounded range of contexts complicates experimental design and the collection of human annotations that capture meaning as it occurs in real-world language. Prior work has primarily proposed semantic representations along two distinct directions. One line of research studies isolated concepts using feature-based representations grounded in cognitive and neuroscience theories (Binder et al., 2016; McRae et al., 2005; Devereux et al., 2014; Lynott et al., 2020). These representations are interpretable and have been successfully used to model seman-tic structure and predict brain activity (Mitchell et al., 2008; Anderson et al., 2017). However, even when extended to sentence-level modeling (Anderson et al., 2017), these approaches assume that word meanings remain fixed across contexts and combine them using simplified compositional schemes. In addition, these representations often rely on manual feature annotation and fixed vo-cabularies, making them difficult to scale to arbitrary natural language inputs. A second line of work aims to study semantics using distributed representations derived from large language models (LLMs) (Devlin et al., 2019; Touvron et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Grattafiori et al., 2024). These representations are contextual, scalable, and have been shown to predict neu-ral responses during natural language comprehension (Toneva & Wehbe, 2019; Pereira et al., 2018). However, because internal LLM representations entangle semantic, syntactic, and other lexical prop-1arXiv:2610.21518v1 [cs.AI] 15 Oct 2026 erties of language, their alignment with the brain is difficult to attribute to semantic processing, limiting their interpretability for studying meaning in the brain. In this work, we bridge these two approaches by introducing Scalable Contextual Representations of Interpretable Brain-grounded sEmantics (SCRIBE), an approach for generating scalable, inter-pretable, context-dependent semantic representations. Grounded in a neuroscience-informed se-mantic space (Binder et al., 2016), SCRIBE leverages an ensemble of open-source LLMs to extend traditional feature-based representations to arbitrary natural language contexts. We validate SCRIBE along four complementary dimensions. First, we validate SCRIBE scores against existing human semantic feature norms for isolated out-of-context concepts (Binder et al., 2016), establishing its semantic validity. Second, we extend this evaluation to contextual seman-tics by collecting a new dataset of human ratings of concept–property pairs, both in and out of context, from 921 participants. To our knowledge, this constitutes the first human-annotated re-source of context-dependent semantic feature norms, which we will publicly release. We then show that these judgments are reliable and systematically vary with linguistic context, and that SCRIBE captures these shifts, reaching 75 –81% of the human noise ceiling across conditions. Third, we demonstrate that contextual SCRIBE representations significantly outperform their out-of-context counterparts at predicting neural responses during naturalistic story comprehension. We show that SCRIBE captures unique brain-predictive information beyond the closest interpretable representa-tion, QAEmb (Singh et al., 2025). Finally, variance partitioning with independently defined syntactic features reveals that SCRIBE retains more unique brain-predictive variance than QAEmb while ex-hibiting less overlap with syntactic features in language-related regions, supporting greater semantic specificity of its neural predictivity. Together, results establish SCRIBE as a scalable, interpretable, context-aware foundation for studying how the brain represents meaning in natural language. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Feature-based interpretable semantic representations. A central question in cognitive science is how to represent meaning, with a long line of work modeling concepts through explicit semantic features derived from human judgments and experiential attributes (McRae et al., 2005; Devereux et al., 2014; Lynott et al., 2020; Hoffman et al., 2013). Binder et al. (2016) consolidated this line of work into a comprehensive, neurobiologically grounded semantic space spanning sensory, motor, affective, social, and cognitive dimensions. Such interpretable representations have been used to model brain activity at both the word and sentence level (Mitchell et al., 2008; Fernandino et al., 2015; Anderson et al., 2017). However, these approaches rely on out-of-context representations and manually annotated datasets with limited vocabularies, limiting their scalability to settings where meaning varies across contexts. Our work leverages LLMs to generate context-aware semantic representations, enabling a scalable and semantically grounded feature space. 

LLM-based contextual semantic representations. A second line of work models brain activity using contextualized LLM embeddings (Toneva & Wehbe, 2019; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2022), which capture a wide range of linguistic and semantic phenomena (Jawahar et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020). Despite their impressive brain alignment, LLMs’ distributed and high-dimensional representations are difficult to interpret and to relate explicitly to semantic processing in the brain. In fact, prior work has shown that the brain alignment of pretrained LLMs depends in part on syntax (Oota et al., 2023; Fresen et al., 2024). In contrast, we leverage LLMs as semantic annotators to map concepts to an interpretable and cognitively grounded feature space, which can then be used to predict brain activity. 

Bridging interpretability and contextual semantic representations. Previous work has shown that semantic properties can be recovered from LLM representations using probes (Chersoni et al., 2021; Turton et al., 2021; 2020). However, these approaches do not assess whether modern LLMs can directly annotate such properties through prompting, analogous to the human semantic anno-tation procedure used to construct semantic feature spaces. Moreover, although LLM representa-tions are inherently context sensitive, these approaches average them across contexts rather than modeling how semantic properties vary with context (Turton et al., 2021). More recently, struc-tured prompting has enabled LLMs to directly produce explicit, interpretable features, for exam-ple question-answering-based embedding methods such as QAEmb (Singh et al., 2025). 3 METHODS 

We first describe SCRIBE (Section 3.1), then how we evaluate its alignment with human semantic judgments (Section 3.2), and with brain activity (Section 3.3). 3.1 SCRIBE: INTERPRETABLE AND CONTEXT -AWARE SEMANTIC REPRESENTATIONS 

SCRIBE maps a target concept, optionally embedded in a linguistic context, to a vector of inter-pretable semantic feature scores (Figure 1). 

Semantic feature space. We build on the feature space of Binder et al. (2016), which defines 65 

semantic properties spanning sensory (e.g., color , brightness ), motor (e.g., body parts , movement ), affective (e.g., pleasantness ), and higher-level cognitive dimensions, derived from a synthesis of findings in cognitive neuroscience and psychology (Appendix A.1). Because each dimension is in-terpretable and neurocognitively grounded, we treat this space as a fixed set of semantic dimensions along which meaning can be expressed and modulated by context. Binder et al. (2016) also provide human ratings ( 0–6), averaged across participants, of how strongly each property applies to 535 

English words, which we use as a reference for out-of-context meaning. 

LLM-based annotation. Instead of manual annotation, SCRIBE prompts an LLM independently for each property to rate how strongly it applies to the target concept on the same 0–6 scale used 3by Binder et al. (2016), mirroring their protocol (Appendix A.2.1); the model outputs a single number, obtained with greedy decoding for deterministic annotations. Prompt ablations show that results are robust to variations in prompt wording and format (Appendix A.2.2). We use four open-source instruction-tuned LLMs, llama3.1-8b , mistral-7b , qwen2.5-7b and 

llama3.1-70b (Grattafiori et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), and average their scores to obtain the final representation, mitigating model-specific biases and yielding higher corre-lations with human judgments than any individual model (Sections 4.1 and 4.2). 

Context-aware annotation. Because meaning is shaped by context, SCRIBE conditions each esti-mate on the surrounding text: given a concept and the sentence in which it appears, the LLM rates how strongly each property applies to the concept in that context (Appendix A.2.3). The same con-cept can thus receive different semantic profiles depending on its usage, while remaining in the same interpretable space; because annotation is automated, it scales to naturalistic stimuli. 3.2 EVALUATING ALIGNMENT WITH HUMAN SEMANTIC JUDGMENTS 

We compare SCRIBE against two sets of human ratings: the out-of-context norms of Binder et al. (2016), and our newly collected ratings of concept–property pairs in and out of context (Section 4.2; Appendix B.2.1). For our newly collected dataset, we estimate the reliability of averaged human ratings using the one-way random-effects intraclass correlation ICC(1 , n ) (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979), computed on n = 8 ratings per item (a concept–property pair in a given context condition) from distinct participants and averaged over 20 balanced subsamples; we also report ICC(1 , 1) for single rater agreement (Appendix B.2.2). The estimated human noise ceiling , pICC(1 , n ), represents the expected correlation between a noise-free model and the n-rater mean. We report the Pearson correlation r between SCRIBE scores and the mean human ratings, normalized by this ceiling (with 

1 indicating human-level agreement), with bootstrap confidence intervals over items. We also report the mean squared error (MSE), with significance assessed by permutation tests that shuffle SCRIBE scores across items. For Binder et al. (2016), which provide only participant-averaged ratings, we report raw correlations without noise-ceiling normalization. 3.3 MODELING SEMANTIC REPRESENTATIONS IN THE BRAIN 

Brain encoding. We use a subset of the Moth Radio Hour dataset (LeBel et al., 2023): fMRI recordings from 7 participants listening to autobiographical stories ( 10 –15 minutes each, TR = 2 s). This dataset is widely used in prior work (Moussa et al., 2024; Antonello et al., 2023; Oota et al., 2024) and enables direct comparison with QAEmb (Singh et al., 2025). The encoding models are trained on 9 stories and evaluated on 1 held-out story (Appendix A.4.1). Following standard voxel-wise encoding (Oota et al., 2024; Antonello et al., 2023), word-level SCRIBE features are aligned to the fMRI signal using Lanczos interpolation, augmented with time-lagged copies spanning the preceding 4s, and used to fit ridge regression models with cross-validation. 

Evaluation. Prediction accuracy is the Pearson correlation between predicted and held-out re-sponses, normalized by voxel-wise noise ceilings (LeBel et al., 2023; Moussa et al., 2024). We restrict analyses to voxels with a noise ceiling above 0.4 and report results across all retained voxels and within language-related regions previously defined using the Glasser atlas (Glasser et al., 2016; Moussa et al., 2024); differences are tested with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Appendix A.4). 

Baseline. We compare SCRIBE with QAEmb (Singh et al., 2025), the closest existing interpretable stimulus representation (see Section 2 for a discussion), which represents each word using LLM responses to 35 neuroscience-motivated yes/no questions automatically selected from an initial pool of 606 . We use the publicly released annotations. 3.3.1 ANNOTATION DESIGN : WORD TYPES AND CONTEXT SIZE 

Since function words (e.g., articles, prepositions, auxiliaries) carry limited lexical meaning and may add noise, we annotate only content words (nouns, proper nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, nu-merals, and pronouns), assigning zero-valued features to all other tokens. For context, we compare windows of w preceding tokens with the full preceding context, and use w = 10 . 4 RESULTS 

We evaluate SCRIBE along four directions: semantic validity , whether its scores align with hu-man ratings of isolated concepts (Section 4.1); context awareness , whether human judgments shift with context and SCRIBE captures these shifts (Section 4.2); neural predictivity , how well it pre-dicts brain activity during natural language comprehension (Section 4.3); and semantic specificity ,whether its brain-predictive information is distinct from syntactic features (Section 4.4). 

Implementation details. All experiments were performed on internal compute clusters, using two NVIDIA H100 PCIe GPUs with ∼ 80 GB GPU memory per device. The computation of the experi-ments took a total of ∼ 10 days. 4.1 SCRIBE GENERATES SEMANTIC FEATURES THAT ALIGN WITH HUMAN JUDGMENTS 

We first evaluate whether SCRIBE can produce semantically meaningful representations in the ab-sence of context, treating SCRIBE as a scalable proxy for human semantic ratings over isolated concepts. To this end, we compare SCRIBE scores for isolated concepts (i.e., without context) to human semantic feature ratings (Binder et al., 2016), using the original dataset of 535 words intro-duced in that work and described in Section 3.1, as a reference benchmark. For each concept, we quantify the similarity between the SCRIBE scores and human ratings using Pearson correlation and mean squared error (MSE). We assess statistical significance using permutation tests, obtained by randomly shuffling concept–property assignments 1000 times. Table 1 summarizes the results. SCRIBE aligns significantly with human ratings across all evaluated models, with correlations above 0.6 and MSEs significantly below permutation baselines (see Ap-pendix B.1.1 for further results). The ensemble shows the strongest alignment, reaching r = 0 .77 

and an MSE of 1.07 , indicating that aggregation improves correspondence with human semantic judgments. The Binder et al. (2016) norms themselves also agree closely with the out-of-context ratings we collect (Appendix B.2.3). Together, these results support SCRIBE as a scalable approxi-mation of human semantic ratings for isolated concepts. Additional analyses show that the representations are complementary and that SCRIBE’s performance is robust after dimensionality reduction (Appendix B.3.3 and B.3.4). 4.4 SCRIBE CAPTURES BRAIN -PREDICTIVE INFORMATION BEYOND SYNTACTIC FEATURES 

Although these results demonstrate that SCRIBE captures unique brain-predictive information in re-gions associated with semantic processing, they do not establish whether this information is specifi-cally semantic. Because semantic information is difficult to isolate from other linguistic information in natural language, we assess the relative semantic specificity of SCRIBE indirectly by asking how much of its brain-predictive variance remains after accounting for independently defined syntac-tic features. We focus on syntax because prior work has attributed part of the brain alignment of language-model representations to syntactic rather than semantic information, and its contribution to model–brain alignment remains debated (Oota et al., 2023; Fresen et al., 2024; Kauf et al., 2024). It is also a plausible confound for SCRIBE itself, since annotation is restricted to content-word part-of-speech categories and several semantic dimensions covary with grammatical class (e.g., Motion with verbs). We thus construct a syntactic control space comprising part-of-speech tags, dependency length, and tree depth, and use variance partitioning to quantify its unique and shared contributions with SCRIBE and QAEmb (more details in Appendix B.3.5). 

SCRIBE captures more unique and less syntax-related neural variance than QAEmb. Af-ter accounting for the measured syntactic features, SCRIBE C predicts more unique variance than QAEmb across language-related ROIs, reaching significance in late language regions overall, and specifically the LTC and AG (Figure 12b). At the same time, SCRIBE C shares significantly less variance with syntax for predicting the LTC and AG (Figure 12a). Together, these findings provide complementary evidence for greater semantic specificity: SCRIBE retains more predictive informa-tion after accounting for syntax, while exhibiting less overlap with the syntactic control space. We extend the analysis to Llama2-7b representations (Appendix B.3.6). SCRIBE C outperforms non-contextual Llama2-7b embeddings, while contextual Llama2-7b embeddings achieve higher brain 8predictivity (Figure 14). However, contextual LLM embeddings encode a broader mixture of linguis-tic information. Notably, SCRIBE C shares significantly less variance with syntactic features than Llama2-7b in IFG/MFG (Figure 15), a region often associated with syntactic processing (Dapretto & Bookheimer, 1999; Heim et al., 2003; Fiebach et al., 2005; Friederici, 2011). 

5 DISCUSSION 

Understanding how semantic representations are constructed and modulated in the brain requires models that are both context-aware and interpretable . In this work, we introduced SCRIBE, an approach that combines a cognitively grounded semantic feature space with LLM-based annotation to produce contextualized, interpretable semantic representations for natural language. By using LLMs as scalable semantic annotators, SCRIBE preserves the interpretability of theory-driven fea-ture spaces while extending them beyond isolated concepts to arbitrary words in context. Our human study first establishes that semantic feature judgments are context-dependent: across 921 

participants, controlled linguistic contexts systematically shifted ratings along the targeted semantic dimensions, and these judgments were reliable when averaged across participants. To our knowl-edge, these data constitute the first human-annotated context-dependent semantic feature norms and will be publicly released. SCRIBE aligns with human semantic judgments both for isolated concepts and across contextual conditions, reaching 75 –81% of the human noise ceiling, comparable to the agreement between two independently collected sets of human ratings. At the neural level, incorporating context significantly improves SCRIBE’s prediction of fMRI re-sponses relative to its out-of-context representation, particularly in language-related cortical regions. Contextual SCRIBE explains more unique neural variance than the closest existing method, with significant differences in semantic regions, such as the LTC and AG. After accounting for inde-pendently defined syntactic features, SCRIBE retains more unique brain-predictive variance than QAEmb while sharing less variance with syntax in these regions, showing that its additional brain-predictive information persists after accounting for the measured syntactic features. More broadly, our results show that interpretability and contextuality need not be treated as oppos-ing goals. Prior feature-based models offer interpretable dimensions but rely on static out-of-context annotations, while LLM representations are contextual and scalable but difficult to attribute to spe-cific semantic content. SCRIBE bridges this gap by representing meaning along explicit, human-interpretable dimensions that dynamically vary with context, providing a scalable framework for studying contextual semantics in the brain. 

Limitations and future work. SCRIBE scores depend on the underlying LLMs and the prompting strategy. To assess sensitivity to prompt formulation, we performed ablations, which show that per-formance is largely robust across the tested variations (Appendix A.2.2). To reduce model-specific variability and biases, SCRIBE aggregates scores across multiple LLMs, analogous to aggregating human judgments; the ensemble correlates more strongly with human ratings than any individual model (Section 4.2). While SCRIBE captures the relative ordering of human judgments, its scores are systematically lower than human ratings on average, so applications relying on absolute scores may require calibration. Moreover, individual human judgments of context-dependent properties are noisy, and our conclusions hold at the level of averaged ratings rather than individual annotators. In addition, our contextual stimuli are controlled, LLM-generated sentences designed to shift specific properties; validating SCRIBE against human judgments of naturalistic text remains an important next step. Finally, the use of a fixed semantic space (Binder et al., 2016) constrains representations to a predefined set of dimensions, potentially missing aspects of meaning. However, this design choice provides a principled and interpretable basis grounded in cognitive neuroscience, enabling direct comparison with human semantic judgments and prior work. These limitations suggest several directions for future work, including extending SCRIBE to more diverse datasets and domains, integrating it with mechanistic interpretability methods to link seman-tic features to model internals, and potentially expanding the semantic space to capture a broader range of semantic dimensions. Lastly, our syntactic control space is intentionally minimal; richer structural representations (Reddy & Wehbe, 2021; Caucheteux et al., 2021) and low-level or lexical-statistical features (Deniz et al., 2019) remain to be tested. 9ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

A.1 COGNITIVELY -GROUNDED , INTERPRETABLE SEMANTIC FEATURE SPACE 

Semantic feature space. 

To ground our approach in interpretable and cognitively motivated representations, we build on the semantic feature space introduced by Binder et al. (2016), which provides a cognitively and neu-robiologically informed characterization of conceptual meaning. In this framework, concepts are represented as vectors over a fixed set of 65 semantic properties spanning multiple domains, in-cluding perceptual (e.g., vision, audition, somatosensation), action-related (e.g., motor effectors and interaction), spatial, temporal, and abstract properties (e.g., social, cognitive, and affective features), as summarized in Table 3. These features are grounded in converging evidence from cognitive neuroscience, which suggests that conceptual knowledge is distributed across modality-specific and heteromodal brain systems. Accordingly, the semantic dimensions were shown by Binder et al. (2016) to exhibit a structured organization, clustering into groups that align with known functional divisions of the cortex, such as sensory, motor, and affective systems. This representation offers a principled and interpretable characterization of semantic content: each dimension corresponds to a well-defined aspect of meaning, enabling direct analysis of how specific properties contribute to downstream predictions. In this work, we build on this semantic feature space, preserving its interpretability while extending it to contextual settings using LLM-based annotation. 

Human semantic ratings for out-of-context concepts. The Binder et al. (2016) dataset consists of 535 out-of-context concepts ( 434 nouns, 62 verbs, and 39 adjectives). Each concept is annotated along the 65 semantic dimensions using human ratings, where participants evaluate the degree to which a concept is associated with specific properties (e.g., something that you can easily see , some-thing large in size , something that makes one feel happy , etc.). Aggregating these ratings across participants yields continuous-valued feature vectors, forming interpretable semantic representa-tions in the shared feature space. SCRIBE uses LLMs to estimate semantic feature scores by prompting them to assess how strongly a target concept is associated with each semantic property. This procedure is applied both in an out-of-context setting, where the word is evaluated in isolation without providing any contextual sentence, and in a contextual setting, where the word is evaluated within a specific contextual sentence. In both settings, scores are generated with greedy decoding (temperature 0), making annotations deter-ministic, and the first number in the model output is parsed as the score. A.2.1 OUT -OF -CONTEXT SEMANTIC ANNOTATION 

In the out-of-context setting, SCRIBE obtains semantic representations using LLM-generated anno-tations computed independently for each concept-property pair. For a given target concept, the model is prompted for each semantic property in the semantic feature space (described in Ap-pendix A.1), resulting in 65 queries. Each query asks the model to rate how strongly the word is associated with the corresponding property, returning a scalar score in the range [0 , 6] . These scores are then aggregated to form a semantic feature vector for the concept, which is directly comparable to the human ratings of Binder et al. (2016) when evaluated on the same set of concepts. The prompt takes the following form:

A.2.2 ABLATION OF PROMPT COMPONENTS 

LLMs are known to be sensitive to prompt wording, yet our main experiments use a single prompt formulation. To assess the robustness of SCRIBE to prompt design choices, we performed a prompt ablation study in which we systematically modified individual components of the prompt while keeping all other instructions unchanged. The base condition is the prompt used throughout the main experiments (Appendix A.2.1). All variants were annotated in a separate run from the one reported in Table 1, together with the base prompt; owing to the non-determinism of GPU inference, the base results differ slightly from Table 1 (by at most 0.002 in Pearson correlation and 0.007 in MSE). We split the prompt into four conceptual components: (i) a role instruction assigning the model as an expert, (ii) the task description specifying the semantic property and relation to be rated, (iii) the rating scale, and (iv) output instructions constraining the response format.

21 A.3 SYNTHETIC DATASET FOR CONTROLLED CONTEXTUAL EVALUATION 

To evaluate whether contextual information induces systematic changes in semantic representa-tions, we construct a synthetic dataset designed to isolate the effect of context on individual con-cept–property associations. A key requirement for this evaluation is to control for the baseline strength of each association, as it constrains how much it can change under contextual manipula-tion. We therefore first select a subset of concept–property pairs that span the full range of baseline association strengths (Section A.3.1), and only then generate contexts that induce opposite semantic effects: one that aims to decrease the association (“low” condition) and one that aims to increase it (“high” condition) (Section A.3.2). This design enables controlled testing of whether semantic features respond predictably to targeted contextual manipulations. A.3.1 CONCEPT AND PROPERTY SELECTION 

A key challenge in evaluating contextual effects is that the baseline out-of-context strength of a concept–property association may constrain how much it can change. For example, pairs with very high initial ratings have limited room for further increase, while pairs with very low ratings may be difficult to decrease. To control for this effect, we leverage the 535 concepts from the Binder et al. (2016) dataset, which provides human ratings for each concept–property pair and allows us to explicitly characterize baseline out-of-context association strength. We discretize these ratings into three levels ( low , mid , and high ) using thresholds aligned with the original rating scale: low : [0 , 2) , mid : [2 , 4) , high : [4 , 6] .

This yields a categorical matrix that assigns each concept–property pair to a baseline association bin (see Figure 5 for bin coverage). To ensure controlled comparisons across these levels, we construct a balanced subset of concepts. Not all properties exhibit sufficient variability across concepts (Section A.1). We therefore retain only properties for which each bin contains at least 8 concepts, resulting in 57 properties after filter-ing, and excluding Low, Slow, Toward, Away, Disgusted, Complexity, Taste, Dark . For each retained property, we then select a subset of concepts that spans the full range of baseline associations using a greedy selection procedure: 1. Initialize coverage counts for each (property, bin) cell. 2. At each step, consider all remaining candidate concepts. 3. For each candidate, compute how many under-covered (property, bin) cells it would help fill. 4. Select the concept that maximizes this coverage gain. 5. Update coverage counts and repeat until all cells reach the target. At convergence, we then select 8 concepts per property bin (low, mid, high), yielding 24 concepts per property. This balanced design ensures that contextual effects are evaluated under comparable baseline conditions, rather than being driven by saturation or floor effects. Consistent with this concern, human ratings for low-baseline pairs were not further decreased by low-target contexts (Appendix B.2.1). B.2.1 LARGE -SCALE HUMAN BEHAVIORAL STUDY 

Semantic feature norms provide interpretable representations of conceptual meaning along dimen-sions such as sensory, motor, affective, social, and cognitive properties. However, existing human-annotated semantic spaces typically characterize concepts in isolation, assigning each concept a fixed set of property values regardless of the linguistic context in which it occurs. SCRIBE extends the neurocognitively grounded semantic feature space of Binder et al. (2016) to contextual settings by using LLMs to estimate semantic properties of concepts conditioned on their linguistic context. In its controlled evaluation, SCRIBE used LLM-generated contexts designed to systematically in-crease or decrease individual concept–property associations and showed corresponding changes in its semantic annotations (see Appendix A.3 for more information). To determine whether these con-textual manipulations produce analogous changes in human semantic judgments, we conducted a human annotation study using the same controlled stimuli. We selected 54 of the 57 properties included in the original synthetic dataset, excluding Tempera-ture, Weight, and Texture. These three properties are derived by taking the maximum rating across pairs of opposing dimensions (Hot/Cold, Heavy/Light, and Rough/Smooth, respectively). Extend-ing this procedure to contextual annotations would require both constituent dimensions to be eval-uated within the same sentence, which is difficult to guarantee when generating contexts that in-dependently target each property. For each of the 54 retained properties, we selected six concepts based on their original out-of-context ratings: two with low, two with mid-range, and two with high concept–property association strengths according to the original out-of-context human ratings from Binder et al. (2016). This stratification ensured coverage across the range of baseline con-cept–property association strengths. This resulted in a total of 324 concept–property pairs ( 54 × 6). Each concept–property pair was evaluated under three conditions: a high-target context (H T) de-signed to increase the association with the target property, a low-target context (L T) designed to decrease it, and an out-of-context condition ( O) in which the concept was presented without a sen-tence. This resulted in 324 × 3 = 972 unique annotation items. Participants rated the strength of each concept–property association on a 0–6 scale, with contextual judgments made specifically with respect to the meaning of the concept in the provided sentence. 

Participants and data collection. We recruited participants through Prolific, requiring English fluency, a 98 –100 % approval rate, and at least 100 previous submissions. The study took approx-imately 10 minutes, and participants were paid USD 2 (USD 12/hour). The 324 concept–property pairs were split into two questionnaire versions, each covering half of the pairs, and data were col-lected in two rounds using the same stimuli. In the first round, each participant rated 14 semantic annotation items plus one attention check; in the second round, each participant rated 12 items plus two attention checks. Each participant saw only one condition for any given concept–property pair, and condition assignment was balanced across participants. Each participant took part only once, in a single round and a single questionnaire version. We applied the same quality-control criteria to all data: participants were excluded if they failed an attention check, had a mean response time below 7 seconds per item, or gave the same score to all items (Table 9). In the first round, par-ticipants failing the attention check were already rejected during recruitment. We further excluded one pair (Large/ mouth ) for which an incorrect high-target sentence was shown in the first round, leaving 323 pairs ( 969 items). The final sample comprises 921 participants and 11 ,983 ratings, with on average 12 .4 ratings per item ( SD = 1 .4, median = 12 , range = 8 –20 ). The two rounds rank items consistently: the correlation between the item means of the two rounds is 0.81 (O), 0.71 (LT)and 0.66 (HT), which, after correcting for the noise of each round’s means (Spearman–Brown at the harmonic-mean number of ratings per round), corresponds to 1.02 , 1.03 and 1.02 , i.e., as high as their reliability allows (values slightly above 1 reflect estimation noise). Second-round ratings were on average 0.14 –0.20 points lower in all conditions; since this offset is shared across items, it does not affect correlation-based analyses and, if anything, makes the pooled reliability estimates conservative.

B.2.2 RELIABILITY OF HUMAN JUDGMENTS AND NOISE CEILING 

Balanced subsampling. Items received between 8 and 20 ratings, but the intraclass correlation assumes the same number of ratings per item. We therefore draw n = 8 ratings per item, from 

8 distinct participants, such that each participant contributes at most once to each of the 8 rating columns, and repeat this for 20 random draws. All quantities are computed within each draw and then averaged across draws; ratings themselves are never averaged across draws. 

Intraclass correlation. For each condition, let MS B and MS W be the between-item and within-item mean squares of a one-way ANOVA over the 323 × n rating matrix. The one-way random-effects intraclass correlations are 

ICC(1 , 1) = MS B − MS W

ICC(1 , 1) is the expected correlation between two single raters, and ICC(1 , n ) the reliability of the mean of n raters, i.e., the expected correlation between the means of two independent groups of 

n raters. The two are related by the Spearman–Brown formula, ICC(1 , n ) = n ICC(1 , 1) /(1 + (n − 1) ICC(1 , 1)) , which we use to extrapolate reliability to other numbers of raters. The one-way model treats raters as interchangeable, which matches our design: each item is rated by a different random subset of participants. 

Noise ceiling. A model whose scores equal the true item values correlates with the n-rater mean at pICC(1 , n ), which we use as the human noise ceiling. An independent split-half estimate (cor-relating two halves of 4 raters and applying Spearman–Brown) gives the same ceilings to within 

0.005 .

Equivalent number of annotators. To express alignment in human units, we ask how many human raters m would be needed for their mean to correlate with the n-rater mean as strongly as SCRIBE does. MSE and systematic offset. Because the mean of n raters is itself noisy, even a perfect model would not reach an MSE of zero: its expected MSE is the noise variance of the mean, MS W /n (MSE floor ). We further decompose the MSE as MSE = (¯ y − ¯h)2 + ( sy − sh)2 + 2 sy sh(1 − r), where 

¯y, s y and ¯h, s h are the mean and standard deviation of SCRIBE and human scores: the three terms capture a systematic offset, a difference in scale, and imperfect correlation (Table 15). We investigated this in two ways: first, by quantifying the unique variance explained by each representation through variance partitioning; and second, by testing whether combining their feature spaces improves held-out brain prediction. Variance partitioning showed that both SCRIBE C and QAEmb explain unique variance in neu-ral responses beyond their shared contribution (Figure 10). Despite their similar overall encod-ing performance, SCRIBE C explained more unique variance than QAEmb across several higher-level language and semantic ROIs, with significant differences in the lateral temporal cortex (LTC; 

p = 0 .039 ) and angular gyrus (AG; p = 0 .023 ), and a numerical difference in the late-language net-work. These results indicate that the two representations are not fully redundant and that SCRIBE C

captures brain-predictive information not accounted for by QAEmb. To examine the spatial distribu-tion of these unique contributions, Figure 11 shows the voxel-wise unique-variance contrast between SCRIBE and QAEmb for each subject. To further test whether this complementary information translates into improved brain prediction, we trained an additional encoding model using the concatenation of the SCRIBE C and QAEmb fea-ture spaces and compared its prediction performance with models using either representation alone (Table 21). At the whole-brain level, combining QAEmb with SCRIBE C significantly improved prediction performance over both SCRIBE C and QAEmb individually. The combined represen-tation achieved a mean noise-ceiling-normalized correlation of 0.2022 ± 0.0146 , compared with 

0.1837 ± 0.0144 for SCRIBE C and 0.1778 ± 0.0125 for QAEmb (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, p = 0 .0078 for both comparisons). This corresponds to a 13 .7% improvement in mean correlation over QAEmb alone. The same pattern was observed within cortical regions associated with higher-level language and semantic processing. The combined representation yielded higher mean prediction performance than either individual representation in the late-language network ( 0.1577 ±0.0213 ), LTC ( 0.1644 ±

0.0236 ), IFG/MFG ( 0.1458 ± 0.0196 ), and AG ( 0.1497 ± 0.0290 ). The combined representation significantly outperformed both individual representations in all four ROIs (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, p < 0.05 ; Table 21). Together, the variance-partitioning and joint-model analyses provide converging evidence that 

SCRIBE C and QAEmb capture complementary brain-relevant information. Both representations account for unique variance in neural responses, while SCRIBE C shows greater unique contribu-tions in several higher-level language and semantic regions. Moreover, combining the two repre-sentations consistently improves held-out prediction performance over either representation alone, indicating that the information captured by SCRIBE is not subsumed by QAEmb. 43 QAEmb SCRIBE C

SCRIBE C and QAEmb differ in the dimensionality of their final feature spaces: SCRIBE C con-tains 65 semantic properties, whereas QAEmb uses 35 features selected from an initial set of 606 questions. We therefore investigated whether differences in brain-encoding performance could be explained simply by the larger dimensionality of SCRIBE C.To control for this possibility, we applied sparse feature selection to our contextual variant of 

SCRIBE C, following the feature-selection approach used by QAEmb. Feature selection retained 33 of the original 65 semantic dimensions, resulting in a representation with approximately half the dimensionality of the original SCRIBE C space and a dimensionality comparable to QAEmb. The selected dimensions were: Bright, Dark, Large, Small, Fast, Slow, Face, Temperature, Pain, Weight, Audition, High, Music, Taste, Smell, Head, UpperLimb, LowerLimb, Landmark, Toward, Number, Time, Long, Self, Cognition, Happy, Sad, Angry, Disgusted, Fearful, Surprised, Needs , and Arousal .Despite reducing the representation from 65 to 33 dimensions, whole-brain encoding performance was preserved (Table 22). The full SCRIBE C representation achieved a mean noise-ceiling-normalized correlation of 0.1837 ± 0.0144 , compared with 0.1859 ± 0.0126 after feature selection. The difference was not statistically significant (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0 .5781 ). This preservation of performance was also observed across the examined language and semantic ROIs. The full and feature-selected representations achieved highly similar correlations in the late-language network ( 0.1460 ± 0.0201 vs. 0.1452 ± 0.0197 ), lateral temporal cortex (LTC; 0.1538 ±

0.0223 vs. 0.1522 ±0.0216 ), inferior/middle frontal gyrus (IFG/MFG; 0.1312 ±0.0182 vs. 0.1344 ±

0.0181 ), and angular gyrus (AG; 0.1399 ± 0.0279 vs. 0.1380 ± 0.0268 ; Table 22). Together, these results indicate that SCRIBE C does not depend on its larger number of semantic dimensions to achieve its brain-encoding performance. Reducing the representation from 65 to 33 dimensions preserves both whole-brain prediction accuracy and performance across the examined semantic-language ROIs, suggesting that the observed results are not simply attributable to differ-ences in feature-space dimensionality between SCRIBE C and QAEmb. 46 B.3.5 VARIANCE PARTITIONING WITH SYNTACTIC FEATURES 

To further characterize the information underlying the brain-encoding performance of SCRIBE, we performed variance partitioning with an explicit syntactic feature space. Syntactic features com-prised one-hot encoded part-of-speech tags and dependency length obtained with spaCy (Honnibal et al., 2020), together with constituency tree depth obtained using the Benepar parser (Kitaev & Klein, 2018). We compared SCRIBE C with QAEmb to assess both how much of their brain-predictive variance overlaps with these syntactic features and how much remains uniquely explained after accounting for them. Figure 12a shows the variance that SCRIBE C and QAEmb share with the syntactic feature space. 

SCRIBE C shares significantly less variance with syntactic features than QAEmb in LTC and AG, with the same numerical direction in Late Language and IFG/MFG. Thus, the greater brain-prediction accuracy of SCRIBE C relative to QAEmb is not accompanied by greater overlap with the measured syntactic information. Complementing this result, Figure 12b shows the variance uniquely explained by each representation after accounting for the syntactic feature space. SCRIBE C retains significantly more unique brain-predictive variance than QAEmb in Late Language, LTC, and AG, with no significant difference in IFG/MFG. Together, these analyses show that, relative to QAEmb, SCRIBE C retains more unique brain-predictive information while exhibiting less overlap with the measured syntactic features, supporting a more semantically selective interpretation of its neural predictivity. To compare SCRIBE with representations extracted directly from an LLM, we evaluated brain-encoding performance using Llama2-7B embeddings. We consider both non-contextual embeddings extracted from layer 0 and contextual embeddings extracted from layer 15. Figure 14 shows the whole-brain and region-wise comparison between the non-contextual (layer 

0) and contextual (layer 15 ) Llama2-7B embeddings, QAEmb , and SCRIBE C . Compared with non-contextual Llama2-7B embeddings, SCRIBE C achieved substantially higher whole-brain pre-diction accuracy ( 0.1837 ± 0.0144 vs. 0.1185 ± 0.0106 ), corresponding to a 55% relative improve-ment. SCRIBE C also significantly outperformed the non-contextual embeddings in the Late Lan-guage ROIs, AG, LTC, and IFG/MFG. In contrast, contextual Llama2-7B embeddings achieved higher overall brain-encoding performance than SCRIBE C (0.2387 ± 0.0215 vs. 0.1837 ± 0.0144 ). The higher predictive performance of contextual Llama2-7B embeddings does not, however, es-tablish which linguistic information drives this advantage. Because contextual LLM embeddings may encode multiple sources of linguistic information, we used variance partitioning with explicit syntactic features to further characterize the information underlying their brain predictivity. Fig-ure 15 compares the variance that contextual Llama2-7B and SCRIBE share with the syntactic feature space. SCRIBE exhibits significantly less shared variance than Llama2-7B in IFG/MFG, with the same numerical direction in Late Language and LTC. This difference is particularly notable given the established involvement of left inferior frontal regions in syntactic processing (Dapretto & Bookheimer, 1999; Heim et al., 2003; Friederici, 2011). Finally, we tested whether the variance uniquely explained by SCRIBE exceeds chance. Permu-tation tests confirmed significant unique variance across all four language-related ROIs (Late Lan-guage, LTC, IFG/MFG, and AG; all p ≤ .002 ; Figure 16). Thus, despite the higher overall brain-prediction accuracy of contextual Llama2-7B embeddings, SCRIBE captures significant unique brain-predictive information and shows reduced overlap with syntactic features in IFG/MFG.
