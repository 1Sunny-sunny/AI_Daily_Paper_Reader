# How large an effect would we have seen? Positive controls, detection floors and dataset pitfalls in scalp-EEG speech decoding 

Blessing Isoyiza Adeika 1,∗ and Onyema Osuagwu 1  

> 1Morgan State University, Baltimore, Maryland, United States of America

> E-mail: blade3@morgan.edu
> Keywords: imagined speech, electroencephalography, electromyographic artifact, positive control, detection floor, brain–computer interface

Abstract 

Objective. Studies that decode imagined speech from scalp electroencephalography (EEG) commonly validate their pipelines with a positive control, usually overt speech, and report null results without stating what effect size the pipeline could have detected. Approach. Using ds003626 (“Thinking out loud”), KaraOne and Track 3 of the 2020 International Brain–Computer Interface Competition, we audited decoding of overt speech, articulator preparation and imagined speech with artifact-channel subsets, band-limited decoding and trial selection, under a protocol that enforces split integrity by assertion. We injected class-dependent effects of known amplitude into the real recordings to measure the detection floor of five decoder families, and we checked the ds003626 labels against the dataset’s acquisition protocol. Main results. On ds003626, two lip electrodes decoded overt speech at 0.7430 and the eight external channels at 0.7068, against 0.4231 for the 128-channel scalp montage, and no decodable signal survived in the 8–30 Hz band. On the other two corpora, within-subject accuracy rose monotonically with frequency into the range dominated by muscle activity. Band-power logistic regression detected an injected 8–30 Hz desynchronization of 0.54 μV under a criterion fixed in advance, whereas a compact convolutional network (EEGNet) detected nothing within subject up to 4.31 μV. In ds003626, the condition codes are the reverse of the intuitive reading, the class encoding changed between public releases, and condition is a deterministic function of time within each session. Significance. In these corpora the overt-speech positive control is dominated by myogenic signal and cannot validate a decoding pipeline. We recommend that speech-decoding studies report band-limited and artifact-channel accuracy, the direction of accuracy across frequency, and a measured detection floor. 

# 1 Introduction 

Speech neuroprostheses have advanced rapidly with intracranial recordings. Electrocorticography and intracortical arrays now decode attempted speech in people with paralysis at 62 to 78 words per minute and with vocabularies of up to 125,000 words [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], and these systems are being considered for clinical use in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and the locked-in state [6, 7, 8, 9]. Scalp electroencephalography (EEG) would make such a device non-invasive and widely available, which is why decoding imagined or covert speech from the scalp has attracted a sustained research effort [10, 11, 12, 13]. Progress there has been much slower. Reviews describe small vocabularies, heterogeneous paradigms and results that are difficult to compare across studies [12, 13, 14, 15], and two recent re-evaluations that applied a common protocol across public corpora found within-category imagined-speech decoding at or near chance [16, 17]. Because imagined speech has no behavioral output, a decoding pipeline cannot be checked against what the participant actually intended. It is instead validated indirectly, most often by showing that it decodes overt speech or the contrast between speaking and rest. That inference is sound only if the signal the pipeline finds in the control condition is cortical. Articulation produces electromyographic (EMG) activity at the scalp that is far larger than the cortical signal of interest [18, 19]. Its spectrum is broad, overlaps the high-frequency range of neural activity [20, 21], and under neuromuscular blockade most scalp power above 20 Hz disappears [22]. Ocular micro-saccades can mimic induced gamma-band responses [23], and even silent verbal tasks can raise orofacial muscle activity [24, 25]. A positive control can therefore succeed for reasons 1arXiv:2610.20942v1 [eess.SP] 14 Oct 2026 unrelated to cortical speech processing. For the intended users, people who cannot articulate at all, a decoder that relies on articulator activity would not work [26]. A second gap concerns null results. Decoding studies usually report accuracy and, at best, whether it exceeds chance [27, 28], but rarely state how large an effect the pipeline could have found. Evaluation is itself fragile: information can leak between training and test data in several ways [29, 30, 31], selection performed outside cross-validation inflates accuracy [32, 33], and cross-validated estimates from small samples carry wide error bars [34]. Simulation frameworks that place known sources into EEG are well established for method development [35, 36], yet they are seldom used to attach a sensitivity bound to a decoding null. In this paper we address three questions. First, is the overt-speech positive control neural in the corpora most often used for imagined-speech decoding? Second, what effect sizes can commonly used decoders detect in these recordings, and how does this depend on the type of effect? Third, do the labels of a widely used corpus mean what they appear to mean? We answer the first with channel-subset, band-limited and trial-selection controls on three corpora, the second by injecting effects of known amplitude into the real recordings, and the third by checking ds003626 against its acquisition protocol. The same evaluation protocol underlies a companion paper that reports cross-subject decoding of imagined-speech content on these corpora [37]. 

# 2 Related work 

## 2.1 Imagined-speech decoding from scalp EEG 

Early work used common spatial patterns to discriminate imagined /a/, imagined /u/ and a no-imagery control, reporting 68–78% accuracy for three participants [38]. Open corpora followed. KaraOne records EEG, facial motion and audio while participants imagine and then articulate seven phonemes and four words chosen as phonological minimal pairs; its authors reported up to 90% accuracy for separating imagined consonants from vowels and up to 95% for separating the stimulus period from active imagination, using deep belief networks on engineered features [39]. Later work on the same corpus reported a mean of 83.42% over six binary phonological tasks [40]. A corpus of five imagined Spanish vowels and six command words is also openly available [41]. The ds003626 corpus, “Thinking out loud”, records inner, pronounced and visualized versions of four Spanish direction words from ten participants [42, 43]; on its four-class inner-speech task, EEGNet reached 29.7% [44] and a bidirectional recurrent network 36.1% [45]. Track 3 of the 2020 International Brain–Computer Interface (BCI) Competition asked entrants to classify five imagined phrases, and the winning entry reached 82.6% with the second at 75.5% [46]. Two recent evaluations reached more sobering conclusions. Van Dyck applied seven decoders under one nested cross-validation protocol to seven public and two in-house corpora and found within-category imagined-speech decoding mostly at or near chance, with a best pairwise balanced accuracy of 62.7% on the 2020 competition data [16]. Large EEG models pretrained on thousands of hours of recordings have been proposed as a route to generalization [47, 48], but a benchmark on speech found that they offered no consistent advantage over a small convolutional network, and that every model tested was at chance on the competition data when the test participant was held out [17]. Differences in evaluation protocol are a plausible source of this spread, which is why the protocol used here is described in detail (section 3.4). 

## 2.2 Myogenic and ocular contamination 

Muscle activity is the best documented contaminant of scalp EEG. Frontalis and temporalis contraction produces broadband scalp activity from below 20 Hz to beyond 200 Hz, detectable at the vertex even at low contraction levels [20]. Recordings made during neuromuscular paralysis show that most scalp power above 20 Hz in the awake state is of muscular origin [22], and muscle activity can masquerade as neural effects down to the alpha band, with independent component analysis removing it adequately in some protocols but not others [49]. A review of high-frequency activity in MEG and EEG concluded that the spectra of muscle and neural activity overlap entirely and recommended specific controls [21]. Miniature saccades generate spike potentials that appear as induced gamma-band responses [23]. Speech production magnifies the problem. The neural signature expected from speech motor cortex is different in kind: a band-limited decrease of mu (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) power over sensorimotor regions [53, 54]. In intracranial recordings, high-frequency activity best decodes overt speech, whereas low-frequency power and cross-frequency coupling contribute to decoding imagined speech [55]; spectrotemporal features and word pairs of covert speech have also been decoded from high-gamma activity recorded directly from cortex [56, 57]. 

## 2.3 Reference choice and volume conduction 

The common average reference assumes dense, even scalp coverage and redistributes activity from every electrode into every other [58, 59]. Alternatives such as the reference electrode standardization technique approximate a reference at infinity [60]. For connectivity analysis the consequence is well known: a shared reference and volume conduction create zero-lag coupling between electrodes that share no source, and measures that discard the zero-lag component, such as the imaginary part of coherency and the weighted phase-lag index, were introduced to address it [61, 62, 63, 64, 65]. 

## 2.4 Evaluation, leakage and sensitivity 

Leakage between training and test data is now recognized as a pervasive problem in machine-learning-based science [29, 30]. In EEG, splitting segments of the same recording across training and test inflates deep-learning accuracy [31], and block designs allow classifiers to read slow temporal drift in place of the experimental variable [66]. Reviews of deep learning for EEG report inconsistent evaluation and limited reproducibility [67]. On the statistical side, theoretical chance levels are misleading for small test sets, and binomial thresholds or permutation tests are required [27, 28]. What is missing from most imagined-speech studies is a measured sensitivity: simulation toolboxes place sources of known strength into realistic EEG [35, 36], but their outputs are rarely used to state the detection floor of a reported null.

## 3.2 Processing and modelling pipeline 

Every analysis in this paper passes through the same sequence of stages, summarized in table 2 and detailed in the subsections that follow. The stages are ordered so that nothing estimated from data is fitted before the split that defines training and test data, and the last column of the table records, for each stage, which data any estimated parameter is fitted on. A stage that fits nothing cannot leak information; a stage that fits on training rows only cannot leak it across the split; the only stage that uses data from a held-out participant without labels is per-participant re-centering, and runs that use it are flagged.

## 3.4 Evaluation protocol and split integrity 

Figure 1 summarizes the protocol. Every trial carries an identifier built from provenance alone: corpus, participant, session, trial index, condition and prompt. A single software module constructs every split, and before the first optimizer step an assertion stops the run if any identifier appears in more than one of the training, validation and test sets, if the test participant appears in training data under leave-one-subject-out evaluation, if a class present in the test set is absent from training, if the test set contains a single class, or if a model that performs selection has no validation data. A SHA-256 hash of the sorted identifiers is recorded for every fold, so that any change to the split machinery that alters a split is detectable. Early stopping, checkpoint selection and decision thresholds draw only on training data. We report within-subject stratified 5-fold cross-validation, leave-one-session-out and leave-one-block-out evaluation within subject, and leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) evaluation across participants. Results that exceed the ranges expected for this problem were put through seven further checks before being reported: a shuffle-label control, a rebuild of the data cache with three fresh split seeds, replication on a second corpus, physiological plausibility of the learned spatial filters and frequency profile, decoders restricted to artifact channels, the highest band, the pre-cue baseline and the trial index, graceful degradation under channel and trial ablation, and the expected 5ordering of protocols and conditions. A result counted as verified only if all seven passed. Every run writes an append-only record containing the code version, a checksum of the input data, the random seed, the per-fold split hashes and the model configuration. The project archive holds 1,890 such records. The number of computational threads was fixed at four for all runs, because thread count alone changed accuracy by approximately 0.0018 through floating-point reduction order. 

## 3.5 Decoders 

A null result is informative only about the decoder that produced it, so we used decoders that differ in what they can represent: a linear model on band power, which reads amplitude changes directly; convolutional networks, which learn spatial and temporal filters from the raw signal; covariance-based classifiers on the Riemannian manifold, which capture cross-channel structure; and linear models on phase and coupling features, which capture timing relations that band power ignores. The band-power decoder computes the logarithm of mean in-band power for each channel and band over the analysis window, standardizes the features with statistics fitted on training rows only, and applies L2-regularized multinomial logistic regression with the inverse regularization strength fixed at 0.1 [69]. It performs no model selection and therefore needs no validation set. Features were computed either in single bands or in a six-band bank (1–4, 4–8, 8–13, 13–20, 20–30 and 30–45 Hz). EEGNet [70] was run with a temporal kernel of 64 samples and two filter configurations: 16 temporal filters with a depth multiplier of 4 ( F1 = 16, D = 4), and a smaller configuration with 8 temporal filters and a depth multiplier of 2 ( F1 = 8, D = 2). We also implemented ShallowConvNet [71] with seven temporal filters of 25 samples, a spatial convolution, and the square, mean-pool (75 samples, stride 15) and logarithm sequence that makes it an explicit band-power estimator. All networks were trained in PyTorch with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −4), batches of 32, dropout of 0.3 and at most 100 epochs, on inputs z-scored per trial and channel. Twenty percent of the training data, drawn from training participants under LOSO, formed a validation set; validation macro-averaged F1 was evaluated every five epochs and training stopped after eight evaluations without improvement. The Riemannian decoders estimate a spatial covariance matrix per trial, reduce it with a principal component projection fitted on training covariances, apply shrinkage toward a scaled identity [72], re-center each participant’s matrices at their Riemannian mean [73], and classify with the minimum distance to Riemannian mean, its geodesically filtered variant, or logistic regression in the tangent space [74, 75, 76], implemented with pyRiemann [77]. The phase decoders apply the same logistic regression to three feature families computed per trial, band and region pair: the signed weighted phase-lag index [63], the complex phase–amplitude coupling vector with phase from theta and amplitude from beta [78, 79], and the phase of each region’s analytic signal at a reference latency, encoded as its cosine and sine. The regions were a language set comprising left inferior frontal and left superior temporal cortex with their right homologues, and a central sensorimotor cap. 

## 3.6 Statistical analysis 

For every result we report the number of test trials, chance level, the exact binomial significance threshold for a single participant at α = 0 .05 [27], the number of participants at or above that threshold, a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test of participant accuracies against chance [80], and a bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap 95% confidence interval (CI) on the mean over 10,000 resamples [81]. A participant exactly at the threshold counts as clearing it, because the threshold is defined as the smallest accuracy whose one-sided p-value does not exceed α. Families of related tests were corrected by controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [82]. Where a result is described as surviving its shuffle-label control, labels were permuted within each participant and the identical pipeline was run 200 times, or 50 times for the ds003626 network cells, where every draw retrains the network; permuting across participants would also destroy the structure that distinguishes participants and could inflate the null for reasons unrelated to the class labels. The observed value was recomputed by the same code path as the null draws. A result was rejected if the mean of the null distribution was significantly above chance or if the observed value 6did not exceed the 95th percentile of the null. Empirical p-values were computed as ( b + 1) /(m + 1) for b null draws at or above the observed value out of m [83]. 

## 3.7 Artifact audit of the positive controls 

The discriminating features of cortical speech-motor activity and of muscle activity are frequency and location: the former is a mu and beta power decrease over central sites [53, 54], the latter is broadband from about 20 Hz upward, increases with contraction and is largest at the periphery of the montage [20, 21]. We therefore decoded each positive control from restricted sets of channels, bands and trials. For ds003626 pronounced speech we compared the 128-channel scalp montage with a central sensorimotor cap of 40 channels, a peripheral and temporal rim of 52 channels, all eight external (EXG) channels, the two lip electrodes over orbicularis oris (EXG7–8), the ocular channels (EXG3–6, and their horizontal and vertical pairs separately), the earlobe reference pair (EXG1–2), and all EXG channels during the cue interval. We also decoded each of five bands separately (1–8, 8–13, 13–20, 20–30 and 30–45 Hz), and repeated the decoding after aggressive amplitude and gradient rejection and on the cleanest and dirtiest halves of each participant’s trials. These cells used EEGNet ( F1 = 16, D = 4) within subject with 5-fold cross-validation. For phoneme decoding in the KaraOne pre-imagery interval, where a dedicated EMG channel exists, we compared all 68 stored channels, the 62 scalp channels re-referenced to their own mean, a 21-channel frontocentral speech-motor strip, a 22-channel peripheral rim matched in size, the non-scalp channels, the EMG channel alone and the ocular channels, and decoded five bands at 128 Hz and three bands (8–30, 30–45 and 45–100 Hz) at 512 Hz. These cells used EEGNet ( F1 = 16, 

D = 4) and band-power logistic regression, within subject and under LOSO. For the competition corpus, which has no EMG channel, we decoded single bands up to 100 Hz with band power. 

## 3.8 Injected effects of known size 

To measure what each decoder could detect, we injected class-dependent effects into the real ds003626 inner-speech trials. The amplitude effect suppresses the participant’s own ongoing 8–30 Hz rhythm by a multiplicative depth instead of adding a synthetic oscillator, so the injected effect inherits the real phase statistics and spatial covariance of each recording. Per-participant rotation and tilt of the injected topography, a per-class positional offset, and amplitude and latency jitter across participants and trials ensure that the mapping from class to topography differs between people, as it would for a real effect. The effect is scaled by a factor k times the mean 8–30 Hz standard deviation over the central sensorimotor region, which is 5.20 μV in these recordings, times each participant’s amplitude scale, whose mean is 1.036. One unit of k therefore corresponds to 5.392 μV at the peak of a desynchronization sustained for 1.5 s, and k = 0 .1 is a 10% reduction of the ongoing rhythm, or 0.54 μV. We swept k from 0 to 0.8. A decoder was considered to detect an effect only if, under a criterion fixed before the sweep, its mean accuracy reached the mean per-participant threshold, at least eight of ten participants individually cleared their threshold, and the group Wilcoxon test gave p < 0.05; where several seeds were run, every seed had to meet the criterion. We extended the injection to four effect types and all three corpora: the amplitude effect above, an evoked deflection, class-dependent phase locking, and class-dependent coupling between theta phase and beta amplitude. This grid of 240 cells was evaluated with band power and with a Riemannian tangent-space decoder, under both protocols, at five values of k. Because accuracy on real data can sit above chance before any injection, a grid cell counted as detected when participant accuracies exceeded those of the same cell at k = 0 under a one-sided paired Wilcoxon test with a mean gain above one percentage point, after Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all 240 cells. Finally, two effects were injected specifically to validate the phase features: a class-dependent phase lag between two regions, obtained by replacing a fraction k of one region’s in-band component with a lagged copy of the other’s, and a class-dependent coupling phase, with k the depth to which theta phase modulates the beta envelope. Both preserve band power by construction. The phase-locking value [84] was computed alongside the lagged measures as a negative control, because a common average reference manufactures zero-lag phase agreement between electrodes that share no source [61, 65]. 73.9 Integrity checks on the dataset labels 

For ds003626 we compared the condition codes read by our loader with the dataset authors’ acquisition code and events decoder, the block structure of every session, the authors’ EMG rejection flags, and the EXG power in each condition. We also compared the class encodings of public releases 1.0.0 and 2.1.2 of the dataset, and we tabulated the within-session position of every trial by condition. For KaraOne we compared the duration and position of every annotated segment, in all 14 participants, with the trial structure described by the dataset’s authors [39]. 

# 4 Results 

## 4.1 Three ways ds003626 is misread 

The condition codes of ds003626 are 0 = pronounced, 1 = inner and 2 = visualized. Because the corpus is named for inner speech, reading code 0 as inner speech is natural, and our own loader did so for the first months of this project. The correct mapping is established independently by the acquisition protocol’s stimulation code, whose paradigm order contains one pronounced run followed by two inner and two visualized runs; by the observed 40/80/80 block structure in all 30 sessions; by the authors’ events decoder and loading interface; by their EMG control, which excludes condition 0 by name and contains none of the 115 flagged trials in it; and by EXG power between 20 and 45 Hz, which is elevated approximately ninefold in condition 0 in all ten participants. Under the corrected mapping, inner speech comprises 2,236 trials, not 1,128, and the within-subject accuracy of 0.342 that we had previously reported as inner speech is an overt-speech result. The class encoding changed between public releases. In version 1.0.0 the four classes are coded 1 to 4, and in version 2.1.2 they are coded 0 to 3, with class identity and order preserved. A pipeline written for the earlier release that selects classes 1 to 4 silently drops one class and retains exactly 75% of every condition when run on the current release, without raising an error. We normalize both encodings at every point where the events are read and guard the conversion with a regression test. Condition is also collinear with time. Across all 30 sessions, pronounced trials occupy within-session positions 0 to 39, inner trials 40 to 119 and visualized trials 120 to 199. A single threshold on trial position separates inner from visualized trials with an accuracy of 1.0000 in every session, so condition is a deterministic function of time within session and cannot be separated from it by regression. Decoding inner versus visualized speech within subject reached 0.8604 before we recognized this. The only available control is geometric: comparing the last w trials of one block with the first w of the next keeps the groups adjacent in time while preserving the condition difference. Because a block boundary is also a run boundary, the control must itself cross a run break with condition held constant, and that calibrating contrast decoded at 0.5029 to 0.6333 on its own. Under a paired per-participant test, the inner-versus-visualized contrast survived correction at only one of four window widths, whereas the overt contrast survived at all four. This is the block-design confound described for EEG classification elsewhere [66], and it means that the condition axis of ds003626 cannot serve as a covert-speech control. 

## 4.2 A KaraOne interval annotated as speech contains none 

KaraOne’s annotation file lists two speaking segments per trial in a single alternating sequence, one between rest and imagery and one after imagery. Only the second is speech. The first lasted a median of 2.00 s in every participant and ended where imagery began, whereas the second varied in length from trial to trial, as a self-paced response does. The authors’ protocol places the speaking state after imagery and, before imagery, a stimulus state that ends with a 2 s period in which participants move their articulators into position [39]. The first segment is therefore the pre-imagery interval: the prompt has been presented and the articulators are being positioned, but nothing is spoken. Our conversion of the recordings to the BIDS format took the first segment of each pair and exported it as spoken speech, and we analyzed it under that name until we checked it against the protocol. ## 4.4 Non-cortical channels survive channel selection 

KaraOne stores 68 channels typed as EEG, six of which are not cortical: two mastoids, vertical and horizontal electro-oculography, electrocardiography and a dedicated EMG channel. ## 4.5 Detection floors for amplitude effects 

Table 4 and figure 4 give the accuracy of each decoder on the injected amplitude effect. Band-power logistic regression detected the effect from k = 0 .1, a 0.54 μV desynchronization, under both protocols, and this held across three seeds. At k = 0 .05 (0.27 μV) its mean accuracy exceeded the threshold but only seven of ten participants cleared it, so the pre-specified criterion was not met. EEGNet with F1 = 16, D = 4 did not detect the effect within subject at any amplitude tested, reaching 0.2881 at 4.31 μV, while under LOSO it detected the effect from 2.16 μV. Its within-subject floor therefore lies above 4.31 μV, about eight times the band-power floor. With 

F1 = 8, D = 2, EEGNet detected nothing under either protocol, including at 4.31 μV, where band power reached 0.9807 within subject. We re-ran the project’s earlier EEGNet inner-speech analyses at the larger capacity with identical splits, and the conclusions held (within subject 0.2392 against 0.2434, LOSO 0.2505 against 0.2439), but only the larger configuration’s results can be interpreted. The difference is architectural and not only a matter of size. EEGNet applies an exponential linear unit [85] followed by average pooling. The mean of a zero-mean oscillation is close to zero whatever its amplitude, and the exponential linear unit is nearly linear around zero, so an amplitude modulation is largely removed before the classifier sees it. ShallowConvNet squares, mean-pools and takes the logarithm, which is an explicit band-power estimate [71], and on the same arrays, folds and training schedule it detected the effect at 2.16 μV under both protocols. The injected effect is an oscillatory amplitude change, the same class of effect that imagined speech is expected to produce in sensorimotor cortex [53, 54], so this limitation applies to the intended target. We did not test EEGNet’s sensitivity to phase-locked evoked responses. The ordering between protocols is also informative. The injected effect is identical in both protocols by construction; what differs is the amount of training data, about 179 rows per within-subject fold against about 2,012 per LOSO fold. At k = 0 .4 the three decoders order themselves by the number of parameters per training row (table 5). Band power, with 512 parameters, performed better within subject, whereas ShallowConvNet and EEGNet performed better across participants, with the largest reversal for the model with the most parameters per row. Accuracy under LOSO exceeding within-subject accuracy is usually treated as a sign of leakage; here the splits were asserted valid and the effect is the same in both arms, and the reversal reflects only the elevenfold difference in training data.

# 5 Discussion 

The central finding is that the positive controls available in these corpora do not demonstrate that a pipeline decodes cortical speech activity. On ds003626 the evidence is direct: two electrodes over the lips outperform the full scalp montage, 0.7430 against 0.4231, nothing survives in the mu and 14 beta bands, and even the earlobe reference decodes above threshold. On KaraOne and the competition corpus the evidence is spectral: within subject, accuracy climbs with frequency into the range where muscle activity dominates scalp recordings [22, 20]. These are the signatures that reviews of muscle contamination describe [21, 49], and they appear in exactly the conditions used to argue that a pipeline works. The failure is easy to miss for a structural reason. Overt speech is chosen as a positive control because it is expected to decode well, so a high accuracy is read as confirmation rather than as a reason to look further. Our own analyses treated the ds003626 overt result as a passing control for several months. For clinical translation the consequence is direct: a decoder validated on healthy speakers through articulator activity would fail for users who have no articulatory control, and the population for whom the technology is intended is the one in which this failure cannot be observed during development [26, 8]. The detection floors turn null results into bounded statements. On ds003626, a band-power decoder reliably detects a 10% reduction of the ongoing mu and beta rhythm, 0.54 μV at its peak, and a more sensitive paired criterion lowers this to 0.27 μV. The floors also show that decoders differ sharply in what they can see. A compact convolutional network commonly used for EEG was blind within subject to an amplitude effect about eight times larger than the band-power floor, amplitude- and covariance-based decoders were blind to phase locking and cross-frequency coupling on every corpus, and phase features detected lagged mixing and coupling of 5–10% but only because they were built for those effects. A null result therefore needs to name the decoder and the effect type it bounds. Intracranial studies suggest that imagined speech may be carried by low-frequency and cross-frequency features [55], which are precisely the effects that band-power and Riemannian decoders did not detect here. The ds003626 findings concern how a corpus is read, not errors in the deposited data. The condition mapping, the change of class encoding between releases and the blocked acquisition order are each documented in the dataset’s own code and files, yet each can silently change a result. The collinearity of condition with time is a known hazard of block designs in EEG classification [66] and deserves explicit mention wherever ds003626 is used to contrast conditions. These observations lead to specific recommendations for scalp-EEG speech-decoding studies: report accuracy in the mu and beta bands alone; report accuracy from peripheral or artifact channels alone, and record EMG where the paradigm allows; report how accuracy changes with frequency, treating a monotonic rise into the range above 30 Hz as evidence of contamination until shown otherwise; re-reference any channel subset to its own mean when the original reference included non-cortical channels; state a measured detection floor for each decoder and effect type used to support a null; and check label semantics and acquisition order against the source code of the dataset, and segment annotations against its published trial structure. Several limitations apply. On KaraOne and the competition corpus the myogenic interpretation rests on the frequency profile: the competition corpus has no muscle channel, KaraOne’s EMG channel alone does not carry the effect, and an attempt to locate it by recovering the decoders’ per-channel weights failed our own consistency criterion (mean pairwise map correlation 0.078 to 0.118 against a required 0.30), so spatial evidence is missing, not contrary. A corpus recording masseter and laryngeal EMG alongside scalp EEG would allow a direct test. The ds003626 shuffle-label controls used 50 draws instead of 200, because each draw retrains the network for every participant, so their smallest attainable empirical p is 0.020; no draw in any of the six controls came within 0.14 of the observed value. The three KaraOne EEGNet controls used 20 draws, for the same reason, so their smallest attainable p is 0.048. The KaraOne audit concerns the pre-imagery interval, not speech, and KaraOne’s spoken productions were not analyzed. Finally, each injected effect is a model of a real effect; the floors bound effects of the types injected, and a phase effect locked to a jittered internal event would escape the fixed-latency phase features. 

# 6 Conclusions 

On three public corpora used for imagined-speech decoding, the overt-speech and high-frequency effects that would ordinarily validate a scalp-EEG pipeline are dominated by muscle activity. Measured detection floors show that band power reliably detects a 0.54 μV mu and beta desynchronization on ds003626, that a widely used convolutional network does not detect it within subject at about eight times that amplitude, and that amplitude- and covariance-based decoders do not detect phase effects at all. Three features of ds003626, and a KaraOne annotation that labels 15 an interval without speech as speaking, can silently alter results that use them. Reporting band-limited and artifact-channel accuracy, the frequency profile of accuracy and a measured detection floor would make positive and null results in this field considerably easier to interpret.
