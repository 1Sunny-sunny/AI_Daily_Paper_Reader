# Cross-subject decoding of imagined speech from scalp EEG: a leakage-controlled evaluation on three public corpora 

Blessing Isoyiza Adeika 1,∗ and Onyema Osuagwu 1  

> 1Morgan State University, Baltimore, Maryland, United States of America
> ∗Author to whom any correspondence should be addressed.

> Keywords: imagined speech, electroencephalography, cross-subject transfer, brain–computer interface, representational similarity analysis, negative results

Abstract 

Objective. A decoder of imagined speech that works for a new user without calibration would make a non-invasive speech interface practical. We tested whether imagined-speech content recorded with scalp electroencephalography (EEG) transfers across people, and examined why accuracy within a single person remains low. Approach. We analyzed three public corpora, ds003626, KaraOne and Track 3 of the 2020 International Brain–Computer Interface Competition, under a protocol that enforces split integrity by assertion and reports exact binomial thresholds, group tests and bootstrap confidence intervals. We trained decoders on each participant and tested them on every other, compared representational geometry across participants without a classifier, applied subject-level alignment, and tested five explanations for the within-subject ceiling. Main results. 

Across 482 ordered pairs of participants, no pair survived correction for multiple comparisons; mean pair-transfer accuracy lay within 0.006 of chance on every corpus, while every within-subject reference lay above it. Imagery against pre-stimulus rest generalized at about 0.61 after artifact and elapsed-time controls, but its decoder labelled the interval preceding imagery as imagery as often as imagery itself, so the contrast reflects a post-stimulus state, not imagery. Within subject, only the competition corpus decoded above threshold for every participant (0.3147; chance 0.200); syllable count explained little of this, but its frequency profile failed an artifact audit. Significance. 

In these corpora, imagined-speech content did not transfer between people through any representation we examined. Trial count per class was the only variable that ordered the corpora, which favors larger single-condition datasets over further model development. 

# 1 Introduction 

Most brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) must be calibrated for each user, because the signals they rely on differ between people and between sessions [1, 2]. Transfer learning aims to reduce that burden by reusing data from other users [3, 4]. For speech the stakes are high. Intracranial speech neuroprostheses now reach high accuracy after short calibration [5, 6], whereas scalp electroencephalography (EEG), the only modality that could reach users without surgery, has produced imagined-word decoding that remains mostly at or near chance when corpora are re-evaluated under a common protocol [7, 8, 9]. A decoder that generalized across people would indicate that imagined speech leaves a shared, measurable trace at the scalp; one that did not would locate the difficulty in the signal itself, not in the absence of calibration data. Direct tests of cross-subject transfer for imagined speech are scarce. A recent benchmark of pretrained EEG models on the 2020 BCI Competition data found every model at chance when the test participant was held out [10], and a cross-dataset evaluation found within-category decoding mostly at or near chance even within subject [9]. A failure of leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) decoding is, however, ambiguous. Participants may share a representational geometry that pooling across them blurs into noise, in which case selecting compatible source participants or aligning their data could succeed [11, 12, 13]. Alternatively, nothing may be shared, in which case no alignment or adversarial adaptation [14] can create transfer, and attempts to force it risk negative transfer [15]. Distinguishing the two requires testing transfer between individual participants and comparing representations without relying on a classifier. The within-subject ceiling raises a parallel question. Accuracies close to chance within one person admit several explanations: too many features for the available trials, a representation that 1arXiv:2610.20954v1 [eess.SP] 14 Oct 2026 misses cross-channel structure, an analysis window that dilutes a brief effect, too little training data, or a model that is too weak. Each makes a different prediction, and following the logic of strong inference [16], we gave each an experiment that could overturn it. We report these tests on three public corpora under a single leakage-controlled protocol. The protocol, its artifact audit of the positive controls available in these corpora, and the detection floors that bound the null results reported here are described in a companion paper [17]. Here we ask whether imagined-speech content transfers between participants, whether any shared structure is detectable without a classifier, whether subject-level alignment changes the answer, why within-subject accuracy is low, and whether phonological features generalize to prompts not seen in training. 

# 2 Related work 

## 2.1 Reported performance on these corpora 

Each of the three corpora comes with a published benchmark. KaraOne was designed around phonological minimal pairs, and its authors reported up to 90% accuracy for binary consonant-versus-vowel classification of imagined phonemes, within subject, using deep belief networks [18]; a hierarchical deep network later reached a mean of 83.42% over six binary phonological contrasts [19]. On the four-class inner-speech task of ds003626, EEGNet reached 29.7% [20] and a bidirectional recurrent network 36.1% [21], against a chance level of 25%. In Track 3 of the 2020 BCI Competition, which comprises five imagined phrases, the leading entries reached 82.6% and 75.5% [22]. Pooling pronounced and imagined trials has been reported to improve within-subject decoding of some imagined word pairs [23]. Reviews of the field describe a wide range of results that are hard to compare across studies because paradigms, vocabularies and evaluation schemes differ [7, 8, 24, 25]. Re-evaluations that apply a common protocol report much lower figures. When seven decoders were compared on nine corpora under one nested cross-validation scheme, decoding within a category of imagined speech was mostly indistinguishable from chance, and the best pairwise balanced accuracy, 62.7%, was obtained on the 2020 competition data [9]. On the same data, a benchmark of five models, including two pretrained EEG foundation models, reported within-subject balanced accuracies of 25.7 to 30.2% for five classes and chance for every model under LOSO evaluation [10]. 

## 2.2 Why reported accuracies diverge 

Several mechanisms can raise accuracy without speech decoding. When segments of one recording are distributed across training and test sets, a classifier can learn to recognize the recording instead of the class, and segment-wise splitting has been shown to inflate deep-learning accuracy on EEG substantially [26]. Feature selection or model tuning performed outside cross-validation, and selection on the test data themselves, bias results in the same direction [27, 28, 29, 30]. In block designs, slow drift in the recording becomes a proxy for the experimental condition [31], a hazard that applies to the condition axis of ds003626 [17]. Confounds correlated with the label are a further route: in KaraOne, the prompt type alone, word versus phoneme, decodes at 0.8371 with all 14 participants above threshold in our data, so any contrast whose classes differ in their proportion of words can succeed on duration and prompt type without phonological information. Restricting KaraOne to its seven phonemes is the corresponding control: it reduced the effect in the 2 s interval that precedes imagery by 53% and removed an imagined-speech effect entirely (a gain over chance of +0.0288, p = 0.0011, became −0.0015 with no participant above threshold, p =0.514). Finally, with the small test sets typical of these corpora, theoretical chance levels are misleading and single cross-validated estimates carry wide error bars [32, 33]. 

## 2.3 Cross-subject transfer 

Transfer learning in BCIs has been reviewed extensively [3, 4], and inter-subject variability is recognized as its central obstacle [2]. Euclidean alignment whitens each participant’s trials by their mean spatial covariance [11, 34], Riemannian alignment re-centers each participant’s covariance matrices at their geometric mean [12], and Procrustes analysis extends re-centering with stretching and rotation [13]. These methods presuppose that participants share class structure that differs only by a transformation; when that premise fails, transfer can reduce accuracy instead of improving it [15]. Representational similarity analysis tests the premise directly by comparing the geometry of class representations across individuals without a decoder [35]. 

## 2.4 Representations beyond band power 

Covariance-based classifiers on the Riemannian manifold are among the most reliable decoders for small EEG datasets [36, 37, 38]. Convolutional networks designed for EEG, such as EEGNet and ShallowConvNet, learn spatial and temporal filters from raw signals [39, 40], and attention-based architectures such as EEG Conformer extend them [41]; systematic reviews note, however, that reported gains are often hard to reproduce [42]. Foundation models pretrained on large EEG collections aim to transfer across tasks and people [43, 44], but did not show a consistent advantage on speech [10]. Intracranial recordings point to low-frequency power and cross-frequency coupling as informative features for imagined speech [45], which motivates phase-based measures such as the weighted phase-lag index and phase–amplitude coupling [46, 47, 48], provided they are computed in forms that are robust to volume conduction and the reference [49, 50]. 

# 3 Materials and methods 

## 3.1 Corpora and tasks 

Table 1 lists the decoding tasks. The recordings, their trial structure, the preprocessing and the corrections required to read ds003626 correctly are described in the companion paper [17]; in brief, ds003626 is analyzed with the corrected condition mapping (code 1 is inner speech) over the 1.0–3.5 s action interval [51, 52], KaraOne over the imagery interval using the 62 scalp channels re-referenced to their own mean [18], and the competition data over the whole 3.1 s epoch [22]. Each KaraOne trial passes through four states: rest, a stimulus state in which the prompt is shown and played and which ends with a 2 s period in which participants move their articulators into position, imagined speech, and speaking aloud [18]. For comparison we also analyzed the 2.0 s interval between rest and imagery, which we call the pre-imagery interval; it contains perception of the prompt and preparation to articulate it, but neither imagery nor speech. The recordings’ segment annotations list this interval among their speaking segments, so it is easily mistaken for spoken speech [17]. KaraOne’s spoken productions, which follow imagery, were not analyzed. Unless stated otherwise, data were band-pass filtered at 1–45 Hz and resampled to 128 Hz. 

## 3.2 Protocols and decoders 

Every split was generated by one module and checked by an assertion that halts the run if a trial appears in more than one set, if the test participant appears in training data under LOSO, or if a test class is absent from training [17]. Within subject we report stratified 5-fold cross-validation, which pools sessions and is reported because the literature uses it, together with a held-out protocol that tests on an unseen session (ds003626) or an unseen contiguous block of trials (KaraOne and the competition corpus). Across participants we report LOSO evaluation. No selection signal was taken from test data. The reference decoder is L2-regularized multinomial logistic regression on the logarithm of band power per channel in six bands from 1 to 45 Hz, with the regularization fixed [53]. We chose this 3feature set before running any analysis and use it as the fixed comparator against which every alternative is judged. Comparators were EEGNet with 16 temporal filters and a depth multiplier of 4 [39]; Riemannian classifiers (minimum distance to mean, its geodesically filtered variant, and tangent-space logistic regression) with optional principal component reduction and per-participant re-centering [54, 36, 37, 55]; ShallowFBCSPNet and EEG Conformer from the Braindecode library, version 1.2.0 [56, 40, 41]; and logistic regression on phase, connectivity and coupling features [46, 47]. The two Braindecode models were used at their published default hyperparameters, except that ShallowFBCSPNet’s dropout was set to 0.3, the value used for every network in this study; on the ds003626 input shape they have 208,324 and 488,132 parameters. All networks were trained with the Adam optimizer, a learning rate of 10 −3, weight decay of 10 −4, batches of 32 and at most 100 epochs, with early stopping on a validation set drawn from training data. The detection floors of these decoders, measured by injecting effects of known size into the same recordings, are reported in the companion paper [17]. 

## 3.3 Analyses 

Content decoding. Each corpus was decoded with the reference decoder under all three protocols. Pairwise word separability was measured on matched 128 Hz, 1–45 Hz data in three bands (8–13, 8–30 and 30–45 Hz); for the competition corpus each pair was also decoded in a 62–100 Hz band and in the 8–30 and 62–100 Hz bands combined, from a 256 Hz version of the data. To test whether utterance length could explain separability on the competition corpus, its pairs were grouped by whether the two phrases have the same number of syllables (one for “stop” and “yes”, two for “hello”, “help me” and “thank you”), which gives four matched and six mismatched pairs; matched and mismatched accuracies were averaged per participant and compared with a paired one-sided Wilcoxon test. 

Subject-level alignment. Euclidean alignment was computed transductively from all of a participant’s trials and inductively from their first 10, 20 or 40 trials, and Riemannian alignment re-centered each participant’s covariances at their Riemannian mean. Every alignment was fitted per participant from that participant’s own unlabeled trials, including the held-out participant, with no shared reference; these runs are flagged as using unlabeled target data. 

Pair transfer and representational similarity. For every ordered pair of participants we trained on one and tested on the other, so that the test participant contributed nothing to fitting. The within-subject reference on the diagonal was computed by 5-fold cross-validation. For the classifier-free comparison, each participant’s representational dissimilarity matrix was built from Riemannian distances between class-mean covariance matrices [35, 37], and matrices were compared across participant pairs with Spearman correlation. The null distribution permuted class labels within each participant 200 times, which destroys class structure while preserving each participant’s covariance scale and channel geometry. 

State versus content. On KaraOne we decoded imagery against rest, using the re-referenced scalp channels. Because rest always precedes the stimulus and imagery follows it, the contrast is entangled with elapsed time; we therefore compared rest and imagery windows at different separations and trained and tested on different halves of each session. Splits were grouped by trial so that a trial’s rest and imagery segments never fell into different folds. To separate imagery from what follows the stimulus, we cut the pre-imagery interval from the same recordings with the same preprocessing, taking it directly as the gap between the end of rest and the start of imagery. We decoded each pair of the three windows (rest, −2.0 to 0.0 s; pre-imagery, 0.0 to +2.0 s; imagery, from a median of +2.03 s for 2.0 s) under LOSO, and applied the rest-versus-imagery decoder, trained on the other participants, to all three windows of the held-out participant. A decoder that reads imagery should label the pre-imagery interval as rest. 

Explanations for the within-subject ceiling. We tested five explanations against the reference decoder, each with a paired per-participant test and Benjamini–Hochberg correction within its family. (i) Upstream failure: the same decoder was applied to the three-way condition contrast of ds003626, balanced per participant and session. (ii) Dimensionality: a single 8–30 Hz band, principal component projections to 16, 32 and 64 components fitted on training data, an anatomical speech-motor region, and cropped training that multiplies training rows fivefold. (iii) Missing cross-channel structure: 23 Riemannian configurations combining spatial reduction, filter banks, re-centering and the three classifiers. (iv) The analysis window: 500 ms windows in 50 ms steps, tested with a cluster-based permutation over time [57]. ## 3.4 Statistical analysis 

Each result carries four statistics: the smallest accuracy that is significant for a single participant under the exact binomial distribution at α = 0 .05 [32] and the number of participants who reach it; a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test of participant accuracies against chance [58]; and a 95% confidence interval (CI) on the mean from a bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap [59]. Where several related tests were made, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [60]; q denotes the adjusted p-value. Every positive result reported here was also checked with a shuffle-label control in which labels were permuted within each participant and the identical pipeline re-run (table 2); the result stands only if the null mean is not above chance and the observed value exceeds the 95th percentile of the null. Two features of the null distributions matter for interpretation. Cross-subject nulls fall on chance (0.4988 to 0.4999 here, and 0.4999 on average across 40 further cells), whereas within-subject nulls fall slightly below it (0.3317 against 0.3333, and 0.1969 and 0.1959 against 0.2000, with a mean of 0.4910 against 0.500 across 40 further binary cells). Training on permuted labels within a participant does not simply remove signal; it teaches the model a mapping that is anti-correlated with the held-out fold. Empirical p-values are therefore anti-conservative within subject, and we use the binomial threshold and the group test to decide whether accuracy exceeds chance, reserving the shuffle control for the separate question of whether the pipeline manufactures signal. The three-way condition result is 0.6065 in table 2 and 0.6079 in section 4.6 because the control averages over folds, which weights sessions equally, whereas section 4.6 averages over participants.

## 4.2 Within-subject decoding on the competition corpus and pairwise separability 

The competition corpus is the exception within subject: 0.3273 with sessions pooled and 0.3147 with a block held out, with all 15 participants above threshold in the latter. Decomposed into its ten phrase pairs, every pair separated above chance within subject and survived FDR correction (table 4, figures 1 and 2). Three observations limit how this result can be read. First, the effect is present in the mu band (0.5577), which lies below the range where muscle activity dominates scalp recordings [61, 62], but it grows with frequency and is largest in the bands most exposed to muscle; the highest single cell on this corpus, 0.3815 for five-class decoding in 62–100 Hz, belongs to the artifact audit of the companion paper [17]. Second, the effect does not replicate on the other corpora: the upper limits of their participant-level intervals in the mu band, 0.5268 for ds003626 and 0.5405 for KaraOne, lie below the competition corpus’s point estimate of 0.5577. The intervals themselves overlap, so this does not exclude an effect of the same size, and differences in trial count, language and vocabulary would confound any direct comparison. ## 4.4 Pair transfer and representational similarity 

Training on one participant and testing on another, for every ordered pair, gave 210 pairs on the competition corpus, 90 on ds003626 and 182 on KaraOne (table 6). Not one of the 482 pairs survived Benjamini–Hochberg correction within its corpus. Mean pair-transfer accuracy lay within 0.006 of chance on every corpus ( −0.0004, −0.0052 and +0.0027), whereas every within-subject reference lay above chance (+0.1273, +0.0095 and +0.0486). On the competition corpus, where every participant decodes above threshold within subject, the distribution of pair-transfer accuracies is centered on chance and does not overlap most of the within-subject values (figure 3). Representational similarity between participants did not exceed its null on any corpus (table 6). Shared geometry was therefore not merely unexploited by our decoders; it was undetectable by a measure that uses no decoder. A decoder that barely decodes its own participant cannot show that nothing is shared, only fail to show that something is. We tested this directly by asking whether participants who decode their own data best also produce the decoders that transfer best. They did not (figure 4): the Spearman correlation between within-subject accuracy and mean accuracy on other participants was +0.073 (p = 0.80) on the competition corpus, +0.139 ( p = 0.70) on ds003626 and +0.004 ( p = 0.99) on KaraOne, and correlations with the accuracy that other participants’ decoders achieve on each participant were likewise null. The strongest version of the claim rests on the competition corpus, whose within-subject reference lies 0.1273 above chance while transfer is flat. ## 4.5 A post-stimulus state generalizes, content does not 

On KaraOne, imagery against rest decoded across participants, and unlike the content results it survived its controls. With all 68 stored channels the contrast reached 0.9802 under LOSO with band power, and 0.88 to 0.92 with EEGNet, but most of that accuracy came from non-cortical channels carried by the common average reference [17]. On the 62 scalp channels re-referenced to their own mean it reached 0.6515 (threshold 0.514, 12 of 14 participants, p = 0.00074, 95% CI [0.612, 0.694]), and no single band reproduced this value. The first explanation, a failure upstream of the decoder, was not supported. Applied to the three-way condition contrast of ds003626 (5,640 trials), balanced per participant and session (an unbalanced analysis would reach 0.4035 by predicting the majority class, against a nominal chance of 0.3333), the same pipeline reached 0.6079 with all ten participants above threshold ( p = 0.0010) under leave-one-session-out evaluation. Epoching, labels and windows therefore carry decodable structure. Because condition in this corpus is confounded with position within the session [17], this result shows that the pipeline can read structure the recordings contain, not that it reads speech. The second, too many features for the available trials, was not supported either. Under the held-out protocols, training folds contained about 265, 150 and 33 rows against 384, 768 and 372 features. Across a family of 20 reductions (a single 8–30 Hz band with six times fewer features, principal component projections to 16, 32 and 64 components, an anatomical speech-motor region, and cropped training with five times more rows), seven cells reached adjusted significance and all seven were losses. On the competition corpus, projection to 16 components cut the feature count 24-fold and raised the ratio of training rows to features from 0.69 to 16.67, the highest in the grid, and produced the lowest accuracy on that corpus (0 of 15 participants improved, q = 0.013). The third, that per-channel power misses cross-channel structure, was tested with 23 Riemannian configurations on the same folds. None outperformed band power and four were significantly worse. Contrary to our expectation, the least constrained configuration, tangent-space classification of the full-montage covariance with 2,080 dimensions from 267 training rows, was the best Riemannian configuration on the competition corpus, and every principled reduction was worse; its advantage over band power was not significant (11 of 15 participants, q = 0.40). The fourth, that the fixed window dilutes a brief effect, was also not supported (figure 5). The best 500 ms window, selected on the data it was scored on and therefore an optimistic estimate, reached 0.2810, 0.2758 and 0.2631 on the three corpora, which is −0.0337, −0.0030 and +0.0048 relative to the full window. On the competition corpus a single cluster spanned the entire epoch (0.00–3.10 s, p = 0.0001) and every window decoded above chance on its own, so the effect is sustained and integrating over the full trial is appropriate. ## 4.7 Phonological features and held-out prompts 

Holding out prompts instead of participants asks whether a decoder has learned a phonological feature or has memorized the prompts. A decoder trained on nasality over nine prompts and tested on two it has never seen cannot rely on prompt identity. Table 9 reports all five contrasts. Only one cell survived correction, the /iy/ contrast over all 11 prompts (0.5883, 12 of 14 participants above threshold), and its own control accounts for it. Restricted to the seven phonemes, the same contrast gave 0.5117 with 4 of 14 participants and p = 0.38, despite retaining 12 valid hold-outs. The /iy/ contrast is the only one whose positive class consists entirely of phonemes (/diy/, /iy/, /piy/, /tiy/), while 42.9% of its negative class are phonemes, an imbalance of 57.1 percentage points in prompt type, against 16.7 to 21.4 points for the other contrasts (supplementary table S3). Since prompt type alone decodes at 0.8371 on KaraOne, a contrast with this imbalance can exceed chance without phonological information, and removing the words removed the effect. The unseen-participant form behaved the same way (0.5504, q = 0.074). No phonological feature in KaraOne generalized to a prompt the decoder had not seen. The /uw/ contrast has a single positive prompt among the phonemes, so its phoneme-only form cannot be constructed and is absent rather than null.

# 5 Discussion 

Across three corpora, imagined-speech content did not transfer between participants, and the pair-transfer and representational-similarity results indicate why pooled cross-subject decoding fails. If participants shared a class geometry that pooling blurred, some pairs of individuals would transfer, good source participants would transfer better than poor ones, and representational dissimilarity matrices would correlate across participants. None of these held. Source selection therefore has nothing to select from, and alignment or adversarial adaptation lacks the shared class structure it presupposes [12, 11, 14]; forcing agreement between representations that share no class structure would be expected to produce negative transfer [15]. This conclusion is strongest on the competition corpus, where within-subject decoding is robust while transfer is flat, and it applies to the representations computed here. It does not exclude shared structure in a representation we did not compute. The one contrast that did generalize, imagery against rest on KaraOne, was not specific to imagery. A decoder trained on it labelled the interval before imagery, in which participants perceive the prompt and prepare to articulate it, as imagery as often as imagery itself. The contrast therefore reads a change of state that follows the stimulus. Before a state-versus-rest result is attributed to imagery, it should be compared with a post-stimulus interval that contains no imagery, as was possible here. The null results are bounded. The companion paper shows that the band-power decoder used as the reference detects an injected mu and beta desynchronization of 0.54 μV on ds003626 under a pre-specified criterion, and that the phase features detect lagged mixing and coupling of 5 to 10% [17]. The same study identifies what the bounds do not cover: amplitude- and covariance-based decoders did not detect class-dependent phase locking at any injected size, and an effect whose phase is tied to an internal event with variable timing would not be captured by phase measured at a fixed latency. A reader who suspects that imagined-speech content is present in these recordings would need to locate it in an effect of those types, or below those amplitudes. The within-subject ceiling is low for a reason that no representation addressed. Reducing dimensionality lowered accuracy, including on the corpus with the most statistical power, covariance features were indistinguishable from band power, shorter windows did not help, and two published deep architectures did not exceed the linear baseline. Two of these outcomes were the opposite of our expectations: the most constrained feature set performed worst, and the least constrained Riemannian configuration performed best. The one variable that ordered the three corpora was the number of trials per class, from 12 to 80 per participant, and adding trials from other conditions of the same words did not substitute for it. The most direct route to a better within-subject ceiling is therefore more trials of the target condition per participant, or methods that extract more from few trials than any tested here. Our results are consistent with the independent re-analyses. On the competition corpus, our within-subject figures of 0.2980 to 0.3273 with sessions pooled (8–30 Hz band power and the six-band reference, respectively) and 0.3147 with a block held out are comparable to or above the 25.7 to 30.2% reported for five models under a within-subject protocol, and both studies find chance under LOSO [10]. They are also consistent with the conclusion that within-category imagined-speech decoding is mostly at or near chance [9]. The much higher accuracies reported in earlier work on these corpora [18, 19, 22] were obtained with different protocols, feature engineering and task framings, and we cannot fully account for the difference. Our own strongest binary result 14 on KaraOne, the /iy/ contrast over all 11 prompts (0.5883 with the test prompts held out, table 9), was explained by prompt type, and binary KaraOne results reported without a phoneme-only control may reflect the same confound. The study has several limitations. Within-subject figures with sessions pooled are optimistic: holding out a session or run changed the ceiling by +0.0162 on average across six corpus and feature-set combinations, with the largest reduction, 0.0402, on KaraOne (supplementary table S5); for ds003626 inner speech the change was small or reversed, so that null does not depend on session leakage. The analysis windows were fixed per corpus, although time-resolved decoding showed no better window. The deep architectures were trained from scratch on each corpus at their published settings without tuning, so they test architecture and not scale, and we did not evaluate foundation models pretrained on large EEG collections. Finally, no window in these paradigms isolates imagery: the interval before imagery on KaraOne combines perception of the prompt with preparation to articulate it, so the comparison of that interval with imagery is confounded with both and with time in the trial. 

# 6 Conclusions 

Under a leakage-controlled protocol on three public corpora, imagined-speech content recorded with scalp EEG did not transfer between individual participants, no shared representational geometry was detectable without a classifier, and subject-level alignment did not change this. A contrast between imagery and rest did generalize, but the interval before imagery carried the same distinction, so it reflects a state that follows the stimulus rather than imagery. Within subject, accuracy was above chance for every participant on only one corpus, where it did not pass an artifact audit; of five explanations for the low ceiling, four were excluded by experiment, and the fifth, the number of trials per class, was the only variable that ordered the corpora. These results locate the main obstacle to calibration-free imagined-speech decoding from the scalp in the signal and the amount of data per condition rather than in the choice of decoder.

# S2. Multi-condition training on ds003626 

The test set contains inner-speech trials only, and the split is drawn on inner trials alone, so it is identical to the baseline’s. Additional trials were taken only from sessions already present in the training fold; adding them from the held-out session would have supplied that session’s electrode placement and drift. Arms were run separately because the pronounced condition is dominated by muscle activity (companion paper), so any gain would have needed to be attributed to a source.
