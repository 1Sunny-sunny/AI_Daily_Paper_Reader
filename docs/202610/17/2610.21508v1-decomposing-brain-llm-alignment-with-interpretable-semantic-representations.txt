# Decomposing Brain-LLM Alignment with Interpretable Semantic Representations 

Camila Kolling Mariya Toneva 

Max Planck Institute for Software Systems (MPI-SWS) 

{ckolling, mtoneva}@mpi-sws.org 

Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) predict neural responses during naturalistic lan-guage comprehension, but the linguistic information underlying this brain align-ment remains difficult to interpret. We use SCRIBE, an interpretable contextual semantic representation spanning 65 neurocognitively grounded semantic prop-erties, as a lens for decomposing the brain alignment of one LLM, LLaMA2-7B. Using variance partitioning between LLaMA2-7B and SCRIBE on naturalistic fMRI data from seven participants listening to narrative stories (Moth Radio Hour), we separate brain-predictive variance shared between the two representations from variance unique to each. We find that shared variance is the dominant component across the whole brain and language-related regions, while both representations also retain significant unique contributions. We then examine whether the con-creteness of individual semantic properties relates to shared and SCRIBE-unique brain-predictive variance. Concrete properties show greater shared variance with LLaMA2-7B than abstract properties across the whole brain and most examined regions, whereas abstract properties do not show greater SCRIBE-unique variance. Finally, property ablations reveal that semantic contributions extend across multiple dimensions, with cumulative effects on both shared and SCRIBE-unique variance. Together, these results show that, for LLaMA2-7B on this dataset, a substantial portion of brain–LLM alignment is associated with interpretable semantic informa-tion, while revealing complementary brain-predictive semantic information beyond the LLaMA2-7B representation. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) provide powerful models of language and have been shown to predict neural responses during naturalistic language comprehension [11, 9]. Yet, what linguistic information drives this brain-LLM alignment remains unclear [8, 5]. Because LLM representations jointly encode semantic, syntactic, and other linguistic information, brain predictivity alone does not establish which aspects of language underlie this alignment. Interpretable semantic representations provide a way to isolate and characterize its semantic compo-nent. Rather than representing meaning as a high-dimensional distributed embedding, feature-based approaches describe concepts along explicit semantic dimensions grounded in cognitive and neurobio-logical theories. SCRIBE [5] extends this approach to naturalistic language: it uses LLMs as semantic annotators to map words in context onto a fixed set of 65 interpretable semantic properties spanning perceptual, motor, spatial, temporal, social, cognitive, and affective dimensions. This provides an explicit semantic basis against which the brain-predictive information captured by LLMs can be compared.

Here, we use SCRIBE to decompose and characterize the semantic information underlying the brain alignment of LLaMA2-7B on the Moth Radio Hour fMRI dataset [6]. We perform variance partitioning between LLaMA2-7B and SCRIBE to separate brain-predictive information that is shared between the two representations from information unique to each. We then characterize these components at the level of semantic properties. Specifically, we (1) quantify how much of LLaMA2-7B’s brain alignment is shared with interpretable semantics, (2) test whether concrete and abstract semantic properties differ in their shared and SCRIBE-unique contributions, and (3) estimate, through systematic ablations, the unique contribution of each property given all others, as well as the contribution of cumulative sets of properties. We focus on one LLM and one dataset for comparability with prior work (limitations in Section 5). Together, these analyses move beyond measuring whether LLMs align with the brain toward identifying what interpretable semantic information underlies that alignment. 

2 Related Work 

Brain alignment of language models. Contextual language-model representations predict neural responses during language comprehension [11, 9, 10], with brain predictivity generally increasing with model scale and performance [1]. Recent work has moved beyond overall predictivity toward identifying the linguistic information underlying this correspondence, including the contribution of syntactic information to brain–LLM alignment [8]. 

Interpretable semantic representations. Feature-based semantic models represent meaning through explicit, cognitively grounded dimensions and have been used to characterize semantic organization and predict neural responses [2, 7, 4]. SCRIBE [5] extends this framework to naturalistic language by using LLMs as contextual semantic annotators, producing interpretable semantic representations that predict brain activity beyond existing interpretable stimulus features. Our work connects these two lines of research by directly decomposing the neural variance captured by LLM and interpretable semantic representations. 

3 Methodology 

3.1 Representations Interpretable semantic representations. We use SCRIBE [5] to obtain contextual and interpretable semantic representations of the language stimuli. SCRIBE represents each content-bearing word along 65 neurocognitively grounded semantic properties spanning sensory, motor, spatial, temporal, social, cognitive, and affective domains [2]. For each word, semantic property scores are estimated conditioned on its linguistic context and averaged across an ensemble of four LLM annotators (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). SCRIBE scores have been validated against contextual semantic judgments from 921 human participants, reaching 75 –81% of the estimated human noise ceiling [5]. We use the best-performing configuration reported in [5], considering all semantic part-of-speech categories and a context window of k = 10 

tokens. 

LLM representations. We use contextual representations from LLaMA2-7B [12], extracted fol-lowing the feature extraction procedure of Antonello et al. [1]; we chose this model for direct comparability with this established procedure and with the LLaMA2-7B baselines used to evaluate SCRIBE [5]. We use layer 15 of 32 , an intermediate layer, because intermediate LLM layers are typi-cally among the most brain-predictive [11, 1]. For each stimulus word, we extract the contextualized hidden state given the preceding words of the story, using the standard sliding context window of this procedure ( 256 to 512 preceding words), and temporally align the resulting feature sequence with the fMRI responses. LLaMA2-7B thus has access to a much longer context than SCRIBE’s 

k = 10 -token window; we keep each representation in its standard configuration, so variance unique to LLaMA2-7B may partly reflect longer-range context (Section 4.1); matching the context lengths would isolate this contribution. 23.2 Brain encoding 

We evaluate brain alignment using the naturalistic fMRI dataset from the Moth Radio Hour [6], in which seven participants listened to autobiographical stories while undergoing fMRI. Word-level representations are temporally aligned with the fMRI signal using Lanczos interpolation and expanded with four delayed copies (1–4 TRs, approximately 2–8 s) to account for the hemodynamic response. For each voxel, we fit a ridge-regression encoding model using nine stories for training and evaluate predictions on a held-out story using Pearson correlation between predicted and observed responses. The ridge regularization parameter is selected per voxel through bootstrapped cross-validation on the training stories; in the property ablations, the splits are fixed across full and reduced models. Analyses are restricted to voxels with an estimated noise ceiling above 0.4. We report results across the whole brain and within predefined language-related ROIs, including the late language network, angular gyrus, lateral temporal cortex (LTC), and IFG/MFG. 

3.3 Variance partitioning

3.3.1 Semantic property analysis 

First, we use the concreteness norms of Brysbaert et al. [3], which rate approximately 40 , 000 English expressions on a five-point abstract–concrete scale. Ratings are available for 61 of the 65 SCRIBE properties; we select the 10 most concrete and 10 most abstract properties (Table 1, Appendix A) and fit separate SCRIBE and LLaMA2-7B+SCRIBE encoding models for each set, comparing their shared and SCRIBE-unique variance across participants. Second, we perform leave-one-property-out ablations across all 65 dimensions, retraining the SCRIBE and joint models after removing each property and ranking properties by their mean whole-brain decrease in shared and SCRIBE-unique variance. Because SCRIBE properties are correlated (Ap-pendix B), a leave-one-out decrease measures a property’s unique contribution given the other 64 ,not its overall importance. Both rankings are listed in Appendix C. From each ranking, we construct nested top-5, top-10 , and top-20 sets, yielding six ablation conditions. For each condition, we train reduced SCRIBE and joint encoding models after removing the corresponding properties from the full 65 -dimensional representation, recompute the variance partitioning, and compare the resulting shared and SCRIBE-unique components with the full model. Because US is small (Section 4.1), its percentage changes are unstable; we therefore rank and test on absolute decreases and summarize US

changes by the median percentage across participants.

Thus, a substantial portion of LLaMA2-7B–brain alignment overlaps with information represented along SCRIBE’s interpretable semantic dimensions: in the whole brain, LLaMA2-7B and SCRIBE alone explain r2 = 0 .039 and 0.028 (joint model: 0.039 ), and the shared component (0.028 ) corresponds to 71% of the variance explained by LLaMA2-7B and 98% of that explained by SCRIBE ( 69 –74% and 97 –98% across ROIs). The overlap was not complete: LLaMA2-7B retained significant unique variance across all regions (whole brain: r2 = 0 .011 , 29% of its explained variance), which may partly reflect its longer context; conversely, SCRIBE’s much shorter 10 -token context did not prevent nearly all of its explained variance from being shared with LLaMA2-7B. SCRIBE, in turn, retained a small but significant unique component (whole brain: r2 = 0 .0007 ) that was positive in all seven participants and exceeded the 95th percentile of its permutation null in every region, indicating a consistent rather than noisy contribution. 44.2 Concrete semantics are more strongly shared with LLaMA 

We next asked whether shared information differs with property concreteness, comparing repre-sentations built from the 10 most concrete and 10 most abstract SCRIBE properties (Figure 2a). A consistent difference emerged for shared variance: concrete properties yielded greater shared LLaMA2-7B–SCRIBE variance than abstract properties across the whole brain and in three of the four language-related ROIs ( p ≤ 0.016 ; 6–7 of 7 participants), with LTC showing the same direction without reaching significance ( p = 0 .19 ; 4 of 7 participants). This distinction did not extend to the information captured uniquely by SCRIBE. Contrary to our hypothesis, abstract properties did not explain significantly greater SCRIBE-unique variance than concrete properties in any region (all p ≥ 0.08 ; Figure 2a). Thus, concreteness distinguishes the semantic information SCRIBE shares with LLaMA2-7B, but not the semantic information SCRIBE contributes uniquely beyond it. 

4.3 Semantic contributions extend across multiple properties 

Individual properties had small unique effects: the largest whole-brain leave-one-out decrease in shared variance ( Number ) was about 3% of the shared component, and only 7 properties decreased it in all participants (Appendix C), as expected given the correlations among SCRIBE properties (mean 

|r| = 0 .23 , median variance inflation factor 5.3; Appendix B): when one property is removed, part of its information remains available through correlated properties. We therefore used the rankings to select property sets rather than to estimate individual importance, and tested whether the properties identified by the leave-one-out analysis have cumulative effects when removed together, removing the top 5, 10 , and 20 properties from the whole-brain shared and SCRIBE-unique rankings (Figure 2b). Properties selected from the shared ranking produced a clear cumulative reduction in shared LLaMA2-7B–SCRIBE variance: the decrease increased from the top-5 to the top-20 ablations across all regions, reaching approximately 25 –29% for the top-20 set. In the whole brain, these decreases exceeded the sum of the individual leave-one-out decreases (top-20 : 27% vs. 17% ), consistent with correlated properties carrying redundant information that is lost only when removed together. Thus, properties with individually small effects collectively have a substantial impact on shared semantic variance when removed together. The SCRIBE-unique component showed a similar cumulative pattern: removing properties selected from the unique ranking reduced unique variance increasingly as more properties were removed (whole-brain median 28 –66% ). Moreover, removing properties selected from the shared ranking also substantially reduced SCRIBE-unique variance (median 22 –60% ), consistent with the large overlap between the two rankings ( 14 of the top 20 properties; Appendix C). Every cumulative ablation significantly reduced both components in every region ( p ≤ 0.016 ; all seven participants in 57 of 60 

tests), so SCRIBE-unique effects are consistent despite the small size of this component. Together, both components show cumulative effects that are driven largely by a common set of semantic properties. 

5 Conclusion 

We used SCRIBE to dissect the semantic information underlying LLaMA2-7B–brain alignment in the Moth Radio Hour dataset. A substantial component of brain-predictive information is shared with interpretable semantics, while both representations retain complementary unique contributions; this shared information was greater for concrete than abstract properties across most regions, whereas concreteness did not explain SCRIBE-unique variance. Property ablations reveal contributions from multiple semantic dimensions, with cumulative effects for both shared and SCRIBE-unique variance. Together, these findings show how interpretable semantic representations can move beyond aggregate brain-prediction scores to characterize the information underlying brain–LLM alignment. 

Limitations. We study one LLM (LLaMA2-7B, one intermediate layer), one fMRI dataset, and seven participants; our conclusions are specific to this setting, and sensitivity to the choice of layer and generalization to newer or larger LLMs, whose brain alignment varies with scale [1], remain untested. Two of SCRIBE’s annotators (Llama-3.1-8B and -70B) share LLaMA2-7B’s model family; SCRIBE’s validation against human judgments (Section 3) indicates that the shared component reflects human-aligned semantics, though an LLM-specific contribution cannot be fully excluded. 5Acknowledgments

Figure 3 shows Pearson correlations between the 65 SCRIBE properties, computed on the TR-level features that enter the encoding models (all stories). The mean absolute correlation between properties is 0.23 (median 0.18 ); 10% of property pairs have |r| > 0.5 and 0.7% have |r| > 0.8, and the median variance inflation factor is 5.3 (maximum 15 .2, Human ; seven properties exceed 10 ). Properties form clusters of related dimensions, for example visual form ( Vision, Pattern, Shape, Complexity ), negative affect ( Unpleasant, Angry, Harm, Fearful ), social communication ( Social, Communication, Speech, Human ), motion ( Path, Biomotion, Motion, Fast ), and time ( Short, Time, Duration ). Several properties at the top of the leave-one-out rankings have highly correlated partners (e.g., Landmark –

Scene , r = 0 .82 ; Speech –Human , r = 0 .84 ). This redundancy is why leave-one-property-out decreases (Section 3.3.1) are small and should be read as unique contributions given the remaining properties: removing a property whose information is shared with correlated properties changes little, even if the property is brain-predictive on its own.
