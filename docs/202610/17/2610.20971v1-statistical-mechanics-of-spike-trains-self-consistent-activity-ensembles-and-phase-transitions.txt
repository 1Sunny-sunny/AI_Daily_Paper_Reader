(Dated: October 16, 2026) We introduce a statistical-mechanical framework for spiking neuronal systems in which entire spike trains, defined as spatiotemporal configurations of discrete events, are treated as microstates of the system. Instead of modeling explicit network dynamics, we construct an ensemble over complete spike histories and assign to each configuration an effective energy functional that quantifies its consistency with intrinsic synaptic interactions. This formulation leads to a Boltzmann-like distribution over spike configurations, where an effec-tive temperature captures the influence of external inputs acting as a stochastic drive. Macroscopic states are described in terms of coarse-grained observables, such as population firing-rate densities, and arise from a competition between energetic contributions favoring interaction-consistent spike patterns and entropic contributions favoring spatio-temporal disorder. Within this framework, we primarily characterize the transition between oscillatory and non-oscillatory activity. The non-oscillatory regime contains both broadly distributed, approximately uniform states and temporally localized collapsed states, whereas the oscillatory regime is charac-terized by repeated activity peaks. These regimes are diagnosed using order parameters derived from the population spike-rate density, including its entropy, temporal localization measures, and autocorrelation-based indicators. To complement the analytical treatment, we perform Monte Carlo simulations that sample the ensemble of admissible spike configurations using a MetropolisHastings scheme defined directly on spike trains. The simulations reveal the emergence of the predicted macroscopic regimes and provide a direct sampling-based probe of the free-energy landscape. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Spiking neuronal systems are usually described by specifying a dynamical rule for the evolution of neu-ronal states and then studying the spike (action poten-tial) trains generated by this rule. In such approaches, spikes appear as outputs of an underlying membrane or population dynamics. Here we take a complementary point of view. We treat complete spike trains themselves as the microscopic states of the system and define a sta-tistical ensemble directly on the space of such configu-rations. This trajectory-based perspective is inspired by probabilistic formulations of dynamical systems in which deterministic evolution laws are represented through en-sembles of evolution paths or histories [1]. In this formulation, a microstate is a spatiotemporal pattern of discrete spike events indexed by neuron iden-tity and time over a finite observation window. The net-work’s internal structure is encoded in an intrinsic re-sponse operator, which maps a spike configuration to the endogenous activation field generated through internal membrane-potential dynamics, synaptic weights, trans-mission delays, and response kernels. An effective energy functional then measures the consistency between the spikes present in the configuration and the activation field generated by the same configuration. Spike configura-tions are assigned lower energy when their events coincide with strong internally generated activation, while con-figurations that are poorly supported by the network’s intrinsic interactions are energetically penalized. This construction leads to a Boltzmann-like ensemble over complete spike histories. The inverse temperature controls the relative importance of endogenous network consistency and residual fluctuations attributed to exter-nal drive, channel noise, unobserved inputs, and other in-fluences not represented explicitly by the network model. In the low-temperature regime the ensemble concentrates on configurations that are self-consistent with the intrin-sic interactions, while in the high-temperature regime ad-missible spike configurations become increasingly domi-nated by entropy. Although the underlying neuronal dy-namics is open and driven, the present construction uses an equilibrium-like Boltzmann ensemble over complete spike histories. In this sense, it connects spiking net-work theory with the language of trajectory ensembles, maximum-entropy statistical mechanics, and disordered arXiv:2610.20971v1 [q-bio.NC] 14 Oct 2026 2interacting systems [2–4]. Macroscopic activity states are obtained by coarse-graining the trajectory ensemble. In particular, we use the population spike-rate density as a central observ-able and study its entropy, spectral content, and local-ization. The competition between the energy favour-ing interaction-consistent spike patterns and the en-tropy favouring temporal disorder gives rise to distinct macroscopic regimes. In the excitatory delayed networks analysed below these include temporally uniform states, delay-induced oscillatory states, and temporally localized discharge-like states. These regimes are related, at the phenomenological level, to asynchronous, rhythmic, poly-chronous, and synchronized population activity observed in spiking network models [5–8]. Our approach differs from traditional population-level mean-field descriptions, which usually derive determin-istic or low-dimensional evolution equations for macro-scopic observables such as firing rates or population volt-ages [9–12]. Here the ensemble is defined first at the level of complete spike histories, and mean-field theory is introduced only as a subsequent coarse-graining. This distinction is important: the microscopic object is not an instantaneous population dynamical state but a full trajectory, while the population-rate density is an observ-able extracted from the ensemble or a macrostate used in an approximate theory. The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II we intro-duce the general spike-train ensemble, define the energy functional, and describe two realizations of the intrin-sic response: a biologically motivated integrate-and-fire construction and a reduced discrete transfer-operator re-alization used in the Monte Carlo simulations. In Sec. III we derive a mean-field reduction in terms of population-rate macrostates. In Sec. IV we analyse the correspond-ing mean-field phases of excitatory delayed networks. In Sec. V we sample the underlying finite-size microscopic ensemble using a Metropolis–Hastings procedure on spike trains and compare the sampled regimes with the mean-field picture. In Sec. VI we outline how the same formal-ism can be inverted for connectivity inference. Sec. VII contains the discussion and conclusions. The central aim of this work is not to replace de-tailed dynamical models of spiking neurons, but to pro-vide a statistical-mechanical description of spike-train en-sembles complementary to them. The resulting frame-work places neural spike patterns in the language of mi-crostates, energies, entropies, free energies, and phase transitions, while retaining the explicit temporal struc-ture of complete spike histories. From this perspective, experimentally observed spike trains can be interpreted as samples from an underlying ensemble shaped by both intrinsic connectivity and external inputs. More gen-erally, by placing spike trains within the broader class of trajectory-based statistical systems, the approach es-tablishes a conceptual bridge between theoretical neuro-science and statistical physics, and suggests a route to-ward free-energy-based inference, model comparison, and data analysis for experimentally measurable neuronal ac-tivity [13, 14].   

> II. SPIKE-TRAIN ENSEMBLE: GENERAL FRAMEWORK AND SINGLE-NEURON REALIZATIONS A. Network model

We consider a population of N neurons whose con-nectivity is encoded by a directed, weighted graph G =(V, E, W, ∆) , where V = {1, . . . , N } is the set of neurons and E ⊆ V × V the set of directed synaptic connections. Each edge (i, j ) ∈ E carries a synaptic weight Wij ∈ R

(positive for excitation, negative for inhibition) and a non-negative transmission delay ∆ij ≥ 0. In addition to this connectivity structure, the graph is equipped with a single-neuron response rule specifying how incoming synaptic events are converted into an internal activation field and, in more detailed realizations, into membrane-potential dynamics. This rule may range from simple linear response kernels to explicit spiking-neuron models such as integrate-and-fire or more elaborate hybrid dy-namical systems [15]. We adopt the convention Wij = 0 

whenever (i, j ) /∈ E and write pre( j) = {i : ( i, j ) ∈ E} for the presynaptic set of neuron j. For any single realiza-tion of the model the graph G is treated as a quenched structure: the spike-train ensemble defined in Sec. II B is conditioned on G.The general framework accommodates an arbitrary di-rected graph and arbitrary real-valued weights. Two spe-cialisations of this structure recur throughout the paper, and we introduce them here for later reference. 

a. Random-graph ensemble. In both the analytical mean-field treatment (Secs. III–IV) and the Monte Carlo simulations (Sec. V), the graph itself is drawn from a sparse random-graph ensemble [16]: each ordered pair 

(i, j ) with i 6 = j is independently included in E with con-nection probability pconn , with self-loops excluded. Afresh realization of G is sampled for each Monte Carlo run. In the numerical phase map below, each point of the parameter grid is represented by one such quenched graph-and-delay realization and one finite Markov chain. Thus the plotted observables should be interpreted as single-realization finite-size samples rather than disorder averages at fixed parameter values. Disorder-averaged phase diagrams could be obtained by repeating the sim-ulations over many graph realizations for each parameter point, but this is not done in the present numerical study. 

b. Weight and delay distributions. Synaptic weights and delays are assigned from prescribed distributions 

PW and P∆, and are taken to be independent unless stated otherwise. The analytical mean-field treatment in Secs. III–IV and the Monte Carlo simulations in Sec. The simplest non-trivial numerical setting, adopted in our Monte Carlo simulations, is the homogeneous ex-citatory network with binary unit weights, PW (W ) =

> B. Microstates and the abstract ensemble

In the present framework, a spike is not modeled as a detailed voltage waveform or as the full biophysical process of action-potential generation. It is treated as a discrete event assigned to a neuron and to a time. Thus, a spike records the fact that neuron i emitted an ac-tion potential at time t, while the underlying waveform, ionic currents, and subthreshold membrane-voltage dy-namics are represented only implicitly through the in-trinsic response operator introduced below. This event-based viewpoint is therefore well suited to the trajectory ensemble: a microstate records which neurons spike and when, while the underlying voltage traces are represented only implicitly. It also reflects the form in which network activity is often accessed in modern electrophysiological experiments, where network activity is commonly anal-ysed in terms of detected spike events and population activity patterns [13]. > Relation to existing statistical-mechanical formulations

The present framework shares a maximum-entropy foundation with pairwise models of spike data [18–21]: in both cases, Jaynes’ principle leads to a Boltzmann distribution over neural activity patterns. Three differ-ences are worth emphasising. First, the microstate. In those models the microstate is an instantaneous binary vector σt ∈ { 0, 1}N over a single time bin, and the en-semble lives on instantaneous configurations; here the microstate is the full spatiotemporal spike train s over 

[0 , T ], and the ensemble lives on trajectories. Second, the constraints. Pairwise MaxEnt models constrain mea-sured firing rates and pairwise correlations, yielding an Ising-like Hamiltonian whose couplings are inferred from data; here the energy (4) is constructed from a known graph G and single-neuron rule, while the inverse prob-lem in Sec. VI treats connectivity parameters and a sep-arate inference-precision parameter as objects of estima-tion. Third, the role of time. The energy (4) couples spikes across time through the causal kernel η and the delays ∆ij ; the construction is therefore closer in spirit to the generating-functional / path-integral formulations of stochastic neural networks [22–24], with β playing a role analogous to a noise amplitude rather than a true thermodynamic temperature.

> V. MONTE CARLO SAMPLING OF THE MICROSCOPIC ENSEMBLE A. Purpose: testing the mean-field phase picture

The mean-field analysis of the previous sections pre-dicts three broad classes of macrostates: temporally uni-form states, periodic states selected by the delay kernel, and collapsed discharge-like states driven by the attrac-tive quadratic energy. These predictions are obtained after replacing the microscopic spike train by the pop-ulation density ρ(t), averaging over the random graph, linearizing the thresholding nonlinearity, and working with the rescaled mean-field inverse temperature βMF .The purpose of the Monte Carlo simulations is to return to the microscopic ensemble and test how these mean-field regimes are modified when the full discrete raster, quenched connectivity, refractory constraints, and non-linear thresholding are retained. In the Monte Carlo sampler the control parameter is the microscopic inverse temperature β of the discrete ensemble, not the rescaled mean-field parameter βMF . Since the spike count, synap-tic scale, and kernel normalization are fixed in each nu-merical sweep, varying β nevertheless varies the same ef-fective balance between endogenous self-consistency and entropy. Consequently, we compare the Monte Carlo and mean-field phase maps only at the level of qualitative regimes, without attempting a pointwise identification of 

β with βMF .

At small microscopic β, corresponding to a high-temperature or weak-structure regime of the sam-pled ensemble, spike events are distributed throughout the observation window and the population-rate profile remains broadly dispersed (Fig. 2C). This corresponds to the uniform macrostate of the mean-field theory. At larger β, or for delay distributions that favour strong re-current self-consistency, the sampled rasters become tem-porally localized (Fig. 2D-E). This realizes the collapse tendency predicted by the linearized quadratic mean-field energy. The distinction between oscillatory and non-oscillatory localized regimes is therefore not simply a matter of stronger or weaker localization. Rather, it is a distinction between temporally ordered localization, where activity forms repeated windows separated by a delay-selected period, and temporally disordered local-ization, where activity remains localized but lacks a reg-ular recurrence structure (compare Fig. 2 D3 vs E3). The former corresponds to the oscillatory-collapsed regime of the mean-field picture, while the latter is closer to a glassy non-oscillatory collapsed regime. The Monte Carlo phase map also shows systematic de-viations from the idealized mean-field prediction. The boundaries between regimes are broadened rather than sharp, as expected for finite systems and finite sampling times. Additional irregularity is introduced by the use of a single quenched graph-and-delay realization at each parameter point. More importantly, complete tempo-ral collapse is suppressed in the microscopic ensemble. Several mechanisms contribute to this effect. First, re-fractory exclusion is represented in the mean-field pic-ture only through a coarse temporal cutoff, whereas in the microscopic Monte Carlo ensemble it is imposed as a neuron-resolved hard constraint. This limits how densely spikes from the same neuron can be packed in time and suppresses the idealized complete temporal collapse al-lowed by the one-density mean-field description. Sec-ond, the nonlinear thresholding function introduces sat-uration: once a spike is sufficiently supported by con-verging presynaptic activity, additional presynaptic in-put no longer lowers the energy proportionally. Third, quenched graph disorder and finite connectivity preserve neuron-specific network structure that is absent from a description based only on ρ(t). These effects redistribute activity over several time bins, over multiple cycles of an oscillation, or over neuron-specific activity groups. This is consistent with the interpretation of the linearized col-lapse boundaries in Sec. IV as outer estimates: the lin-earized mean-field theory over-rewards large endogenous fields, while the microscopic simulations include satura-tion and hard admissibility constraints that reduce the tendency toward complete collapse. Thus the Monte Carlo simulations support the mean-field classification at the level of broad macroscopic regimes, but also identify the limitations of the annealed, linearized, one-density approximation. The mean-field theory captures the energetic tendency toward uniform, periodic, or collapsed temporal organization, whereas the microscopic ensemble reveals how this tendency is reshaped by refractory constraints, threshold satura-tion, finite-size fluctuations, and graph-specific structure. The formation of structured activity groups in the sam-pled trains is therefore not a failure of the statistical-mechanical framework, but rather a sign that the rele-vant macrostates cannot always be parametrized by the population-rate density alone.

> VII. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

We have introduced a statistical-mechanical frame-work in which complete spike trains, rather than under-lying neuronal state variables, constitute the microscopic degrees of freedom of the system. This perspective differs fundamentally from most existing approaches in theoret-ical neuroscience, which begin with dynamical equations for membrane potentials, conductances, adaptation vari-ables, or population rates and only subsequently derive spike statistics [9–12, 27–30]. Here, the spike train it-self is the primary object of the theory. To our knowl-edge, this trajectory-ensemble formulation represents a novel statistical-physics description of neuronal activity, allowing neural systems to be analyzed directly in terms of experimentally observable quantities. An important advantage of this construction is its direct applicability to experimental data measurable using modern setups. Internal neuronal state variables are generally inaccessi-ble in vivo and often only partially observable even in detailed electrophysiological preparations. In contrast, spike trains can be measured directly using multielec-trode arrays [14], calcium-imaging techniques, and mod-ern optogenetic approaches [13]. Because the formalism is defined on spike histories rather than on latent dynam-ical states, it naturally lends itself to data-driven analysis and inference without requiring reconstruction of unob-servable membrane or network variables [18–21]. Even though the energy functional depends on an assumed network model, the neuronal heat bath accommodates, to an extent, uncertainties about its parameters along with external interactions. Despite its abstraction, the framework captures several biologically relevant collec-tive phenomena. In particular, it predicts that, in purely excitatory systems, the competition between energetic 17 self-consistency and configurational entropy gives rise to temporally localized discharge-like states and population bursts. Such bursting phenomena are ubiquitous in neu-ral systems and are implicated both in physiological pro-cesses, including development and memory consolidation as well as pathological dynamics such as epileptic inter-ictal discharges and seizures [37–40]. The emergence of these states as distinct phases of the trajectory ensemble suggests that bursting may be understood as a generic collective phenomenon arising from the statistical orga-nization of spike trains. The present analysis is restricted to homogeneous ex-citatory delayed networks. Within this scope, the frame-work captures both delay-selected oscillatory organiza-tion and temporally localized discharge-like states as competing minima of the spike-train free-energy land-scape. This places population bursting, rhythmic tempo-ral order, and non-oscillatory localized activity within a common statistical-mechanical description of spike-train space. A notable feature of the Monte Carlo ensemble is the emergence of structured activity groups rather than com-plete temporal collapse. These activity clusters resem-ble neuronal assemblies and polychronous groups de-scribed previously in large-scale spiking network simu-lations, particularly those reported by Izhikevich [5, 6]. Their appearance indicates that finite-size effects, refrac-tory constraints, threshold nonlinearities, and quenched network structure can stabilize rich mesoscopic organiza-tion beyond the simplest mean-field picture. The repro-duction of such grouped activity patterns suggests that the proposed ensemble retains important features of bi-ologically realistic spiking networks despite its reduced description. From a broader neuroscience perspective, the observed transitions between asynchronous, oscilla-tory, and burst-like phases may be especially significant because neuronal systems are widely believed to oper-ate near dynamical bifurcations or critical phase tran-sitions [41–46]. A substantial body of theoretical and experimental work has suggested that proximity to crit-ical points can optimize information transmission, dy-namic range, sensitivity to stimuli, computational capac-ity, and flexibility of network responses [41–47]. Within the present framework, these notions acquire a natu-ral statistical-mechanical interpretation: different modes of neural computation correspond to distinct regions of the free-energy landscape, while changes in information-processing capabilities may be associated with proximity to phase boundaries, allowing for metastability. Perhaps the most striking conclusion emerging from the analysis is the central role played by synaptic delays. In the mean-field reduction, the effective delay kernel appears as an irreducible quantity governing the free-energy landscape. The locations and existence of phase transitions are de-termined primarily by the shape of this kernel rather than by microscopic details of individual neurons. Narrow de-lay distributions favor oscillatory states, whereas broader distributions promote temporally localized discharge-like states. While synaptic delays are known to influence syn-chronization, oscillations, and polychronization [6, 48– 50], our results suggest that they may constitute one of the fundamental organizing variables of collective neural activity and therefore deserve greater attention in both theoretical and experimental studies. Several limitations remain. The current analysis re-lies on simplifying assumptions, including weak-coupling approximations, homogeneous populations, and idealized delay distributions. Extending the framework to het-erogeneous cell classes including inhibitory interneurons, multi-class synaptic interactions, structured connectivity, plastic synapses, and experimentally inferred interaction kernels represents an important direction for future work. To conclude, statistical mechanics defined directly on spike-trains is sufficient to generate a rich phenomenology encompassing asynchronous activity, oscillations, neu-ronal assemblies, and bursting states. More generally, the framework establishes a bridge between modern statisti-cal physics and experimentally accessible neural activ-ity, providing a route toward ensemble-based inference, model comparison, and the characterization of phase transitions in neuronal systems.
