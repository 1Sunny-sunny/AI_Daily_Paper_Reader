# A hidden Markov model for unsupervised EEG-based tracking of auditory attention to a single voice 

Tianyi Li 1, Simon Geirnaert 1,3, Davina Van den Broek 2, Elien Bellon 2, Bert De Smedt 2 and Alexander Bertrand 1

Abstract —Objective: Absolute auditory attention decoding (aAAD) aims to track how strongly a listener attends to asingle speech source using electroencephalography (EEG). Unlike selective auditory attention decoding, aAAD lacks a competing speech reference, making reliable attention tracking challenging. Approach: We propose a fully unsupervised aAAD pipeline combining neural envelope tracking (NET) with hidden Markov model (HMM)-based temporal inference. Using EEG signals and the target speech envelope, the framework estimates a continuous attention trajectory without explicit attention-state labels. We evaluate the approach using EEG recordings from 30 adults, investigate the impact of analysis window length, and compare HMM post-processing with moving-average (MA) smoothing and alternative EEG features. Main results: With 1-s analysis windows, non-causal HMM post-processing improves the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for all 30 participants, increasing mean AUC from 0.547 for raw NET to 0.832. A 5-s window achieves the highest mean AUC of 0.858. Compared with non-causal MA smoothing, the HMM achieves higher AUC across the examined smoothing scales. Individual switch detection times are comparable, while the HMM generally achieves lower group-level detection times. NET also outperforms alpha power and spectral entropy as an observation feature in the unsupervised HMM framework. Significance: The proposed framework provides a label-free approach for estimating temporal fluctuations in auditory atten-tion, demonstrating the utility of probabilistic temporal inference for continuous attention tracking in single-speaker listening scenarios. 

Index Terms —Absolute auditory attention decoding, neural envelope tracking, hidden Markov model, unsupervised temporal inference, EEG.

Understanding how auditory attention evolves over time can therefore provide valuable insights into learning processes in the classroom and enable applications such as attention monitoring systems that track students’ engagement dynamics and provide student-specific feedback [3]. In the past decade, extensive research has demonstrated that auditory attention to natural speech can be decoded from EEG signals with stimulus reconstruction approaches [4]–[7]. Compared to commonly used EEG markers associated with attention such as alpha power [8], [9] and spectral entropy [10], neural envelope tracking (NET) has been regarded as a stimulus-specific metric of auditory attention, as it reflects neural responses to the speech signal itself rather than general mental effort [10]–[12]. To compute the NET metric, a neural decoder is trained to reconstruct the temporal envelope of the attended speech from EEG recordings, and the Pearson correlation between the reconstructed envelope and the corre-sponding speech envelope is used as a measure of auditory attention. This NET-based approach has been successfully ap-plied in selective auditory attention decoding (sAAD) [4]–[6] and absolute auditory attention decoding (aAAD) paradigms [11], [13]–[15]. In sAAD, attention is measured by comparing correlations across multiple competing speakers, with the speaker yielding the highest correlation identified as the attended one. In con-trast, aAAD considers scenarios in which only a single speech stream is present and aims to continuously track how attention to this target evolves over time. It directly uses the NET score as a measure of the degree of attention to the target speaker. However, as there is no relative reference from competing speakers to compare with, aAAD poses a fundamentally more challenging inference problem than sAAD, as attention must be estimated from a single, neural evidence stream. Moreover, instantaneous NET estimates are typically noisy, in particular when measured over short time intervals. They can also be influenced by confounding factors that are not attention-driven, such as temporal variations in the dynamic range of the speech envelope [13], [16]. As a result, raw NET values can be difficult to interpret directly on a window-by-window basis. Motivated by these limitations, it is therefore natural to post-process the NET score by treating the NET sequence as a time series and applying smoothing techniques that take temporal coherence into account. This is reasonable because attention states in neighboring windows are not ex-pected to be independent, especially when the window length arXiv:2610.21691v1 [eess.SP] 15 Oct 2026 2

is short. Instead, attention is likely to be piecewise stable in natural learning scenarios, as people are unlikely to switch attention every second and tend to remain focused on the target for at least several seconds, or even minutes [17]. In this work, we present a modular aAAD pipeline that combines NET-based evidence extraction with probabilistic temporal inference based on a hidden Markov model (HMM), a powerful framework that is able to infer attention states from NET observations, inspired by similar work in sAAD [18]. The proposed pipeline is designed for off-line analysis and is fully unsupervised (i.e., no attention labels are used for training), relying only on a small set of tunable and interpretable parameters. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion II introduces the proposed aAAD pipeline, including the NET-based evidence extraction module and the HMM-based temporal inference module. Section III describes the exper-imental protocol, EEG preprocessing procedure, evaluation metrics, and default parameter settings. Section IV presents the experimental validation of the proposed framework. We first demonstrate the added value of HMM post-processing com-pared with raw NET, and then analyze the effect of analysis window length on the area under the curve (AUC) and switch detection time (SDT). We further compare the HMM with moving-average (MA) smoothing to assess the advantage of state-based temporal inference over fixed-window averaging. Finally, we compare NET with alpha power and spectral entropy as observation features for the unsupervised HMM framework. Section V concludes the paper. II. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Pipeline Overview 

This section presents an overview of the proposed pipeline for off-line analysis of absolute auditory attention, which is conceptually visualized in Fig. 1. The pipeline operates in a fully unsupervised manner and is controlled by a small set of interpretable parameters. Starting from the input EEG recordings and the target speech signal, the proposed pipeline first performs EEG pre-processing and speech envelope extraction, followed by neural decoding to compute a windowed neural envelope tracking (NET) sequence, which serves as stimulus-specific evidence of attention to the target speech. Concretely, a linear spatio-temporal decoder is first trained to extract the neural responses to the target speech, thereby removing irrelevant EEG signal components. To this end, we use the so-called stimulus-reconstruction paradigm, which aims to reconstruct the speech envelope from the pre-processed EEG by solving a least-squares regression problem on all data. The trained decoder is then applied to the EEG to obtain a reconstructed envelope, which is compared with the target speech envelope using the Pearson correlation coefficient computed over multiple non-overlapping 1 windows. The resulting correlation values form the NET sequence. Although a linear least-squares decoder is used in this work, the proposed pipeline is not restricted to a 

> 1We use non-overlapping windows, as the HMM post-processing assumes independent observations.

specific decoding method, and any alternative linear or non-linear decoder (e.g., CCA-based [19] or neural network-based models [20], [21]) can be incorporated, as long as it provides a correlation-based neural tracking metric between EEG and the target stimulus. Next, an HMM is applied to the NET sequence—using either causal or non-causal inference—to explicitly model temporal dependencies and obtain a smoothed attention estimate in the form of the posterior probability of being attentive. This posterior probability is finally reported as the continuous attention score over time. Importantly, both the neural evidence extraction and the temporal inference stages operate without requiring attention-state labels.

2) Label-Free Decoder Training: In this work, the neural decoder is trained using all available data and subsequently applied to the same dataset to compute the Pearson correlation between the reconstructed and the true speech envelope on a window-by-window basis. This validation strategy differs from many previous studies [18], [20], [22], [23], where a strict train-test split is enforced. Nevertheless, we argue that it is appropriate for the present aAAD setting, as previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of unsupervised aAAD without a separate training dataset [24]. Specifically, the decoder is designed to capture the stimulus-response relationship between EEG signals and the speech en-velope, rather than to explicitly discriminate between attentive and inattentive states. Under this formulation, inattentive EEG does not correspond to a distinct neural pattern, but rather to a weaker or noisier manifestation of the same stimulus-driven neural response. Accordingly, we adopt the perspective that aAAD is better formulated as a signal matching problem with attention-dependent signal-to-noise ratios, rather than as a binary classification problem with separable EEG patterns. Within this framework, correlation-based evaluation pro-vides a meaningful measure of neural tracking strength, even when the decoder is applied to the same data used for training. This formulation constitutes one of the key ingredients that enables a fully unsupervised aAAD framework, as it allows stimulus-specific neural evidence to be extracted without re-quiring explicit attention labels. It should be noted that the training of the neural decoder requires sufficient baseline data during which the participant actually attends to the speech, in order to reliably estimate the stimulus–response mapping. If the available baseline segments are too limited in duration, the estimated decoder may become unstable, which can subsequently affect the quality of the neural tracking feature used for temporal inference. 

C. Temporal Inference Module 

Given a NET sequence (containing one NET value for each window t), we employ a hidden Markov model (HMM) as a temporal inference module to obtain a smooth estimate of the underlying attention dynamics, inspired by similar work in sAAD [18]. The HMM takes the NET sequence as its sole observation input and infers a latent attention state sequence that evolves over time. We note that the temporal inference module operates independently of how the NET sequence is obtained. Although NET is computed in this work using a linear neural decoding model, the HMM can in principle be applied to any stimulus-specific evidence sequence derived from linear or non-linear models [18].

B. Data Acquisition and Preprocessing 

EEG signals were recorded using a BioSemi Active Two system with 64 electrodes arranged according to the 10-20 system, at a sampling rate of 2048 Hz. Prior to analysis, the recordings were processed using a standard preprocessing pipeline. To remove slow drifts and very low-frequency components, we first applied a first-order infinite impulse response (IIR) high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.01 Hz. The data were then downsampled to 128 Hz to reduce computational load while preserving the relevant EEG information. The EEG signals were further filtered to the 0.1-4 Hz frequency band proposed in [12], [13], which corresponds to typical word-rate dynamics around 2.5 Hz. This band was extracted using a fourth-order IIR bandpass filter, after which the signals were downsampled to 16 Hz for subsequent processing. The speech envelope was adopted as the acoustic feature, motivated by previous findings showing phase-locked neural responses to speech envelope fluctuations [4], [5], [7], [10]– [13], [22]. Envelope extraction followed the procedure de-scribed in [22], which combines a gammatone filterbank with power-law compression to approximate cochlear frequency selectivity and nonlinear loudness perception. For consistency with the EEG processing, the extracted envelope was filtered using the same frequency band (0.1-4 Hz by default) and downsampled to 16 Hz. 

C. Evaluation Metrics 

In this work, performance is evaluated based on the ability of the resulting metrics (either the raw NET values or the HMM outputs) to discriminate between the ‘no manipula-tion’ attentive and ‘disengagement’ inattentive conditions. We assess performance along two complementary dimensions: 

steady-state discriminability , which quantifies how well the two conditions can be discriminated from each other over the course of the session, and transition timing , which quantifies how accurately the timing of attention-state changes is cap-tured. The former is captured by the Area Under the Curve (AUC), while the latter is captured by the Switch Detection Time (SDT). Unless stated otherwise, AUC is used as the primary performance metric. SDT is reported only in analyses where transition timing is directly relevant. 

1) Area Under the Curve: The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) provides a ranking-based measure of separability. It can be interpreted as the probability that a randomly selected attention metric value from the baseline (attentive) condition is larger than a ran-domly selected attention metric value from the disengagement (inattentive) condition. Formally, given samples y1 from the baseline condition and 

y2 from the disengagement condition, we estimate the AUC using its rank-based (Mann-Whitney) formulation [30], [31]: 

AUC = #( y1 > y 2) + 0 .5 #( y1 = y2)#( y1) #( y2) , (24) where #( ·) denotes the number of occurrences meeting the specified condition. A higher AUC value indicates stronger discrimination between the two experimental conditions and, consequently, improved aAAD performance.

A. Added value of HMM 

To illustrate the added value of HMM post-processing, we compare the Fisher-transformed raw NET trace with the corresponding HMM-processed trace obtained via non-causal inference, which provides an off-line estimate of the latent attentional state. B. Effect of Window Length on AUC and SDT 

In this subsection, we investigate how the analysis window length affects both AUC and SDT for the non-causal HMM output. We consider window lengths of 1, 5, 10, 15, and 30 s. For each window length, AUC is evaluated across participants, whereas SDT is evaluated for individuals and for the group as described in Section III. Fig. 7 shows the results. Mean AUC increases from 0.832 at 1 s to its highest value of 0.858 at 5 s, then gradually decreases to 0.827 at 30 s. Individual SDT ranges from 25.95 to 32.02 s, and the proportion of missed transitions across participants ranges from 29.0% to 31.0%. The 1-s window yields both the lowest individual SDT and the lowest miss rate. Group SDT ranges from 13.17 to 18.17 s, with the lowest value at 5 s. The numbers of missed group transitions for window lengths of 1, 5, 10, 15, and 30 s are zero, one, one, one, and two out of seven, respectively. The 1-s window detects all seven group transitions with a group SDT of 18.00 s. Together with its mean AUC of 0.832 and its performance for individuals, these results support the use of 1 s as the default window length.

D. Why NET is Particularly Suitable as Evidence for aAAD 

The proposed HMM framework is presented using NET as the observation feature throughout this paper. Here, we justify this choice by comparing NET with two commonly used EEG-based attention markers, namely alpha power [8] and spectral entropy [10], using the same HMM framework. A key distinction lies in the type of attention captured by each of these features. NET directly reflects neural tracking of the attended speech stimulus and therefore provides a clear and consistent measure of auditory attention to a specific target speech signal. In contrast, alpha power and entropy are more general neural markers that have been linked to overall attention in a broader sense (e.g., arousal or cognitive effort), but that are not specifically tied to attention toward a particular auditory source. Specifically, alpha power is defined as the mean band power of the EEG signal in the 8-12 Hz range [8], averaged across all electrodes. It is commonly used as a marker of attentional state [8], [9], although the direction of modulation can vary across individuals and paradigms [11]. Spectral entropy reflects the regularity of the EEG signal and is commonly computed from the power spectrum over a broad frequency range (0.5-32 Hz in [10], [11]). V. C ONCLUSION 

We have presented a practical, modular pipeline for unsuper-vised absolute auditory attention decoding (aAAD) in single-speaker listening scenarios. The proposed pipeline extracts a windowed neural envelope tracking (NET) sequence as stimulus-specific evidence and applies an HMM for prob-abilistic temporal inference, enabling continuous attention tracking without relying on explicit attention labels. Across all participants, HMM post-processing consistently improves the separability between ‘no manipulation’ (attentive) and ‘disengagement’ (inattentive) periods compared to the raw NET trace, yielding a more interpretable attention trajectory. We further investigate the effect of analysis window length on AUC and SDT. Compared with MA smoothing, the HMM achieves higher discriminability across the examined smooth-ing scales. Although the individual SDTs of the two methods are comparable, the HMM provides lower group-level SDT over most smoothing scales after participant averaging. Finally, we show that NET outperforms alpha power and spectral entropy in terms of AUC within the proposed framework, supporting its use as the observation feature for HMM-based attention tracking.
