Abstract
Simulation-based inference (SBI) provides a powerful way to fit mechanistic mod-
els to data, but offers little insight into which information in the data shapes the
inferred parameter values. It could therefore benefit from robust and inexpensive
interpretability methods. Learned weighting mechanisms such as feature gates
provide a potential route to low-cost interpretability, although their interpretation
assumes that predictive training reveals which inputs drive performance. We ex-
amined this assumption in a computational neuroscience setting, where neural
posterior estimation (NPE) is used to infer four physiological parameters of a dy-
namical model of interacting neuronal populations (Jansen-Rit). We tested whether
the learned gate weights captured feature importance by comparing them with
a retrain-based ablation benchmark, in which each feature was removed in turn
and the NPE retrained. We considered two jointly trained gates: independent
sigmoid weights and competing softmax weights. If these reliably reflect feature
importance, they should align with the ablation-based estimates. Across nine
independent training seeds, however, gate-based and ablation-based importance
were negatively correlated (Spearman’s rho approximately -0.61 for sigmoid gat-
ing and -0.56 for softmax gating). The feature that ablation identified as most
important, total signal power, was among the features the gate down-weighted
most, despite gating costing negligible predictive accuracy. These results extend
a pattern previously documented for attention mechanisms in natural language
processing and for explainability methods in electroencephalography (EEG) deep
learning to the simulation-based inference setting, and suggest caution when inter-
preting learned gate weights as measures of causal feature importance in scientific
machine-learning pipelines.

Introduction
Mechanistic models of neural activity, from single-compartment Hodgkin-Huxley neurons to neural
mass models of cortical columns, offer parameters that are biologically interpretable in a way purely
statistical decoders do not provide. The Jansen–Rit model [1] is a canonical case: a six-state neural
mass model of a cortical column that reproduces electroencephalography (EEG) rhythms from a
small number of physiologically grounded parameters. Fitting such models to data remains hard
[2–4]. Their dynamics are nonlinear, and the likelihood of the observed data given the model’s
parameters p(x | θ) has no closed form, since it would require marginalizing over the full space of
stochastic input trajectories consistent with the observed output [5]. Classical approaches, including

Simulation-based inference (SBI) completely sidesteps evaluating intractable likelihoods by training
a neural density estimator directly on simulation outputs, learning an amortized mapping from data
features to a posterior over parameters [6–8]. Gonçalves et al. [9] established this approach for
mechanistic neuroscience models, applying neural posterior estimation to single-neuron and small-
circuit models, and showed that hand-designed summary-statistic embeddings are often sufficient for
accurate, well-calibrated inference.
In scientific applications of SBI, it becomes crucial to understand how each feature of the data
contributes to parameter inference. Normally, a feature’s contribution is established only after
training; the gold standard in SBI is retrain-based ablation, in which the model is retrained with
one feature excluded and the change in the inferred posterior is quantified, a causal measure by
construction. This is the procedure the current practice guide points to for assessing which features
constrain parameters [10], and the criterion against which Beck et al. [11] validated a faster analytic
alternative, Feature Selection through Likelihood Marginalization. Both measure the effect that
excluding a feature actually has on the posterior: one by performing the exclusion directly, the other
by approximating it analytically. An appealing, cheaper alternative is to learn the weighting itself by
incorporating a small differentiable gate, trained end-to-end with the rest of the network. The gate
multiplies each feature by a weight in (0, 1), and after training it can be read as importance at no
extra cost. Indeed, gates of exactly this form are reported in various settings: as electrode importance
in EEG deep learning [12, 13]; and, in tabular learning and forecasting, the reading is built into the
methods themselves, through learned input masks [14], attention-based feature selection [15], and
variable-selection layers [16]. In SBI, however, the idea appears not to have been taken up (although
it has been suggested [17]), and we believe it could be attractive in principle, since the standard
ablation test costs one full retraining per feature.
Evidence regarding the interpretability of learnable gates in deep-learning pipelines is still mixed,
however. On the one hand, architectures built for feature selection do sometimes recover known
relevant variables on synthetic data. For example, TabNet’s sparse masks identified ground-truth
features [14]; importantly, however, their weights are not generally guaranteed to represent importance.
More negative evidence comes from the UCI Adult dataset, where learned mask and attention scores
showed little agreement with KernelSHAP, a method that estimates each feature’s contribution to a
prediction [18]. Results from natural language processing (NLP) are similarly cautionary: attention
weights sometimes tracked input effects, but too inconsistently to provide reliable importance
measures [19]. In EEG, explanation quality also depends on the method: saliency failed a model-
randomization check, whereas DeepLIFT did not [20, 21]. More fundamentally, jointly trained

selectors can encode predictions in their masks [22], while unconstrained multiplicative scales may
be non-identifiable under downstream rescaling [23]. Taken together, these findings suggest that
learned weights can be informative without necessarily providing reliable importance estimates. That
uncertainty extends to SBI, where the relationship between learned weights and retrain-based ablation
remains largely unexplored.
We compare the gating and ablation measures of importance directly in a Jansen–Rit EEG model (Fig-
ure 1). Neural posterior estimators (NPEs) are trained over four mechanistic parameters: connectivity
strength, input drive, the joint excitatory/inhibitory timescale-scaling factor, and inhibitory synaptic
gain. These parameters are inferred from simulated EEG, using seven statistical-summary features
computed at five electrodes. Two gated variants, independent (sigmoid) and competing (softmax)
per-feature weighting, are compared against an ungated baseline in terms of model performance, and
against the gold-standard measure of feature importance, obtained by retraining the full pipeline from
scratch with each feature excluded in turn and measuring the resulting drop in posterior accuracy.
We only use simulated EEG data in order to have a clean ground truth to evaluate the feasibility of
the gating method. Indeed, many more challenges are introduced by real data which are beyond the
scope of this work.1
2
Methods
2.1
Simulation and forward model
We simulated EEG using the standard six-state Jansen–Rit neural mass model of a cortical column
[1] (Figure 1a), integrated with a fixed-step Euler scheme at 1000 Hz. We varied and subsequently
inferred four parameters: connectivity strength (C), mean input drive (µ), a joint scaling factor for
the excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic time constants (κ), and inhibitory synaptic gain (g). κ
scales both time constants proportionally and holds their ratio fixed at 2:1; these time constants are
what set the model’s oscillation frequency [24]. Excitatory gain was held fixed deliberately, so that
the excitatory-inhibitory gain degeneracy reported for larger Jansen–Rit parameter sets [25] cannot
arise here by construction. All four parameters were given independent log-uniform priors, enforcing
positivity and giving roughly equal prior weight across their plausible range; the ranges are listed in
Appendix A.
We projected the single-source output to scalp-sensor space using MNE-Python’s [26, 27] fsaverage
template head model and a three-layer boundary-element (BEM) forward solution, with the source
located in the visual cortex. From the resulting lead-field, we selected the five electrodes with the
highest absolute sensitivity to this source, then projected the source signal using each electrode’s
actual (signed) lead-field value rather than its magnitude (Figure 1a), preserving realistic scalp
topography rather than collapsing to a single scalar channel. We added pink (1/f) noise to each
simulated trial, with signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) drawn independently per trial from a uniform
distribution over 5–25 dB during training, a range specifically chosen to span moderate to light signal
corruption. Simulation and preprocessing parameters are given in full in Appendix A.
2.2
Feature embedding and gating
Figure 1b illustrates the embedding and gating. From each of the five electrode channels, we
computed seven hand-crafted summary statistics: skewness, kurtosis, spectral slope, total signal
power, dominant frequency, Hjorth mobility, and Hjorth complexity [28]. In gated configurations,
each feature value was first z-scored per feature and channel using the training-set statistics, giving zi
for feature i.2 A small multilayer perceptron mapped the seven features, pooled (averaged) across
channels, to seven per-feature, sample-dependent gate weights αi ∈(0, 1). These were then tiled
back out across channels and applied multiplicatively, so that the density estimator received zi αi in
place of zi. Note that all five channels of feature i shared the same weight αi. Appendix B discusses
the design choice.
We compared two gate activation functions: an element-wise sigmoid and a softmax. The sigmoid
weighted each feature independently with no explicit temperature term, whereas softmax introduced

The per-feature importance we report for the gate (Table 1) is |zi| · αi, averaged over samples,

section.
2.3
Inference, ablation, and evaluation
We estimated the posterior over the Jansen–Rit parameters given the feature embedding using single-
round neural posterior estimation as implemented in the sbi package [29], with a neural spline flow
[30] density estimator. Every configuration was trained independently across nine seeds, with training
parameter sets redrawn independently for each seed; architecture and training hyperparameters are
given in Appendix C.
We estimated feature importance by retrain-based leave-one-feature-out ablation: for each of the
seven features and each seed, we retrained the full pipeline from scratch with that feature excluded
from the embedding, using the same simulated training set as the corresponding full-feature run for
that seed. We quantified importance as the drop in mean R2 relative to the full-feature model trained
on the same seed, where R2 is the coefficient of determination between true and posterior-mean
parameter values, computed on a held-out set for each of the four parameters and then averaged over
them. We used this retrain-based procedure, rather than a post-hoc masking heuristic, as it measures
each feature’s causal contribution by construction.
We computed all accuracy metrics on a fixed set of 250 held-out simulations per seed, drawn as
described in Appendix C. For each held-out simulation we drew 1,000 posterior samples and took the
posterior mean as the point estimate, from which we computed R2 and the fraction of 50%, 90%, and
95% credible intervals containing the true value, following the spirit of simulation-based calibration
checks [31], which we will refer to as credible-interval coverage henceforth. Additional metrics and
per-parameter breakdowns are given in Appendix D. For paired comparisons across seeds, such as
ablation- versus gate-based importance rankings, we used an exact sign-flip test over all 29 = 512
possible ±1 sign assignments of the nine per-seed statistics, avoiding both distributional assumptions
and Monte Carlo approximation error.3
3
Results and Discussion
Across nine independent training seeds, ablation identified total signal power as the most important
of the seven features by a wide margin: excluding it dropped mean R2 by 0.210 (SD 0.009), roughly
five times the drop from the next-largest feature. Testing each feature’s own ablation effect against
the null with the exact sign-flip test described in Methods, six of the seven features had an effect
significantly different from zero (Table 1), spectral slope being the only exception (p = 0.727).
In contrast, the gate did not favor total signal power. In fact, it assigned the feature the lowest or
second-lowest weight under both activations (Table 1), while its two most-favored features, dominant
frequency and Hjorth mobility, had ablation effects an order of magnitude smaller.
This disagreement was systematic. We computed the Spearman rank correlation between ablation-
based and gate-based importance separately for each seed (seven features per seed) and tested the
seed-averaged correlation against zero with the exact sign-flip test. Independent (sigmoid) gating gave
ρ = −0.607 and competing (softmax) gating gave ρ = −0.556, both at p = 0.0039. Meanwhile,
the two gating variants agreed with each other on which features mattered (ρ = 0.643, p = 0.0039).
Thus, the gate was internally consistent and reproducible across activation functions; it simply tracked
something other than what ablation tracked.

We next asked whether this disagreement could reflect a poorly performing model. A priori, it
seemed possible that the gate’s disagreement with ablation simply reflected a network that was
performing poorly overall. This was not the case. Gating cost little in raw accuracy: mean R2
was 0.791 without gating, 0.792 with per-feature (sigmoid) gating, and 0.761 with competing
(softmax) gating, differences small relative to their seed-to-seed spread. Credible-interval coverage
was close to the nominal 50%, 90%, and 95% levels under all three configurations, thus showing
no evidence of systematic over- or under-confidence from either gating variant. Moreover, ordering
the four parameters by per-parameter R2 resulted in an identical ranking under every configuration
(Appendix D).
Our main finding was that ablation-based and gate-based feature importance were negatively corre-
lated for both gating variants. This disagreement is arguably more pronounced than a weak correlation,
such as, for example, that reported by Jain and Wallace [32] in a natural language processing setting.
Our results therefore reinforce the broader conclusion that mechanisms purely optimized for predic-
tive accuracy should not automatically be interpreted as reliable indicators of feature importance. In
the terms of Jacovi and Goldberg [33], the gate was plausible but not faithful: its feature-importance
rankings were consistent across training seeds and moderately similar between the sigmoid and
softmax variants (ρ = 0.643), yet it assigned the least importance to the feature whose removal most
impaired performance.
Even if a measure of causal importance were extractable from the network’s learned parameters, we
have shown that in this setting it is not as simple as reading gate values. What the gate tracked instead
is not established by this study, nor is whether the disagreement persists where ground truth is not
simulated and ablation must be scored against a proxy such as held-out predictive likelihood.
4
Conclusion
A feature-gating mechanism trained end-to-end alongside a simulation-based posterior estimator did
not recover the feature importance established by gold-standard retrain-based ablation, and did so
while costing negligible accuracy and being optimized on exactly the objective the modeler cares
about. Practitioners using learned gating or attention as a cheap stand-in for feature-importance
analysis in scientific, simulation-based inference pipelines should treat this as a caution rather than a
shortcut. A gating mechanism optimized purely for predictive accuracy is not thereby guaranteed to
possess explanatory power.

Responsible-use statement.
The risk this work identifies is misplaced trust: a coherent-looking
gate can support an incorrect scientific conclusion under the appearance, rather than the substance,
of mechanistic justification. We therefore recommend that learned weighting be validated against a
causal measure, such as the retrain-based ablation used here, before it is reported as importance.

A
Simulation details
In Section 2.1, all four inferred parameters were drawn from independent log-uniform priors across
the following ranges: C ∈[135, 270], µ ∈[120, 350], κ ∈[0.75, 1.25], and g ∈[0.5, 2.0]. Input
noise standard deviation (σ) was fixed at 4.5 rather than inferred, since once observation noise is
added downstream, σ and observation noise are not jointly identifiable from the signal alone. Each
simulation discarded an initial 2 s transient and retained the following 3 s, decimated from the
internal 1000 Hz integration rate to a 250 Hz output rate. Training parameter sets were drawn using
scrambled Sobol sequences, for better prior coverage than independent sampling would give, redrawn
independently for each of the nine training seeds used throughout.
The signed lead-field projection described in Section 2.1 preserves sign inversion across electrode
pairs, a feature of realistic scalp topography that a magnitude-only projection would discard. The
per-trial SNR draw over 5–25 dB ensures that the density estimator learns a posterior robust to a
spread of data quality rather than overfitting to a single noise level.
B
Gating design details
In Section 2.2, one gate weight αi was applied to all five channels of feature i, rather than learning a
separate weight for each of the thirty-five (feature, channel) pairs. We made this choice so the gate and
the ablation procedure would operate on the same unit of analysis: ablation excludes a feature across
all five channels at once, never a single channel of a single feature, so a gate that weighted channels
independently would not be answering the same question. Table 1’s two importance columns are
comparable by construction because both are computed over that same seven-feature unit.
C
Training and evaluation details
In Section 2.3, we estimated the posterior with a neural spline flow using five transform layers
with 125 hidden features per layer. We trained on 32,768 simulations by default, with batch size
512, learning rate 5 × 10−4, for up to 250 epochs with early stopping after 15 epochs without
validation-loss improvement, and a 15% validation split.
The held-out evaluation set of 250 simulations was drawn once per seed and reused across every con-
figuration and ablation condition, so comparisons were not affected by which simulations happened
to be drawn. Normalized RMSE (NRMSE) is root-mean-squared error divided by each parameter’s
prior range, which makes error comparable across parameters despite their different prior scales.
Credible-interval coverage at a given level is the fraction of held-out simulations for which the true
parameter value fell within the corresponding symmetric, equal-tailed percentile interval of the 1,000
posterior samples drawn for that simulation.
All experiments were run on a single consumer NVIDIA RTX 4050 Laptop GPU. The 90 training
runs reported here comprised three gating configurations and seven leave-one-feature-out ablation
conditions across nine seeds each, and took approximately 5 GPU-hours in total, or roughly 3 minutes
per run including simulation, training, and evaluation.
