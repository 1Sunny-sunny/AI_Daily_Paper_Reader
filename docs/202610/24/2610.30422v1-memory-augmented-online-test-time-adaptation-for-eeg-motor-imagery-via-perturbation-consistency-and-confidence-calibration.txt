Memory-Augmented Online Test-Time Adaptation for EEG Motor
Imagery via Perturbation Consistency and Confidence Calibration
Kunkun Zhang, Kehao Wu, Zhitao Chen, Haigen Hu, Member, IEEE, and Qianwei Zhou, Member, IEEE
Abstract—Recently,
electroencephalography
(EEG)-based
brain–computer
interfaces
(BCIs)
have
gained
traction
as
portable
neural
measurement
systems.
However,
the
practical deployment of these instruments is often hindered by
nonstationarity and cross-subject distribution shifts, which lead
to significant intersubject inconsistency. Existing adaptation
methods remain impractical for real-time measurement scenarios,
as they typically require offline access to source or aggregated
target data. To address these challenges, this article proposes
memory-augmented online test-time adaptation (OTTA) with
perturbation consistency and confidence calibration (MOPC),
a source-free framework designed to enhance the reliability of
BCI instrumentation. MOPC enables the measurement system to
update its decoding parameters on the fly during the inference
stage,
facilitating
single-pass
adaptation
without
accessing
sensitive source data. We introduce random-mask perturbations
to enforce prediction consistency against inherent EEG signal
noise and acquisition artifacts. Furthermore, a confidence-
calibrated reweighting loss is designed to integrate feature-space
similarity, effectively mitigating unreliable pseudolabels and class
imbalance during continuous signal acquisition. To ensure system
stability and prevent the catastrophic forgetting of established
neural patterns, a lightweight memory module is incorporated
to
regularize
parameter
updates
within
a
tight
latency
budget. Evaluated on the BCI IV IIb and OpenBMI datasets,
MOPC achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming
prior methods by 2.22 % and 3.67 %, respectively, while
keeping
the
per-trial
adaptation
latency
to
approximately
40–60 ms. This study provides a robust, recalibration-free
solution for real-time EEG decoding, marking a significant
advancement in the precision and usability of practical BCI
measurement instruments. The source code is available at
https://github.com/KunKun-Zhang/MOPC.git

RAIN-COMPUTER interfaces (BCIs) serve as advanced
neural measurement instruments, establishing direct path-
ways between the human brain and external devices for neu-
rorehabilitation and robotics [1, 2]. Among various paradigms,
electroencephalography (EEG)-based motor imagery (MI) is
a pivotal measurement technique due to its noninvasiveness
and portability [3]. By decoding imagined movements from
EEG signals, MI-based BCIs can restore motor function in
paralyzed patients and enhance human capabilities in virtual
environments. These systems have been successfully applied
in multiple fields, including wheelchair control [4] and lower
limb exoskeleton assistance systems [5].
Early MI-BCI decoding methods relied on manual feature
extraction techniques, such as common spatial patterns (CSPs)
and statistical bandpower measures, followed by conventional
classifiers [6, 7]. The advent of deep learning has led to
the development of end-to-end neural models that automat-
ically learn spatial–temporal representations from raw EEG,
resulting in significant improvements in measurement accuracy
and robustness [8, 9]. Despite these advancements, MI-EEG
models trained on a set of subjects (source domain) often
experience a sharp decline in decoding reliability when applied
to new individuals (target domain) [10]. This is attributed
to the differences in signal amplitude and frequency char-
acteristics between subjects [11]. In metrological terms, the
decoder is an instrument whose reliability and reproducibility
must hold across subjects and over time, yet this cross-subject
variability behaves as instrument drift whose usual remedy,
per-subject recalibration, lengthens setup and undermines re-
peatability [12].
Because such drift degrades any model transferred to a
new user, robust transfer learning has become essential [13–
15]. Domain adaptation (DA) addresses it by transferring
knowledge from a labeled source to an unlabeled target [16],
and is commonly divided, depending on whether the source
data remain accessible, into unsupervised DA (UDA) and
source-free UDA (SFUDA). UDA aligns the two distributions
during joint training [17]. DANN, for instance, learns domain-
invariant features with a gradient-reversal adversary [18].
The instrumentation and measurement literature has pursued
this line to reconcile cross-subject and cross-device record-
ings, through multiview contrastive learning [19], prototype-
supervised transfer across multiple source brains (PSATs) [20],
interdevice sample reweighting (SRENet) [21], multisource
associate alignment across subjects and sessions [22], and
semisupervised multisource joint-distribution adaptation that
further exploits a few labeled target trials [23], as illustrated in
Fig. 1(a). Instead, SFUDA adapts a pretrained model without
revisiting the source, thereby preserving subject privacy [24].

SHOT aligns the target with the source hypothesis through
information maximization and pseudolabeling [25], while
USFDA adds a generative classifier for out-of-distribution
rejection [26], as shown in Fig. 1(b). Effective as these
methods are, they all operate offline: each collects the entire
target set, or even a labeled calibration subset of it, and revisits
it over several passes before producing a prediction, and the
UDA variants further require the source recordings to remain
accessible at the deployment site. This assumption does not
fit a BCI that must decode a continuous stream one trial at a
time.
Online test-time adaptation (OTTA) lifts the offline re-
quirement by updating the model during inference, in a
single pass over the incoming data [27, 28], which matches
the low-latency operation a deployed BCI needs, as de-
picted in Fig. 1(c). Existing OTTA methods fall into four
families. Entropy minimization sharpens predictions by tun-
ing batch-normalization (BN) parameters, as in TENT [29]
and the sharpness-aware SAR [30]. Pseudolabeling super-
vises the model with its own outputs, as in the paired-
view DPLOT [31] and the backpropagation-free T3A [32].
Consistency regularization enforces stable predictions across
perturbed views, as used for EEG sleep staging [33] and
for masked self-supervision [10]. Antiforgetting regularization
preserves source knowledge over long runs, as in the teacher-
based CoTTA [34] and the probabilistic PETAL [35]. All four
were designed for image streams, however, and overlook two
properties of EEG: its trials are short and noisy, and their class
balance drifts as the user’s engagement varies. The few EEG-
specific efforts remain partial. MD-CAT copes with evolving
distributions, but by continual retraining rather than test-time
adaptation (TTA) [36]; Wang et al. [37] refine a personalized
MI decoder trial by trial, retraining on the user’s own cue-
locked online trials to track day-to-day nonstationarity; Jiao
et al. [38] combine subject-specific stimulation and filter-
bank settings, optimized offline, with periodic retraining of
the decoder during online use; neither considers label-free,
source-free transfer to a new user; and T-Time, the closest
online baseline, maximizes information over an ensemble yet
treats trials as independent, so it cannot exploit their temporal
order [39]. Offline prototype networks such as SST-DPN
confirm that temporal context is informative [40], but they
cannot adapt once deployed.
We close this gap with MOPC, a memory-augmented
OTTA method with perturbation consistency and confidence
calibration. MOPC keeps a short first-in-first-out memory
of recent prealigned trials together with their predictions,
and this memory serves two purposes. First, it continuously
re-estimates the Euclidean alignment (EA) of the incoming
stream, turning a normally static prealignment [41] into an
adaptive one. Second, it provides the context for three coupled
objectives tailored to EEG: a perturbation-consistency term
that keeps the prediction stable when the trial is corrupted
by a random time-mask [10], a joint entropy term that sharp-
ens the predictions on both the original and masked views,
and a confidence-calibrated reweighting term that discounts
unreliable pseudolabels and offsets class drift using feature
similarity among same-class neighbors in memory together
with an effective-number class weight [42]. Every arriving trial
is first labeled by an ensemble of N=5 models and then used
to update only the BN parameters of one member, selected in
round-robin. Since the update touches nothing else, its cost is
independent of the number of classes, which keeps the method
fast enough for real-time use. The overall MOPC framework
is illustrated in Fig. 2.
MOPC therefore differs from prior work in both setting and
design. Unlike the offline adaptation of PSAT and SRENet or
the continual retraining of MD-CAT, it adapts to each trial in a
single streaming pass and never revisits the source; unlike the
personalized online MI-BCIs of [37] and [38], which train
the decoder on the user’s own recordings and refine it by
retraining on that user’s online trials, it starts from a cross-
subject source ensemble and adapts from unlabeled trials in
a single pass without any retraining, so it can serve as the
decoder-adaptation component of such systems. Unlike the
entropy-only, memoryless T-Time, it uses its memory both
to stay stable and to follow the changing class balance. Its
novelty lies not in any single mechanism, several of which
exist in isolation, but in their integration into one lightweight
scheme suited to nonstationary EEG. Practically, this targets
the outcomes that matter in deployment: it sustains decoding
reliability as the signal drifts, reduces the per-subject recali-
bration burden, and runs on the acquisition hardware in real
time, extending the cross-subject transfer line from offline
calibration to online operation.
The main contributions of this work are as follows.
1) We formulate cross-subject MI decoding as source-free,
single-pass OTTA under real-time constraints, in which
target trials arrive one at a time and the source is never
revisited. This setting is distinct from offline source-free
DA and from methods that rely on continual retraining.
2) We
propose
MOPC,
in
which
a
fixed-capacity
FIFO memory drives a dynamic EA of the stream
and couples three EEG-aware objectives: a time-
masked perturbation-consistency loss, a joint entropy-
minimization loss, and a confidence-calibrated reweight-
ing loss that suppresses noisy pseudolabels and corrects
class imbalance.
3) We keep the adaptation lightweight: each trial updates
only the BN parameters of a single member of an N=5
ensemble, so the number of trainable parameters does
not grow with the number of classes.

C. Source Model Training
Given the constraints of real-time BCI instrumentation,
we avoid computationally heavy architectures. We employ
EEGNet [9] as the backbone due to its efficiency in extracting
spectral–spatial features. In particular, we configure the first
convolutional block with a temporal kernel size of (1, 64)
and a depthwise kernel of (C, 1) to capture sensorimotor
rhythms. We set the pointwise convolution filter size to 32 and
append a global average pooling (GAP) layer. Consequently,
the resulting latent feature vector zi for each trial has a
dimension of R32.
To enhance generalization, we apply leave-one-subject-out
(LOSO) cross-validation. In each iteration, data from all
subjects except the target are used for training with cross-
entropy loss Lce. We perform N = 5-fold cross-validation,
retaining the best model from each fold. These N models
form an ensemble for robust measurement inference.

E. Optimization of MOPC
To jointly optimize MOPC in the streaming setting, we
minimize a hybrid objective that combines perturbation
consistency regularization, joint entropy minimization, and
confidence-calibrated reweighting
Ltotal = Lpcr + Ljem + Lccr.
(11)
For each incoming trial, we first obtain its prediction and
subsequently perform a single adaptation step via one gra-
dient update with Ltotal while keeping all network weights
fixed except for the BN layers. The update touches only
the affine scale-and-shift parameters of the BN layers in the
selected ensemble member, while the convolutional kernels,
the depthwise and pointwise filters, and the classifier weights
remain frozen. Each trial is thus handled in one streaming
pass: one soft-voted ensemble prediction over the N = 5
members, followed, once the memory bank is populated, by a
single BN-only gradient step over the B = 24 memory entries,
with no trial ever revisited. This per-trial schedule couples
inference with adaptation to enable continuous online learning.

III. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
A. Datasets
To evaluate the classification performance of our frame-
work in the OTTA setting, we conducted experiments using
two public datasets: BCI Competition IV IIb and OpenBMI.
Because each benchmark defines its own measurement front-
end, we detail below the acquisition instrumentation, electrode
montage, and sampling resolution of the two datasets, comple-
menting the processing pipeline summarized in Table I. A third
dataset, the four-class BCI Competition IV 2a, is introduced
only as the basis for the preliminary multiclass validation of
Section IV-C, and is not part of the binary experiments.
1) BCI IV IIb: The BCI IV IIb [43] dataset contains EEG
signals from 9 healthy subjects (B01–B09), recorded across
five separate sessions at a sampling rate of 250 Hz using 3
EEG channels (around C3/Cz/C4 under the 10–20 system).
Each subject performed two MI tasks: left-hand (LH) and
right-hand (RH) movement imagery. The first two sessions


6
consisted of 120 trials each, without feedback, while the last
three sessions included 160 trials each, with a smiley face
displayed on the screen as feedback. In our experiments, we
extract a 4-s segment of EEG data following the visual cue
as an EEG trial. At acquisition, these three derivations were
recorded as bipolar channels, accompanied by three monopolar
EOG channels retained solely for artifact processing and never
used for decoding; Fz served as the ground. The amplifier
applied a 0.5–100-Hz recording bandpass and a 50-Hz notch,
with its dynamic range set to ±100 µV for the two no-
feedback sessions and ±50 µV for the three feedback sessions.
Each 4-s cue-locked trial therefore corresponds to a 1000-
sample analysis window, evenly split between the left- and
RH classes, and the cross-subject protocol yields about 5760
pooled source trials with 320 test trials per subject.
2) OpenBMI: The OpenBMI [44] dataset contains multi-
session, multiparadigm EEG signals recorded from 54 healthy
subjects (subj01-subj54) at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, using
62 EEG electrode channels. In this study, we focused on
the MI paradigm using a subset of 20 channels located over
the FC, C, CP, and P rows to balance spatial resolution and
computational latency [44]. Each subject participated in two
sessions, each consisting of an offline training phase and an
online testing phase, with 100 trials in each phase. We consider
the EEG data from [0, 4] seconds after the visual cue as a
trial. Acquisition used a BrainAmp amplifier (Brain Products,
Munich, Germany) with Ag/AgCl electrodes placed according
to the international 10–20 system. Recordings were nasion-
referenced and grounded at AFz, with electrode impedance
kept below 10 kΩ. After the down-sampling summarized in
Table I, the 0–4-s cue-locked segment gives a 1000-sample
analysis window, evenly split between the two motor-imagery
classes, for a pooled source set of 21 200 trials and 200 test
trials per subject.
3) BCI IV 2a: The four-class BCI Competition IV 2a
dataset [45] is the multiclass sibling of BCI IV IIb, recorded at
the same 250-Hz sampling rate under the shared Graz acquisi-
tion protocol. It comprises 9 subjects (A01–A09) and, unlike
the three bipolar derivations of IIb, provides 22 EEG channels
and four motor-imagery classes (left hand, right hand, feet,
and tongue), with 288 trials in each of two sessions (training
and evaluation). As in the other datasets, we take the 0–4 s
cue-locked segment, yielding a 1000-sample analysis window
at 250 Hz. This dataset is used solely for the preliminary
multiclass validation of Section IV-C, and not for the main
binary experiments.

C. Experiment Settings
To evaluate the generalization capability across different
individuals, we employ LOSO cross-validation to simulate
cross-subject deployment. In each iteration, one subject’s data
is treated as the unlabeled target domain (test set), while the
labeled data from all other subjects form the source domain.
The labeled source domain is then divided into N = 5 folds
for cross-validation. In each of the N rounds, one fold is held
out for validation, while the remaining N −1 folds are used for
training, resulting in N pretrained source-domain models. The
hyperparameters for the Lccr were set to β = 0.999 following
the effective-number weighting of [42], and γ = 0.5 as a
standard focal-modulation setting.
All sessions from the source subjects are pooled for training
and validation, and only the test sessions from the target
subject are used for testing. Under this setup, the BCI IV IIb
source set contains approximately 5760 trials, and the test set
contains 320 trials per subject. For OpenBMI, the source set
comprises 21 200 trials, and the test set contains 200 trials per
subject.
The source domain models are trained using the PyTorch
framework on an Intel1 Xeon1 Silver 4210R CPU @ 2.40 GHz
and an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. Offline, all network
parameters of each EEGNet instance are trained with Lce by
AdamW under the cosine-annealing schedule of Table I, and
the checkpoint with the best accuracy on the held-out inner
fold is retained; neither training nor checkpoint selection ever
sees the target subject, and the procedure is repeated over five
random seeds.
In the target DA process, simulating an online scenario, all
updates run on the CPU and use the same 4-s trial window
as offline training (T = 1000 samples at 250 Hz). Adaptation
is deliberately conservative. Per trial, a single AdamW step
with a reduced learning rate of 6 × 10−4 updates only the
BN affine parameters of one ensemble member, selected in
round-robin, with the loss Ltotal evaluated over the B entries
of the memory bank. Each ensemble member keeps its own
optimizer; no optimizer state is carried over from the offline
stage, and every trial is predicted before the update it triggers
and is never revisited. The hyperparameters required no tuning
beyond B: β and γ follow the settings given above, the mask
length was fixed a priori to W = 200 samples (0.8 s, one
fifth of the trial window), and the three losses are summed
with equal weights. The capacity B itself was chosen from
the sweep of Fig. 3 as a single value shared by both datasets
and all subjects. No hyperparameter is tuned per subject or
per dataset, and the same configuration is reused for the class-
imbalanced protocol, the other backbones, and the four-class
experiment. Table I consolidates the complete configuration so
that the pipeline can be replicated from a single reference.

D. Baseline Methods
To rigorously assess the measurement reliability of the
MOPC framework, we benchmarked it against three distinct
categories of transfer learning methods: direct transfer (DT),
DA, and TTA. The DT baselines include three end-to-end
models: EEGNet [9], ConvNet [8], and LMDANet [47]. The
DA group consists of the unsupervised domain adaptation
(UDA) method DANN [18] and the source-free unsuper-
vised domain adaptation (SFUDA) method SHOT [25]. The
TTA baselines include four established methods: TENT [29],
CoTTA [34], T-Time [39], and DPLOT [31]. All methods
are evaluated under the same LOSO protocol with balanced
classes, and we report cross-subject performance metrics,
including accuracy, F1-score, Cohen’s kappa, and standard
deviation. For all transfer learning baseline methods, EEGNet
is used as the backbone, and the same domain prealignment
(DPA) procedure is applied during model transfer, so that the
adaptation mechanism is the only factor that differs between
the DA and TTA rows of Tables II–IV; the absolute values are
therefore not meant to be compared with those reported in the
original papers, which use other datasets, splits, or backbones.
E. Results
1) Class-Balanced Accuracy Evaluation: MOPC consis-
tently outperforms DT, DA, and TTA baselines on both the
BCI IV IIb and OpenBMI datasets, as reported in Tables II
and III. On BCI IV IIb, MOPC achieves an average accuracy
of 81.23 %, exceeding the best direct-transfer model EEGNet
by 4.95 %, surpassing the leading domain-adaptation method
SHOT by 2.91 %, and improving on the strongest TTA base-
line T-Time by 2.22 %. These gains are observed for nearly
all subjects, indicating robust cross-subject generalization and
stable performance. On OpenBMI, MOPC attains an average
accuracy of 78.94 %, which represents a 3.67 % improvement
over T-Time at 75.27 %. In addition, MOPC reduces cross-
subject variability and increases the F1-score to 78.39 %,
while Cohen’s kappa rises to 57.88, reflecting more reliable
and balanced decoding across users. Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests [48] on paired accuracies confirm that all improvements
are significant (p-value < 0.05).
The per-subject columns of Table II show where these
gains land: MOPC wins 77 of the 81 paired comparisons
against the nine baselines, with two ties and two losses of
at most 1.25 points. Its margins are smallest on B02 and B03,
whose source-only accuracies are near chance, and largest
(7.5–8.8 points over EEGNet) on B04, B06, and B08, whose
source models already partially separate the classes. This is the
expected behavior of a label-free method: the three objectives
can realign a decision boundary that the source representation
encodes, as Fig. 4 illustrates for B04 and B06, but cannot
create class structure that it lacks. The one subject where T-
Time leads, B03, lies in this near-chance regime.
The mechanisms behind the family level ordering are also
visible. Although SHOT revisits the entire target session over
several epochs, it still trails the single-pass MOPC by 2.9 and
4.6 points on the two datasets. Thus, for nonstationary EEG,
the most recent B trials are more informative than global target
statistics. TENT updates the same BN affine parameters as
MOPC but uses only the current trial, and it gains far less over
EEGNet (1.2 and 0.6 points, against 5.0 and 5.4 for MOPC).
Removing the memory bank from MOPC reproduces this drop
(see Section III-F), identifying the memory-driven context as
the primary source of the gain. Against the strongest baseline,
T-Time, which treats trials independently, MOPC further raises
kappa on OpenBMI by 7.3 points. It also attains the lowest
cross-subject standard deviation of all ten methods (11.59)
while raising the mean, so the improvement is not carried by
a few well-performing subjects.

RH trials, and for OpenBMI we removed 50 % of LH trials.

to those used in the balanced setting. As reported in Ta-

imbalance, outperforming T-Time (which includes a marginal-
distribution regularizer intended for imbalanced test data) by
1.09 % and 3.05 % on BCI IV IIb and OpenBMI datasets,
respectively.
Because plain accuracy on an imbalanced set is dominated
by the majority class, Table IV should be read together with
the balanced results: the ranking of the two strongest methods
and of the direct-transfer models is preserved, and MOPC’s
margin over the strongest baseline stays close to its balanced
value on OpenBMI (3.05 versus 3.67 points) while narrowing

gain is retained rather than traded when the class prior

term drop in Table V on both datasets (−1.65 and −1.78
points), identifying the confidence-calibrated reweighting as
the component that counters the drifting prior.

IV. INTERPRETABLE ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION
A. Feature Visualization
To evaluate feature space adaptation, we applied t-SNE to
BCI IV IIb embeddings (subjects B04 and B06), as shown in
Fig. 4. Under DT [Fig. 4(a) and (c)], LH and RH imagery sam-
ples remain heavily intermingled due to severe cross-subject
domain shifts. On the contrary, by dynamically updating the
network’s BN statistics via our unsupervised hybrid loss,
MOPC effectively mitigates this variability. Consequently, the
adapted embeddings [Fig. 4(b) and (d)] exhibit clearly sepa-
rated LH and RH clusters. This sharpened decision boundary
confirms that our calibration-free approach produces highly
discriminative representations on unseen data, significantly
enhancing online classification robustness for real-world BCI
applications.

adaptation methods; a full multiclass benchmark against such
methods, with multiseed statistics, remains future work.
V. CONCLUSION
In this study, we presented MOPC, a memory-augmented
OTTA framework explicitly designed for robust EEG instru-
mentation in nonstationary measurement environments. By in-
tegrating perturbation consistency regularization, joint entropy
minimization, and confidence-calibrated reweighting, MOPC
enables efficient, source-free, single-pass cross-subject decod-
ing. Extensive experiments on two public MI-BCI datasets
show that MOPC consistently surpasses DT, DA, and state-of-
the-art TTA baselines while maintaining highly stable cross-
subject measurement reliability. Ablation studies confirm that
each loss term and architectural component is integral to
overall performance, contributing to robust and stable online
adaptation. Furthermore, model updates incur fewer than 60
ms of latency on a standard CPU, satisfying real-time BCI con-
straints. However, current limitations include empirical valida-
tion centered on binary classification (with a preliminary four-
class result confirming that the framework operates unchanged
and improves over source transfer), while the design extends
to multiclass decoding at O(1) adaptation cost and relies on
initial pseudolabel quality for effective reweighting. Future
work will investigate adaptive augmentation strategies, mul-
timodal physiological fusion, and comprehensive multiclass
benchmarking, spanning four-class MI and continuous control,
to further enhance robustness and practical applicability in
real-world BCI systems.
