DRAFT: DECODING AND RECONSTRUCTING
3D ROOM LAYOUTS FROM FMRI
AND CAMERA TRAJECTORIES
Chi-Wei Lee1, An-Kai Chen1, Cheng-Han Chiang1, Yi-Chun Chang2, Po-Chih Kuo1
1National Tsing Hua University
2National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University
ABSTRACT
While functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) decoders reconstruct im-
ages and 3D objects, recovering the metric geometry of rooms from a moving
viewpoint remains an open challenge. We introduce, to our knowledge for the
first time, the task of reconstructing 3D layouts of held-out scenes using only
the known camera trajectories and the fMRI signals of participants watching in-
door walkthroughs, without any visual frames. The task is hard because of the
temporal mismatch between rapid visual changes and blurred fMRI signals. Our
method, DRAFT (Decoding and Reconstructing 3D room lAyouts from fMRI and
Trajectories), extracts a compact layout representation from the early visual cor-
tex and fuses this neural code with the geometric free space derived from camera
motion. To evaluate it, we propose layout r, the Pearson correlation between pre-
dicted and ray-cast log depths. On ten held-out scenes (20 participants), DRAFT
reaches a layout r of 0.480, outperforming the camera path alone (0.446) and
seven fMRI decoders (at most 0.429). DRAFT also works as a plug-in that im-
proves all seven decoders without retraining, raising their layout r from 0.171–
0.429 to 0.487–0.493. Brain activity and camera motion can thus recover the
metric layout of unseen rooms, a first step toward reconstructing the 3D spaces
people see.

INTRODUCTION
Reconstructing a room needs more than a plausible picture. Walls must sit at the right distances, and
views taken from different places must describe the same space. Decoders of functional magnetic
resonance imaging (fMRI) now reconstruct images and videos (Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023; Scotti
et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023b), depth images (Gaziv & Irani, 2021; Xia et al., 2024) and isolated
3D objects (Gao et al., 2024), yet to our knowledge none has recovered metric room geometry as
the viewpoint moves. We ask whether fMRI responses to indoor walkthrough videos support layout
and surface reconstruction in scenes the model has never seen.
This is hard for three reasons.
The viewpoint changes every second, the blood-oxygen-level-
dependent (BOLD) signal of fMRI blurs several seconds together, and recordings are few. So we
do not decode depth maps directly. We decode a small layout code, 22 numbers per second that
describe wall distances, floor extent, openings and depth range. Ridge regressions, the readout,
predict it from early visual cortex (EVC) and camera motion. EVC responses map the image point
by point, so they carry image structure that correlates with depth. Scene-selective regions also
respond to room layout (Section 2), but in our data EVC predicted the code better (Section 5.2).
The camera path also helps. Every visited position is free space, just as steps through a dark room
show where the walls are not. We use it as a learned prior, not a bound on wall distance.
Our method DRAFT (Figure 2) combines both and, like an architect’s draft, fixes the coarse layout
first and the details later. It renders the code as coarse depth, and a small generator trained on real
readout errors adds detail to eight consecutive views. DRAFT then replaces the broad structure
(low spatial frequencies) of the views with a 3×3 DRAFT layout, predicted from the code and the
camera path, and places the views in 3D at the known poses.

Across ten held-out scenes, the DRAFT layout’s cell depths correlate with the true ones at 0.480
(layout r, Section 5.1). This is above the best of seven adapted fMRI decoders (0.429), the fixed
template (median training-scene depth, 0.324) and the generator alone (0.303). Most of this comes
from the camera path (0.446), but the brain adds to it. Adding the readout to the camera path raises
layout r in all ten scenes, and brain-input controls lower it in all ten (Section 5.2).
The 3D surfaces tell a weaker story (Section 5.4). Because ridge regressions shrink the depth range,
DRAFT ends with a range calibration, whose factor we fixed after earlier results on the same scenes.
Even so, unmodified MindEye and Brain-Diffuser have slightly better mean surface scores, and so
does the generator alone with a milder calibration. Guided by the DRAFT layout, these two image
decoders reach the best fMRI-based mean surface scores, within noise of their unmodified versions.
We contribute a readout of a layout code from EVC and camera motion, and the DRAFT layout,
which fuses this code with the camera path and raises the layout r of all seven tested decoders
without retraining (five post hoc). We also contribute a held-out-scene test of room geometry from
fMRI, with ray-cast ground truth, fMRI-free and wrong-scene references and brain-input controls.

RELATED WORK
Image, depth and video reconstruction from fMRI.
fMRI decoding reconstructs movies (Nishi-
moto et al., 2011) and images (Shen et al., 2019) through learned feature mappings (Kay et al.,
2008; Naselaris et al., 2011). The Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) (Allen et al., 2022) supports
diffusion-based image decoding (Takagi & Nishimoto, 2023a; Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023; Scotti
et al., 2023; 2024). MinD-Vis adds brain pretraining and MinD-Video extends it to video (Chen
et al., 2023a;b). Gaziv & Irani (2021) reconstruct depth and color images with monocular-depth
supervision, and DREAM decodes depth, color and semantics for image reconstruction (Xia et al.,
2024). We instead test metric layout in held-out scenes against ray-cast geometry.
3D decoding and scene-selective cortex.
MinD-3D reconstructs object meshes from fMRI of ro-
tating objects (Gao et al., 2024), and Neuro-3D reconstructs colored object point clouds from elec-
troencephalography (EEG) (Guo et al., 2025). Closest to our setting, Zeng & Cai (2024) decode the
depth structure of first-person driving videos in a held-out virtual town by matching decoded embed-
dings to those of the town’s own scenes. DRAFT instead predicts metric depth without the held-out

TASK, DATA AND EVALUATION
Task.
A participant watches a first-person walkthrough video of a furnished indoor scene during
fMRI. We split each video into routes of eight consecutive one-second views. For each route, the
model predicts eight depth maps and the 3D surface they form. Its inputs are the brain responses
and the camera path (position, heading and pitch in each second), never the video frames.
Data.
Twenty participants, 6 to 19 per scene, watched 80-second videos of ten furnished indoor
scenes (Table 4). Each scene was rendered in up to four interior styles that share the floor plan
and a similar camera path but differ in materials, colors and furniture. This gives 36 videos and
416 recordings (one participant watching one video). Each recording has 74 one-second samples,
because the last six seconds are left for the response delay. Viewing was free, with no fixation task
or eye tracking, and the same participants appear in training and held-out scenes. Ground-truth
radial depth (distance along each camera ray) is ray-cast from the 3D scene models at the recorded
poses (90° horizontal field of view, 48 × 64 pixels). Nine non-overlapping routes per video give
324 routes. Nuisance regression and z-scoring use whole scanner runs, which can mix training and
held-out scenes but never a depth target (Section A.1).
Protocol.
We use leave-one-scene-out cross-validation over the ten scenes. Every model behind
a test prediction is fitted without any style, recording or route of the held-out scene. Models that
take other models’ predictions as input are fitted on out-of-fold predictions: for each training scene,
models fitted without that scene predict it (Section A.2). At test time, predictions are averaged over
the recordings of the held-out video and over another style at matching poses, without depth labels
(Section 4.1). So results use pooled recordings, not single trials or single participants.
Comparisons.
We adapt seven fMRI decoders to our folds. Five are published: image and depth
decoding (Takagi & Nishimoto, 2023a;b), the DREAM depth branch (Xia et al., 2024), Brain-
Diffuser (Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023) and MindEye (Scotti et al., 2023). Two are simple ridge
decoders: linear depth decoding and retrieval of CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training)
image embeddings (Radford et al., 2021), like the retrieval branch of MindEye. The published
decoders keep their released components and original brain regions.
Depth Anything V2 (DA
V2) (Yang et al., 2024b), with its scale calibrated on the training scenes, turns decoded images into
radial depth. The decoders may read scene-selective regions and use large pretrained models, but
they see no camera input and decode each view separately. Trained on nine scenes, Brain-Diffuser
and MindEye identify the viewed frame only weakly (Section C). We also use two fMRI-free
references. The fixed template is the median training-scene depth, and the per-second median is this
median taken separately at each video second (Section G).
Metrics.
Our main metric, layout r, asks whether a prediction knows which parts of a view are
near or far. It is the Pearson correlation between the predicted and true median log depths of the
nine cells of a 3×3 grid over the view. Row order is the fraction of correctly ordered cell pairs
in the same row, and left/right the fraction of views whose deeper half is identified. Pixel MAE

Readout.
Six ridge regressions map EVC responses and 12 camera-motion regressors to the
code y, standardized on the training scenes. They differ in voxel count, ridge penalty and response
window, and their mean is the readout ˆyt (Equation (5)). EVC comprises V1, V2, ventral V3
(V3v) and human V4 (hV4), whose most reliable voxels are reduced to 16 principal components.
The regressors are a constant, speed and turning speed at lags of 0 to 4 s, and a linear trend. The
regressions are fitted to participant-averaged responses of the nine training scenes and applied to
each held-out recording.

Pooling across styles.
One recording is noisy, and other styles of a scene often pass the same
poses. So each second we average the readout of the evaluated recording with the participant-
averaged readout of at most one other style, weighted by recording counts. Pooling never uses depth
labels or pairs different seconds, but furniture may differ across styles (Section A.3). The pooled
readout of each code entry j is its posterior mean µj. Because readout reliability varies, each entry
also gets a posterior spread sj, its out-of-fold residual standard deviation (Section A.2). Together
they form a point estimate with an error bar, not a full Bayesian posterior.

Range calibration.
Ridge regressions shrink predictions toward the mean, so the cell log depths
of the DRAFT layout have only 0.49 of the true spread (generator 0.78). Before lifting, we therefore
map log Dt to m + a(log Dt −m), with m the video’s mean predicted log depth, which uses no
test depth. This map keeps layout r, row order and left/right except where depth is clipped. Inner
validation on each fold’s training scenes selects a = 2 in every fold by Chamfer distance, or 2 or
2.5 by F-score (Section G). We added this step and its selection after earlier results on these scenes.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
LAYOUT ACCURACY
The DRAFT layout is more accurate than every fMRI decoder that does not use it, even with
the camera path. Its layout r is higher than that of linear depth in seven scenes and of MindEye
and Brain-Diffuser in all ten (no sign-flip test, Table 5). Unlike these decoders, the fusion head is
fitted to the cell depths that layout r scores. Given the camera path through the trajectory layout,
each decoder still trails DRAFT by 0.024 to 0.032 (ten-scene means). It trails in eight to ten scenes
(sign-flip p ≤0.010), and this comparison is post hoc for five decoders (Table 5).
The gain is more than a good guess about indoor rooms. Indoor views share a common shape,
floor near and walls farther away, so a template gets part of the layout right. The DRAFT layout

5.3
GUIDING OTHER DECODERS AND AGREEMENT ACROSS VIEWS
Other decoders inherit the DRAFT layout. All seven decoders score best with it in all three
metrics of Table 2, with a 95% CI excluding zero in 17 of 21 cases, all but the surfaces of
Brain-Diffuser and MindEye. For Brain-Diffuser, it raises layout r from 0.324 to 0.493 in all ten
scenes, the highest fMRI-based value. The other six reach about 0.49 (five post hoc), above their
versions with the trajectory layout in all ten scenes (Section C.2). Layout r then mostly scores the
DRAFT layout (Section 4.3), so surfaces are the real test.
The DRAFT layout also makes the views agree. The DRAFT layout lowers the change in 3D error
between views 1 s apart for every scored depth source. Yet this measure also rewards views that are
wrong in the same way (Table 11 and Section F.2). DRAFT views agree more than Brain-Diffuser’s
with the DRAFT layout (mean change 0.63 against 0.81 m, all ten scenes, Section F.2). But linear
depth with the same layout, also decoded view by view, agrees as well or better (0.58 m).
5.4
EIGHT-VIEW 3D SURFACES
After range calibration, the DRAFT layout costs no measurable surface accuracy. Calibration
brings the cell log depths of DRAFT to 0.97 of the true spread. DRAFT has a lower Chamfer
distance than the generator alone (eight scenes), a higher precision (all ten) and a similar F-score
(Table 3). But its Chamfer distance is not lower than the generator’s at its own selected factor (1.5,
Table 15). The readout with a 3×3 target, without free space, reaches a similar F-score (0.485).
Without calibration, fusion lowers the F-score of the generator alone from 0.477 to 0.416 and raises
its Chamfer distance by 0.065 m in all ten scenes. The DRAFT layout also lowers the uncalibrated
F-score of the three decoders with the best unmodified surfaces, because ridge heads shrink the
depth range (Table 13 and Section C.2).

The calibration factor was fixed after earlier results on these scenes. Selecting the factor by
F-score instead of Chamfer distance picks 2 or 2.5 and lowers the held-out F-score of DRAFT to
0.477. At their Chamfer-selected factors, DRAFT, the generator alone, Brain-Diffuser and MindEye

Unmodified MindEye and Brain-Diffuser reach slightly higher mean F-scores than DRAFT
(0.494 and 0.492 against 0.484) and lower Chamfer distances (Table 2). Only the lower Chamfer
distance of MindEye holds beyond noise (nine scenes, Section G). Their detail comes from large
pretrained image models, ours from a small generator trained on nine scenes. Removing our detail
lowers the F-score by 0.042 in all ten scenes (Table 3), but this detail is mostly one pattern shared by
all scenes and correlates with the true detail at only 0.026 (Section G). So scene-specific geometry
comes mainly from the DRAFT layout. Against decoders with the trajectory layout, DRAFT is
within noise of CLIP retrieval, Brain-Diffuser and MindEye in F-score. It is ahead of the other
four, each in at least eight scenes (post hoc sign-flip tests, Table 5). With the DRAFT layout, Brain-
Diffuser’s detail raises recall over DRAFT and slightly lowers precision, in all ten scenes. This gives
the best fMRI-based mean F-score (0.499), within noise of both unmodified decoders (Section G).
MindEye with the DRAFT layout ties it post hoc and has the lowest Chamfer distance (0.376 m).
Surface scores separate the methods little, for two reasons. First, even a wrong scene scores
well. Depth predicted for another scene and lifted at the evaluated poses (post hoc) keeps most of
the F-score of DRAFT, Brain-Diffuser and MindEye. The correct scene adds only 0.055 to 0.069,
in all ten scenes each (Table 12, Figure 4f). Subtracting this wrong-scene score does not favor
DRAFT over MindEye (post hoc, Section I). Second, the scores reward depth range. DA V2 gives
decoded images nearly the true range, 0.84 of the true spread against 0.46 to 0.54 for our ridge
layouts (Section C.1). This raises recall even for a wrong layout.
Once depth scale is removed, DRAFT has lower depth error than the unmodified image
decoders, but this is a low bar. Scored without range calibration for every method, DRAFT
has a lower scale-invariant depth error (SI-log, Eigen et al., 2014) than the trajectory head, the
generator, Brain-Diffuser and MindEye in all ten scenes (sign-flip p = 0.002). After post hoc
median alignment of each view to the true depth, its F-score beats these four in nine or ten scenes
each. Yet Brain-Diffuser and MindEye have a higher SI-log than the fixed template (57.3 and 59.5
against 52.8, and 49.6 for DRAFT). Linear depth ties DRAFT, and Brain-Diffuser with the DRAFT
layout nearly ties it in SI-log and beats it after alignment. The fixed template, Brain-Diffuser and
MindEye also have more pixels within a factor of 1.25 of the true depth (Section F.1 and Table 10).
6
DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
To our knowledge, DRAFT is the first method to decode from fMRI the metric layout of rooms
seen from a moving viewpoint and to test it against ray-cast ground truth in held-out scenes. It reads
a layout code each second from EVC and camera motion, fuses it with the camera path and turns it
into depth maps and 3D surfaces. In ten held-out scenes, its layout r is 0.480, above seven adapted
fMRI decoders (at most 0.429) and the camera path alone (0.446), and brain-input controls lower
it in every scene. Used as a plug-in prior without retraining, the DRAFT layout raises the layout
r of all seven decoders to about 0.49 (five post hoc) and gives Brain-Diffuser and MindEye the
best fMRI-based mean surface scores. Its fMRI-free and wrong-scene references and brain-input
controls separate what the camera path, the brain and pretrained image models contribute.
Limitations. DRAFT needs known camera paths and pooled recordings, was developed on these
scenes with several analyses post hoc, and its brain gain was not measurable beyond low-level
image features and replicated within only two of five withheld scenes (Sections J.1 and J.2).
Outlook.
Designed routes, more participants and estimated camera paths can test single-trial
decoding in new scenes, and generative 3D scene models could turn the DRAFT layout into
complete room reconstructions.

A.1
DATA
Stimuli.
Each video is an 80-second first-person walkthrough of a furnished indoor scene at 30
frames per second. The four styles of a scene (modern, Scandinavian, wabi-sabi and minimalist)
share the floor plan and visit the rooms in the same order. Their camera paths are not frame-
synchronized, so pooling across styles only pairs videos whose poses agree at the same second
(Sections A.3 and 4.1). Seven scenes have all four styles, and the others two or three (Table 4).
Ground truth.
For every second we cast rays from the recorded camera pose through a 384×512
pinhole grid with a 90° horizontal field of view and record the radial depth to the first surface of the
3D model, not the optical-axis depth. Depth is stored at 96 × 128 and averaged in inverse depth to
48 × 64. Depth Anything V2 predicts optical-axis depth z, converted to radial depth r = z/uz (u:
unit camera ray) before downsampling. Pixels closer than 5 cm or without hits are invalid.

A.3
WHEN POOLING ACROSS STYLES APPLIES
Pooling helps only if partners exist and see similar depth, so we measure both. A video of another
style is a partner at a second only if the two poses agree within 5 cm in each position coordinate,
2° in heading and 0.5°/s in turning speed. Pitch is not constrained, and the largest pitch difference
is 1.092°. When several videos qualify, the one with the most recordings is the partner, ties broken
by video name, never by scores. A partner is used at 1,473 of 2,592 video-seconds (56.8%), from
0% to 92.7% by scene, and never in scene 9. The depths of the two partners differ by a median of
0.314 m (IQR 0.172 to 0.563 m). This mixes small pose mismatch with changes in furniture.

EVALUATION AND STATISTICS
The ten held-out scenes are the units of the bootstrap and sign-flip tests. Response windows of 2
to 6 s make neighboring views share BOLD samples, so per-view scores are not independent. For a
mean metric such as layout r or pixel MAE, we take the difference between two methods on every
route and average it within each scene. We then resample the ten scene differences with replacement
20,000 times and report the ten-scene mean with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles (paired scene boot-
strap). For rate metrics such as row order, each scene pools its eligible cell pairs before differencing.
Main-table layout and pixel-MAE point estimates instead pool all routes or pairs (route-pooled val-
ues), so they can differ slightly from ten-scene means. All intervals are descriptive 95% intervals
without correction for multiple comparisons. All ten scenes were used during development, and each
model was trained with one seed. Pose noise was tested at registration (Section F.3) and, post hoc, in
the camera-path inputs (Section I). Single recordings with the corrected generator were tested only
post hoc (Section I). Filmed real scenes, single-participant models and unobserved surfaces were not
tested. Each analysis uses one fixed bootstrap seed, released with the code. Intervals of the historical
models, trained before the loss correction (Section H), were route-weighted and are not combined
with the corrected contrasts.
Sign-flip tests.
Percentile intervals from ten scenes can be too narrow. For four main layout-
r contrasts we therefore also run an exact two-sided sign-flip test. The ten scene differences are
multiplied by all 210 sign patterns, and p is the fraction of patterns whose absolute mean is at least
the observed one. With ten scenes, the smallest possible value is 0.002. The contrasts are the
DRAFT layout minus the generator alone (p = 0.002), minus the trajectory head (p = 0.002) and
minus the fixed template (p = 0.004), and Brain-Diffuser + DRAFT layout minus Brain-Diffuser +
trajectory layout (p = 0.002). After Holm correction for these four tests, the largest value is 0.008.
The same test gives p = 0.002 for the four brain-input controls of Section 5.2: feature replacement
in layout r and in F-score at a = 2, the circular shift in F-score at a = 2, and EVC minus the
camera-motion regressors alone in cosine similarity. Each favors the intact input in all ten scenes.
All these contrasts were named after the results were known and were not preregistered.

C
BRAIN-DECODING BASELINES
All decoders share the folds, routes, targets and metrics of DRAFT, and every trainable part is fit on
the nine training scenes of each fold.

Protocol.
Decoders read participant-averaged responses, averaged over 2 to 6 seconds after each
view, from seven regions of interest (ROIs). These are V1, V2, V3v and hV4 in early visual cortex
and the scene-selective occipital place area (OPA), parahippocampal place area (PPA) and retros-
plenial complex (RSC). A method that prescribes early visual areas uses V1 to hV4, and one that
prescribes higher visual areas uses hV4, PPA, OPA and RSC. Each view’s video frame is resized to
the input size of each model without cropping. Reconstructed images are converted to metric depth
with Depth Anything V2 (DA V2) Metric Indoor Large (Yang et al., 2024b). It overestimates depth
on our rendered scenes by about a factor of 1.75, so we correct its scale with a log-affine map fitted
on the frames of the training scenes (DA V2 scale calibration). Predictions were frozen before scor-
ing under a written plan. The decoders read responses averaged over 418 recordings, and DRAFT
over 416. The two extra recordings (one participant, minimalist style of scenes 9 and 10) have no
second style to pool with. The trajectory layout uses no fMRI, and decoders with the DRAFT layout
combine the two sets of responses.
Methods.
Linear depth decoding maps fMRI to the first 64 principal components of log depth with
ridge regression (Hoerl & Kennard, 1970), a standard linear approach to fMRI decoding (Naselaris
et al., 2011). CLIP retrieval maps fMRI to the image embedding of CLIP (Contrastive Language-
Image Pre-training) ViT-L/14 (Radford et al., 2021) with ridge regression, a simple analogue of the
contrastive retrieval branch of MindEye (Scotti et al., 2023), and averages the depth maps of the ten
nearest training frames. Image decoding follows Takagi & Nishimoto (2023a). Early visual cortex
predicts the Stable Diffusion latent, and higher visual cortex predicts the text embedding of a cap-
tion. Takagi & Nishimoto (2023a) averaged the five human-written captions of each image in the
COCO dataset. Video frames have no captions, so we instead caption each frame with BLIP, an im-
age captioning model (Li et al., 2022). Image-to-image diffusion then generates the image (strength
0.8, 50 denoising diffusion implicit model steps, guidance 5.0). Depth decoding (Takagi & Nishi-
moto, 2023b) predicts hidden states of the dense prediction transformer DPT-large (Ranftl et al.,
2021), whose head outputs relative depth calibrated on the training scenes. Brain-Diffuser (Ozcelik
& VanRullen, 2023) predicts latents of a very deep variational autoencoder (VDVAE) (Child, 2021)
and CLIP image and text embeddings that guide Versatile Diffusion (Xu et al., 2023) (strength 0.75,
mixing 0.4). It keeps its released step that re-scales its predictions to their mean and spread over the
held-out videos. This step uses no labels, but it uses all held-out predictions at once (it is transduc-
tive). MindEye (Scotti et al., 2023) trains an MLP (width 1024, 64 epochs per fold) to predict CLIP
ViT-L/14 hidden states, and a low-level branch starts Versatile Diffusion image variation (strength
0.85). Our version omits the ConvNeXt contrastive term and uses the Versatile Diffusion autoen-
coder for low-level targets. We did not reproduce Gaziv & Irani (2021), full DREAM (Xia et al.,
2024) or MinD-Video. Our adaptations decode each view alone, so they cannot show that DRAFT
beats temporal fMRI-to-video decoders.
Regularization.
On the penalty grids of Takagi & Nishimoto (2023a) and of depth decoding, every
fold chose the largest penalty, so the best one could lie outside these grids. With these penalties
image decoding also produced textureless color fields. We therefore report the stronger “wider
ridge” variants, fit on a wider grid. For linear depth, a wider grid selected the same penalty.
Image similarity.
We test whether CLIP ViT-L/14 embeddings pick the viewed frame over another
frame of the same held-out scene. This two-way identification is 0.500 and 0.525 for the official and
wider-ridge versions of Takagi & Nishimoto (2023a), 0.627 for Brain-Diffuser and 0.624 for Mind-
Eye (chance 0.5). The grayscale structural similarity (SSIM) of the wider-ridge decoded images to
the viewed frame is 0.651, against 0.649 for another frame of the same scene. So the images are
indoor scenes that match their specific view weakly at best (Section K).

These controls ask what the brain adds to camera motion, which cortical input carries layout infor-
mation and whether the pipeline depends on it. None tests biological causation.

D.1
CAMERA MOTION, FREE SPACE AND EVC
One test of what EVC adds to camera motion keeps the motion input fixed. We fit the readout
with a 3×3 target, on the same folds, to the 12 camera-motion regressors alone (motion alone). It
reaches a layout r of 0.338 (route-pooled), and adding EVC raises it by 0.098 [0.053, 0.151], in
nine scenes (p = 0.004). A second comparison starts from the trajectory head on the 72 motion
features alone (layout r 0.390, Table 1). Two alternatives beat it by the same amount, each in eight
scenes: the readout with a 3×3 target from EVC and the 12 regressors, by 0.050 [0.015, 0.092]
(p = 0.025), and the trajectory head with free space added to the motion features, by 0.050 [0.022,
0.076] (p = 0.012). The readout also lowers pixel MAE by 0.117 m [0.061, 0.172] in eight scenes
(p = 0.010). The two read different motion inputs, so this comparison alone does not isolate EVC.
D.2
MATCHED-BUDGET COMPARISON OF CORTICAL REGIONS
To test the choice of EVC (Section 4.1), we compare EVC (V1, V2, V3v and hV4), scene-selective
regions (OPA, PPA and RSC) and their union under one readout fixed before scoring: 1,000 most
reliable voxels, 16 principal components, ridge penalty γ = 0.1 and a 2 to 5 s lag window. Voxel
ranking, principal components and standardization use only the nine training scenes of each fold.
Every readout includes the 12 camera-motion regressors, and a ridge on these alone is the reference.
The comparison tests the readout alone, without a noise ceiling.
EVC predicts the code best (Table 6). In cosine similarity, it beats the scene-selective regions by
0.160, in nine scenes, and these still beat motion alone by 0.059, also in nine scenes (intervals in
Tables 3 and 6). Adding the scene-selective regions to EVC changes little: the union minus EVC
is −0.0003 [−0.0030, 0.0018] in cosine similarity, and the union’s mean squared error is lower by
only 0.0010 [0.0002, 0.0021]. This is expected, because the union’s reliability ranking keeps 979 to
992 EVC voxels and only 8 to 21 scene-selective voxels per fold (PPA 5 to 18, OPA 0 to 3, RSC
0 to 2). The union therefore does not test a separate budget for the scene-selective regions, and no
comparison shows that a region is necessary. For every input, wall distances are the best predicted
of the four groups of entries, a property of these targets and this readout.
The responses used here average 25 and 37 recordings in scenes 9 and 10, against 24 and 36 for
DRAFT, so this is not a full-pipeline ablation with one cohort. Camera-motion regressors do not
control image motion from texture, occlusion and parallax, so it shows predictive information, not a
3D representation independent of image features (Shafer-Skelton et al., 2026).
Lag profile and circular-shift control.
A gain carried by the BOLD response should peak a few
seconds after the stimulus and vanish when the BOLD series is shifted. We refit the readout with
single-second lags from 0 to 6 s and, for the 2 to 5 s window, after circular shifts of the BOLD series
by 20 offsets from 10 to 70 s (Figure 5). The gain over motion alone is largest at 3 and 4 s, in all
ten scenes (0.205 [0.143, 0.266] and 0.204 [0.135, 0.271]). It is smallest at 0 s, at 0.054 [0.010,
0.091], in eight scenes. Some gain at 0 s is expected, because the layout code changes slowly and
the hemodynamic response is spread in time. The unshifted gain (0.219) exceeds all 20 shifted gains

and its pooling partner we set the scores of the EVC principal components to their training-scene
center and keep the fitted readout. Camera-motion regressors, model weights, pooling partners,
posterior spreads, trajectory layouts and reliability masks stay fixed. We recompute the posterior
means and everything that depends on them: coarse depth, uncertainty maps and DRAFT layouts.
With intact (unaltered) features, the layout r of DRAFT is higher by 0.0373 and its F-score at a = 2
by 0.0363, in all ten scenes. Its pixel MAE falls by 0.0418 m and its Chamfer distance by 0.0251 m,

are worse, although layout r and F-score improve. For the generator alone, the Chamfer change at

signal. The posterior spreads keep information from the intact fit, and centered features can combine

SURFACE ANALYSES
This section tests three explanations of the surface scores: depth range (Section F.1), agreement
across views (Section F.2) and pose drift (Section F.3).
F.1
DEPTH SCALE
To test whether the surface gap between methods is only a matter of depth range, we align each
predicted view to its ground truth in log depth before lifting. Median alignment matches the medi-
ans (Zhou et al., 2017). The log-affine alignment also fits a slope, so it undoes range calibration as
well (compare Ranftl et al., 2022). A least-squares slope shrinks noisy predictions toward the mean.
This fit therefore partly measures per-view correlation again, and even the fixed template reaches F-
score 0.575 under it. Both alignments use test depth and are post hoc. We also report three unaligned
pixel metrics: the scale-invariant log error (SI-log, ×100) (Eigen et al., 2014), the mean absolute
relative error (AbsRel), and the share of pixels within a factor of 1.25 of the true depth (δ<1.25).

F.3
POSE DRIFT DURING REGISTRATION
Camera poses of a new recording may be noisy, so we test how pose drift changes the surfaces, at
the registration step only. The predicted depth is fixed and uncalibrated (a = 1). Only the poses
that lift predicted points and merge them on the 5 cm voxel grid drift, and ground truth keeps the
clean poses. The drift is a horizontal translation (5, 10 or 20 cm), a yaw (1, 2 or 5 degrees) or both
(10 cm with 2 degrees). It grows linearly from zero at the first view to its final value at the eighth,
with five fixed seeds per route shared across levels and methods. Each method is compared with its
own surface under clean poses.
The primary measure, fixed in advance, is the change in the F-score of DRAFT under joint drift. It
is −0.026 percentage points [−0.100, +0.039], an interval that includes zero but does not establish
equivalence. The Chamfer distance of DRAFT rises by 0.35 mm [0.02, 0.67]. The largest effect,
20 cm of translation for the generator, lowers the F-score by 0.165 percentage points and raises
the Chamfer distance by 1.57 mm. Every mean change in every condition is below 0.2 percentage
points in F-score, precision and recall and below 2 mm in Chamfer distance (all conditions omitted;
Section K). These results do not show that upstream pose estimation or the full range-calibrated
pipeline (a = 2) is robust, and they do not rank the two methods. Section I perturbs the camera-path
inputs instead (post hoc).

The correction.
The penalty reads the layout code of a generated depth map q through a fixed
affine inverse of the renderer, R+
aff(q) = y∗+ J+ vec(q −q∗) with q∗= R(y∗). Here y∗is the
mean training layout code and J a central-difference Jacobian of R at y∗, mean eye height and
zero pitch. This inverse returns the code in its original units, but the posterior is standardized. The
correction standardizes the inverse like the posterior mean of each sampled recording, giving the
map Ai of Section A.2. We call checkpoints trained before this change historical and the retrained
ones corrected. The historical generator without brain input received a zero code, which need not
render as the mean training layout.
Effect on the results.
All ten folds were retrained with everything else unchanged. Predictions
were frozen before scoring, and no checkpoint or factor was chosen with the new scores. The
corrected generator has a lower layout r (0.303 against 0.369) but a lower pixel MAE and better
surfaces at a = 1. The DRAFT layout stays close to its historical value (0.480 against 0.486).
Table 13 lists the historical and corrected rows side by side. Range calibration raises every pixel
MAE. The main paper uses the corrected models throughout. An independent replay reproduced
surface scores to within 4.75 × 10−5 in F-score and 2.71 × 10−6 m in Chamfer distance.

PENALTY ABLATION
To test whether the penalty of Equation (2) helps, we retrained the corrected generators of all ten
folds without it (λ = 0) and compared them with λ = 0.1. Both versions share seed, data, archi-
tecture and evaluation. The primary measure, fixed in advance, was the Chamfer distance of the
generator at a = 1. Without the penalty it rose by 0.1032 m [0.0710, 0.1399], in all ten scenes, and

its F-score changed by −0.065 [−0.087, −0.045]. The surfaces of DRAFT also worsened, with
F-score changes of −0.021 (a = 1) and −0.022 (a = 2) in all ten scenes. The generator’s F-score
at a = 2 changed within noise.
The penalty does not help every measure. Without it, the generator’s layout r rose by 0.1172 (from

Fixed design settings and acquisition blocks.
To test whether choosing the fixed settings on these
scenes created the brain’s gain, we refitted the trajectory and fusion heads, with the same readout
inputs and generator, for 300 combinations of the trajectory window K (4, 8, 16, 37 and 73 s), the
trajectory penalty (10 to 104), the fusion penalty (1 to 104) and the low-pass width σ (2, 4 and 8
pixels) (Section K). DRAFT beats the trajectory head in all 300 settings, by 0.010 to 0.064 (median
0.039), in at least eight scenes in 293 settings. The other seven settings have seven positive scenes,
and six of them combine K = 73 s with a penalty of 104. The gain over feature replacement ranges
from 0.030 to 0.052 (median 0.041), in at least nine scenes in all 300 settings. The paper’s setting
does not favor the brain, because its gains (0.035 and 0.033) are at or below these medians. It does
favor both heads’ absolute scores. Only 16 of the 300 settings reach its DRAFT layout r of 0.480
(median 0.457, range 0.422 to 0.488), and the trajectory head is also near its best (0.446 against a
median of 0.415). The paper’s absolute layout r values are therefore optimistic by 0.02 to 0.03 for
both heads, but their difference is not. DRAFT stays above linear depth alone (0.429, not re-tuned)
in 273 of 300 settings, and all 27 failures have σ = 8. Split into the three acquisition blocks (scenes
1 to 4, 5 to 7 and 8 to 10), both brain-input controls and the gain over the path are positive in every
block. This follows from their being positive in all ten scenes, so it is not separate evidence, and
the blocks share most participants. The one block contrast that could fail, DRAFT minus linear
depth alone, is +0.094 and +0.035 in the first two blocks but −0.011 in scenes 8 to 10 (one of three
scenes). Refitting both heads without the held-out block keeps the gain over the trajectory head at
0.041, in all ten scenes (readout and generator not refitted). Over 55 settings of voxel count, penalty,
lag window and ensembles, the readout with a 3×3 target stays above its motion-alone version by
0.079 to 0.102 in at least nine scenes, but it is never above the trajectory head (best 0.442).
Where the brain’s gain arises.
To test whether the brain helps most where the camera path says
little, we define the path coverage of a view as the number of its 12 azimuth bins of 7.5° that the
camera visits within ±4 s, the window of the trajectory head. The primary test found no negative
within-scene association between coverage and the per-view gain of DRAFT over feature replace-
ment (mean Spearman correlation +0.042, negative in only three scenes). Descriptively, the gain is
0.013 in views whose path covers no in-view bin (five scenes positive) and 0.043 in the others (ten
scenes). The gain does not fill in where the path is blind.

Noisy camera paths.
At test time we added independent per-second jitter to horizontal position
and heading, at five levels from 5 cm and 1° to 1 m and 20° (10 seeds each). For scale, the clean
camera moves 0.33 m and turns 14° per second on average. The jitter enters the trajectory features,
the readout’s camera-motion regressors and the fusion head, all trained on clean paths. Pooling
partners and masks come from the clean path. The primary test, that the gain over the trajectory
head grows with noise, was not supported (slope −0.0006, p = 0.81). The gain rises from 0.035 to
0.063 at 0.5 m and 10° (ten scenes at each level) and falls to 0.013 at 1 m and 20° (seven scenes).
DRAFT itself is not robust. Its layout r falls from 0.473 to 0.468, 0.460, 0.419, 0.284 and 0.083,
about as fast as that of the trajectory head (0.438 to 0.070). From 0.5 m and 10° on, DRAFT is
below the readout with a 3×3 target, which keeps 0.393 and 0.240 (differences −0.109 and −0.157,
no scene favoring DRAFT), and below a version of itself without the path input (0.405). The fusion
head relies on the path, so a path this noisy is worse than none. The gain over feature replacement
stays positive at every level (0.025 to 0.053, ten scenes each), as expected, because the brain term is
the same at every level. The generator detail was not resampled. Section K summarizes these results
and the checks with re-matched pooling partners, cumulative drift and heads refitted on noisy paths.

0.055 for MindEye. Their difference, +0.014 in six scenes, comes mostly from scenes 4 and 6,

below that of MindEye (−0.018, four scenes, added after the result). The F-score of DRAFT minus
that of MindEye is +0.005 at 10 cm (seven scenes), −0.010 at 25 cm (four), −0.020 at 50 cm (one)
and −0.011 at 1 m (three). The primary test, that DRAFT gains on MindEye at coarser thresholds,
therefore went against the hypothesis (slope −0.0053 per doubling, Holm-adjusted p = 0.049), with

pipeline, the layout makes the surfaces more scene-specific than the generator alone calibrated in the

extrapolated.
