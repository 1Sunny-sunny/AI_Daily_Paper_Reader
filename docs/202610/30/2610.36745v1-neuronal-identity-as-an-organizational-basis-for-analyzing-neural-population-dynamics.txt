Abstract
Individual neurons exhibit diverse molecular, morphological, and electrophysi-
ological properties that shape their distinct roles in neural circuits. While these
multimodal features are largely shared across individuals, they remain an underuti-
lized source of information in analyzing neural population dynamics. We posit that
these shared biological profiles provide a common reference for relating neural
dynamics across subjects and recording sessions. Our framework leverages this
structure by routing neurons into latent groups based on their biological profiles and
analyzing population dynamics through group-level representations. Because these
assignments are anchored in biological properties that are consistent across subjects,
the learned organization supports transfer to unseen animals without retraining or
neuron-wise correspondence. We evaluate our framework on whole-brain calcium
imaging in larval zebrafish and large-scale electrophysiology in the mouse visual
cortex. Across both species and recording modalities, the framework delivers
state-of-the-art performance in both neural decoding and manifold analysis: it
achieves superior decoding of behavior and stimulus labels while better preserving
the temporal consistency and local geometric structure of latent trajectories on
unseen data. Our experiments demonstrate that not only does providing biological
features improve baseline performance, but our proposed framework achieves even
stronger results by using these properties to explicitly structure the population
rather than treating them as auxiliary inputs. Together, these results suggest that
organizing neural populations by their multimodal biological features offers a
promising framework for generalizing population dynamics across subjects and
recording sessions.

Introduction
Recent advances in large-scale neural recording and analysis have opened new opportunities for
studying how neural populations give rise to perception, action, and cognition, and have spurred a
growing body of deep learning approaches for analyzing population dynamics [1–8]. Yet existing
approaches still struggle with generalization across individuals. Every recording captures a different
set of neurons, and activity patterns shift with recording conditions, internal states, and individual

variability [9, 10]. Because the recorded populations themselves differ across animals, there is no
guaranteed correspondence between the neural populations, presenting a significant challenge for the
direct transfer of learned representations to unseen individuals.
The multimodal biological features of individual neurons, or neuronal identity, represent a com-
plementary source of information that has remained largely underutilized in population dynamics
analysis. Neurons can be characterized along multiple biological axes, including transcriptomic,
regional, and electrophysiological features, which techniques like Patch-seq integrate at single-cell
resolution to link molecular identity with distinct functional roles in neural circuits [11]. For example,
in the mouse visual cortex, Pvalb, Sst, and Vip interneurons play specialized, non-redundant roles
in gain control, novelty detection, and state-dependent modulation [12], illustrating how neuronal
identity shapes individual contributions to circuit computation [13]. Large-scale transcriptomic
studies further demonstrate that neurons close in molecular profile participate in related circuit
computations [14–17]. Importantly, these features are largely shared across individuals of the same
species [15], suggesting they may offer a more stable basis for analyzing population dynamics across
individuals.
We propose to exploit this structure through an identity-guided, permutation-invariant framework for
analyzing neural population dynamics. Neurons are routed into latent groups based on their biolog-
ical profiles, including transcriptomic, regional, and electrophysiological features, and population
dynamics are analyzed at the group level through shared parameters conditioned on these features.
Because neuron ordering in a neural recording dataset is arbitrary and varies across recordings,
the framework should also be permutation-invariant. Standard permutation-invariant architectures
such as DeepSets [18] achieve this by treating all neurons as interchangeable units through shared
element-wise transformations followed by symmetric global aggregation. Our framework instead
maintains permutation invariance by encoding each neuron’s identity and activity through shared
neuron-wise networks, then routing these representations into latent groups via assignment-weighted
aggregation. Because these group assignments are determined by biological properties that are stable
across individuals, the learned organization can be applied to unseen animals without retraining or
neuron-wise correspondence.
This design contrasts with existing approaches for analyzing neural population dynamics. Recent
large-scale models, such as POYO [9] and NEDS [19], achieve cross-session transfer by learning
session-specific embeddings and relying on statistical regularities in observed dynamical patterns
to bridge recordings. Neural manifold analysis methods such as CEBRA [3] and MARBLE [4]
learn low-dimensional embeddings of population dynamics that reveal how neural circuits represent
and transform information, providing an approach for relating population activity to behavior and
perception. However, because these approaches rely primarily on activity patterns, they often struggle
to generalize to new animals or sessions. Our framework instead grounds each neuron in a common
biological coordinate, thereby inducing a shared group structure that supports transfer to held-out
animals and sessions without any neuron-wise correspondence.
We evaluate our framework on two large-scale datasets spanning distinct species, recording modalities,
and available biological features: a whole-brain larval zebrafish calcium imaging dataset that jointly
acquires neural activity and single-cell transcriptomics [20], and a large-scale mouse visual cortex
Neuropixels electrophysiology dataset [21]. Across both settings, our framework achieves state-of-the-
art cross-session behavior and stimulus decoding when applied directly to held-out recordings without
retraining or neuron-wise correspondence. While supplying baselines with these same neuronal
identity features improves their performance, our identity-guided routing framework consistently
outperforms all baselines. These results demonstrate both the inherent value of multimodal biological
features of individual neurons and the critical importance of explicitly using them to organize
population dynamics rather than just treating them as auxiliary inputs. Our contributions are as
follows:
• We introduce, to our knowledge, the first neural population analysis framework that leverages
multimodal biological features of individual neurons to support direct transfer across animals
and recording sessions without retraining or neuron-wise correspondence.
• We propose an identity-guided routing method that uses multimodal biological features
as the organizing basis to assign neurons to latent groups, thereby analyzing population
dynamics at the group level through shared parameters.

Method
We seek a framework for analyzing neural population dynamics that transfers across sessions without
neuron-wise correspondence. The core challenge is that neurons cannot be matched across animals
by recording index because neuron order in a neural recording dataset is arbitrary and changes
across animals and sessions. Neuronal identity provides a shared description of each neuron across
individuals. Neurons with high overlap in neuronal identity tend to support similar circuit roles even
across individuals, and can serve as a stable basis for organizing the population. This leads to the
following two properties:
• Permutation invariance. The model output should be independent of how recorded neurons
are ordered.
• Identity-based organization. Neurons with shared properties should be considered together,
reflecting the group structure observed in biological neural circuits.
Permutation-invariant models treat the neural population as an unordered set, requiring aggregation
across neurons in a way that does not depend on their ordering [22, 18]. A global sum or mean over
all neuron representations achieves this, but treats all neurons identically regardless of their neuronal
identity, discarding the organizational structure that identity encodes. This motivates an aggregation
organized by neuronal identity, where neurons with overlapping identity profiles are pooled into
a shared group representation, contributing to population dynamics through a common functional
organization. We therefore analyze population dynamics through two components:
1. Identity-guided routing. Each neuron is assigned to a latent group on the basis of its
neuronal identity features.
2. Group-level dynamics analysis. Neural activity is aggregated into latent group representa-
tions, which are then processed to obtain the final population state.
Because group assignments are determined by neuronal identity, which is shared across individuals,
the learned organizational structure can be applied across subjects and sessions without retraining or
neuron-wise correspondence. Section 2.1 specifies each stage in detail.

3
Experiments
We evaluate on two datasets spanning different species, recording modalities, and degrees of richness
of available neuronal identity information (Section 3.1). We compare against three decoding baselines
and four manifold methods (Section 3.2), and assess performance under direct transfer to held-out
animals and sessions without retraining using decoding and geometric metrics (Section 3.3).

3.1
Datasets and Experimental Setup
Whole-brain Neuronal Activity and RNA Profiling (WARP).
The WARP dataset [20] provides
whole-brain calcium imaging and single-cell transcriptomics from three larval zebrafish at 6 days
post-fertilization, each with approximately 77,000 neurons registered to the mapzebrain atlas [23].
Recordings span approximately 70 minutes at 2 Hz and include structured visual stimuli such
as grating in four directions, bright and dark flashes, and bilateral looming. Stimulus labels are
derived from the visual stimulus conditions and used as targets for stimulus classification, whereas
behavioral labels are derived from fictive swimming signals recorded through extracellular motor
nerve measurements and used as targets for behavior decoding (Appendix A.1, Figure A3).
To construct multimodal biological features, we use each neuron’s transcriptomic expression profile,
three-dimensional position, anatomical region, and electrophysiological features motivated by met-
type profiling [11]. Because jointly acquired gene expression and neural activity data are difficult to
obtain in practice, we additionally evaluate our framework using independently acquired, public gene
expression data from the mapzebrain atlas. This atlas provides hybridization chain reaction (HCR) in
situ RNA profiles [23, 24], which we assign to each neuron based on its atlas coordinates. Feature
construction details are provided in Appendix A.3.
Allen Visual Coding Neuropixels.
The Allen Visual Coding Neuropixels dataset [21] provides
large-scale extracellular electrophysiology recordings from the mouse visual cortex. We use both
available datasets: the Brain Observatory dataset with 26 sessions and the Functional Connectivity
dataset with 25 sessions, comprising over 80,000 units in total. Each session uses six Neuropixels
probes to record spiking activity, with unit locations registered to the Allen Common Coordinate
Framework [25]. Recordings include high-frequency spiking activity over varied durations and cover
structured visual stimuli such as drifting gratings, static images, and natural scenes, grouped into five
functional classes per dataset. These stimulus categories are used as targets for stimulus classification,
and behavioral data consist of running speed, which serves as the target for behavior decoding

(Appendix A.1, Figure A3). This dataset allows us to test whether our framework improves held-out
transfer performance even when molecular profiles are unavailable, utilizing only the remaining
neuronal identity features described in Appendix A.3.
3.2
Baselines
First, we evaluate neural decoding performance by comparing our method to three decoding baselines:
POYO [9], NEDS [19], and DeepSets [18]. POYO uses cross-attention over tokenized neural events,
relying on learned session and unit embeddings to aggregate activity across recordings. NEDS uses
a transformer with a multi-task masking strategy over neural activity and behavior. Both methods
operate across sessions without requiring neuron-wise correspondence, but rely on fine-tuning for
transfer to new sessions. DeepSets serves as the permutation-invariant baseline, aggregating neuron-
wise activity representations by summation without identity-guided routing. Full details of the
baseline adaptation and identity feature integration are provided in Appendix A.6. To isolate the
effect of the group-routing architecture from access to multimodal biological features, each baseline
is evaluated in two variants. The first is its original form, and the second is an augmented form
that receives the same per-neuron identity features as our method, with all other training details
unchanged. Concretely, neuronal identity features are incorporated into neuron-level embeddings
(Appendix A.10).
Second, we evaluate neural manifold analysis by comparing the manifolds learned from our method
with those produced by CEBRA [3], MARBLE [4], LFADS [26], and Joint PCA. CEBRA learns
low-dimensional embeddings of neural population activity through contrastive learning. MARBLE
models local flow fields over the manifold structure using geometric deep learning. LFADS infers
single-trial population dynamics through a sequential variational autoencoder. Joint PCA provides
the linear baseline, projecting jointly concatenated population activity from source animals into a
shared low-dimensional space. Full details of this adaptation are provided in Appendix A.8.
3.3
Evaluation metrics and protocol
To evaluate decoding performance, we report the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) for behavior
regression and accuracy scores for stimulus classification, both evaluated on held-out data.
To assess the neural manifold analysis, we quantify how the latent representation preserves the
temporal geometry of population dynamics on held-out data. We compute three metrics on the latent
trajectory {zt}T
t=1 that evaluate global temporal organization and local structure (Appendix A.4).
• Temporal Distance Correlation (↑) measures the Spearman correlation between pairwise
latent distances ∥zt −zs∥2 and temporal distances |t −s|. Higher values indicate that states
nearby in time remain close in latent space while temporally distant states separate.
• Normalized Latent-Neighbor Time Gap (↓) measures the temporal distance to each
point’s nearest neighbor in latent space, normalized by recording length and averaged over
time. Lower values indicate higher temporal consistency in local latent neighborhoods.
• Local Trajectory Linearity (↑) measures the fraction of variance explained by the first
principal component within short sliding windows of the latent trajectory [27]. Higher
values indicate smoother local dynamics.
Held-out transfer protocol.
We evaluate transfer at the level of complete held-out recordings.
All methods are trained exclusively on source animals or sessions and then applied directly to the
held-out recordings. During inference, learned parameters are strictly frozen. At evaluation time, the
entire held-out recording is analyzed, leveraging all available data to construct the per-neuron identity
features prior to inference (see Appendix A.3 for construction details). All behavioral and stimulus
labels from the held-out recordings are used only to compute the reported evaluation metrics. To
ensure fair comparison, this exact transfer protocol is maintained across all baseline evaluations.
For the WARP dataset, we train on two fish and evaluate on the remaining fish. We average these
results across all three held-out combinations and five random seeds. For the Allen mouse visual
cortex electrophysiology datasets, we train on a fixed set of 20 sessions and evaluate independently on
the remaining held-out sessions (six for the Brain Observatory and five for the Functional Connectivity

Results
4.1
Behavior and Stimulus Decoding Under Direct Held-Out Transfer
Table 1 reports behavior and stimulus decoding performance under held-out transfer to unseen
animals or sessions without retraining across all three datasets. Our framework achieves the best
performance across all datasets and tasks, outperforming prior methods in all cases. On the WARP
dataset, our method achieves a PCC value of 0.793. On the Allen Brain Observatory and Functional
Connectivity datasets, our method yields PCC values of 0.734 and 0.694, respectively, marking the
highest performance in each setting.
Next, we investigate whether the performance gains stem simply from the inclusion of neuronal
features, or from how our framework explicitly utilizes them. Accordingly, we provide POYO, NEDS,
and DeepSets with the same multimodal biological features, leaving their original implementation de-
tails unchanged (Appendix A.6). Across datasets, these identity-augmented baselines show consistent
improvements over their original counterparts. Behavior decoding on the WARP dataset increases
from 0.304 to 0.489 for POYO, from 0.248 to 0.574 for NEDS, and from 0.160 to 0.466 for DeepSets.
Similar trends are observed for stimulus decoding and for the Allen datasets, where identity features
are based mainly on position and anatomical region (Appendix A.10). However, their performance
remains below that of our method across all datasets. This pattern demonstrates that our framework’s
superior performance cannot be fully explained by just the availability of these biological features.
Instead, the key advantage lies in how they are incorporated through identity-guided organization.
We further assess our framework’s robustness to the choice and availability of identity modalities by
evaluating ablations where we remove one modality at a time, reported in Appendix A.11. Across
both datasets, all ablated variants remain competitive and continue to outperform baseline methods,
showing that the performance gains are not attributable to any single modality. The model without
transcriptomic descriptors achieves a behavior PCC of 0.727 and a stimulus balanced accuracy of
0.711, compared with 0.793 and 0.835 for the full model. This suggests that the framework remains
competitive even when costly jointly acquired transcriptomic measurements are unavailable.

Related Work
Neural population dynamics analysis methods.
Recent work in neural population dynamics has
increasingly explored the use of deep neural networks [28, 29]. Methods such as POYO, POCO, and
NEDS learn representations that aggregate neural activity across recordings through tokenization
or latent mapping, enabling cross-session and cross-task decoding without requiring neuron-wise
correspondence [9, 19, 30, 31]. Across these methods, neurons are represented through learned,
session-specific representations derived from observed dynamics and implicit neuron indexing,
without explicit grounding in biological identity. While some recent work has begun to explore
learning neuron identity from population context [10], cross-session generalization still primarily
relies on statistical regularities in observed dynamics rather than on molecular or spatial information
that makes neurons comparable across individuals.
Neural manifold methods.
A central observation in neuroscience is that the high-dimensional
activity of neural populations often lies on low-dimensional manifolds whose geometry reflects the
structure of the underlying dynamics [3, 4, 26]. Methods for characterizing these manifolds include
sequential variational autoencoders, contrastive embedding approaches that align neural activity
with behavioral or stimulus variables [3], and geometric deep learning methods that analyze local

flow fields over manifold structure [4]. These approaches have become standard tools for relating
population-level dynamics to cognition, perception, and movement [28, 29].
Multimodal neuron profiling and cell-type atlases.
Recent work in systems neuroscience shows
that neurons can be characterized along multiple biological axes. Multimodal profiling approaches,
including Patch-seq and spatial transcriptomics, enable large-scale measurement of molecular and
spatial properties [11, 17, 32–35]. Large-scale atlas efforts have produced spatially resolved gene
expression maps and anatomical reference frameworks at cellular resolution across whole-brain
volumes [14–16, 23, 24, 36]. More recent datasets jointly profile or align neural activity with
transcriptomic identity within the same brain [17, 20]. Across species, multimodal measurements
show that molecular and spatial identity are associated with functional circuit organization [13,
37]. This growing availability of per-neuron identity information provides a natural foundation for
incorporating biological structure into methods for analyzing population dynamics.

Discussion
We propose multimodal biological features as an organizational basis for analyzing neural population
dynamics. Our method incorporates these features to route neurons into latent groups and analyze
dynamics at the group level. This design preserves invariance to neuron order while enabling neurons
with similar biological context to contribute through a shared functional organization. As a result,
populations from new animals or sessions can be mapped into a common identity-defined structure
without requiring neuron-wise correspondence or retraining.
Across zebrafish and mouse visual cortex datasets, our method achieves strong behavior and stimulus
decoding while producing geometrically consistent neural manifolds under direct held-out transfer.
Baseline methods also improve when provided with the same neuronal identity features, yet our
routing-based framework consistently outperforms them. This suggests that effective generalization
depends not only on the availability of neuronal identity information, but also on how it is used to
impose a shared organization on population dynamics.
Our method also remains effective when identity features are not jointly recorded with neural activity,
performing comparably when using independently acquired atlas data. This expands applicability to
9


existing datasets where neural activity is available but neuron-level biological feature measurements
are not collected in the same animals.
The performance of the method is shaped by the availability and quality of identity features. Joint
measurements of neural activity and transcriptomic identity remain limited [17, 20, 32, 33, 35],
and using independently acquired atlas data introduces additional challenges in registration [38–40].
Variability across individuals and imperfections in alignment can introduce noise into identity features,
affecting group assignments. Improvements in multimodal registration and joint data acquisition are
therefore likely to further strengthen identity-based population analysis.
More broadly, these results highlight shared biological structure as an underexplored signal for neural
population analysis. Neuronal identity provides a complementary axis for organizing population
representations across individuals, suggesting a path toward methods that can integrate data across
subjects without requiring exact neuron-wise correspondence.

A.5
Implementation Details for the Proposed Method
All experiments use the identity-guided population method described in Sec. 2.1. At each evaluation
time point, neuronal activity and neuronal identity features are provided as input. Routing weights
are computed only from neuronal identity features, and neuron-level activity representations are
aggregated into latent groups using the resulting identity-guided routing weights. The group represen-
tations are processed by the group interaction module Γ. Here, Γ is implemented as an MLP mixer
over the latent group axis, whose output dimension is set by the corresponding objective, following
the common contrastive manifold learning setup of CEBRA [3].
The same architecture is used for supervised decoding and manifold learning, with task-specific
output dimensions, training objectives, and hyperparameters (Tables A2-A3). For behavior decoding,
Γ outputs one scalar. For stimulus decoding, Γ outputs a vector whose dimension is the number of
stimulus classes. For manifold learning, Γ outputs a three-dimensional latent vector zt ∈R3, which
is ℓ2-normalized before applying the contrastive objective.
When a recording contains more neurons than the specified neurons-per-subset value in Tables A2–A3,
we apply the model to uniformly sampled neuron subsets. During training, one subset is sampled
per minibatch. During evaluation, we use a fixed set of sampled subsets and aggregate their outputs:
predictions are averaged for behavior regression, class predictions are combined by majority vote for
stimulus classification, and latent states are averaged for manifold analysis. All reported metrics are
computed from these aggregated outputs. The same subsampling and evaluation ensembling protocol
is used for all decoding baseline methods.

POYO [9] and NEDS [19] were originally developed for spike-based neural recordings. In this work,
we evaluate these baselines on datasets with different signal modalities, including calcium signals
in the WARP dataset and spiking activity in the Allen datasets. For each dataset, we adapt only the
input representation to match the signal modality, while preserving the original architectural design
of each model as much as possible.
Calcium-based Input for the WARP dataset.
For NEDS, we extend the input stage to process
continuous calcium traces. Calcium activity is first divided into fixed-length temporal windows,
yielding a neuron-by-time representation within each window. Each neuron’s calcium signal is
embedded into a latent space and concatenated with the corresponding neuron identity embedding.
Since this representation includes both neuron and temporal dimensions, we use an attention-based
adapter to aggregate neuron-level representations into temporally indexed tokens aligned with the
input structure of NEDS. The resulting tokens are then passed to the original NEDS transformer
encoder and task-specific decoding heads, yielding a calcium-adapted NEDS.
For POYO, we use a POYO+ [30] style calcium input representation. Specifically, neuron identity
embeddings are added to the original unit embeddings, and calcium value embeddings at each
time point are added to these representations. This allows POYO to process continuous calcium
observations while retaining its token-based architecture, resulting in a calcium-adapted POYO.
Spike-based Input for the Allen Datasets.
The Allen datasets consist of spike recordings, and
thus both POYO and NEDS are used in their original spike-based formulations without modifying
the input representation.
To incorporate neuronal identity information, we extend each model in a manner consistent with
its original design. For NEDS, neuron identity embeddings are combined with neural activity
embeddings before being processed by the model. For POYO, identity embeddings are included
with the unit embeddings from the original implementation. These unit embeddings were initialized
following the official implementation. These modifications allow both models to incorporate neuronal
identity information while preserving their original architectural assumptions.

A.7
Hyperparameter Settings for Decoding Baselines
POYO is implemented as a transformer with cross-attention and self-attention, using a token dimen-
sion of 64, 6 layers, 2 cross-attention heads, and 8 self-attention heads. NEDS is implemented as a
transformer encoder with a hidden size of 128, 3 layers, and 4 attention heads. In both models, neu-
ronal identity features are encoded as 32-dimensional embeddings produced by an MLP comprising
linear layers, an activation function, and layer normalization.
For training, the batch size is set to 64 across all experiments. POYO uses a learning rate of
3.125 × 10−5 for both WARP and Allen datasets, while NEDS uses 3 × 10−4 for WARP and
3 × 10−5 for Allen.
A.8
Manifold Baseline Implementation Details
We evaluated CEBRA, MARBLE, joint PCA, and LFADS under the same held-out transfer protocol
defined in Section 3.3. Unless otherwise stated, all baseline embeddings were three-dimensional and
were evaluated on the same held-out animals or sessions as the proposed model.
For CEBRA, we used the official CEBRA Python implementation. We trained a time-conditional
CEBRA encoder on each source recording and applied the trained encoder directly to the held-
out recording without refitting. We used the offset1-model architecture with time_offsets=1,
output dimension 3, learning rate 3 × 10−4, and temperature 1.0. WARP transfer runs used batch
size 512 and 10,000 optimization iterations; Allen runs used batch size 256 and 2,000 iterations.
For MARBLE, we used the public MARBLE implementation. Neural activity was first normalized
and projected to 10 principal components. We used the following setup: k = 60, 64 eigenvectors, and
inner_product_features=False. MARBLE was trained for 160 epochs on the source recording.
Held-out activity was transformed using the source PCA basis and the trained source MARBLE
checkpoint.
For joint PCA, we concatenated source activity along time, fit PCA on the source recordings only,
and applied the resulting projection to held-out activity. LFADS baselines used precomputed LFADS
latent trajectories from the corresponding LFADS training environment; no LFADS parameters were
refit during manifold comparison. For source-encoder baselines on Allen, metrics were averaged over
source-session encoders.

A.10
Effect of Neuronal Identity on Decoding Baselines
To evaluate the contribution of neuronal identity, we incorporate multimodal biological features into
baseline models. Details of the implementation and input construction are provided in Appendix A.6.
For each baseline, we compare the original model with a variant that incorporates per-neuron identity
features. Training is performed only on source data, and models are evaluated on held-out recordings
without any fine-tuning.
Baselines with identity features are distinguished based on the composition of multimodal biological
features used. For the WARP dataset, we utilize transcriptomic profile (T), spatial position (S), HCR
gene expression marker (H), anatomical region (R), and electrophysiological features (P), denoted as
POYO + (T, S, H, R, P) and NEDS + (T, S, H, R, P). In contrast, for the Allen datasets, we use spatial
position (S), anatomical region (R), and electrophysiological features (P), denoted as POYO + (S, R,
P) and NEDS + (S, R, P).
As shown in Table A4, incorporating neuronal identity generally leads to improved baseline perfor-
mance across datasets. Notably, performance gains are observed for NEDS and POYO even under
the held-out transfer setting. This suggests that neuronal identity provides information that is useful
for transfer without requiring retraining or neuron-wise correspondence.

A.12
Best-Ordering Analysis for Manifold Baselines
Several manifold baselines operate on fixed input coordinates: their learned encoders, projection
matrices, or latent mappings are tied to the neuron indices used during source training. In held-out
transfer across animals, the target recording contains a different set of neurons, and no neuron-wise
correspondence to the source recording is available. As a result, applying these baselines to a held-out
animal requires assigning an arbitrary order to the target neurons.
To make this comparison more favorable to the index-dependent baselines, we do not rely on a
single target-neuron ordering. Instead, for each held-out recording and each baseline, we sample
1000 random permutations of the target neuron order, apply the source-trained baseline under each
ordering, and recompute the three geometric trajectory metrics. We then examine the full distribution
of metric values as well as the best value obtained across sampled orderings.
Figures A6–A8 show the resulting distributions. Across baselines and metrics, our method generally
outperforms the best sampled ordering. This indicates that the observed gap is not due to choosing
a single unfavorable target ordering for the baselines. Rather, the index-dependent baselines lack a
mechanism for mapping an unaligned held-out population into a stable shared coordinate system.
