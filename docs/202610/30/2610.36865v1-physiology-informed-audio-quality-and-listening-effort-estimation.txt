PHYSIOLOGY-INFORMED AUDIO QUALITY AND LISTENING EFFORT ESTIMATION
Niall McGuire1
Dimitra Emmanouilidou2
1University of Strathclyde, Glasgow, UK
2Microsoft Research, Redmond, WA, USA
ABSTRACT
Audio systems aim to maximise the listener’s perceived quality, tra-
ditionally evaluated through mean opinion scoring (MOS), a sub-
jective process that is slow, costly, and collected only after play-
back. Machine learning now predicts MOS directly from the audio,
but in generalising across participants it tends to discard the sub-
ject variance that defines individual perception. Neurophysiological
measures may offer a complementary signal taken directly from the
listener, objective, implicit, and continuous, though prior work has
relied on laboratory-grade EEG and fNIRS. We ask how practical
consumer-grade devices compare, and whether they might recover
population-level audio-quality trends and, when calibrated per lis-
tener, track that listener’s effort. Recording 32-channel laboratory
EEG alongside wearable EEG, fNIRS, and eye tracking from thirty
listeners, our results suggest commercial wearable and optical sens-
ing can decode audio quality at statistically significant levels and re-
cover listening effort per listener. These findings may point towards
platforms tuned not only to perceived quality but to the effort a clip
demands, read from the listener’s own response as they listen.
Index Terms— audio quality, EEG, fNIRS, wearable sensing
1. INTRODUCTION
Audio systems aim to maximise perceived quality, traditionally
evaluated through mean opinion scoring (MOS), where listeners
rate clips and scores are averaged into a single value. Whilst long
the industry standard [1], MOS has clear limits. A listener’s rating
depends on personal preference, prior experience, and a shifting
interpretation of the scale, producing substantial variability across
listeners and sessions [2, 3]. Due to this variance, it requires large,
costly panels, and as ratings are gathered only after playback, MOS
cannot support automated or adaptive evaluation [4].
Machine
learning methods address these limitations by predicting MOS
directly from audio, providing fast, reproducible, human-free la-
belling. Reference-based metrics such as PESQ [5] and SDR [6]
compare degraded audio against a clean reference unavailable dur-
ing live playback, while reference-free models such as DNSMOS
for speech [7] and Fr´echet Audio Distance for music and generated
audio [8] operate on the degraded signal alone.
However, these
methods still predict a single population-average score, discarding
the subject-specific variation that defines individual perception [3,4].
Neurophysiological measures provide a complementary source
of information by capturing the listener’s ongoing perceptual and
cognitive response to audio stimuli [9,10]. The most practical for ev-
eryday use are electroencephalography (EEG) and functional near-
infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), compact and movement-tolerant in

ways functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is not. EEG re-
solves activity at the millisecond scale, matching the temporal struc-
ture of perceived quality, while the slower haemodynamic response
of fNIRS may track effort or demand [3,11]. Prior work has shown
that speech degradations alter EEG responses and that physiologi-
cal measures can be used to estimate perceived speech quality di-
rectly [12, 13]. Recorded as the participant listens, such signals are
objective in not requiring the listener’s conscious judgement, im-
plicit and continuous, indexing perceptual and cognitive state with-
out interrupting for a score rating [3,4,14].
Physiology also exposes a dimension a single rating collapses
into one value: the cognitive effort the audio costs the listener. Two
clips given the same quality rating can demand very different pro-
cessing, and that hidden effort accumulates into fatigue and dis-
engagement over time [15, 16]. Pupil dilation grows with spectral
degradation even on trials that reach the same correct answer, a cost
a quality rating does not register [15, 17], and effortful listening to
continuous speech raises pupil dilation while lowering parietal alpha
power [18]. Additionally, pupillometry and EEG could each cap-
ture effort but perhaps different components of it [19]. And for a
real-time adaptive audio system, measuring quality and effort can
help personalise experiences to the listener, both in terms of quality
perception and effort to process [11,20].
This line of work, however, rests on laboratory-grade equip-
ment. From the early Berlin studies to recent EEG-MOS models,
high-density wet-electrode systems tether the listener to the labo-
ratory [3,4,12,14], with the strongest results reported from subject-
specific models. Whether perceived quality survives on low-channel,
dry-electrode consumer hardware, and whether quality and effort can
be decoded simultaneously, still remains unclear. Our contribution
is therefore narrow and feasibility-focused: a simultaneous compari-
son of laboratory and consumer sensing (wearable EEG, fNIRS, and
eye tracking) on the same listeners, and a joint treatment of audio
quality and listening effort. We do not claim physiology outperforms
signal-based metrics, which the evidence does not support [3]. We
address three questions:
• RQ1: to what extent can neurophysiological signals recover
audio quality?
• RQ2: does degraded audio increase listening effort, and can
that effort be decoded from physiology?
• RQ3:
how does decoding performance with consumer-
grade wearable EEG, fNIRS, and eye tracking compare to
laboratory-grade EEG?
To answer them we present listeners with graded-quality audio while
recording 32-channel laboratory EEG, wearable EEG, fNIRS, and
eye tracking, and relate the resulting features to the audio’s objective
quality labels and to each listener’s effort ratings, evaluated across
unseen and calibrated listeners.

2. METHODS
2.1. Participants, Apparatus, and Stimuli
Thirty listeners took part, of whom 21 completed the pre-session
questionnaire (16 male, 5 female, aged 33.3 ± 11.6 years, 9 native
English speakers, and 7 with prior listening-test experience). Re-
cruited participants reported no history of neurological disorder or
concussion within the preceding six months, and each received a
$10 gift card. The study held institutional ethics approval and all
participants gave written informed consent. All thirty were consid-
ered for every task, with the eligible sample reported per task since
a listener contributes only when a valid label and the relevant sensor
stream are both present.
We recorded four physiological streams from three devices, two
of them commercially available wearables. The laboratory reference
was a 32-electrode Brain Products LiveAmp at 256 Hz in the stan-
dard 10/20 montage. Wearable EEG and fNIRS came from a Muse
Athena headband, an off-the-shelf consumer device, providing the
four scalp channels TP9, AF7, AF8, and TP10 at 256 Hz and fNIRS
at 64 Hz from four source-detector pairs at 730 and 850 nm. Eye
tracking used a Pupil Labs Neon, a commercially available wear-
able eye tracker, recording binocular gaze and pupillometry at 200
Hz. All streams were synchronised through the Lab Streaming Layer
and stored as XDF, with stimulus onsets and offsets marked from the
presentation software. Audio was played over M-Audio Studiophile
BX8 near-field monitors, with no spatial manipulation beyond the
reverberation injected into a controlled subset of degraded clips, and
rating prompts shown on a 16-inch laptop, in a quiet, windowless
room with constant ambient lighting. Before playback, every clip
was loudness-normalised using A-weighting,and playback gain held
fixed across sessions, so the level did not covary with quality.
Stimuli were drawn from established MOS-annotated corpora,
NISQA for degraded speech [21] and ODAQ for music [22], span-
ning codec, additive-noise, reverberation, and packet-loss condi-
tions. The graded set was speech only, sampled to populate all five
objective MOS categories with the corpus MOS as the graded qual-
ity target. Separate extreme blocks presented clean and degraded
clips whose clean or degraded label defines the binary quality target,
with the degraded clips balanced at five per artefact type within each
content family. In total 144 clips were presented, 102 speech and 42
music, each about 10 ± 2 s long.
2.2. Procedure
Each session opened with anchor clips, one per artefact type and
MOS level, showing the range of degradations before rating began,
a step added after pilot ratings drifted toward extremes never actu-
ally encountered. Trials then fell into two families. On graded trials
the listener rated clip quality on a five-position scale and listening
effort on a separate rating scale, and on extreme trials they made a
categorical high or poor quality judgement alongside an ordinal ef-
fort rating. Throughout, a concurrent visual task tracking zero, one,
or three moving targets sustained engagement, its load randomised
independently of audio quality and its responses recorded but not
modelled here. Across both families the effort ratings supply the ef-
fort targets, whereas the quality targets come from the fixed corpus
MOS and the clean or degraded label, not the listener’s own rating, a
choice quantified in Table 4. Artefact type and MOS level were each
presented in randomised order.
2.3. Physiological Processing and Features
All streams were epoched from a two-second prestimulus baseline
and baseline-corrected, to the audio offset for EEG, gaze, and pupil-
lometry and to a fixed post-offset interval for fNIRS. EEG was line-
notched, band-pass filtered from 1 to 45 Hz, common-average refer-
enced, and cleaned of ocular and high-kurtosis components by Fas-
tICA [23], with laboratory-EEG trials after cap removal discarded.
fNIRS intensities were converted to optical density [24], motion-
corrected [25], band-pass filtered from 0.01 to 0.2 Hz, and sum-
marised over a fixed-latency window aligned to the canonical au-
ditory haemodynamic response, which peaks roughly five to seven
seconds after onset [26]. The Muse’s four forehead optodes sit over
prefrontal cortex rather than auditory cortex, so its fNIRS indexes
frontal engagement and listening effort more than auditory quality
processing directly, and carrying no short-separation reference, sys-
temic haemodynamics may mix with the cortical signal. The band-
pass and baseline attenuate but do not fully remove it, so we read
fNIRS as a feasible optical channel rather than a clean cortical mea-
sure. Pupil traces were validated against the device worn flag, gap-
interpolated, and expressed as proportional change from the prestim-
ulus median. Per EEG channel we took absolute and relative delta-
to-gamma band power, total power, spectral entropy, peak frequency,
and the upper triangle of a shrinkage channel covariance [27]. Each
fNIRS channel contributed amplitude, slope, area under the curve,
time to peak, and early, middle, and late means, and the eye tracker
gaze velocity, dispersion, and path length alongside pupil dilation
amplitude, slope, time to peak, and binocular agreement.
2.4. Prediction Tasks and Evaluation
We evaluate four tasks, two for audio quality (AQ) and two for lis-
tening effort (EF), each as a binary contrast (-2) and a five-point
target (-5). For quality, AQ-2 separates extreme clean from extreme
degraded clips by their objective label and AQ-5 regresses the five-
point corpus MOS. For effort, taken from the listener’s rating, EF-2
separates low (1–2) from high (4–5) ratings and EF-5 regresses the
five-point rating. The binary tasks are fit by minimising binary cross-
entropy and the regression tasks by minimising mean squared error,
each against the targets above.
We score binary tasks by balanced accuracy against a chance
level of 0.5, and continuous tasks by the Pearson correlation against
a null of zero and by the root mean squared error (RMSE) in rat-
ing units, reporting mean and standard deviation across outer folds
and testing significance for balanced accuracy and correlation only.
Within each outer training fold, model family and hyperparameters
were chosen by three-fold participant-grouped cross-validation over
SVM, elastic-net, XGBoost, MLP, and CatBoost, each searched over
standard ranges, adding PLS for regression and an OAS-covariance
[27] Riemannian tangent-space model [28] for EEG, with the outer
test fold never informing selection. Two outer regimes answer dif-
ferent questions. Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) holds out one lis-
tener entirely, measuring generalisation to unseen listeners, whereas
a pooled five-fold within-subject (WS) regime holds out a fifth of
each listener’s trials, so the score reflects subject-dependent predic-
tion. Its correlation is pooled across listeners within a fold rather
than averaged per listener, so a stable between-listener offset can
inflate it. Both regimes reuse the fixed clip set, so they establish
cross-listener transfer, not generalisation to unseen audio: because
the clips recur, a decoder may partly exploit stimulus-evoked re-
sponses rather than perceived quality, which a leave-one-clip-out
regime, not attempted here, would isolate. Relatedly, the corpus
MOS we regress is a population label, so decoding it need not mean

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Behavioural validation. We first check that listeners perceived the
intended quality and that it carried an effort cost. Their subjective
quality ratings track the corpus MOS closely (Pearson r 0.75, Spear-
man ρ 0.76), so each clip appears heard at its labelled quality, sup-
porting the corpus MOS as the modelling target. Mental-effort rat-
ings move the opposite way, rising as quality falls (r −0.57), so
degraded clips also seem to have demanded more effort even though
the two were collected as separate judgements (Figure 1).
Recovering audio quality (RQ1). Objective quality appears recov-
erable from physiology (Tables 1 and 2). On the binary contrast, full-
support wearable EEG, fNIRS, and eye tracking exceed chance for
unseen listeners at balanced accuracy 0.60 to 0.65, and again when
calibrated to the listener. The graded five-point target seems harder
but still tractable: correlations with the corpus MOS are positive and
significant, strongest for wearable EEG and fNIRS under LOSO (r
0.38 and 0.36, RMSE near 1.18 to 1.20 MOS points) and rising to
0.41 to 0.43 under subject-dependent prediction. Because the clip
set recurs across folds, these figures reflect recovery of the corpus
label under cross-listener transfer. The 32-channel laboratory EEG
appears the exception throughout, neither exceeding chance on the
binary task nor correlating with MOS for unseen listeners, a pattern
we return to below.
From laboratory to wearable sensing (RQ3). The wearable and
optical streams stay above chance in both regimes, whereas the 32-
channel stream does not exceed chance on the binary contrast for
unseen listeners. We cannot claim that the wearables outperform
laboratory EEG, because the comparison is additionally confounded
by the fewer trials of the laboratory stream, its different channel
count, a mid-study protocol change, and a wet-electrode cap harder
to hold at stable impedance than the dry wearables. The one below-
chance cell, laboratory EEG at 0.37, likewise need not be a result,
as train and test distribution shift, contact instability, or a pipeline
artefact could each produce it. The safer reading is that laboratory
recording carries real practical costs in this setting, while the wear-
able and optical streams still recover quality with adequate promise.
The matched condition equalises trials across all four modalities, and
there fNIRS is the only modality to exceed chance (0.65) and to cor-
relate with MOS (r 0.19) for unseen listeners, so we treat fNIRS as
the most consistent modality once support is equalised.
Decoding listening effort (RQ2). The behavioural analysis already
suggested that degraded audio raises rated effort. Tables 1 and 2 then
ask whether that effort is decodable, and point to recovery mainly
under subject-dependent prediction. Wearable EEG, fNIRS, and eye
tracking then appear to separate low from high effort and to track
the graded rating, eye tracking most strongly (r 0.37, RMSE 0.89).
Across unseen listeners the signal does not yet appear to generalise:
no modality decodes the five-point rating after correction, and only
fNIRS reaches significance on the binary contrast, with a small effect
at 0.52. Effort therefore seems to leave a physiological trace a model
can read once it has seen the listener, but not one that transfers to new
listeners here. One caveat applies even within listener: rated effort
correlates negatively with quality (r −0.57), so an effort decoder
may partly read the quality contrast. The concurrent visual load is a
second possible confound for the gaze and pupil streams, though its
level was randomised independently of quality and the added multi-
target load appeared on only a minority of trials, so we expect little

Supplementary analyses. Two descriptive checks probe the qual-
ity tasks. Within-subject figures are pooled across listeners within
a fold, as noted above, so they reward a shared population axis.
First, on the supervising label (Table 4), training on the objective
corpus MOS rather than the listener’s rating lowered pooled RMSE
against objective MOS in every case and raised the pooled correla-
tion, clearest under subject-dependent prediction, where r rose from
around 0.17 to between 0.41 and 0.43 for wearable EEG, fNIRS,
and eye tracking. A fuller model search sat behind the objective
arm, so we read this as an upper bound rather than a controlled con-
trast. Second, this pooled gain appears to be shrinkage rather than
denoising. Objective-trained predictions vary far less than the raw
ratings among clips of equal objective MOS (within-group disper-
sion falling 55 to 94 percent), yet scored within listener they worsen,
rank correlation falling from around 0.79 to between 0.12 and 0.33.
Read together, objective supervision may recover the population axis
the pooled metrics reward by regressing each clip towards its group
mean, which within-listener ranking penalises. We therefore super-
vise the deployed models on the objective label, leaving a single
listener’s ordering as an open problem.
Speech versus music. Splitting the extreme binary contrast by con-
tent suggests the quality signal is carried mostly by speech (Table 3).
This descriptive, single-seed run matches clean and degraded tri-
als within each listener-content block, so the families differ only
in their listeners.
Speech then exceeds music in every modality,
at participant-mean balanced accuracy 0.69 to 0.83 against 0.38 to
0.62, several music cells at or below chance, with the pooled accura-
cies of Table 1 in between. Equalising trials left some families with
very few listeners (three for speech fNIRS, four for laboratory EEG),
so this is a descriptive pattern rather than a controlled effect.
4. CONCLUSION
Physiological sensing offers tentative support on all three questions.
Binary quality can be decoded above chance from wearable EEG,
fNIRS, and eye tracking across unseen listeners, graded MOS at
lower but significant correlations, while the laboratory EEG shows
the practical costs of high-density recording and fNIRS is most con-
sistent once support is equalised. Effort appears decodable per lis-
tener, eye tracking and fNIRS most strongly. These signals recover
the corpus-level quality axis, and, once calibrated to a listener, that
listener’s effort ratings. Whether they can also recover a single lis-
tener’s own quality ordering, rather than only this shared population
axis, remains open. These are feasibility results, not a claim to re-
place signal-based metrics: correlations are modest, effort does not
yet transfer to unseen listeners, and the between-modality compar-
isons rest on uneven cohorts. Scoring the speech clips with a pre-
dictor such as DNSMOS to bound the stimulus ceiling, and a leave-
one-clip-out test of generalisation to unseen audio, are natural next
steps. Even so, the results hint at systems tuned to perceived quality
and processing effort alike.
