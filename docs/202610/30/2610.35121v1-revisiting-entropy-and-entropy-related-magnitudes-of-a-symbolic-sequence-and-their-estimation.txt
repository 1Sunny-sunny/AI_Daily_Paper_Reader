This article revisits entropy and related magnitudes—entropy density, effective measure complex-
ity, and informational distance—for the analysis of sequential data. We present their mathematical
foundations within information theory and ergodic theory, establishing connections between proba-
bilistic Shannon entropy and algorithmic Kolmogorov complexity. A central result, Ziv’s theorem,
demonstrates the asymptotic equivalence between the normalised number of distinct words and
entropy density, providing the theoretical basis for estimation via Lempel-Ziv factorisation.
We
examine the convergence properties of these magnitudes and introduce estimation procedures for
finite sequences, including a method for the effective measure complexity and the transfer entropy
rate.
An open-source software library implementing parallel algorithms for efficient Lempel-Ziv
factorisation is presented. The practical utility of the framework is illustrated through three case
studies: functional brain connectivity analysis from fMRI data, characterisation of spatiotempo-
ral dynamics in coupled nonlinear oscillators, and electrocardiogram-based classification of atrial
fibrillation, demonstrating how entropic measures capture meaningful structural and dynamical
properties across diverse domains.

Many complex systems are known to us through a single recorded signal, from which regularity, memory,
and randomness must be disentangled. Entropy density, effective measure complexity, and informational
distance measure exactly that – how unpredictable a signal is, how much memory it carries, how far
two signals lie apart – yet their theory sits scattered across decades of literature, and their estimation
is frequently done wrong. This article assembles that theory in one place, links Shannon’s entropy to
Kolmogorov’s algorithmic complexity, and shows how Lempel-Ziv factorisation turns both into practical,
parameter-free estimators, backed by open-source software that processes sequences of a billion symbols.
Applied unchanged to fMRI recordings, a ring of coupled nonlinear oscillators, and electrocardiograms, the
same measures recover task-related brain activity, detect collective behaviour near the onset of chaos, and
separate healthy from arrhythmic heart rhythms with clinically relevant accuracy, showing that a single
information-theoretic toolkit captures memory and unpredictability across strikingly different systems.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Data from complex dynamical systems often appear as one or a few time series, from which the system’s dynamics
must be extracted [1]. In data analysis, the usual aims are to assess regularity versus randomness, to identify patterns,
to discover governing laws, or to predict future system behaviour [2]. One useful approach is to treat the dynamical
system as an information source and apply information-theoretic tools alongside statistical descriptions [3]. In general,
a single or just a few data sets are insufficient to fully understand the underlying dynamical system that drives the
information source, yet these are often the only available data. In such cases, assuming ergodicity becomes necessary
to extract the relevant information from the data [4].
For most systems, a fundamental quest is to find the relation between randomness and pattern in the system signal
[5]. Randomness can be considered noise or, in a more useful perspective, the intrinsic uncertainty of the system,
while a pattern is related to the deterministic laws that govern the system’s behaviour. It has been argued that for
a complex dynamical system, the behaviour lies between complete predictability and total randomness [5]. Entropy
density captures the irreducible unpredictability in a signal. Entropy density, also known as Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy,
entropy rate, among other names, has been defined in several fields, including information theory, ergodic theory, and
dynamical systems [3, 6]. A lesser-used measure of complexity is the effective measure of complexity, introduced by
Grassberger [4], which aims to capture a system’s intrinsic memory and, in time-series analysis, its reliance on the past
to define its future. Both magnitudes can be theoretically justified by Kolmogorov’s algorithmic complexity, which
laid a solid foundation for the analysis of data beyond the ensemble-statistical point of view [7]. Kolmogorov’s theory
of complexity builds on the existence of a universal computational machine, as demonstrated by Turing, and on the
computational theory of recurrent functions [8]. From Kolmogorov theory, several concepts emerge that are closely
related to entropy density and effective measure complexity; significantly, it also allows us to define the informational
distance as a measure of the distance between two data sets [9].
The informational theory related concepts have been used extensively in analyzing experimental and theoretical
models and there is an extensive literature regarding their use [2, 3, 6, 10–14].
In particular there is abundant
literature dealing with the estimation of entropy density, and in a much lesser extent, effective measure complexity
and informational distance [15–22]. In spite of that, there persist some confusion in the validity of the estimation
procedures, specially in the latter magnitudes, and its use, and the mathematics of the whole subject appears scattered
in the scientific literature, some misconceptions also persist [20, 23].
This article revisits key concepts and their mathematical foundations, including estimation and practical use. It
gathers work previously scattered across various sources, including new, unpublished results from the authors. The
aim is to offer a comprehensive treatment, from theory to practice, in a single source. Publicly available software and
a library for estimating these magnitudes are also introduced.
What this article is not, is a review, there are some important areas left out, specially the early work of Grassberger
[4, 23] and the further studies by Crutchfield and Feldman [5] on entropy estimation for finite state automata, although
some analytical results from their work will be used to compare estimated values of entropy density, and effective
measure complexity with the exact values.
The article comprises three main parts: (1) mathematical foundations of information theory (sections II, III, IV,
V); (2) analysis of Lempel-Ziv factorisation, complexity, and entropic magnitude estimation (sections VI, VII, VIII);
and (3) case studies (section IX). Conclusions follow. The appendix contains additional material, including proofs
and explanations of procedures. Table I collects the notation used throughout.

II.
STRINGS, ERGODICITY AND STATIONARITY
The intuitive notion of a string corresponds to a sequence of successive symbols, which take values from a finite
set, such as a binary alphabet. The index that traverses the positions of the symbols in a sequence is called time.
Although in practice one must deal with finite strings, the concept extends to infinite strings, and this extension is
used in proving certain theorems. However, it is worth clarifying that there are several types of infinite strings; the
most general ones never begin or end (e.g., . . . 1011010000111010100110011111110 . . .), and are called bi-infinite. If a
point is taken in a bi-infinite string, to the right there will be an infinite string that has a starting point but never ends
(e.g., abbbaccbaab . . .); and to the left we will have one that ends but does not have a starting point (. . . 32214441233).
Any point in a bi-infinite string can be taken as the middle point.
Formally, a string is a successive concatenation of symbols u = . . . u−2u−1u0u1u2 . . . ui . . . emitted by a symbol
source. ui is known as a character and takes values from an alphabet A, ui ∈A; ∀i ∈Z. The index i will be called
time even though it does not represent that physical variable. The number of symbols available to the source will be
taken as finite, |A| < ∞; the cardinality of the alphabet will be denoted by α(= |A|). Throughout, string positions
are indexed from zero; u(ℓ) = u0u1 . . . uℓ−1 denotes the prefix of length ℓ. Unless otherwise stated, log ≡logα; if the
sequence is binary, then α = 2 and entropies are expressed in bits.
When a string is finite, we call it a word. A word w has length |w|. It is important to include the null sequence Λ,
which has length (|Λ| =)0. If we have two strings w and u, the sequence wu is the concatenation of both sequences
in the indicated order, and its length would be |w| + |u|.
It is convenient to consider a symbol source or emitter as generating discrete strings uninterruptedly; its output is
bi-infinite strings. To circumvent the problems associated with time in generating bi-infinite strings when considering
the occurrence of multiple strings, we can imagine that instead of a single emitter, we have a set, called an ensemble,
of countably infinite identical emitters, each generating a bi-infinite string. Since, from a practical point of view, it is
useful to consider finite strings, they are taken as the result of observing an emitter’s output over a finite time interval
(. . . 100100000 11000111111 10010000111 . . .). For a source, the set of rules it follows as they generate the bi-infinite
string is called a grammar and denoted by G (e.g., a binary source that only emits an even number of 0s).
The set of all strings of finite length (words) will be denoted by A∗and those of infinite length by A∞.
Consider an infinite string u ∈A∞. If we define the set of all finite substrings of u, it is possible to define a
σ-algebra on that set and assign a probability measure to the set.
For example, take the set of all strings of finite length. Consider the initial collection formed by subsets of strings,
where each subset contains strings of the same length. From this initial collection, a σ-algebra can be generated, and
probabilities can be assigned to the strings based on their length. A similar probability assignment can be done to
other partitions of the strings into classes.

The two most important properties required of a source are ergodicity and stationarity.
Ergodicity means that the strings emitted by the source are statistically indistinguishable: for almost every string
u ∈A∞emitted by an ergodic source ψ, the relative frequency with which any finite word w occurs along u converges
to the same value P(w), whatever the string (e.g., Pu(w) = Pv(w) for two emitted strings u and v). A single string
then characterises the source, and ensemble averages can be replaced by averages along one realisation.
Stationarity refers to the temporal invariance of the emitter’s statistical properties. In other words, the probability
of a specific finite string, of any length, occurring in a bi-infinite string does not depend on the instant in time at
which the finite string occurs.
Stationarity implies that the probability of occurrence of a substring in an infinite string emitted by the source
depends only on the base word of the substring and not on the starting time. A stationary source implies that for all
u ∈A∞, P(u) = P(Θ(u)).

Kolmogorov complexity, also known as algorithmic complexity, quantifies the information content of a finite string
[8]. Formally, for a given Universal Turing Machine (UTM) U and a string w, this measure corresponds to the length
of the shortest possible program w∗that can generate w as output:

produced when U executes program w∗. As changing the UTM amounts to adding a constant to the Kolmogorov
complexity regardless of the string w, the use of another UTM just adds an O(1) upper-bound correction to the
defined magnitudes; the subscript U can be dropped, and in what follows we use K(w). Kolmogorov complexity of a
string represents its inherent (irreducible) information content.
There are certain constraints needed for the shortest possible program w∗. First of all, it must halt after a finite
number of steps, and it will also be required that once it halts, its only output must be the string w. Another convenient
condition usually imposed on w∗is that it must be self-delimiting, that is, w∗carries with it the information about
its size, which implies an additional correction of log |w|, we will drop the requirement in what follows for clarity.
The joint algorithmic complexity of strings w1 and w2 is defined as the size of the smallest program that computes
both strings. The following inequality holds,
K(w1, w2) ≤K(w1) + K(w2) + O(1).

V.
INFORMATION DISTANCE
Algorithmic complexity provides an adequate framework for defining the informational distance between two strings
[9, 28]. What is sought is a measure of the minimal amount of information needed to compute one string from the
other and vice versa. If this measure is to be considered a distance, apart from being non-negative, it must satisfy
the identity axiom, that the distance from one string to itself of zero; the symmetry axiom, where the distance from
string one to string two is equal to the distance from string two to string one; and the triangle inequality that states
that the distance between two string is smaller or equal to the sum of the distance of both strings to a third one.
Let w1 and w2 be two strings. We define the information distance as the length of the shortest program that
computes w1 from w2 as well as w2 from w1. It can be shown that if
K(w1|w2) > K(w2|w1),
(41)
Then the program that computes w1 given w2 can be made to contain the information needed to generate the program
that computes w2 given w1 [28]. This last fact leads to defining the (absolute) informational distance as
d∥(w1, w2) = max{K(w1|w2), K(w2|w1)}.
(42)
The defined absolute distance is clearly non-negative and complies with the symmetric axiom, as well as the identity
axiom up to a term O(1/K(wi)), where wi is the string with the largest algorithmic complexity. In [9], it is shown
that it also complies with the triangular inequality, also up to a term O(1/K(wi)) where wi is now the string with
the maximum Kolmogorov complexity of the three strings involved in the triangle inequality.
Zurek reasoned about the relation between algorithmic complexity and thermodynamic cost [27, 29]: the minimum
increase of thermodynamic entropy for a computation that turns string w1 into w2, without retaining memory of the


11
input or of the performed steps, should be equal to K(w1|w2) + O(1). The demonstration is based on the Landauer
principle that states that an erasure of a single bit implies a dissipation of energy of no less than kBT ln 2, where
kBT is the Boltzmann product [30]. If w2 contains all the information necessary to compute w1, then the process is
reversible, and there is no increase in thermodynamic entropy. On the contrary, if w1 cannot be recovered from w2,
information has been lost, and thermodynamic entropy has increased. The absolute information distance can then be
related to the maximum thermodynamic cost required to produce one string from the other, and vice versa.
The distance defined by (42) encounters practical difficulties when comparing strings of different lengths. Two
independent random strings of small length will have a smaller distance than two algorithmically related strings of
much larger length, with just a small amount of dissimilar information. To avoid this dependence on the string length,
the normalised informational distance has been defined as
d(w1, w2) = max{K(w1|w2), K(w2|w1)}

The information distance should not be confused with entropy-based divergences between probability distributions,
such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence or the Jensen-Shannon divergence [31], whose square root is a metric [32].
Applied to two sequences, such divergences compare their (empirical) word distributions, and therefore quantify
whether both sequences share the same statistics, that is, whether they look like realisations of the same source.
The information distance quantifies instead how much information one individual sequence carries about the other.
Two independent realisations of the same source have a vanishing Jensen-Shannon divergence but an information
distance close to one, while two strongly coupled sequences with different statistics can be far apart in the Jensen-
Shannon sense and close in the sense of d. The information distance is thus the appropriate measure when coupling,
rather than statistical similarity, is sought, as in the case studies of Section IX. In addition, the Kullback-Leibler
divergence is neither symmetric nor satisfies the triangle inequality, and both divergences require estimating length-ℓ
word distributions over αℓcells, with the undersampling problems discussed in Section V A for the transfer entropy,
whereas the estimate of d introduced in Section VII requires no block length.

B.
Effective measure complexity
Estimating the effective measure complexity is non-trivial. As a measure of correlation at all length scales, or the
memory of the system, its precise evaluation implies, in the general case, sums over infinite terms [4] and convergence
is usually slower than estimating entropy density. This makes any estimation more cumbersome than estimating the
entropy density, both analytically and numerically, and less reliable for smaller sequence length.
One of the approaches followed, avoiding direct data estimation, is to try to build the minimal computational
machine able to statistically reproduce the data. This approach was pioneered by Grassberger [4] and further developed
by Crutchfield and Feldman [5]. In practice, such an approach has not gone beyond finite-state approximations of the
data, and even then, the reconstruction procedure has been applied only to simple sources.

It is left to define a convenient limit n for the sum in Eq. (73). For a sequence of length N, the maximum n that can
be examined with reliable statistical significance is inherently limited. A common estimate derives from assuming the
sequence contains roughly N/n independent n-words, yielding the condition N ≥n αn. This approximation is only
accurate for independently and identically distributed sequences, where any dependence among n-words arises solely
from their overlapping nature – the same caveat that applies, one level down, to the sliding-window histogram of
Eq. (H1) itself: consecutive windows share ℓ−1 symbols and are therefore not independent draws, even though there
are N −ℓ+ 1 of them. Sliding rather than partitioning changes how much of the sequence’s information content is
sampled at each ℓ, easing the severely-undersampled regime identified above; it does not change how much information
content the sequence has at that ℓ, so it does not relax the bound below.

the n-words and must be accounted for. By interpreting the entropy rate h as the average amount of new independent
information contributed per observed symbol, the nominal sequence length can be replaced with an effective length

Let us clarify this last statement. We are not interested in abstract mathematical sources but rather in physical
processes that can be considered sources of symbols (e.g., DNA, RNA, or the discretisation of a time signal). The
physical laws governing the process will ultimately define a grammar for the source and the operations that can be
regarded as within the “natural order” of the analysed system. It is within that set of rules that possible operations
must be sought that can lead to a sequence transformation that could be missed by the Lempel-Ziv factorisation.
Fortunately, this set of rules is almost always simple operations, and there is no need to seek involved “non-natural”
transformations of the symbol sequence to compare with.
In most cases, a simple procedure as follows will suffice for calculating the distance between two strings w1 and w2:
1. Compute the distance bd between w1 and w2.
2. Compute the distance bd between w1 and the reverse of w2.
3. Compute the distance bd between w1 and some well defined negation of w2.
4. Take the distance as the smallest one among the three calculated distances.

VIII.
LEMPEL-ZIV ALGORITHM
We developed lzcomplexity [39], an open-source library implemented in C++ and Python that computes the en-
tropic magnitudes described from the Lempel-Ziv factorisation. The algorithms have been optimised to fully exploit
current computing power by implementing parallel processing that significantly accelerates performance. The parallel
implementation of the algorithms uses the Intel oneTBB library [40].
The algorithm used to obtain the factorisation consists of three phases [41]. The initial phase lexicographically
sorts all suffixes of the input sequence. From the array of all sorted suffixes (SA), the lengths of the longest common
prefixes (LCP) between consecutive suffixes are obtained. With the sorted suffixes, the second phase proceeds, where
the longest previous factor (LPF) [42] is computed for each position. Finally, the LZ76 factorisation is obtained from
the LPF array. Details will be given elsewhere.
The implementation shows execution-time improvements of more than 10-fold compared to sequential algorithms
for long (> 106) strings, and supports processing strings as large as 109 symbols. The developed software can be
obtained at https://github.com/pleros-ai/lzcomplexity [39].

CASE STUDIES
A.
Brain connectivity
The human brain’s function relies on a complex interplay of organisation across multiple scales, from individual
neurons to large-scale networks. The “connectome,” or the map of structural and functional connections between
brain regions, is central to understanding cognition and behaviour.
Abnormalities in this network are linked to
various neurological and psychiatric conditions, making its study crucial for diagnosis and treatment. Inferring these
connections from imaging data such as fMRI is challenging: current methods often rely on strong assumptions, are
limited to linear dynamics, or are sensitive to user-defined parameters, obscuring the brain’s inherently non-linear
behaviour.
To overcome these limitations, we proposed a novel, model-free analytical approach grounded in entropic magnitudes
introduced above. Instead of assuming linear correlations, the method characterises each region by its unpredictability
(entropy density bh) and its memory (effective measure complexity bE), and uses the informational distance bd to assess
functional connections. It thus detects both linear and non-linear dynamics without prior assumptions about the
data, providing a generalisable characterisation of cognitive states across experimental conditions and signal types.
The fMRI data used in this study were sourced from the Human Connectome Project (HCP), for subjects performing
a motor task cued for finger tapping, toe squeezing, or tongue movement. The brain was parcellated into 360 regions
of interest (ROIs) using the Glasser atlas [43] (180 ROIs per hemisphere).
The data is discretised before estimating the entropic magnitudes. For each time series, the mean value is computed
and used as the binarisation threshold. Binarisation results in the loss of information from the original signal, but it
is assumed that the binarised strings still contain sufficient relevant information about the system’s dynamics. If this
is the case, it can only be asserted by the results themselves.
Figure 6(a) shows the complexity-entropy of the different regions of the brain in the motor task. For this task
all regions lie above bh = 0.75 and bE = 0.5, placing them in a regime between complete predictability and total
randomness, the hallmark of complex dynamics. The points form a continuum rather than distinct clusters, so no
fixed cut separates active from inactive regions. We segment the map objectively from the residuals of a linear fit: a
local threshold is computed for each point’s neighbourhood, cleanly splitting the map in two. Informed by previous
studies suggesting that inactive regions exhibit more random signal patterns [44], the side of the map with lower
entropy density (and higher complexity) is taken as active. This criterion identifies a small subset of regions for every
task; for the motor task, 8.61% of regions are classified as active.
The position of the active regions relative to the linear fit carries functional information.
Most primary and
sensory motor regions lie above the fit, generating more pattern complexity than the trend predicts for their entropy
density, whereas the main visual areas lie below it. Reading the regression as the mean bE–bh relation, the motor
and sensory cortices “overperform” in pattern generation while the visual areas “underperform” during this task:
the motor response demands precisely coordinated movement, whereas the sustained visual cue allows looser visual
engagement. This separation of regions that share an activation status but differ in dynamical regime is information
that a single-amplitude activation measure cannot provide.
Projecting the normalised bh onto the cortical surface (Figure 6(b)) shows that the active set is not scattered but
anatomically coherent. The lowest bh values concentrate in the occipital visual areas and in the motor and sensory
cortices of the posterior precentral gyrus, the two systems the task engages: a visual cue followed by a coordinated
movement of fingers, toes, or tongue. The activation pattern recovered by the entropic criterion matches the known
substrate of the task, obtained without a design matrix, a hemodynamic model, or any tuned parameter.
Functional connections were obtained from the LZ-distance bd between all 360 ROIs. The resulting distance matrix,
ordered by increasing bh (Figure 6(c)), was clustered with the Neighbour-Joining method to produce the dendrogram
of Figure 6(d). Regions with similar bh are close in bd (the diagonal band), but this band narrows sharply over the active
block, indicating tighter functional coupling among active regions. Active regions cluster together at low levels of the
dendrogram, and regions of similar functional roles group consistently: visual areas of the occipital lobe form one
cluster, primary motor/sensory and premotor regions another, and a third, heterogeneous cluster integrates occipital,
lateral-frontal, and lateral-parietal regions with mixed visual, motor, and attentional roles. The full region-by-region
assignment is reported in the companion study [45].
This example uses the motor task to illustrate the pipeline. Applied unchanged to memory, emotion, language,
and resting-state data, the same measures recover task-relevant activation and modular functional structure, and the
active sets agree with GLM and meta-analytic references; these results are detailed in [45].

Non-linear locally coupled oscillators
Coupled non-linear oscillators under weak coupling have been extensively studied since the foundational work
of Winfree [46], which established the oscillator phase as the key dynamical variable, superseding amplitude-based
descriptions. This framework was anticipated by Adler’s analysis of locking in feedback circuits [47], leading to the
phase equation for Adler-type oscillators:
dθ
dt = −γ sin θ + ∆ω,
(80)
where θ denotes the phase, γ a control parameter, and ∆ω the frequency detuning.
This formulation was later
generalised to ensembles of coupled oscillators, culminating in the Kuramoto model [48], which describes globally
coupled oscillators via harmonic signals and has become a paradigm for studying synchronisation across physics,
biology, and neuroscience [49].
Recent work has introduced a system of locally coupled Adler-type oscillators arranged in a ring with nearest-


24
neighbour interactions as shown in Figure 7(a)[50]:
dθi
dt = ω + γ cos θi + (−1)i [cos θi−1 + cos θi+1] .
(81)
Equations (81) are integrated numerically with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, and the entropic magnitudes
are computed from the discretised phase activity sin θi, following Ref. [51]. The phase activity is binarised with its
mean value as threshold: values greater than or equal to the mean are mapped to 1 and the remaining ones to 0.
The temporal evolution of each oscillator thus becomes a binary string, and the spatial configuration of the ring at a
given time a binary string of length equal to the number of oscillators.
This model exhibits a diverse configuration diagram as seen by the bh value, as a function of two control parameters
(Figure 7(b)), revealing regimes with distinct spatiotemporal dynamics: an absorbing region with near-zero entropy,
a chaotic region with high sensitivity and disorder, a wedge region characterized by long-range correlations, and a
needle region displaying intermediate complexity and intricate patterns [51]. Notably, the system exhibits a transition
to chaos as the needle regions is abandoned while γ decreases, where computational capabilities—namely information
storage, transmission, and modification—are enhanced near the edge of chaos [52–54], supporting the hypothesis that
dynamical systems poised at this transition can function as optimized computational substrates [55–58].
The needle region has an estimated entropy density near zero, while the effective measure complexity approaches
3. At the lower boundary of the needle region, there is a surge in the bE value and a sudden fall to zero. It is at
this surge point that enhanced system performance is expected. One such point is at ω = 2.225, γ = 1.206, and the
spatial-temporal diagram shown in Figure 7(c) exhibits a complex pattern with local spatial arrangements seen as
wave-like patterns in the oscillator’s behaviour, signalling that lasts for long temporal intervals.
When carefully studying the system behaviour with ω = 2.225 fixed and γ varied (Figure 7(d)), several distinct
pictures emerge. What is striking is that couplings seem to emerge between odd-indexed oscillators and between
even-indexed oscillators, as seen in the bd maps shown in Figure 7(e). While at point 1, within the chaotic region no
recognizable pattern is seen in the informational distance map, but the bE vs bh complexity-entropy map (Figure 7(f))
clearly shows that both set of oscillators, even and odd, can be segmented by their values of both entropic magnitudes.
The even oscillators exhibit higher entropy density and lower bE values. As the studied points approach the edge and
the needle region, clear patterns emerge in the distance map (points 2, 3, 4 in Figure 7(d)) for both sets of oscillators.
The entropic behaviour of the two groups of oscillators converges, as evidenced by the entropy-complexity map (Figure
7(f)). Patterns in the distance map are a sign of coupling in the oscillators and therefore of collective behaviour. Well
within the needle region, at point 5 in Figure 7(d) the entropy-complexity map again shows a clear segmentation of
odd and even oscillators but, compared to what was seen in point 1, the range of bh values for both groups overlaps,
and the difference is mostly seen as a gap between the bE values of both sets.
C.
Electrocardiogram analysis
Electrocardiogram (ECG) classification has become a fundamental tool in modern cardiology. Usually, a complete
ECG comprises signals from 12 sensors or leads. However, manual interpretation of ECGs faces several challenges,
including limited access to specialists in rural areas, inter-observer variability, and low sensitivity in short recordings.
ECG is the most widely used tool for diagnosing cardiac diseases. Figure 8(a) shows the ECG of a healthy subject
(SR) and of a patient with atrial fibrillation (AFIB). There have been attempts to use machine learning techniques to
diagnose such illnesses from ECGs. The most commonly used approach in this regard is to train the neural network
directly on labelled ECG data and use the trained model for diagnosis. We followed a different approach.
From the ECG, the recurrence matrix is computed (Figure 8(b)). An entry rij in the recurrence matrix is the
absolute value of the difference between the signal at time index i and time index j:
rij = |(ecg)i −(ecg)j|
Figure 8(b) shows the average recurrence matrices corresponding to AFIB and SR subjects.
Visually, the matrices reveal fundamental differences. The graph for the SR group exhibits a pattern of continuous
diagonal lines parallel to the main diagonal. According to recurrence matrix theory, this characteristic indicates more
deterministic, regular underlying dynamics, consistent with the periodic nature of SR. The presence of these diagonal
lines indicates that trajectory segments evolve similarly at different time instants.
In contrast, the recurrence matrix obtained for the AFIB group suggests more complex cardiac dynamics, with a
significant stochastic component. The lack of extended temporal recurrences in the trajectory reflects the inherent
irregularity and loss of periodicity characteristic of this arrhythmia.

D.
Further applications
We applied the same magnitudes to other systems in previous and ongoing work; those results are not reproduced
here for lack of space but are summarised for completeness. In written language, bh and bE were estimated for the
books of different writers, each text treated as a symbolic sequence of characters, without any grammatical or syntactic
analysis. On the complexity-entropy map the authors occupy distinct regions, so the measures capture their different
styles. Also, dendrograms built from the Lempel-Ziv distance matrix segmented the authors and each author’s work by
genre. Surrogates obtained by randomising each text at the sentence, word and character level allow the contribution
of each level of organisation to be separated: the difference in bh between consecutive randomisations measures the
information invested in organising characters into words, words into sentences, and sentences into the text, and the
corresponding difference in bE measures the effective measure complexity gained at each of those levels, which decreases
as one moves up the hierarchy [59].
In gene expression data, the magnitudes were estimated from whole-genome expression levels ordered by pathway,
for thirteen tumour types taken from the TCGA repository. The complexity-entropy map separates tumour from
normal samples in nine of the thirteen types, with success rates above 98% in several of them, and bd discriminates
in addition between tumour types, as shown for two kidney tumours. Neither gene preselection nor a model of the
disease is required, and the ordering by pathway, rather than the identification of individual up- or down-regulated
genes, is what makes the trend in the map emerge [60].
For trajectories, a two-dimensional curve is discretised with the Freeman chain code, which yields a sequence over
an alphabet of eight symbols and requires no embedding or threshold. The magnitudes estimated from that sequence
separate regular from chaotic orbits in the H´enon-Heiles system, and, applied to centre-of-pressure trajectories from
a public human balance dataset, provide the features with which a neural network classifies subjects by fall history
under different postural conditions [61]. In all cases the procedure is the one followed above: discretise the signal,
factorise it, and estimate bh, bE and bd from the factorisation, without tuning parameters or assuming a model for the
source.
X.
CONCLUSIONS
In this article, we have revisited the fundamental concepts of entropy and related magnitudes for the analysis
of sequential data, providing a unified treatment of their mathematical foundations within information theory and
ergodic theory. We have established the relationships between block entropy, entropy density, entropy gain, effective
measure complexity, and informational distance, clarifying their interpretations and convergence properties.
The
connection between Shannon’s probabilistic entropy and Kolmogorov’s algorithmic complexity has been emphasised,
with Ziv’s theorem providing the crucial link that enables practical estimation via Lempel-Ziv factorisation.
The Lempel-Ziv factorisation has been examined in detail, demonstrating its utility for estimating entropic magni-
tudes from finite sequences. We have presented estimation procedures for entropy density, effective measure complexity
via coverage-adjusted block entropies, informational distance, and the transfer entropy rate, including error estimates
and convergence considerations. The accompanying open-source software library, lzcomplexity [39], implements par-
allel algorithms capable of processing sequences up to (109) symbols, making these analyses accessible for practical
applications.
The three case studies illustrate the framework’s broad applicability.
In brain connectivity analysis, entropic
measures revealed task-specific activation patterns and hierarchical functional organisation, providing a model-free
approach to characterise cognitive states. The study of coupled nonlinear oscillators demonstrated that entropy density
and effective measure complexity capture transitions between dynamical regimes and identify collective behaviour
through patterns of informational distance. Finally, electrocardiogram analysis showed that entropy-based features
extracted from recurrence matrices can achieve clinically relevant classification performance for atrial fibrillation with
only two leads, highlighting the approach’s practical diagnostic potential.
Taken together, these results demonstrate that the entropic magnitudes discussed—entropy density, effective mea-
sure complexity, and informational distance—provide a robust, theoretically grounded framework for characterising
complex dynamics across diverse domains. Their estimation via Lempel-Ziv factorisation offers a practical, parameter-


28
free methodology that captures both randomness and memory in sequential data, with promising applications in
physics, biology, neuroscience, and medicine.

Appendix H: Block entropy from a coverage-adjusted frequency estimator
Given a sequence u the surrogate-and-Lempel-Ziv step is replaced with a direct, coverage-adjusted estimate of
Hℓ(u) from the block frequency histogram of the sequence itself, following Chao and Shen [37] which avoid the need
of building surrogate sequence and no Lempel-Ziv factorisation of it is taken: entropy computed from a frequency
histogram is manifestly insensitive to the order in which the blocks occur, so the shuffling step was never intrinsic to
estimating Hℓ(u) – it was only ever necessary to make an order-sensitive estimator (Lempel-Ziv complexity) behave
as if the blocks were exchangeable. Working from the histogram directly removes that intermediate step and its
associated bias.
Two choices complete the construction. First, the histogram is built from sliding, overlapping length-ℓwindows of
u rather than a non-overlapping partition: mℓ= N −ℓ+ 1 windows rather than N/ℓblocks. The two constructions
estimate the same quantity – the ergodic average over the source’s length-ℓblock distribution is, by definition,
insensitive to which of the ℓpossible phases a given window happens to start on – but the non-overlapping partition
realises only one such phase per sequence, which can measure something other than that average for sources with
structure commensurate with ℓ: e.g. a sequence with exact period ℓhas every non-overlapping ℓ-block identical, so
a non-overlapping estimate of Hℓ(u) reads exactly 0 regardless of the source’s real per-block entropy, purely as an
artifact of the single phase sampled. Sliding by 1 removes this phase-alignment sensitivity entirely, and incidentally
supplies far more samples per scale, directly easing the #ℓ-vs-mℓimbalance identified above.
Second, entropy is read off each histogram with the Good-Turing/Horvitz-Thompson coverage correction of Chao
and Shen, rather than the maximum-likelihood estimate. Writing ni for the count of the i-th distinct length-ℓblock
observed among the mℓwindows, the Good-Turing coverage estimate is bCℓ= 1 −f1,ℓ/mℓ, with f1,ℓthe number of
blocks observed exactly once; each block’s coverage-adjusted probability is bpi = bCℓ(ni/mℓ), and

Appendix J: Convolutional neural network for heart disease diagnosis.
The structure of the CNN is given by:
• A first convolutional layer, with 64 filters and a kernel size of 5, designed to identify simple, low-level local
patterns.
• A ReLU activation function to introduce nonlinearity, allowing the network to learn more complex relationships.
• A Max-pooling layer with a kernel size of 2.
• A second convolutional layer, with 128 filters and a kernel size of 3. By having more filters and a smaller kernel,
it seeks to combine the simple patterns detected by the first layer into more complex features.
• Then a ReLU function and a Max-pooling layer are applied again, and subsequently the output is flattened.
• Three fully connected layers: the first reduces dimensionality to 128 neurons, the second to 64, and the final
one to a single output neuron. Between these layers, ReLU activation functions and Dropout layers with a rate
of 0.3 are applied.
• The final output neuron uses a Sigmoid activation function. This function compresses the output to the range
0 to 1, allowing it to be interpreted as the probability that the patient belongs to each class.
