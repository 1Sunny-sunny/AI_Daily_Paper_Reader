Comparing Temporal Deep Learning Architectures
for Discrete Cross-User Myoelectric Control
Ethan Eddy∗, Evan Campbell∗, Scott Bateman†, Erik Scheme∗
∗Department of Electrical Engineering, University of New Brunswick, Canada
†Faculty of Computer Science, University of New Brunswick, Canada
Abstract—The recognition of dynamic gestures via discrete
myoelectric control is becoming an attractive input modality
for event-based human-machine interaction. While recent work
suggests that discrete cross-user models are possible (i.e., no
calibration data is required from the end user), few have
evaluated techniques to optimize these systems and their gen-
eralizability to new randomly prompted data simulating real-
world discrete control is relatively unknown. Using the 612-user
EMG-EPN dataset, this work benchmarks five temporal deep-
learning approaches, evaluates the tradeoff between performance
and model complexity, and compares hand-crafted and machine-
learned feature extraction techniques. The CNN-GRU achieved
the best accuracy of 93% when tested on 184 previously unseen
users. These models were then evaluated on a different, more
realistic, unsegmented dataset, wherein users were randomly
prompted to elicit discrete gestures during a continuous data
collection. This dataset was recorded five years apart and in
a separate country from the data used to train the original
model. Results show that the cross-user results scale to this new
dataset (accuracies of 96%) even though no feedback was given to
users during the elicitation of gestures. These findings highlight
the exciting potential of discrete zero-shot cross-user myoelectric
control and offer valuable insights for the continued advancement
of this promising technology.
Index Terms—Cross-User, Deep Learning, Electromyography,
Gesture Recognition, Myoelectric Control, Zero-Shot
I. INTRODUCTION
As human-machine interaction (HMI) continues to expand
beyond conventional computing environments, the need is
arising for new enabling technologies to replace traditional
inputs. Leveraging the physiological electromyogram (EMG)
signal, the electrical potentials generated by muscles when
they are contracted, myoelectric control is one possibility for
these emerging use-cases [1]. However, despite its promise
and history of success in controlling powered prostheses, the
widespread use of this technology for HMI has largely been
hindered by the need for regular and personalized end-user
calibration [2].
Compared to the traditional use case of continuous myoelec-
tric control, whereby decisions are made on small windows of
data [3], discrete control maps dynamic gestures (e.g., a double
tap or finger snap) to event-based commands [2]. Although
developing true cross-user models that require no training
data from the end user is challenging for continuous control,
recent work is beginning to show that the temporal structure of
dynamic gestures may help overcome this challenge [1], [4].
Moreover, discrete control might even be more appropriate
for HMI as many inputs, such as button clicks and swipes,
are inherently event-based [2], [5], [6].
Although previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of
cross-user discrete control without recalibration [1], [4], little
work has evaluated techniques and approaches to optimize
these systems. This work correspondingly benchmarks five
temporal deep learning architectures and compares hand-
crafted feature extraction to machine-learned CNN-based ap-
proaches on the 612-user EMG-EPN dataset [7]. Additionally,
to test the real-world performance and generalizability of these
models, a never released dataset with 14 participants was
used, where users were randomly prompted to elicit discrete
gestures during a continuous data collection. This required that
these gestures be detected and segmented from the continuous
input stream, which is often ignored in offline studies. The
results suggest, for the first time, that even under these real-
world settings (i.e., data collected years apart, in a different
country, and in the context of randomly prompted gestures),
classification accuracies can reach up to 96% on data from
unseen users. These results provide valuable insights for the
future development of discrete cross-user myoelectric control
systems and further highlight the potential viability of this
promising technology.
II. METHODS
A. Datasets
This work leveraged two datasets that were recorded nearly
five years apart: (1) EMG-EPN-612 [7] and (2) MyoDisCo
[8]. Each dataset was recorded with the Myo Armband, an
8-channel surface-EMG device with a sampling rate of 200
Hz [9]. The armband was placed in approximately the same
location on each subject, with the LED on the extensor and
the USB port facing down toward the hand. The six common
gestures used here were: (1) hand open, (2) hand close, (3)
wrist flexion, (4) wrist extension, (5) double tap, and (6) rest.
1) EMG-EPN-612: Recorded by Benalcazar et al., this is
the largest publicly available EMG dataset, with recordings
from 612 users [7]. During the data collection, users were
prompted to elicit each discrete gesture at some point during
a five-second window (this included the ramp on and off
of the gesture). Indices indicating the start and end of each
gesture are included in the dataset’s metadata. Labelled data
are available for 50 repetitions of each gesture from 306 users
and 25 repetitions from the other 306 (a total of 137,700

discrete gesture templates). A more detailed description of this
dataset can be found in [4] and [10].
2) MyoDisCo:

B. Classification
The deep learning architecture used for classifying the
discrete gestures was inspired by the two closest previous
works [1], [4]. The data for each discrete gesture in the dataset
were first processed and windowed (size=50 ms and incre-
ment=25 ms). For the handcrafted feature case, the wavelet
energy (WENG) feature was extracted from each window
[11]. For the machine-learned feature extraction case, three
1D convolutional layers (kernel size of 3 and stride of 1) were
applied to the raw windowed data with output channel sizes
of 16, 32, and 64. Each layer maintained constant padding
and had batch normalization, max pooling, a ReLU activation
function, and dropout of 0.2. Each template (padded with
trailing zeros to ensure constant template sizes in the dataset)
was then passed through a network of 1 to 7 temporal layers
with respective hidden sizes of 128 neurons and dropout of
0.2. Only the final output encodings from the last window
of the gesture were forwarded to the classification network
and used to update the network. The classification network
was composed of three linear layers of size 128, 64, and
32 before being projected to a final layer of 6 neurons
representing the 6 possible classes. ReLU activation functions
were added between each linear layer. An Adam optimizer
[12] and the cross-entropy loss function were used in all cases.
Additionally, gradient norm clipping was employed to mitigate
the issue of exploding gradients during training. Each model
was trained for 50 epochs with an initial learning rate of 0.001
and a batch size of 1000. 80% of the labelled EMG-EPN
dataset was used for training (428 users), and the other 20%
(128 users) for validation. No additional tuning or training was
done when evaluating the models with the MyoDisCo dataset.
All code was developed in Python and leveraged PyTorch
[13] for deep learning, and LibEMG [14] for EMG-specific
data processing.
1) Algorithms: This work evaluated five temporal archi-
tectures. The LSTM, bi-directional LSTM (BILSTM), and
GRU all had identical structures as highlighted above. For
the RNN, however, the nonlinearity added between each layer
was modified from Tanh to ReLU to account for vanishing
gradients that were observed during tuning. The transformer
was designed with a d model of 128 to have a similar
hidden representation size as the hidden layers of the other
models. The dim feedforward was set to 512, the number
of attention heads to eight, and positional embeddings were
learned during training
C. Gesture Segmentation
As participants returned to rest between gestures in the con-
tinuous data collection, an energy-based thresholding approach
could be used to extract potential gesture templates from the
continuous stream of EMG data [15], [16]. The Teager-Kaiser
energy was taken across each channel of EMG data and then
averaged and smoothed using a window size of 150 ms with
an increment of 25 ms [17]. The first step in the gesture
extraction process involved waiting until the energy fell below
a desired threshold tready. Next, a gesture was assumed to have
started when the energy surpassed an onset threshold tonset.
The gesture extractor then waited until the energy reached its
maximum amplitude, with a buffer (i.e., it waited up to 100
ms without an increase before stopping). Finally, the gesture
detector waited until the energy reached a local minimum and
the extracted gesture was passed through the classifier. Based
on pilot studies, tready and tonset were set to 75 and 150,
respectively.
D. Statistical Analysis
Using a Shapiro-Wilk test, all data were first checked to see
if they followed a normal distribution. As all data followed a
non-normal distribution, Friedman tests were used to check for
significant differences between the factors of interest and, post-
hoc, paired Nemenyi tests were used to check for significant
differences between specific pairs. A non-parametric Wilcoxon
test was applied for any comparisons where the number of
groups was less than three.

IV. DISCUSSION
This paper evaluated five temporal deep learning architec-
tures for discrete myoelectric control. The GRU consistently
performed best, followed by the BILSTM, LSTM, Trans-
former, and RNN. Subtle differences in the generalization of
some models (e.g., BILSTM) to the MyoDisCo dataset may


be due to differences in how gestures were segmented (it is
unknown how segmentation was achieved in the EPN dataset).
This suggests that minor variations in the framing of input tem-
plates may affect model robustness. While initial differences
were significant when averaging across configurations, these
results were less pronounced when comparing the optimized
configuration from each model. This suggests that all of the
proposed architectures are viable if configured correctly.
The BILSTM and Transformer architectures required sub-
stantially more parameters than the other models. As these
control systems will likely be deployed on embedded systems,
this becomes an important design consideration. While in-
creasing the layers improved performance on the EPN dataset,
the best models on MyoDisCo were not necessarily the largest,
suggesting that the additional parameters did not lead to im-
proved generalizability. While the CNN-based feature extrac-
tion did not significantly improve performance, the highest-
performing models on the MyoDisCo dataset leveraged the
CNN architecture, indicating that it generalizes quite well.
Future work could likely optimize these systems further for
improved performance. Overall, these results provide valuable
insights into the future design of discrete cross-user myoelec-
tric control systems.
