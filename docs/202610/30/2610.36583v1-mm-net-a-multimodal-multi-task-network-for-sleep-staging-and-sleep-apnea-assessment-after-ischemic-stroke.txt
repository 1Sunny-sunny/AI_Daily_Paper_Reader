A B S T R A C T
Automated polysomnography after ischemic stroke presents challenges arising from altered
sleep-related neural activity and concurrent respiratory disturbances. Existing stroke-specific
approaches have primarily addressed sleep-stage classification, leaving the joint analysis of sleep
architecture and respiratory events insufficiently investigated. Furthermore, adapting pretrained
EEG encoders introduces a representation inconsistency when downstream models are trained on
embeddings from previously observed patients but evaluated on unseen individuals. We propose
MM-Net, a multimodal multi-task framework that addresses this inconsistency through cross-
fitting and ridge-based embedding alignment. Sleep-adapted EEG–EOG representations and en-
gineered neural descriptors are integrated with learned cardiorespiratory features through cross-
attention and shared temporal modeling to support simultaneous sleep staging and respiratory-
event detection. On iSLEEPS, comprising 99 patients with subacute ischemic stroke, ten-fold
patient-independent evaluation achieved 76.3% sleep-staging accuracy (Cohen’s 𝜅= 0.656) and
0.825 respiratory-event AUC, outperforming the evaluated baselines under matched protocols.
Controlled experiments showed that the sleep-adapted encoder improves the downstream model
when its embeddings are aligned, raising staging accuracy by 1.4 percentage points over
the frozen encoder (𝑝= 0.020), against 0.2 points without alignment. The framework also
achieved 86.4% staging accuracy on healthy Sleep-EDF, above every single-EEG architecture
reimplemented on the same recordings, and 80.3% staging accuracy with 0.889 respiratory AUC
on ISRUC-I, exceeding its retrained staging baselines. Without retraining, the iSLEEPS-trained
model retained respiratory AUCs of 0.833 on ISRUC-I and 0.841 on UCD. These findings
support representation-consistent neural adaptation and joint physiological modeling across
stroke, healthy-sleep, and sleep-disorder cohorts.
1. Introduction
Sleep disturbances are common after ischemic stroke and have been associated with neurological recovery
and functional outcomes [1–4]. Sleep-disordered breathing is particularly prevalent in this population, making the
assessment of sleep architecture and respiratory disturbance relevant to post-stroke evaluation. Polysomnography
(PSG) records the physiological information required for both assessments, including electroencephalographic (EEG),
electrooculographic (EOG), electromyographic (EMG), respiratory, and cardiovascular signals. Sleep-stage scoring
primarily depends on neural and muscular activity, whereas respiratory-event identification relies on changes in
ventilation, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and related physiological responses. Their concurrent analysis
therefore requires a model capable of learning from heterogeneous signals whose relevance differs across prediction
tasks.
Automated sleep staging has progressed from convolutional and recurrent architectures to attention-based sequence
models and pretrained neural representations [5–12]. Multimodal approaches have expanded this direction by inte-
grating physiological signals, temporal context, and complementary representations. Recent studies, including MMS-
SleepNet [13] and CrossFusionSleepNet [14], demonstrate the growing use of physiological knowledge, modality-
specific encoding, and attention-based fusion for sleep staging. Respiratory-event detection has developed alongside

The application of these methods to ischemic stroke introduces additional physiological and computational consid-
erations. Cerebrovascular injury can alter sleep spindles, slow-wave activity, and hemispheric EEG characteristics [17–
22], potentially affecting representations learned from healthy or conventional sleep-disorder populations. The recently
released iSLEEPS dataset [23] provides multimodal overnight PSG recordings from 100 patients with ischemic
stroke, including expert annotations of sleep stages and respiratory events. Its initial analysis established sleep-staging
baselines for this population. Bose et al. [24] subsequently investigated the generalization of EEG-based staging
models between healthy and stroke cohorts, reporting performance degradation and changes in the physiological
patterns emphasized by the model. More recently, Alhazmi [25] introduced SleepStageNet, a compact five-channel
staging architecture evaluated on 95 iSLEEPS recordings, together with physiological attribution analyses and transfer
to an independent healthy-sleep cohort. Collectively, these studies have advanced stroke-specific sleep classification
and demonstrated the importance of population-dependent evaluation. The joint prediction of sleep architecture and
respiratory-event onset from stroke PSG, however, remains insufficiently investigated.
This gap also raises a question concerning the organization of multimodal physiological information. Neural and
cardiorespiratory signals provide different evidence for sleep state and respiratory disturbance, although both tasks
depend on temporal context and interactions among physiological systems. A shared model must therefore integrate
information across modalities while retaining features relevant to individual outputs. Furthermore, respiratory-event
annotations are closely associated with recorded airflow and respiratory-effort signals. Improved discrimination
from multimodal inputs cannot, by itself, establish whether a model has learned complementary physiological
relationships or relies predominantly on channels directly associated with event scoring. Controlled modality and
channel interventions are necessary to distinguish these contributions and characterize the dependence of each task
on its available signals.
A separate methodological difficulty arises when pretrained EEG encoders are adapted to a clinical cohort
and subsequently used to train a downstream prediction model. Adaptation can improve the relevance of learned
representations to post-stroke sleep, but embeddings generated for patients used during encoder training may differ
from those produced for previously unseen patients. Cross-fitting addresses the in-sample dependence by generating
downstream training embeddings from encoders that have not observed the corresponding patients, consistent with
the cross-fitting principle of double machine learning [26]. However, independently adapted encoders may produce
representations in different feature spaces, introducing an additional inconsistency with the encoder used at deployment.
Consequently, reliable integration requires both out-of-sample representation generation and alignment with the
deployed encoder’s embedding space. The contribution of these operations can be evaluated directly through controlled
encoder-integration comparisons.
To address these problems, we develop MM-Net, a multimodal multi-task framework for joint sleep-stage
classification and respiratory-event-onset detection. The neural stream combines physiologically defined descriptors
with embeddings from a sleep-adapted LaBraM encoder [9]. Cross-fitting produces out-of-sample representations
for downstream training, and ridge-based alignment maps these representations into the embedding space of the
encoder used for inference. A complementary convolutional stream extracts information directly from cardiorespiratory
waveforms. Cross-attention and bidirectional temporal modeling integrate the representations, while task-specific
prediction pathways support five-class sleep staging and respiratory-event scoring. A direct cardiorespiratory pathway
preserves respiratory information for event detection, and the resulting scores additionally support patient-level apnea–
hypopnea index estimation.
We evaluate the framework using patient-independent protocols across stroke, healthy-sleep, and sleep-disorder
populations. The primary analysis uses 99 iSLEEPS patients, supported by matched staging and respiratory baselines,
controlled encoder comparisons, and physiological signal interventions. Controlled experiments further establish
the contribution of embedding alignment to sleep-adapted encoder integration. To examine applicability beyond
the development population, the framework is separately evaluated on Sleep-EDF and ISRUC-Sleep. Additional
experiments assess transfer of the iSLEEPS-trained model to independent cohorts without retraining. Together,
these evaluations investigate both the methodological contribution of representation-consistent adaptation and the
applicability of joint physiological modeling across different sleep populations.

The principal contributions of this study are threefold:
1. Representation-consistent integration of sleep-adapted EEG features. We address the mismatch arising when an
EEG encoder adapted on training patients supplies representations to a downstream model evaluated on unseen
patients. A cross-fitting and embedding-alignment procedure integrates out-of-sample neural representations
into a common deployment space, enabling the downstream framework to benefit from sleep-specific adaptation
without relying on in-sample encoder outputs during training.
2. Task-specialized multimodal modeling of post-stroke sleep physiology. We develop a unified framework for
sleep-stage classification and respiratory-event detection that combines neural and cardiorespiratory represen-
tations through shared temporal modeling while retaining task-specific respiratory information. This design
enables concurrent characterization of sleep architecture and respiratory disturbances in subacute ischemic
stroke, with patient-level AHI estimation as an additional output.
3. Evidence for physiological specialization and cross-cohort applicability. Through controlled signal interventions
and patient-independent evaluations, we examine the dependence of each prediction task on its physiological
inputs and establish the performance of the integrated framework against matched baselines. Independent-cohort
experiments further assess the transfer of the learned representations beyond the stroke population.
The remainder of this paper presents the related literature, datasets, proposed methodology, experimental evalua-
tion, and discussion of the findings.
2. Related Work
2.1. Automatic Sleep Staging
Automatic sleep staging has progressed from feature-based classifiers to deep models that learn temporal and
spectral representations directly from PSG signals. DeepSleepNet combines convolutional feature extraction with
recurrent sequence modeling [5], and SeqSleepNet formulates sleep staging as sequence-to-sequence classification
across consecutive epochs [6]. AttnSleep uses multi-resolution convolution and attention to capture complementary
temporal characteristics [7], and TinySleepNet reduces model complexity while retaining recurrent modeling of stage
transitions [8]. U-Time approaches staging as dense time-series segmentation [27], whereas XSleepNet combines
raw-signal and spectral representations within a common sequence model [28].
More recent work has expanded the range of temporal models and learned representations. SleepTransformer
applies attention-based sequence modeling
[29], L-SeqSleepNet extends modeling across longer sleep-stage se-
quences [30], and ZleepAnlystNet and FlexSleepTransformer investigate alternative training strategies and flexible
channel configurations
[31, 32]. State-space architectures based on Mamba have also been explored for sleep
staging [33, 34]. In parallel, large pretrained EEG models such as LaBraM, CBraMod, EEGPT, and BIOT provide
representations learned from substantially larger collections of neural recordings [9–12]. Such encoders are typically
used frozen or fine-tuned end to end for a single task. Using a fine-tuned encoder as the input of a second model
trained on the same patients raises the requirement addressed by cross-fitting, namely that the second model be trained
on out-of-sample first-stage outputs [26, 35].
Most of these developments have been established on healthy or general sleep datasets. Performance can change
substantially when models are transferred across cohorts because acquisition protocols, channel configurations, patient
characteristics, and sleep physiology differ between datasets [36]. This issue is especially relevant after stroke, where
the neural patterns used in conventional sleep staging may themselves be altered by neurological injury.
2.2. Multimodal and Joint PSG Analysis
Clinical PSG contains several complementary physiological modalities, and a parallel line of work has examined
how these signals can be incorporated into automatic sleep analysis. U-Sleep, for example, was developed to operate
across different EEG and EOG derivations and multiple sleep cohorts [37]. Other multimodal approaches combine
neural, ocular, muscular, cardiac, or respiratory signals through early fusion, modality-specific encoders, or higher-level
feature fusion. Recent work has also extended representation learning across PSG modalities, including SleepFM [38].
Cross-scale representations, self-supervised learning, and standardized sleep benchmarks have further broadened the
range of models evaluated across datasets [39–41].
Joint modeling of sleep stage and sleep-related disorders has received less attention than sleep staging alone.
Huttunen et al. [15] trained a single network for simultaneous sleep-stage and respiratory-event scoring across 877

2.3. Respiratory-Event Detection
Automated analysis of sleep-disordered breathing has largely developed as a separate task. Apnea and hypopnea
detectors commonly use airflow, thoracic and abdominal respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and ECG-derived cardiac
measures. These signals describe different stages of the physiological response: airflow reflects reduction or cessation
of ventilation, respiratory effort provides information about continued or absent breathing effort, and oximetry captures
the subsequent change in blood oxygenation. Oxygen desaturation also forms part of the clinical criteria used in
hypopnea scoring [42]. End-to-end deep models have also been developed for respiratory-event scoring, including
detection from a single respiratory-effort belt [43]. Biswal et al. [44] trained a recurrent–convolutional network to
jointly score sleep stages, apnea events, and limb movements from clinical PSG.
Respiratory-event models are typically optimized independently of sleep staging and often operate on temporal
resolutions different from the 30-s epoch used for stage classification. Joint models reduce this separation by learning
both outputs from the same recording.
2.4. Sleep and Sleep-Disordered Breathing After Stroke
Sleep physiology is frequently altered after stroke, and sleep disturbances have been associated with neurological
and functional recovery [1–3]. Changes in sleep-related neural activity have also been reported after cerebrovascular
injury. Previous studies describe alterations in sleep spindles, slow-wave activity, and related electrophysiological
patterns [17–21]; quantitative EEG measures such as the brain symmetry index have also been examined in relation
to hemispheric injury [22]. These changes make stroke sleep a distinct setting for models whose representations were
developed predominantly on non-stroke populations.
Sleep-disordered breathing is also common during the subacute phase, so sleep state and respiratory disturbance
coexist within the same clinical recording. Despite this overlap, automated stroke-specific sleep analysis remains
limited. The iSLEEPS dataset introduced a dedicated PSG resource for subacute ischemic stroke and reported baseline
sleep-staging performance [23]; subsequent work has further examined the behavior of deep staging models on this
cohort [24, 25]. Existing stroke-specific studies have therefore concentrated primarily on sleep-stage classification.
Joint epoch-level modeling of sleep stage and respiratory disturbance remains insufficiently characterized in this
population.
3. Datasets and Preprocessing
Four publicly available polysomnography datasets are used in this study (Table 1): iSLEEPS [23], Sleep-EDF
[45, 46], ISRUC-Sleep [47] with its three subgroups, and the St Vincent’s University Hospital / University College
Dublin sleep apnea database (UCD) [48]. iSLEEPS is the primary cohort used for model development and patient-
independent evaluation. Sleep-EDF is used as a healthy-sleep reference for implementation checks, pretraining of a
transferred baseline, and subject-independent evaluation of the staging model outside the stroke cohort. ISRUC-Sleep
provides independent sleep-disorder and healthy cohorts with both sleep-stage and respiratory-event annotations; it is
used both to train and evaluate MM-Net under subject-independent cross-validation and to test the iSLEEPS-trained
model without retraining. UCD provides a further sleep-apnea cohort recorded on a different acquisition system and
is used for transfer only. No external cohort is used to select the reported iSLEEPS model configuration.
3.1. iSLEEPS: Primary Stroke Cohort
The iSLEEPS dataset contains overnight PSG from 100 patients with subacute ischemic stroke [23]. The released
cohort has a mean age of 50.5 years and includes 77 male and 23 female participants. Sleep is scored into the five
AASM stages: Wake (W), N1, N2, N3, and rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep. Respiratory events are also annotated
throughout the recordings.
The public release contains 100 recordings. SN15 and SN28 contain the same overnight recording, so SN28 is
excluded, leaving 𝑁= 99 patients and 92,560 30-s epochs for the analyses reported here.

3.2. Sleep-EDF: Healthy-Sleep Reference Cohort
Sleep-EDF provides the healthy-sleep reference [45, 46]. The experiments use the Sleep Cassette subset corre-
sponding to subjects 0–20, comprising 40 overnight recordings, with splits constructed at the subject level because
individual subjects contribute more than one night. Sleep-EDF serves to check the reimplemented staging architectures
in the healthy-sleep setting in which they were originally evaluated, to pretrain the transferred baseline, and to evaluate
the proposed staging model on an independent healthy-sleep cohort. The database provides the EEG derivations Fpz-Cz
and Pz-Oz but not the cardiorespiratory signal set or respiratory-event annotations, so respiratory performance is not
evaluated on Sleep-EDF.

3.3. ISRUC-Sleep: Independent Sleep-Disorder Cohort
ISRUC-Sleep provides the independent evaluation in populations with and without sleep disorders [47]. Subgroup I
contains 100 patients with sleep disorders recorded on one night each, subgroup II eight patients recorded on two nights
each, and subgroup III ten healthy adults. The recordings include central and occipital EEG derivations corresponding
to C4, C3, O2, and O1 referenced to the contralateral mastoid, two EOG derivations and chin EMG, together with
oronasal airflow, nasal pressure, thoracic and abdominal effort, SpO2, and ECG, and annotations of obstructive, central,
and mixed apneas and hypopneas; the annotations of the first scorer are used. Summed effort is formed as the sum of
the thoracic and abdominal traces, and pulse as the beat-to-beat heart rate derived from ECG R-peaks, so that every
iSLEEPS input has an ISRUC counterpart. In 30 of the 126 ISRUC recordings the EDF header declares the EEG and
EOG range as ±25 𝜇V, which places their N3 peak-to-peak amplitude at 9–23 𝜇V against 98–283 𝜇V in the remaining
recordings and below the 75 𝜇V that defines N3; these channels are restored by a factor of ten, a correction decided
from the header alone. One subgroup-I recording stores monopolar EEG and EOG, from which the mastoid-referenced
derivations are formed.
ISRUC-Sleep therefore supports evaluation of every output of the proposed framework: sleep-stage prediction,
respiratory-event prediction and, in subgroup I, AHI estimation across the full severity range.
3.4. UCD: Independent Sleep-Apnea Cohort
The UCD database [46, 48] contains overnight PSG from 25 adults referred for suspected sleep-disordered
breathing, with annotated AHI between 1.2 and 81.2 events/h. It records C3 and C4 EEG only, which also supply
the occipital inputs, two EOG channels, chin EMG, ECG, oronasal airflow, thoracic, abdominal and summed effort,
SpO2 and pulse, and its scored apneas and hypopneas carry onset times. The EDF headers of this database carry no
physical unit, so EEG, EOG and EMG are scaled per recording to the median whole-night amplitude of iSLEEPS,
using no labels. UCD is used only for transfer.
3.5. Signal Selection and Harmonization
The iSLEEPS recordings contain 22 physiological and auxiliary traces. Fifteen signals are retained for modeling
and divided into neural and cardiorespiratory groups. The neural group contains four EEG derivations (C4:M1, C3:M2,
O2:M1, and O1:M2), two EOG derivations (E1:M2 and E2:M2), and the submental EMG. The cardiorespiratory group
contains ECG, oronasal thermal airflow, nasal pressure, thoracic effort, abdominal effort, summed respiratory effort,
SpO2, and pulse.
Signals associated with periodic limb movement, snoring, plethysmography, body position, ambient light, and
device operation are not included in the model. The snore channel is additionally excluded from respiratory prediction
because it is closely related to the airway information used during respiratory-event assessment. Twelve iSLEEPS
recordings lack at least one selected cardiorespiratory channel. These patients are retained, and unavailable inputs are
zero-filled. The same convention is used when a selected signal is unavailable in an external dataset.
3.6. Preprocessing and Epoch Labels
All sleep-stage predictions are defined on 30-s epochs. For iSLEEPS and ISRUC-Sleep, the respiratory annotations
are mapped to the same epoch grid. An epoch is assigned a positive respiratory label if a scored respiratory event begins
within it and a negative label otherwise. The primary respiratory task is therefore prediction of respiratory-event onset
within a 30-s epoch, and the patient-level AHI is derived from these predictions (Section 4.8).
iSLEEPS retains only the epochs scored as a sleep stage, so every cardiorespiratory signal and every respiratory-
event label is cut at the true recording time of each retained epoch, keeping it on the same timeline as the EEG. The
annotated AHI is the number of apneas and hypopneas starting in scored sleep per hour of scored sleep.
The EEG, EOG, and EMG signals are resampled to 100 Hz for the engineered descriptors of Section 4.2, and the
four EEG and two EOG derivations are resampled independently to 200 Hz for the LaBraM encoder, which does not
receive the EMG. The eight cardiorespiratory inputs are resampled to a common 25 Hz grid by polyphase resampling
and remain in their recorded physical units; SpO2, recorded at 4 Hz, is placed on the same grid without gaining temporal
resolution. Engineered features are standardized within each recording, which for iSLEEPS is the patient, and the same
per-recording procedure is applied to the external datasets, from which no labels are used to refit parameters, select
thresholds, or adapt the reported iSLEEPS model.

Adaptation Within each training fold, LaBraM-Base (5.8 M parameters) is fine-tuned with a linear staging head on

on the fold’s validation patients is kept. Test patients never reach the encoder.

5.1. Protocol and Metrics
All results use ten-fold, patient-independent cross-validation: no patient appears in both training and test, and
within each training split a disjoint set of patients is held out for early stopping and model selection. Staging is scored
by accuracy, macro-F1 1

MM-Net and its encoder configurations are trained with three seeds (42, 1, 7) on the same folds. The single-model
figure is the mean over the thirty fold-values, ten folds by three seeds; the three-seed figure averages the softmax outputs
of the three seeds before decoding. Every number is a mean over the ten folds with its standard deviation across folds.
Paired comparisons use the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the ten fold means, because the three seeds within
a fold train and test on the same patients; with ten folds the smallest attainable 𝑝is 0.002, and Holm correction is
applied within each family of comparisons.
The frozen-encoder configuration, the initial configuration of the same architecture, serves as the reference for the
encoder analyses. It shares the streams, decoder and heads of MM-Net but reads a frozen LaBraM embedding of the
EEG, seven cardiorespiratory channels, concatenation fusion and square-root class weights. Its stream, decoder and
regularization settings, which MM-Net inherits, were chosen among forty-nine candidates; repeating that selection
inside a nested procedure, with five outer patient-independent folds whose test patients take no part in the choice,
gives 0.734 ± 0.009 accuracy against 0.739, a selection optimism of 0.006, about a third of the fold-to-fold standard
deviation. The encoder, fusion operator and class weighting of the final MM-Net were selected on validation patients
only.
5.2. Implementation and Hyperparameters
The model is implemented in PyTorch and trained on NVIDIA RTX 2060 (6 GB) and RTX 3060 Ti (8 GB) GPUs.
Table 3 lists the training configuration, which is identical across folds and cohorts. The reimplemented baselines
are trained with the optimizer, batch size and schedule of their own papers, because imposing our settings could
disadvantage architectures tuned for other settings.
5.3. Compared Methods

5.5. Integrating the Sleep-Adapted Encoder
Table 6 builds MM-Net from the frozen-encoder configuration one change at a time, every row on the same folds
and seeds. Uniform class weights and cross-attention fusion contribute +0.009 together for the single model. The
largest change comes from the encoder. Adapting LaBraM to sleep raises its own single-epoch accuracy from 0.647 to
0.709, in all ten folds, yet a sleep-adapted encoder whose training embeddings are cross-fitted but not aligned moves
the three-seed accuracy of the downstream model by only +0.002 (𝑝= 0.63) and falls below the frozen encoder
in five folds of ten, by up to 0.023. Aligning the embedding spaces turns the same encoder into a gain of +0.014
(0.755 →0.769, 𝑝= 0.020), and of +0.015 for the single model in nine folds of ten (𝑝= 0.027). Figure 5 shows the
effect fold by fold: without alignment the adapted encoder moves accuracy in either direction, whereas with alignment
it raises accuracy in eight folds of ten, by up to 3.7 percentage points. The downstream benefit of adaptation therefore
appears once the training embeddings share the coordinates of the encoder used at test time.
The complete MM-Net adds three elements that serve REM and the respiratory head more than overall staging: the

ahead of the frozen-encoder configuration in nine folds of ten (+0.018, 𝑝= 0.004; Section 5.10). Figure 6 shows what
adaptation changes in the representation: held-out epochs that the frozen encoder spreads across one mixed cloud form
stage-specific clusters after adaptation, with Wake, REM and N3 separated (silhouette by stage −0.045 →0.047).

5.8. AHI Estimation
Summed over decoded sleep, the respiratory scores estimate the AHI (Section 4.8) with Spearman 𝜌= 0.765
against the annotated index over the 99 test patients, a mean absolute error of 10.4 events/h, and 54% of patients in the

with the annotated index at 𝜌= 0.687, and against that clinical index MM-Net reaches 𝜌= 0.633. In the expected-
count formulation, summing the onset scores over decoded sleep counts closely spaced events separately, whereas
thresholding the scores into runs would merge them, and normalizing by decoded sleep time matches the denominator
of the clinical index. Staging remains stable across sleep-disordered-breathing severity: accuracy is 0.784 in patients
with a normal clinical AHI and 0.754, 0.764 and 0.758 in the mild, moderate and severe groups, with no significant
ordered trend (Jonckheere–Terpstra 𝑝= 0.20; Table 11), so the model stages the most severely affected patients nearly

Table 15 sets the proposed model beside representative alternatives on size and capability. MM-Net holds 2.85 M

6. Discussion
The central finding of this study concerns how a pretrained EEG encoder, once adapted to the target cohort, enters
a downstream model. Adapting LaBraM to sleep raises its own staging accuracy from 0.647 to 0.709, yet the adapted
encoder adds nothing measurable to the downstream model until its training embeddings are cross-fitted and mapped
into the space of the deployed encoder (Table 6). The failure mode, a downstream model trained on embeddings whose
coordinates differ from those it receives at test time, may apply to any pipeline that fine-tunes a foundation model and
then trains a second model on its outputs, and the remedy costs 𝐾additional fine-tuning runs and a ridge regression per
part. The aligned encoder separates Wake, REM and N3 into distinct clusters (Figure 6), and with it MM-Net stages
iSLEEPS at 0.763 (𝜅= 0.656), ahead of every architecture retrained on the same folds. The integration carries over
to ISRUC-I, where the complete model is ahead of the frozen-encoder configuration in nine folds of ten.
The same model delivers the respiratory output and the AHI, and the joint design is supported directly: joint training
improves staging where disordered breathing disrupts sleep, improves respiratory-event detection and yields a closer
M. W. Suva et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier
Page 20 of 24


MM-Net: sleep staging and sleep apnea assessment after ischemic stroke
AHI estimate than two single-task models (Table 8), and each stream carries the output its physiology is closest to, with
respiratory effort and not the scoring airflow channel as the strongest individual respiratory input (Table 9). Huttunen
et al. [15] demonstrated simultaneous sleep staging and respiratory-event scoring in a large suspected-obstructive-
sleep-apnea cohort. Under the common iSLEEPS evaluation their architecture reaches 0.599 staging accuracy against
0.756 for MM-Net, and on ISRUC-I, trained and evaluated on the same labels, 0.691 staging accuracy and 0.863
respiratory AUC against 0.803 and 0.889. This extends joint sleep analysis to a stroke-specific setting, where the
available montage, cohort size, neural physiology, and respiratory annotation differ from those of conventional sleep-
apnea studies, and indicates that joint PSG analysis in stroke benefits from representations and signal organization
adapted to the characteristics of the target cohort. On iSLEEPS the respiratory output reaches 0.825 AUC, 0.152 above
gradient boosting on engineered descriptors and 0.092 above a raw-signal network on seven cardiorespiratory channels,
and it is strongest on obstructive and central apneas. Summed over decoded sleep, the same scores estimate the AHI
with 𝜌= 0.765 against the annotated index, so a single model characterizes both sleep architecture and respiratory
disturbance in stroke PSG.
The respiratory output is trained at the 30-s temporal scale of staging, as a score for the onset of an event within each
epoch, and the AHI follows from it without a separate counting rule. The expected-count formulation counts closely
spaced events separately and normalizes by the decoded sleep time, as the clinical index does, and its agreement with
the annotated index (𝜌= 0.765) exceeds the agreement of the clinical index recorded with the corpus (𝜌= 0.687).
Staging accuracy is 0.784 in patients with a normal AHI and remains at 0.754–0.764 in the mild, moderate and severe
groups, without a significant trend, so the model stages the patients with the greatest respiratory burden nearly as well
as those without it. Sleep architecture and respiratory disturbance remain distinct prediction problems at the signal
level while sharing clinically relevant structure at the level of the overnight recording, which supports their analysis
within a common framework in a stroke population where both processes shape the physiological state of the patient
during sleep.
Performance depends strongly on the population in which a model is evaluated. Deep architectures retrained on
iSLEEPS reach approximately 0.60–0.72 accuracy, against 0.79–0.84 for the same architectures on Sleep-EDF, a
decrease of approximately 0.10 to 0.15 for each model; because population, montage, and acquisition conditions change
together, this difference is most appropriately read as a substantial shift between conventional healthy-sleep data and
subacute stroke PSG. MM-Net reaches 0.864 on Sleep-EDF, 0.803 on ISRUC-I and 0.763 within the stroke cohort.
Trained on ISRUC-I, MM-Net reaches 0.803 accuracy and 0.889 respiratory AUC and is ahead of every retrained
baseline on the same folds. Trained on iSLEEPS alone and applied without retraining, it detects respiratory events
at 0.833 and 0.841 AUC on ISRUC-I and on UCD, an apnea cohort recorded on a different system, against 0.825
in-cohort, and estimates the AHI with 𝜌= 0.631 and 0.653. The learned representations are therefore not confined
to a single recording environment. Nasal pressure was the input whose contribution depended most on the acquisition
system, and the seven-channel variant is the appropriate choice for transfer.
The framework combines sleep-stage and respiratory-event information from the same PSG, preserves the
specialization of the neural and cardiorespiratory streams, and exploits their shared temporal context. The encoder-
integration experiments show how a pretrained EEG representation is brought into the model, the benchmark and
respiratory comparisons show what the integrated model delivers, and external evaluation demonstrates that the
resulting representations retain utility across distinct PSG datasets. For subacute ischemic-stroke PSG, MM-Net
provides sleep staging, respiratory-event detection and AHI estimation from one pass over the recording, built on
a sleep-adapted EEG encoder whose representations are brought into the downstream model through cross-fitting and
alignment.
6.1. Limitations
The stroke-specific evaluation rests on iSLEEPS, recorded at a single center; the external cohorts establish transfer
across acquisition systems and populations, and a second public stroke PSG cohort, once available, would allow a
stroke-specific replication. Staging is decoded over the whole night by the hidden-Markov pass, so the system is
designed for offline scoring of a completed recording. The respiratory output is scored per 30-s epoch and summarized
as an AHI estimate; event-level scoring with onset and duration boundaries is a natural extension.

7. Conclusion
This study addresses the joint characterization of sleep architecture and respiratory disturbances in subacute
ischemic stroke, a setting in which altered neural physiology and heterogeneous cardiorespiratory information
challenge conventional sleep-analysis approaches. MM-Net demonstrates that sleep-adapted EEG representations can
be integrated into a multimodal temporal framework through cross-fitting and embedding alignment, improving sleep-
stage discrimination while supporting concurrent respiratory-event detection.
The findings establish two complementary aspects of the proposed approach. First, the integration of adapted
neural representations provides a means of addressing the training–inference mismatch encountered when pretrained
encoders are incorporated into downstream physiological models. Second, controlled interventions reveal distinct roles
for neural and cardiorespiratory information within the joint framework, showing that shared temporal modeling can
accommodate physiologically specialized prediction tasks.
Patient-independent evaluation and experiments across independent sleep cohorts support the applicability of
this approach beyond a single recording population. Together, these results establish a computational framework for
joint analysis of neural and respiratory physiology after ischemic stroke, while providing a foundation for further
investigation of representation consistency and multimodal generalization in clinical sleep analysis.
