Cross-User Adaptation for sEMG Gesture
Recognition via Continual Learning
Aran Ramakrishnan, Shubhi Mishra, Tyler Ham, Assel Kembay, and Jason Eshraghian
Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering
University of California, Santa Cruz, CA 95064, USA
Email: {aramakr4, smishr21, tham, akembay, jsn}@ucsc.edu
Abstract—Prosthetic and assistive interfaces increasingly de-
pend on decoding hand gestures from surface electromyogra-
phy (sEMG). Because the signal is shaped by person-specific
factors, the recurrent neural networks (RNNs) commonly used
to model these temporal streams must adapt to new wearers
and new gesture classes after deployment. Continual learning
(CL) enables this adaptation without retraining from scratch, yet
applying it to recurrent sEMG models is not straightforward.
Standard recurrent training practices can destabilize learning,
and which CL strategy holds up under realistic cross-user drift
is unestablished. We evaluate three representative CL strategies
under domain- and class-incremental drift on NinaPro DB1, and
find experience replay to outperform elastic weight consolidation
(EWC) and naive fine-tuning. It reaches 83.3% final accuracy
for class-incremental drift while limiting forgetting to 1.8-5.8%
accuracy drop, as against 16.2-28.5% for EWC and naive
training. We further show these models must be trained on
shuffled, independently-windowed segments. Streaming windows
in temporal order collapses the network into predicting a single
dominant gesture, while a shuffled, stateless regime trains stably.
Finally, hand-crafted time-domain features raise per-task accu-
racy by up to ∼40% over raw signals, but only replay preserves
that gain under class-incremental drift.
Index Terms—Electromyography, Continual learning, Gesture
recognition, Catastrophic forgetting
I. INTRODUCTION
Gesture recognition from surface electromyography (sEMG)
has gained particular significance with the growing demand
for precise and reliable control interfaces in prosthetic and
assistive technologies. Recognition is person-specific. Muscle
anatomy, electrode placement, and movement style all influ-
ence the signal, so a model narrowed to a single wearer
typically outperforms a general-purpose decoder. However,
personalization requires regimes that adapt to new wearers
and expanding gesture vocabularies. Continual Learning (CL)
provides exactly this capability, updating a deployed model
on new subjects and gestures without retraining from scratch.
The primary challenge in CL is catastrophic forgetting, where
learning new tasks causes accuracy to decline on past ones [1].
CL methods to counter forgetting are maturing rapidly, but
they are developed and benchmarked almost entirely outside
the recurrent models that dominate sEMG processing, leaving
limited insight on which CL strategies survive realistic cross-
user drift, or whether standard recurrent training practices even
hold up under it.
We evaluate naive, EWC, and experience-replay strate-
gies across domain-incremental (DIL) and class-incremental
(CIL) formulations, using both classic recurrent networks and
modern state-space models [2] on NinaPro DB1 [3], [4].
Our analysis yields three findings that build on one another.
First, comparing CL strategies across both scenarios and both
backbones, we find experience replay the most robust, and
the choice of CL method outweighs the choice of recurrent
architecture. This prompted us to focus on memory retention
strategies, leading to the second finding. Carrying hidden state
across tasks is ill-posed, and naively training in temporal order
collapses the network to a majority-class predictor. This is
an effect we trace primarily to batch ordering rather than to
recurrent memory. We demonstrate a solution by purging the
statefulness between tasks, and also shuffling data samples.
This corrected regime is what makes our third finding hold.
Time-domain features improve how well each task is learned,
but only rehearsal preserves that improvement under class-
incremental drift.
II. PRIOR WORK
Most prior work on CL for sEMG generates empirical
insight into how forgetting-mitigation strategies behave on
this signal [5]–[7]. Chen et al. [5] establish the feasibility
of cross-subject adaptation, though by aggregating subjects
into a few grouped sets rather than treating each as an
independent sequential task. Others examine the choice of
memory mechanism or model families: Kanoga et al. [7]
apply deep generative replay under class-incremental drift, and
Yoneda et al. [6] compare generative against discriminative
model families under the same settings. These studies, how-
ever, evaluate predominantly within a single CL scenario and
over feedforward or generative decoders, leaving the recurrent
models standard for sEMG largely untested.
We build on this body of work by testing more systemat-
ically across both domain- and class-incremental drift, and
across two recurrent families: the classic LSTM [8] and
HGRN [9], a modern state-space model (SSM) representative
of current state-of-the-art sequence architectures. This sys-
tematic comparison is what leads us to improve retention by
altering how recurrent state is handled across windows.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Dataset and Task Formulation
We evaluated on NinaPro Database 1 [3], [4], which con-
tains sEMG data from 27 subjects, which contains three dis-
arXiv:2610.34011v1  [cs.LG]  25 Oct 2026


tinct exercises, each containing a set of isotonic and isometric
hand and wrist gestures. Exercise 1 includes 12 gestures,
Exercise 2 has 17, and Exercise 3 has 23. We split the task
into two continual learning scenarios as outlined in van de Ven
& Tolias [10]:
Domain-Incremental Learning (DIL): In this setup, the
gesture set is statically fixed to the gestures from Exercise 1.
The model is trained sequentially across the 27 subjects, where
each subject is treated as an independent continual learning
task. This tests generalization across anatomical variances and
sensor placement discrepancies between individuals without
forgetting the core temporal patterns.
Class-Incremental Learning (CIL): In this setup, the
model is trained sequentially across an expanding stream of
data (Exercise 1 →Exercise 2 →Exercise 3). The ground truth
labels for the new gestures in subsequent exercises must be
offset sequentially in order to allow for CIL. For the HGRN [9]
outlined in §III-C1, the classification head is sized statically
at initialization to accommodate all classes, with the “rest”
state (class 0) remaining shared throughout all tasks. The
expansion of the class set makes this task more difficult than
DIL, since the model must generalize to new labels, while
retaining performance on past samples.
B. Signal Preprocessing and Feature Extraction
Data was partitioned at the repetition level to ensure that
no samples from the same gesture execution appeared in
both sets, with a 70/30 train/test split. Following this split,
the signals were normalised to achieve zero mean and unit
variance, with the scaler fitted on training data and applied to
both sets. Signals were then segmented using a sliding window
approach [11]. The ground truth label for each window was
determined by the active stimulus at the final sample. Time-
Domain Feature Extraction: Each 200-sample window was
divided into K = 10 equal sub-windows of 20 samples (10
ms) each and transformed into a time-domain (TD) feature
sequence following the Hudgins feature set [12] (MAV, RMS,
WL, ZC, SSC) with a noise-rejection threshold ϵ = 0.01σ
applied to ZC and SSC to suppress spurious crossings [13].
A second standardisation pass was applied to the TD feature
matrix, using per-feature mean and variance fitted on training
windows only.
C. Base Network Architectures
We evaluate two recurrent baseline architectures with a
common configuration: L = 4 layers, hidden dimension
D = 128, and a shared classification head.
1) Long Short-Term Memory: The LSTM [8] mitigates the
vanishing gradient problem through explicit memory cells,
making it a standard sequential baseline for this experiment.
2) Hierarchical Gated Recurrent Network: The HGRN [9]
is a gated linear recurrence model that extends the standard
forget-gate formulation with a hierarchical lower bound (γ ∈
(0, 1)D), initialized proportionally to layer depth (γinit = l/L).
This biases shallow layers to hold short-term relations and
deep layers to have long-range retention. The recurrence is
executed using a hardware-aware chunked linear-recurrence
kernel (FLA/Triton [14], [15]).
Shared Classification Head. For both architectures, the
hidden state at the final timestep is passed to a linear head
mapping to the active class set. Under the TD feature regime,
the input shape is [N, K, 50] (K = 10); under the raw regime,
it is [N, T, C] (T = 200, C = 10).
D. Continual Learning Methods
1) Naive Stateless and Stateful Baselines: We evaluated
two naive training regimes that serve as lower bounds before
any memory retention mechanism is applied. In the Stateless
baseline, the recurrent hidden state is reset to zero (h0 = 0)
at the start of every batch. This is the intuitive choice for
independently-windowed EMG data. Consecutive windows
are extracted with overlap from a fixed-length recording and
carry no causal dependency across gesture boundaries. In the
Stateful baseline, the hidden state is carried across batches
within and across tasks, simulating an unbroken temporal
stream.

IV. RESULTS
A. CL Method Comparison
Across all evaluated configurations, replay-based methods
consistently demonstrated the strongest resistance to catas-
trophic forgetting (Table I), as reflected by their lower forget-
ting values. This trend held regardless of the continual-learning
scenario (DIL or CIL) or the underlying model architecture
(HGRN or LSTM), indicating that rehearsal is a robust strategy
for stabilizing EMG-based learning. Stateless replay yielded
the highest final accuracy, with CIL-LSTM reaching 83.3%
accuracy and CIL-HGRN reaching 81.6% accuracy, while
maintaining forgetting values as low as 1.8%–5.8% accuracy
drop. EWC exhibited substantially lower final accuracies and
larger forgetting (16.2%–28.5%) accuracy drop, and naive
models also had similar forgetting measures.

Despite CIL being the harder task, replay under CIL
achieves lower forgetting (1.8%–2.3% accuracy drop) than
under DIL (4.3%–5.8% accuracy drop). This can likely be
attributed to the size of task streams. The CIL stream com-
prises only three tasks, against the 27 tasks of the DIL stream.
Therefore, forgetting, which is averaged over transitions and
accumulates with sequence length, is expected to be smaller
under CIL on this basis alone.

B. Feature Representation
We evaluated both raw sEMG signals and time-domain (TD)
Hudgins features as input representations, reflected in Table II.
In the DIL setting, TD features consistently produced higher
accuracies, by ∼40%. They also remove the training instability
seen with raw windows. Whereas raw features yield high
seed variance for the weaker configurations (e.g. LSTM naive,
±0.093; LSTM EWC, ±0.067), every TD DIL configuration
converges to a tight range (std ≤0.03).
However, this benefit was scenario-dependent, as in the
class-incremental settings, TD features only help replay, while
for naive and EWC the change was negligible. Although
TD raises immediate accuracy, it also causes an increase
in forgetting in non-rehearsal methods (e.g. LSTM EWC,
12.3% →28.5% drop in accuracy on old tasks), causing the
initial gain to be undone. Ultimately, TD features improve
learning, and rehearsal is able to convert that improvement
into retention.

C. Carrying State
The windowing in §III-B considers each segment to be
independent samples, so recurrent models should read each
window with a zero state. Therefore, we see failures in two
ways depending on batch ordering when trying to use this data
as a continuous stream.
Considering the case where windows are presented in a
temporal order, although carried state is logical, training
collapses. This is because in rest-dominated sets of sEMG data
the gradient is consumed by the rest class causing the network
to fall into a constant predictor. If the models are shuffled, then
holding the state results in irrelevant noise being injected to a
batch’s initial state. This is no different from a stateless regime
with a random start state.

This is confirmed by a 2 × 2 ablation over {stateful,

conditions result in a plasticity collapse (27%–45%, below
60% threshold) into the frozen region. We even see that
stateless+shuffle-off freezes, which shows that ordering drives
the heavy collapse. With shuffling restored, stateful recovers
to 68%–76% but remains below stateless at 94%–95%, for
reasons discussed above.
D. EWC Regularization Quality
EWC’s limited effectiveness in our setting stems from two
compounding factors. First, calibrating λewc is non-trivial at
scale: values tuned on a 6-subject proxy do not transfer
to the full 27-subject sweep, consistent with the theoretical
finding that a fixed λ becomes increasingly rigid as task
count grows [19]. For the TD feature regime, λewc = 30
was the largest value preserving plasticity across the full
sequence. Second, and more fundamentally, an excessively
large λ freezes the network, producing high apparent ac-
curacy through weight immobilization rather than genuine
consolidation [20]. We identify frozen networks via the late-
train plasticity metric (plasticity < 0.60 on final tasks). EWC
configurations that avoid freezing still exhibit forgetting in the
range of 0.162–0.285 accuracy drop, confirming that Fisher-
based regularization is insufficient to constrain parameter drift
under the strong distributional shifts present in both DIL and
CIL.
E. Effect of Recurrent Architecture
LSTM and HGRN perform comparably across CL methods
and scenarios, with no configuration showing a consistent
architectural advantage. The top-ranked configuration uses
LSTM-CIL replay (83.3% accuracy), while the second uses
HGRN-CIL replay (81.6% accuracy), a difference within one
standard deviation.

V. CONCLUSION
We evaluated continual-learning strategies for sEMG-based
gesture classification across 27 subjects and three exercises
under DIL and CIL formulations. Experience replay out-
performed EWC and naive baselines, achieving 83.3% final
accuracy with forgetting as low as 1.8% drop in accuracy. It
is clear that the dominant factor in long-term retention was the
CL method rather than the recurrent architecture. Concerning
stateful collapse, a 2×2 ablation attributes the collapse to
batch ordering rather than recurrent state. We attribute this
to windowing creating independent segments of sEMG data.
Finally, time-domain features improved per-task learning but
required rehearsal to retain that gain under class-incremental
drift. Although we observed lower forgetting under CIL than
DIL using replay, this could be attributed to the CIL stream
being much smaller than the DIL stream (3 vs 27).
These results offer a practical foundation for prosthetic
control systems that must adapt to new users and gestures
over time. While this paper explores the effects of different
CL methods and recurrent architectures, the experiments were
conducted offline, only using NinaPro DB1. Future work
should examine whether results for the aforementioned CL
methods and architectures transfer to other datasets in different
experimental settings.
