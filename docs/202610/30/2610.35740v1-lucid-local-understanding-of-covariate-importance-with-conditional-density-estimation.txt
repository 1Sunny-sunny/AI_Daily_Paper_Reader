Abstract
Computing local-level variable importance (VI) is critical for understanding machine learning
models. Local permutation-based VI metrics offer a simple yet powerful approach, measuring
how much a model’s output changes when perturbing its inputs. However, existing perturbation
strategies often ignore feature dependence or account for it only through restricted approxima-
tions. Perturbing a feature without fully respecting this dependence can generate unrealistic
counterfactuals, thereby evaluating the model in regions unsupported by the observed data
distribution. We address this gap by introducing a new model-agnostic local VI metric and
estimator, Local Understanding of Covariate Importance with conditional Density
estimation (LUCID). LUCID perturbs features of interest using plausible values drawn from the
full conditional distribution given the remaining observed features. We establish theoretical con-
nections between LUCID and existing VI measures, and provide an estimator with convergence
guarantees and uncertainty quantification. Finally, we demonstrate LUCID’s stable performance
and practical utility across simulated and real-world examples.

Introduction
Variable importance (VI) is a fundamental tool
for interpreting machine learning models, en-
abling the characterization of features’ usage and
their effects on the model’s predictions. VI is par-
ticularly useful for probing the otherwise opaque
behavior of black-box machine learning systems
(Guidotti et al., 2018; Casalicchio et al., 2019;
Fisher et al., 2019). Most established VI methods
summarize each feature with a single population-
level score or ranking (Breiman, 2001; Gregorutti
et al., 2015). However, a feature may strongly
influence the model in one region of the covariate
space while being irrelevant in another, so averag-
ing importance across the population can obscure
when, where, and for which observations a feature
matters. For example, in the Bike Sharing dataset
(Fanaee-T and Gama, 2014), the importance of
temperature and humidity changes substantially
over the course of a day, while their global VI
scores, shown as dashed lines, collapse this varia-
tion into single values and obscure the underlying
heterogeneity (Figure 1(a)). More fundamentally,
distinct local importance patterns can share the
same population average, meaning that global im-
portance alone cannot identify how importance
is distributed across observations.
This moti-
vates local VI and its accurate estimation, which
makes observation-level feature usage accessible
and characterizes heterogeneous feature usage
across individual predictions.
A central challenge for local VI is determin-
ing how to perturb the feature.

dependence-aware alternatives, their perturba-

mations whose validity requires additional as-

2023; Borgonovo et al., 2026; Strobl et al., 2008;

Blesch et al., 2025). Such schemes can recover
population-average importance while producing
systematically biased local scores, because er-
rors across observations may cancel after averag-
ing (Section 5.1 DGP 3). Accurate local estima-
tion therefore requires capturing the observation-
specific conditional structure. This motivates flex-
ible estimation of the full conditional distribution,
which existing perturbation schemes may struggle
to capture, particularly under heteroskedasticity,
multimodality, high dimensionality, or complex
interactions.
In this work, we introduce Local Under-
standing of Covariate Importance with con-
ditional Density estimation (LUCID), a model-
agnostic framework for measuring feature impor-
tance at the observation level. For a fixed predic-
tive model, LUCID replaces a feature of interest
with plausible values drawn from its conditional
density given the remaining observed features,
and measures the resulting change in model be-
havior. Our main contributions are:
• We establish an interpretable decomposition of
LUCID that captures local leave-one-covariate-
out (LOCO) importance without refitting the
predictive model and additionally quantifies
conditional prediction variability omitted by
local LOCO.
• We develop a flexible estimation framework
for LUCID that accommodates modern con-
ditional density estimators (CDEs), establish
convergence guarantees, and provide a Bayesian
implementation with posterior uncertainty quan-
tification.
• We extend LUCID to explain feature groups,
enabling observation-level assessment of their
joint importance conditional on the observed
covariates outside the group.
• Through extensive synthetic and real-data ex-
periments, we demonstrate LUCID’s ability to
capture heterogeneous local importance under
complex covariate dependence, together with
the high accuracy, robustness, and computa-
tional scalability of our implementation.

Related Work
Permutation-based variable importance measures
feature relevance through changes in model behav-
ior under feature perturbations, beginning with
the Random Forest permutation importance of
Breiman (2001). Subsequent work has developed
a range of marginal and conditional variable im-
portance methods at both global and local levels.
We provide a detailed discussion of these meth-

ods in Section A. In contrast, LUCID defines
observation-level importance with respect to the
full conditional feature distribution. Accurate es-
timation of this distribution yields replacements
that preserve the dependence between the feature
of interest and the remaining covariates
Other notions of feature importance and lo-
cal explanation target different estimands. We
discuss connections to LOCO (Lei et al., 2018),
SHAP (Lundberg and Lee, 2017; Aas et al., 2021),
LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), grouped variable im-
portance (Gregorutti et al., 2015; Au et al., 2022;
Chamma et al., 2024; Little et al., 2026), and
other explanation paradigms in Section A.

3.3
Understanding LUCID
Under squared loss, LUCID admits a simple de-
composition into two terms, each asking a sepa-
rate question: (i) how varied are the predictions
from g across different counterfactual values of Z;
(ii) how extreme was the prediction for the ob-
served value of Z compared to the predictions for
possible counterfactual values of Z? We first es-
tablish this decomposition for squared-error loss,
illustrating the theoretical connection to local
LOCO. We then extend the decomposition to
Bregman divergences generated by differentiable,
strictly convex functions.

Globally, dispersion and displacement contribute
equally on average over the data distribution
(Corollary C.2). At the local level, however, they
capture distinct mechanisms through which a
feature can be important for a particular obser-
vation. The dispersion term measures how much
the model output varies across conditionally plau-
sible replacements of Z.
In contrast, the displacement term measures
how far the original output lies from the average
prediction across perturbations. So, if we observe
a value of Z that leads to an extreme prediction
far from the central tendency, displacement will
be high. Both quantities measure how important
the feature is for making the prediction, but in
different ways. Dispersion asks if predictions vary
wildly across possible values of Z | x while dis-
placement asks if the prediction for the observed
data point is itself an extreme/outlier compared
to the average prediction. As illustrated in Fig-
ure 2, LUCID can therefore be large because the
perturbed outputs vary substantially (high dis-
persion), because the observed output lies far
from their mean (high displacement), or both.

3.4
LUCID for Feature Groups
Individual features’ LUCID may fail to capture
joint effects when features carry overlapping in-
formation. For example, suppose a prediction
model depends on both temperature and season,
while these features are strongly predictable from
one another. Conditioning on temperature can
substantially restrict plausible replacements of
season, and vice versa, yielding small individual
LUCID scores. Such scores reflect limited unique
information, however, and do not imply that the
two features are jointly unimportant. We there-
fore extend LUCID to feature groups (Gregorutti
et al., 2015; Au et al., 2022; Chamma et al., 2024).
For a group of features Z, let X denote the re-
maining conditioning covariates. We define the
group LUCID as
LUCID(z, x) := EZ′∼P(·|x)
ℓ
 g(Z′, x), g(z, x)

The definition extends directly to a group of fea-
tures by replacing the scalar Z with a vector Z.
Let G denote a feature group with d = |G|. In
general, group LUCID need not equal the sum
of the corresponding individual LUCID scores,
because individual replacements condition on the
other group members, whereas group replacement
allows all members to vary jointly. Examining
individual and group LUCID together provides a
fuller picture, as illustrated in Section 5.2.

Conclusion
We present LUCID, a model-agnostic framework
for local variable importance based on the full
conditional feature distribution. LUCID charac-
terizes where and how strongly a fixed model re-
sponds to conditionally plausible feature changes,
with an interpretable decomposition, convergence
guarantees, and Bayesian uncertainty quantifica-
tion. Its flexible estimation framework accommo-
dates modern conditional density estimators and
feature groups. Future work could extend LU-
CID to outcome-reference importance and model
class reliance (Fisher et al., 2019), moving from
explaining a fixed model toward assessing impor-
tance for the prediction problem.
Also, since
global conditional permutation importance is the
expectation of LUCID, accurate local estimation
also supports accurate global conditional impor-
tance.

Related Work
Perturbation-based variable importance.
Breiman (2001) introduced permutation-based VI
in his seminal work on Random Forests, recognizing that if predictions change when shuffling the
inputs to the forest (i.e., resampling them from their respective marginal distributions), then the
forest likely depends on the feature. This core principle has inspired subsequent approaches to
global perturbation-based variable importance that go beyond sampling from marginal distributions,
but the sampling distribution in these methods is often restricted and its validity requires additional
assumptions. Residual-based approaches (Fisher et al., 2019; Chamma et al., 2023; Borgonovo et al.,
2026) estimate the conditional mean of the feature of interest and use the residual distribution to
generate counterfactuals, which can yield biased importance estimates when the residual distribution
varies with the conditioning covariates, such as under heteroskedasticity or other changes in the
conditional residual distribution. Tree- or subgroup-based approaches (Strobl et al., 2008; Debeer
and Strobl, 2020; Molnar et al., 2024; Chamma et al., 2023) permute or resample feature values
within regions defined by tree-based partitions, which may overlook local variation in the conditional
distribution, while overly fine partitions leave too few observations for resampling. Approaches
based on joint-distribution estimation (Blesch et al., 2025) instead require modeling the full joint
distribution, which can become increasingly challenging as the ambient dimension grows (Izbicki and
Lee, 2016). There are several recent methods that extend permutation-based importance to the local
level, including Individual Conditional Importance (ICI; Casalicchio et al., 2019) and Conditional
Local Importance by Quantile Expectations (CLIQUE; Bladen et al., 2026).
However, these
existing local perturbation-based methods ignore dependence between the perturbed feature and
the remaining covariates. In contrast, LUCID defines observation-level importance with respect to
the full conditional feature distribution. Accurate estimation of this distribution yields replacements
that preserve dependence between the feature of interest and the remaining covariates.
Other explanation and importance paradigms.
Several related methods target different
notions of feature importance. (i) Local explanation methods also explain individual predictions
by assigning contributions to features. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-based methods
(Lundberg and Lee, 2017; Aas et al., 2021) evaluate changes in model outputs as features are added
or marginalized over, with conditional SHAP using conditional feature densities to account for
dependencies between features (Aas et al., 2021; Jullum et al., 2025). However, their estimand
differs from LUCID, as SHAP allocates the deviation of an individual prediction from a baseline
across feature coalitions, whereas LUCID measures the change in model behavior induced by
conditionally plausible replacements of a specified feature while holding the remaining context fixed.
LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), by contrast, constructs a locally linear surrogate approximation of
the prediction function, using the local linear regression’s parameters as explanations for the black
box. Both of these methods can unfaithfully capture nonzero variable attribution to features that
are inactive at the observation being explained, especially when the data exhibits discontinuities.
As we show in Figure 3, LIME, SHAP, and SHAPr (a SHAP variant) all yield non-zero feature
13


attribution even when the feature is completely unused by some specific regions of the known
outcome generating function. (ii) Feature-removal methods such as leave-one-covariate-out (LOCO;
Lei et al., 2018) importance measure how much predictive information is lost by retraining the
predictive model with all-but-one of the original features. In practice, this change can arise from
both information loss due to feature removal and errors introduced by model refitting, making it
difficult to disentangle these effects. Moreover, because LOCO compares two different models, it
does not directly quantify how the original fitted model relies on the omitted feature. Instead, it
describes how much the algorithm that discovered the model relies on the feature (Fisher et al., 2019).
At the population level and under squared loss, however, LOCO with optimal reduced predictor is
proportional to and closely related to conditional permutation feature importance (Reyero-Lobo
et al., 2025). We further explore this relationship at the observation level in Section 3.3. (iii)
Grouped VI methods measure the importance of sets of related features jointly to account for shared
or redundant information (Gregorutti et al., 2015; Au et al., 2022; Chamma et al., 2024; Little et al.,
2026). Our framework naturally extends this idea but to the local level, quantifying conditional
importance for feature groups at the observation level.

conditional distribution further restricted to Xj ∈[Lj, Hj].

replacements takes O(NM(p+τf)) time, while the current batch requires O(AbatchMp) storage.
Materializing each batch enables vectorized tree evaluation and reduces per-call overhead. Using
Abatch ≪N reduces peak sampling storage from O(NMp) to O(AbatchMp). Subsequent predictive-
model evaluation uses replacement batches of size Cbatch, requiring an additional O(Cbatchp)
perturbed-input workspace; it does not reduce the replacements already generated by the sampler.
Feature-specific calculations can be distributed across independent parallel jobs, subject to available
computational resources. The storage bound above is per job; concurrent jobs increase aggregate
memory usage.
Note: Direct sampling does not require storing a conditional-density evaluation matrix. By
generating one replacement per observation at a time and immediately accumulating its loss
contribution, the sampling workspace can, in principle, be reduced to O(Abatchp) while still using M
replacements per observation. The current implementation instead materializes all M replacements
within each evaluation batch, using O(AbatchMp) sampling workspace to enable vectorized tree
evaluation and reduce per-call overhead. This creates a trade-off between memory usage and
computational efficiency when processing very large datasets.

E.7
DGP 4: Interacted Discontinuity.
What happens to local VI when the covariate dependence involves interactions? This
case examines dependence driven by an XOR-type interaction between X2 and X3 in the conditional
distribution of X1. Such interactions can be difficult for recursive tree methods based on univariate
split selection, because their joint signal is not apparent from individual univariate splits (Hornung
and Boulesteix, 2022). We use this setting to assess how this difficulty affects local importance
estimation. As shown in the results for DGP 4 in Table 2, DR-BART (which explores ensembles of tree
structures through Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulations (Orlandi et al., 2021)) yields estimates
closest to the analytical LUCID. The cs-PFI estimates closely resemble marginal permutation,
consistent with its transformation-tree partition failing to recover the pure interaction.
ARF
substantially improves on marginal permutation, but its MSE is still approximately 3× higher than
our DR-BART implementation. These results highlight the importance of recovering interaction
structure in the conditional distribution when estimating LUCID. Additional results are provided in
Figures 12 and 17.
E.8
DGP 5: Extreme Tail.
How does LUCID differ from local LOCO? LOCO provides a natural comparison for LUCID
because both assess a feature’s contribution given the information available in the remaining features.
Although global LOCO and global conditional permutation importance differ only by a factor of two
under squared loss and an oracle regression predictor (Reyero-Lobo et al., 2025), this proportionality
44


does not generally hold locally, as established in Section 3.3. Compared to local LOCO, LUCID
additionally accounts for prediction variability under conditional feature replacement, reflecting how
important it is to know the exact feature value; omitting this component can substantially alter
local feature rankings. We construct a heteroskedastic DGP with an extreme-tail component in the
prediction function to examine the consequences of this distinction. Analytical local LOCO and
analytical LUCID yield different feature rankings with an analytical probability of 67.4% (Analytical
local LOCO and LUCID assign different feature rankings to 68.6% of the evaluated observations.).
Consistent with this theoretical discrepancy, estimated local LOCO disagrees with estimated LUCID
on 69.6% of the evaluated instances, as shown in Figure 8. The disagreement between the analytical
rankings demonstrates that this discrepancy is intrinsic to the two importance measures and cannot
be eliminated by improving LOCO estimation alone. This example shows that LUCID provides
fuller local feature information about dispersion, leading to substantially different feature rankings
from local LOCO despite their proportionality at the global level. Additional results are provided
in Figures 13 and 18.

DGP 4:
Interacted discontinuity.
Figure 12 presents the most structurally challenging
conditional mean in our five DGPs. With X3 = 1, the conditional distribution of X1 undergoes
an abrupt location shift as X2 crosses zero while maintaining approximately constant conditional
variance. DR-BART accurately captures both sides of this discontinuity and transitions sharply at
the relevant boundary. ARF, CMR, and GCMR also capture much of the location shift, whereas
cs-PFI and marginal permutation fail to reproduce the correct regime-specific conditional location
in this configuration. This example highlights the importance of representing interactions in the
conditioning variables rather than only marginal or coarse subgroup structure.
DGP 5: Extreme tail.
Figure 13 shows the conditional distributions in the extreme-tail setting.
Although the conditional mean remains zero, the conditional variance changes across the sign of
X2, producing a moderate but important change in distributional spread. DR-BART again closely
follows the analytical distribution across all five conditioning points and preserves the increase
in conditional dispersion for positive X2. Several alternative methods introduce noticeable local
shifts in the conditional mean or fail to reproduce the change in scale, while marginal permutation
necessarily uses essentially the same unconditional replacement distribution throughout.
Taken together, these results show that the advantage of DR-BART is not tied to a single
type of conditional dependence. It remains stable across independence, smooth location shifts,
heteroskedasticity, discontinuous interactions, and sign-dependent changes in scale. This flexibility is
particularly relevant for LUCID, since errors in the local replacement distribution directly propagate
to errors in observation-level importance.

is required. Methods that estimate a conditional distribution can therefore incur additional finite-
sample estimation error without gaining information, whereas marginal or near-marginal perturbation
schemes can perform particularly well. This explains why the simplest methods obtain very small

substantially harder for DR-BART in this setting, the improvement with increasing sample size
indicates that it is able to recover the underlying conditional structure with sufficient data.
Another notable result is the poor performance of ARF in these higher-dimensional settings,
where it is consistently among the worst-performing methods and often deteriorates markedly as the
ambient dimension increases. This provides further empirical support for our motivation to estimate
the conditional density directly: approaches such as ARF recover the conditional distribution
through an estimated joint distribution of all covariates, a task that becomes increasingly challenging
as the feature dimension grows (Izbicki and Lee, 2016).
Overall, these experiments emphasize that no conditional sampler uniformly dominates across all
dependence structures. Subgroup permutation is particularly effective when the conditional distri-
bution admits a simple tree partition, whereas direct conditional density estimation is substantially
more competitive when dependence is smooth or distributed across many covariates. Importantly,
LUCID itself is agnostic to this choice of conditional density estimator; these results therefore
characterize the statistical trade-offs of the sampling mechanism rather than a restriction of the
LUCID framework.

High predictability indicates that conditional resampling permits less variation on average relative
to marginal resampling because information in this feature is already captured by the remaining

determine the feature of interest, then conditional replacement introduces no variation and LUCID
is zero. Conversely, low predictability alone does not imply large LUCID, because the effect of
replacement also depends on the model’s response: if the model does not use the feature of interest,
its LUCID remains zero. Moreover, R2

Runtime and Memory Scaling on OpenXAI Datasets
To further assess the computational feasibility of our DR-BART-based LUCID estimator beyond
the five-feature Bike Sharing example, we benchmark runtime and peak memory usage on four
tabular datasets from the OpenXAI (Agarwal et al., 2022) benchmark. These datasets provide
complementary stress tests in both sample size and feature dimension: German contains the largest
number of features with p = 60, while GMSC contains more than 105 observations. Table 12
summarizes the datasets used in this experiment.

Nevertheless, the complete LUCID pipeline remains computationally feasible across all four bench-
mark datasets. In particular, we successfully run DR-BART on GMSC with 102,209 observations.
The average feature-specific task requires approximately 9,015 seconds and the largest feature-specific
job uses approximately 41.9 GiB of memory. If all ten feature-specific jobs are executed serially, one
fold requires approximately 1,503 minutes, or about 25 hours of aggregate computation.
Remark 1. Local explanations are often required at prediction time. While training DR-BART
itself requires expensive Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations, the DR-BART model is
fixed at prediction time and runtime for explanations depends only on time to sample posterior
draws, counterfactuals for each posterior, and fitting/comparing the fixed predictive models. In
contrast, other local explanation methods like LIME can require refitting new surrogate models for
each new test observation, increasing explanation-inference time.
