## ABSTRACT 

EEG analysis and decoding underpin brain-computer interfaces, cognitive assess-ment, and sleep monitoring. Fixed pipelines and reward-driven search do not explicitly determine how signal structure, representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics contribute to performance or generalization gaps, leaving inter-vention choice underdetermined. We introduce NeuroAct, an autonomous research agent framework for EEG analysis and decoding. NeuroAct implements dual-loop autonomous research across EEG foundation models, task-specific predictors, and model combinations. In an inner loop, research, development, and review roles use evidence on signal structure, representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics to test hypotheses, select analyses or interventions, and refine candidates using performance, stability, and cost feedback. An outer loop distills outcomes into conditional, testable recommendations with task and model conditions, then validates and revises them on subsequent tasks. We evaluate four settings: (A) EEG General Analysis, (B) Cross-task Model Optimization, (C) Single-task Open Research, and (D) Collaborative Prediction. In Setting A, NeuroAct’s 80.88 macro-average exceeds BrainAgent’s 63.13 by 17.75 points. In Setting B, NeuroAct raises EEGPT’s mean balanced accuracy (BA) by 7.34 points and HBN Pearson’s r from 0.6894 to 0.7362; in Setting C, it improves ADFTD BA by 16.33 points; and in Setting D, it raises Workload BA from 79.69% to 85.02%. Experience reuse raises BCIC-IV-2a BA from 34.66% to 50.81% while reducing LLM tokens by 64.02%, indicating recommendations improve cross-task performance. NeuroAct enables evidence-guided EEG research improved by cross-task experience. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

EEG analysis and decoding support cognitive-state assessment, sleep monitoring, and brain-computer interfaces. Pretrained EEG foundation models (EEG-FMs) provide reusable representations across diverse tasks (Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a; 2025a; El Ouahidi et al., 2025; D¨ oner et al., 2026). Effective task adaptation requires assessing how signal structure, representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics contribute to performance or generalization gaps, then selecting evidence that distinguishes competing explanations. Fixed pipelines and reward-driven optimization do not make this diagnostic decision explicit, motivating an autonomous research agent that improves within-task EEG decoding and refines research strategies across tasks. 

Task-specific EEG analysis must connect signal structure to how a model represents, predicts, and learns. Benchmark studies show model rankings and transfer depend on task and readout design, encoded features can differ from those used for prediction, and token preservation affects frozen-encoder decoding (Tang et al., 2026; ˇ Sirca et al., 2026). When refinement of a single EEG model yields diminishing gains, this diagnosis can test whether other models correct its errors on samples. Error overlap determines whether complementary prediction merits a model-combination experiment. Autonomous research systems combine hypothesis generation, experimentation, and interpretation in continuous workflows (Lu et al., 2026). Model-development systems further use experimental feedback or accumulated experience to revise their approaches (alphaXiv, 2026). For EEG research, open questions concern how diagnostics guide interventions across foundation-model adaptation, task-specific modeling, joint training, and model combination, and how cross-task validation and revision make conditional recommendations useful on subsequent tasks. We introduce NeuroAct , an autonomous research agent framework for EEG analysis and decoding. NeuroAct implements a dual-loop autonomous research framework: an inner loop selects analyses and interventions from evidence on signal structure, representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics, while an outer loop distills, validates, and revises conditional recommendations or experi-ences across research tasks. Within the inner loop, the Coordinator directs the Researcher, Developer, and Reviewer to test competing diagnostic hypotheses through executable analyses or model changes, with predictive performance, stability, and cost informing the next action for each task. Each experi-ence item is a conditional recommendation that records applicable EEG task and model conditions, a proposed action, supporting and counterevidence, and reassessment conditions. Subsequent tasks test its utility, while counterevidence narrows, suspends, or replaces the recommendation. Tab. 1 positions this scope relative to existing agent frameworks. We organize NeuroAct’s evaluation around four research settings : (A) EEG General Analysis ,

(B) Cross-task Model Optimization , (C) Single-task Open Research , and (D) Collaborative Pre-diction . The evaluation covers six datasets (HBN (Alexander et al., 2017; Shirazi et al., 2024), Workload (Zyma et al., 2019), PhysioMI (Schalk et al., 2004), BCIC-IV-2a (Tangermann et al., 2012), ADFTD (Miltiadous et al., 2023), and HMC (Alvarez-Estevez & Rijsman, 2021)), three LLM back-end families (DeepSeek-V4 Flash (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026), GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI, 2026), and Qwen3.8 Flash Next (Qwen Team, 2026)), and the available external baselines CodeAct (Wang et al., 2024c), BrainPilot (Li et al., 2026a), and BrainAgent (Zhou et al., 2026b). With DeepSeek-V4 Flash, NeuroAct reaches an 80.88 macro-average score in Setting A and exceeds 75 with Luna and Qwen on BrainBench (Zhou et al., 2026a). In Setting B, NeuroAct raises mean balanced accuracy (BA) from 47.50 to 54.84 and HBN Pearson’s r from 0.6894 to 0.7362. NeuroAct gains 16.33 BA points on ADFTD in Setting C and raises Workload BA from the best single model’s 79.69% to 85.02% in Setting D. Experience reuse transfers a PhysioMI-derived recommendation to BCIC-IV-2a, raising test BA from 34.66% to 50.81% while reducing LLM tokens by 64.02%. Our contributions are: • Evidence-guided inner-loop EEG research. NeuroAct tests alternatives and selects analyses or interventions using signal, representation, predictor, and training evidence. • Outer-loop conditional strategy improvement. NeuroAct tests conditional recommendations across tasks and revises them using matched performance and cost comparisons when warranted. • Executable EEG analysis and decoding. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 EEG D ECODING MODELS 

Task-specific architectures including EEGNet and EEG Conformer encode local and temporal EEG structure (Lawhern et al., 2018; Song et al., 2023). Pretrained models including BENDR and EEGPT learn reusable representations, while other EEG-FMs differ in treating channels, signal structure, and recording heterogeneity (Kostas et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a; 2025a; El Ouahidi et al., 2025; D¨ oner et al., 2026). These approaches expand candidate space from specialized decoders to transferable EEG representations. Benchmark studies show model rankings, transfer, and generalization vary with task, dataset, and readout design (Banville et al., 2026; Xiong et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). Adaptation studies identify cohort shift, data availability, channel adaptation, and training regime as consequential (Nguyen et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Kokate et al., 2026; Kommineni et al., 2026). NeuroAct turns evidence on signal structure, representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics, including cross-model error overlap, into diagnostic decisions selecting analyses or interventions across adaptation, shared-model training, and model combination rather than a fixed recipe. 2.2 AUTONOMOUS RESEARCH AGENTS 

Autonomous research systems coordinate hypothesis generation, experimentation, and interpreta-tion in sustained workflows (Swanson et al., 2025; Gottweis et al., 2026; Ghareeb et al., 2026). Computational agents extend this workflow through executable tool use, candidate search, targeted refinement, repository-grounded development, diagnostic code updates, and training feedback, using execution outcomes to focus candidates (Jiang et al., 2025; Yamada et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026d; Zhang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). Long-horizon systems support asynchronous experiments and interactive debugging (Hambardzumyan et al., 2026) and allocate effort across competing plans using feedback and remaining resources (Yu et al., 2026a). Within EEG and brain-signal research, EEGAgent and BrainAgent orchestrate signal-processing tools and domain agents, while CogEEGAgent emphasizes deterministic cognitive-analysis execution and independent confirmation (Zhao et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026b; Hou et al., 2026). BrainPilot combines knowledge, skills, and specialists for architecture development under fixed preprocessing and training, whereas NeuroWeaver searches domain-constrained pipelines through tree selection, code refinement, and multi-objective rewards (Li et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026). NeuroAct extends the workflow with an EEG evidence loop that diagnoses gaps using signal, representation, predictor, and training evidence before selecting executable analyses or interventions. 2.3 EXPERIENCE REUSE 

Experience-transfer methods reuse outcomes across attempts or tasks. Reflexion and ExpeL reuse verbal feedback or contrasting trajectories, while ReasoningBank and EvoScientist distill and refine research strategies from outcomes (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Ouyang et al., 2026; Lyu et al., 2026). Voyager and Recuris reuse executable skills or revise the memory governing their use, while SkillsBench and controlled studies examine when prescribed skills, tools, or guidance granularity hinder stronger strategies (Wang et al., 2024b; Yu et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026c; Xu & Wu, 2026). NeuroAct treats experience as a conditional, testable recommendation under explicit EEG task and model conditions, validating its use on new tasks through outcomes and cost before retaining, revising, or narrowing applicability.

Specialized collaboration. NeuroAct separates persona contexts and permissions so that scientific assessment, implementation, and review remain distinct. The Coordinator decomposes the objective into parallel questions and assigns each to a separate research cycle. The Researcher investigates an assigned question using literature, dataset semantics, and EEG evidence, then develops a feasible proposal. The Developer implements the selected proposal and executes the required analyses or training. The Reviewer examines the solution, implementation, and evidence, challenges unsup-ported conclusions, and returns the reviewed outcome to the Coordinator. Each handoff records the requested deliverable, supporting evidence, unresolved issues, and an actionable recommendation; the Coordinator integrates these handoffs, resolves disagreements, and selects subsequent actions.

Progressive disclosure. Role contexts initially receive compact capability indexes and expand selected details on demand. Tools expose domains before operations and input signatures; runtime skills expose indexes before instructions; matched experience exposes summaries before supporting evidence. Only selected capabilities and adopted advice enter specialist assignments, concentrating context on the current question while preserving access to literature, operational details, and evidence. App. B details the tools, skills, and disclosure levels. 

Investigation becomes an experiment. The Researcher proposes an analysis whose possible outcomes distinguish competing explanations and imply different actions. Literature and evidence on signal structure, representations, predictor behavior, and training dynamics identify the uncertainty, a feasible intervention, and its expected effect. When computation is required, the Researcher specifies it for the Coordinator to assign to the Developer. The Developer executes it or implements scoped changes to input processing, pretrained-model adaptation, predictors, or training. Analytical tasks return computed results with evidence-supported interpretations. 

Feedback redirects research. The Coordinator compares observed results and resource consumption with expected effects before selecting the next investigation. Reviewer challenges to an imple-mentation or explanation can trigger further investigation. Auxiliary metrics and training behavior distinguish improved discrimination from decision-rule changes or unstable fitting. This evidence updates the solution, research state, and next decision. 3.2 OUTER LOOP : E XPERIENCE -B ASED SELF -I MPROVEMENT

EEG General Analysis (A). Given a question and EEG recordings, NeuroAct selects computations and sources to produce an evidence-supported answer. This setting exercises analytical reasoning and the investigative capabilities used in subsequent decoding research. 

Cross-task Model Optimization (B). Each program adapts one pretrained encoder across heteroge-neous datasets using task-specific adapters and output modules. NeuroAct can revise task sampling and loss contributions, then inspect the effects of shared updates on each target. Joint classification and regression create coupled objectives requiring balancing predictive utility across tasks. 

Single-task Open Research (C). Given a dataset and prediction objective, NeuroAct can refine a strong candidate or explore another model family, including pretrained and task-specific predictors. This setting exercises open development guided by EEG evidence.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Experimental setup. We evaluate how effectively NeuroAct links EEG analysis to predictive research and improves later studies through experience. Four settings test the breadth of these capabilities; mechanism ablations and experience studies measure effects on prediction and research cost. BrainBench (Setting A) evaluates instruction-driven EEG analysis across foundational, sleep, neurocognitive, and physiological domains. Settings B, C, and D cover Workload, BCIC-IV-2a, ADFTD, HMC, and HBN; PhysioMI supports experience and supplementary studies. DeepSeek-V4 Flash is default LLM; GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3.8 Flash Next assess provider consistency, with CodeAct, BrainPilot, and BrainAgent as baseline agents. For Settings B–D, we adapt the baselines’ task and execution interfaces. Tab. 1 describes originally reported capabilities; Tab. 3(B-D) evaluates the adapted implementations. We include BENDR, BIOT, LaBraM, EEGPT, CBraMod, CSBrain, and REVE in model pools for Setting D, with ShallowFBCSPNet added for ADFTD. Classification uses balanced accuracy and AUROC (BA, and AUC in tables); HBN regression uses Pearson r and prediction errors. Protected test labels remain unavailable during dual-loop research. The research budget for each study is capped at 50 million LLM tokens or 5 GPU hours. Research costs include all attempted experiments. Apps. A and E detail tasks, execution, and protocols; App. D reports member pools and complete metrics.

## 5 CONCLUSION 

NeuroAct advances autonomous EEG analysis and decoding by making domain evidence part of experimental decision-making rather than treating adaptation as undirected search. Its inner loop turns investigation into executable interventions, while the outer loop carries useful experience into later research and revises it with new evidence. The four settings demonstrate EEG General Analysis, Cross-task Model Optimization, Single-task Open Research, and Collaborative Prediction. Fixed-model ablations support the contribution of specialized research capabilities, and experience studies across datasets and model families show both predictive gains and reduced research cost. These results establish a path toward EEG research agents that improve current solutions, accumulate validated recommendations across tasks, and conduct subsequent studies more effectively. 9Preprint

Predictor inputs use 200 Hz, except EEGPT and LUNA at 256 Hz. EEGPT converts microvolt ( μV) signals to millivolts (mV) by multiplying by 0.001; CBraMod, LaBraM, and CSBrain multiply μVvalues by 0.01. The ADFTD ShallowFBCSPNet uses μV signals directly. BENDR maps each window to [−1, 1] using its minimum and maximum over all channels and time points. BIOT divides each channel by the 95th percentile of its absolute values within the window. EEGNet and LUNA apply channel-wise window z-scores. REVE also uses channel-wise window z-scores and clips values to [−15 , 15] . Its HBN data undergo channel-wise standardization over complete recordings before windowing (Banville et al., 2026); candidate settings either preserve these values or apply an additional window-level z-score. B.1 RUNTIME , ROLES , AND EXECUTION 

NeuroAct implements the dual-loop framework using the OpenHands Software Agent SDK (Wang et al., 2025b) and tools version 1.42.1. Its Agent and Conversation abstractions handle LLM interaction and tool execution. NeuroAct builds the research harness on this foundation, coordinating specialists and providing EEG analysis, literature tools, and executable model experiments. It manages evaluation and research evidence, with progressive disclosure of tools and runtime skills and support for experience reuse and revision. All four personas use one runtime definition, configured with shared base instructions and role-specific prompts and permissions. Shell access and workspace editing are assigned to the Developer. Researcher and Reviewer use read-only tools; the Reviewer can inspect the Developer’s code changes together with run and evaluation records. Tool access follows the role bound to each conversation, keeping these permissions consistent as assignments change. To make concurrent investigations complementary, the Coordinator specifies distinct questions and investigative paths before marking independent Researcher and Reviewer assignments for parallel execution. Each receives its own conversation and resource allowance, including concurrent assignments to the same role. Sequential assignments can reuse a compatible conversation to preserve useful context. The coordination host runs authorized assignments within the concurrency limit and forwards specialist handoffs to the Coordinator. This provides separate reasoning contexts for competing approaches while retaining a common point for their scientific assessment. NeuroAct initializes a workspace at a user-specified location and uses bubblewrap to isolate the Developer’s terminal, mounting research code as writable and reference resources as read-only. A submission specifies the implementation and its task and input configuration. Before training, preflight checks verify input shapes, channel and unit requirements, and executable interfaces. Submitted jobs run under the experiment service, which manages progress and cancellation or resumption independently of LLM turns. The experiment host returns execution feedback to the Developer’s conversation. The Developer inspects the results and reports its findings in a handoff to the Coordinator. Run summaries expose training behavior and evaluation aggregates alongside resource use, supplying the concrete evidence needed for subsequent review and research decisions. Formation from research records. To form an experience, the knowledge service assembles the decision rationale and expected effect with linked analyses and experiment outcomes. The Coordinator examines the implemented intervention and the alternatives that informed it, then specifies when to recommend the action and what would warrant reassessment. Each entry links supporting and conflicting evidence to its source records. The shared catalog indexes these entries by source task and model characteristics for retrieval across workspaces. 

Retrieval and synthesis. Retrieval first assesses compatibility between the source and target pre-diction tasks and model conditions, then ranks recommendations by a deterministic relevance score. Exact model agreement contributes 5 points and EEG-paradigm agreement 5; matching prediction type and dataset each contribute 3. Prediction and generalization units and input characteristics refine the score, while goal and analysis-term overlap adds up to 4 points. Results expose matched conditions and potential incompatibilities for the Coordinator’s assessment. Repeated descriptions 17 Preprint of the same source evidence are deduplicated, then entries are grouped by source task and model conditions and action terms. 

Adoption and task context. The Coordinator reviews the synthesis and relevant source details before assigning adopted recommendations to target tasks with a context priority. Provisional recommendations receive lower priority and a proposed low-cost applicability check. The runtime exposes adopted experience to the Coordinator and specialist contexts for the applicable tasks, grouped by action theme and ordered by priority. A default 6,000-character budget bounds this context summary, which retains actions and their conditions with references for on-demand access to complete entries. Long entries are abbreviated across their fields to retain the essential actions. When an experience changes a research decision, the Coordinator links the decision to the recommendation and revision used, connecting the subsequent outcome to that advice. 

Revision from subsequent evidence. Target results can lead the Coordinator to amend an adopted recommendation’s action or applicability, update its priority, or suspend its use, with supporting observations and counterevidence attached to the revision. These task-specific updates retain the original source recommendation. To improve the shared recommendation itself, the Coordinator can reassess its original evidence or incorporate later outcomes, recording a revised recommendation with the reason for the change and links to preceding evidence. Context refreshes propagate active revisions to subsequent decisions and exclude withdrawn or superseded recommendations. B.5 HARDWARE AND SOFTWARE ENVIRONMENT

E.1 EXTERNAL FRAMEWORK ADAPTATIONS 

CodeAct uses the implementation released with BrainBench and its Python execution and feedback loop (Wang et al., 2024c; Zhou et al., 2026a). We use BrainPilot’s open-source implementation, in which the Principal Investigator coordinates specialist agents using the framework’s tools and research skills (Li et al., 2026a). BrainAgent uses a paper-method reimplementation of Supervisor-led task decomposition and domain-agent execution (Zhou et al., 2026b), extended with general Python execution for training and prediction programs. The adapters provide access to task data and model assets, together with interfaces for executing programs and evaluating predictions. Execution logs and metric feedback return to each framework’s own research loop, allowing it to revise experiments and select a final predictor. Setting B requires a shared encoder with task-specific predictors, trained from the supplied LaBraM model and delivered as one checkpoint covering all five targets. BrainAgent uses a custom encoder partly initialized from LaBraM, with per-task sample limits to avoid execution errors during shared-model training. Setting C permits new scientific computing code for preprocessing and model training. Setting D provides member predictions and development labels for learning sample-level prediction selection. The ADFTD CBraMod study tests whether the predictor benefits from access to individual patch representations.
