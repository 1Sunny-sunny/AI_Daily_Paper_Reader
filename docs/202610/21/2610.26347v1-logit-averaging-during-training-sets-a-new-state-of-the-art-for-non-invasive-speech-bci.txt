# Abstract 

Non-invasive brain-computer interfaces are in practice limited by signal-to-noise ratio, and recent state-of-the-art methods address this limitation by aggregating over repeated trials of the same stimulus. Yet multi-trial aggregation has been treated inconsistently across the literature: some work averages brain signals at the model’s input, other work averages predictions at the model’s output, and neither treats aggregation as an architectural choice in its own right. We provide the first systematic comparison of aggregation strategies for non-invasive neural decoding, evaluating six paradigms (including logit averaging during training and three attention-based variants we introduce) across three datasets, four tasks, and two group sizes. We find that aggregation choice matters substantially: training-time logit averaging, the simplest method we propose, outperforms all others in 6 of 7 settings. On the phoneme classification setting of the 2025 PNPL competition, it sets a new state of the art, significantly outperforming the competition winner by 17.4 absolute percentage points in balanced accuracy. We further draw a theoretical connection between logit averaging and joint ensemble training, which has been shown to underperform independent ensembling in the deep learning literature; our results call into question recent explanations of why joint ensemble training fails. Together, these findings establish multi-trial aggregation as a key design choice for non-invasive neural decoding and identify training-time logit averaging as a simple, effective default. 

# 1 Introduction 

Driven by advances in deep learning, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have made substantial progress in recent years. Speech BCIs in particular have begun to restore communication in people with anarthria, decoding attempted speech Moses et al. [2021], Makin et al. [2020], Metzger et al. [2023], Willett et al. [2023], Card et al. [2024], imagined or inner speech Kunz et al. [2025], and attempted handwriting Willett et al. [2021] from cortical activity. The most recent intracranial systems have reached word error rates below 5%, while approaching the speed of natural conversation Card et al. [2024]. These achievements have, however, relied on invasive recordings that require neurosurgical implan-tation. Both the surgical procedure itself and its associated long-term risks restrict the deployment of these systems. Motivated by this constraint, a growing body of work has investigated whether comparable performance can be achieved from non-invasive measurements Jayalath et al. [2024, 2025], d’Ascoli et al. [2024], Défossez et al. [2023], Landau et al. [2025], Özdogan et al. [2025].

# 2 Related work 

2.1 Non-invasive speech decoding 

Recent years have seen rapid progress in decoding speech from non-invasive brain recordings. Özdogan et al. [2025] released the LibriBrain dataset, comprising over 50 hours of MEG from a single participant listening to naturalistic English speech, approximately five times larger than the next-largest within-subject MEG corpus by Armeni et al. [2022]. While many prior MEG speech-decoding datasets focus on multi-subject recordings of shorter duration per participant Gwilliams et al. [2022], LibriBrain enables single-subject decoding at scale. This dataset underpins the 2025 PNPL competition Landau et al. [2025], whose phoneme classification track evaluates submissions on samples formed by averaging 100 trials of the same phoneme. d’Ascoli et al. [2024] introduced a deep learning pipeline for decoding individual words from MEG and EEG, evaluated across 723 participants. Their analysis identified MEG as easier to decode than EEG, and observed that decoding performance scales with the amount of data per participant and with the number of trials averaged at test time. In contrast to the input averaging used in the LibriBrain phoneme task, they apply post-hoc averaging at evaluation time, averaging the predicted probability distributions across trials of the same word. Building on these resources, Jayalath et al. [2024] scaled self-supervised pre-training and reported improvements over prior models on speech-decoding benchmarks. Most recently, Jayalath et al. [2025] extended this line of work to full sentence-level brain-to-text by combining word-classification models with LLM-based rescoring. Even with these methods, the best reported word error rate remains at 88%, far from the levels required for practical deployment. Similar gaps exist for other non-invasive modalities, including fMRI Tang et al. [2023] and EEG Yang et al. [2024]. Together, these results underscore that single-trial decoding from non-invasive recordings remains bottlenecked by signal-to-noise ratio and that multi-trial aggregation is essential for achieving usable performance. 

2.2 Multi-sample prediction 

A multi-trial predictor takes as input a set of g trials {x1, . . . , x g } elicited by the same stimulus and produces a single prediction ˆy over the label space. Since the trials are interchangeable, we want our predictor f to be permutation-invariant : for any permutation π of the indices {1, . . . , g },

f ({xπ(1) , . . . , x π(g)}) = f ({x1, . . . , x g }). This requirement has been studied extensively in the broader machine learning literature on set-structured inputs Dietterich et al. [1997], Maron and Lozano-Pérez [1997]. Lee et al. [2018] introduced the Set Transformer, which aggregates set-structured data via attention while remaining permutation-invariant. Their work inspired the attention-based methods we evaluate in Section 3.3, including a learned query attention mechanism in which a small fixed number of trainable query vectors attend over the set elements. 

2.3 Ensembling 

Multi-trial prediction is conceptually related to ensembling, a long-established technique in deep learning for reducing the variance of model predictions Hinton et al. [2015], Dietterich [2000], Gal and Ghahramani [2016], Krogh and Vedelsby [1994], Heskes [1997]. The most common approach is the deep ensemble Lakshminarayanan et al. [2017], in which multiple copies of the same architecture are trained on identical data from different random initialisations, and their predictions averaged at test time. The theoretical motivation is to reduce the variance contributed to predictions by initialisation, since each ensemble member converges to a different minimum of the loss landscape. 3Our setting is related but distinct. We train a single neural network rather than an ensemble: the same weights process every trial. However, we may think of each trial as the true underlying signal corrupted by per-trial noise sources, including unrelated brain processes and measurement noise. Under this framing, passing g trials through a single shared model under the post-hoc averaging approach (position 4 in Figure 1) is structurally analogous to passing the true signal through an ensemble of g base learners each consisting of a noising operation followed by the shared backbone. The variance being reduced under averaging is therefore variance in the input rather than variance from initialisation, but the basic statistical principle is the same. A structural analogue to training-time logit averaging also exists in the ensembling literature. Jeffares et al. [2023] examined whether ensembles can be trained jointly under a single loss applied to averaged predictions or averaged logits, rather than independently and aggregated at test time, and report that such joint training consistently underperforms independent ensembling. We discuss their theoretical considerations, and how they relate to our results, in Section 5.3. 

# 3 Methods 

3.1 Problem setup 

We consider the problem of predicting a discrete label y ∈ { 1, . . . , K } (e.g. a phoneme or a word) from a group of g corresponding neural recordings. Each recording, or trial , is a multivariate time series x ∈ RC×T with C sensor channels and T time samples. A multi-trial predictor takes as input a group G = {x1, . . . , x g } and outputs a single prediction ˆy ∈ { 1, . . . , K }.All paradigms we consider share a common backbone fθ with parameters θ, which can map a trial to per-trial logits ℓi = fθ (xi) ∈ RK . Per-trial probabilities are obtained as pi = softmax( ℓi).The six paradigms we compare in this paper differ in where along the path from G to ˆy aggregation across trials occurs (1). All paradigms are trained with cross-entropy loss, weighted by the inverse square root of class frequency to handle class imbalance. We evaluate all paradigms using balanced accuracy (the macro-average of per-class recall), reported on held-out test data.

Learned weighted averaging computes per-trial scalar weights from each trial’s features in isolation and combines the per-trial feature maps as a weighted average. Cross-trial attention extends this with multi-head self-attention across trials, so that each trial’s weight depends on all others. Learned query attention uses trainable query vectors that attend over the per-trial features, with a residual connection to the elementwise mean. All three preserve permutation invariance by construction. Architectural details are given in Appendix D. 

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Datasets and tasks 

We evaluate the six aggregation paradigms on seven (dataset, task, group size) conditions spanning three datasets. A summary of all conditions is given in Appendix B. 

LibriBrain. LibriBrain Özdogan et al. [2025] is a single-subject MEG dataset comprising over 50 hours of recordings while one participant listened to naturalistic audiobook recordings of Sherlock Holmes stories. Each recording is downsampled to 250 Hz across 306 sensor channels. We evaluate three classification tasks derived from these recordings: Phoneme Classification (predicting which of the 39 ARPAbet phonemes corresponds to a 0.5 s window aligned to phoneme onset), Phoneme Voicing (a binary task collapsing the 39 phoneme classes into voiced versus unvoiced, on the same windows), and Word Classification (predicting which of the ten most frequent words in the corpus corresponds to a 1.0 s window aligned to word onset). The phoneme task is the setting of the 2025 PNPL competition, which evaluates submissions on samples formed by averaging 100 trials of the same phoneme. 

Armeni et al. [2022]. The Armeni et al. dataset comprises approximately 10 hours of MEG recordings per participant from three participants who listened to an English audiobook of Sherlock Holmes stories. Recordings use 269 sensor channels also downsampled to 250 Hz. We evaluate two classification tasks derived from these recordings, matched to the LibriBrain conditions: Phoneme Classification and Phoneme Voicing .

MegNIST. MegNIST is a 10-class digit classification benchmark recorded with 306-channel MEG from a single participant who imagined saying a digit upon visual cue. Unlike the LibriBrain tasks, MegNIST involves imagined rather than heard speech, providing a complementary evaluation setting. We use precomputed splits of 10,000 training, 1,000 validation, and 1,000 test samples. 4.2 Experimental setup Training protocol. All paradigms are trained with the Adam optimiser without weight decay, using a class-weighted cross-entropy loss as described in Section 3. Training runs for up to 200 epochs with early stopping on validation balanced accuracy and a patience of 10 epochs. We use the convolutional neural network architecture introduced by Özdogan et al. [2025]. For each combination of dataset, paradigm, and group size, we tune the learning rate by validation balanced accuracy. Details of the tuning procedure are given in Appendix C. 

Final evaluation. For each (dataset, paradigm, group size) configuration we run the chosen learning rate with five random seeds. Statistical comparisons against the two prior-work baselines (input averaging and post-hoc averaging) are performed via two-sided paired t-tests on test balanced accuracy, paired by seed. For the phoneme classification setting at g = 100 , we additionally compare training-time logit averaging against the conformer architecture that won the phoneme track of the 2025 PNPL competition Landau et al. [2025]. Both methods are trained on the same data with matched random seeds and compared via a two-sided paired t-test on test balanced accuracy.

# 5 Discussion 

5.1 Why does training-time logit averaging work? 

The improvement of 17.4 absolute percentage points over the 2025 PNPL competition winner does not arise from architectural innovation but from a change to the training objective: averaging logits across grouped trials before applying cross-entropy, rather than applying cross-entropy per trial. In the following, we discuss the mechanism by which this modified objective leads to improved generalisation. Under standard per-trial cross-entropy, the loss for any individual trial saturates quickly as log( ptrue )

approaches 0. At ptrue = 0 .6, the gradient magnitude is half of its value at ptrue = 0 .3, and only one-sixth of its value at ptrue = 0 .1.Under the logit-averaged cross-entropy objective, by contrast, a single trial continues to receive gradient signal until the averaged logits assign high probability to the true class. Even if a single trial is already correctly predicted with high confidence, it continues to receive gradient if other trials in its group are not. This is reflected in the per-trial logit distributions of trained models (Table 3): under logit averaging, per-trial logits have substantially larger magnitudes, inducing sharper, lower-entropy probability distributions. The maximum softmax probability reaches 0.91 on MegNIST and 0.66 on phoneme classification, compared to 0.40 and 0.08 under post-hoc averaging. The margin between the maximum logit and the mean logit is 5–11 × larger for logit-averaged models across four tasks where we computed logit statistics. The logit-averaging objective differs from per-trial cross-entropy in that the model is incentivised not to classify each trial individually, but to produce per-trial logits whose group-averaged prediction is correct. This appears to induce the model to learn per-trial representations that complement each other during averaged prediction. Intuitively, we might expect such complementary behaviour to also yield well-calibrated single-trial predictions: if a model accurately calibrates its confidence on each trial, low-confidence predictions can be more easily overruled by high-confidence predictions that are more likely to be correct. However, our calibration analysis (Appendix G) shows that this is not the case. While group-level predictions of logit-averaged models tend to be better calibrated than those of models trained with standard cross-entropy, single-trial calibration is consistently worse.

Single-trial balanced accuracy under logit averaging is nonetheless higher than under standard per-trial training on the phoneme and phoneme voicing task, suggesting that some of the performance gains of logit averaging stem from an improvement in training dynamics rather than from group-level coordination alone. Conversely, logit averaging exhibits worse single-trial accuracy than per-trial training on the word classification task, even though its group-level accuracy is higher, implying that group-level coordination also plays a meaningful role. In summary, we conjecture that several mechanisms contribute to the success of logit averaging. The joint training objective appears to induce favourable learning dynamics that can improve even single-trial performance, while at the same time producing single-trial logits that tend to be better suited to group-level averaging. We believe these mechanisms warrant further investigation in future work. 

5.2 Why do attention-based methods not consistently outperform logit averaging? 

The four methods we introduced span a range of expressivity. Logit averaging is the simplest: it imposes no parametric coupling across trials. Two multi-head attention-based methods, cross-trial attention and learned query attention, introduce learned interactions between per-trial features, with the goal of producing aggregated representations that exploit structure across trials. Despite this additional capacity, neither consistently outperforms logit averaging (Table 1). Two interpretations are consistent with our results. First, the trials in our setting are by construction noisy realisations of the same stimulus, so the joint distribution of per-trial features is approximately exchangeable: every trial is informative in roughly the same way, and pairwise interactions between specific trials carry little information that uniform averaging would not capture. Modelling these interactions explicitly may therefore add capacity without adding signal, while introducing optimisa-tion difficulties. Second, the attention modules are trained from scratch on dataset sizes ranging from 

10 4 to 10 6 trials, modest by the standards of attention architectures Vaswani et al. [2017], particularly given that all parameters operate over groups of g ≤ 100 items. Larger datasets may eventually reveal cases in which expressive aggregation is beneficial. We also note that the attention mechanisms we evaluated were not explicitly designed to encode an inductive bias toward sharing particular features that we believe may be useful for interpreting other trials. Within the regimes we evaluate, the simpler bias of logit averaging is the more reliable choice. 

5.3 Relation to joint ensemble training 

Training-time logit averaging is structurally analogous to joint ensemble training discussed in Sec-tion 2.3, in which a single loss is computed on the averaged predictions of multiple ensemble members rather than on each member individually. Jeffares et al. [2023] report that this procedure consistently fails for deep ensembles, and explain the failure through the following decomposition, originally derived for regression by Krogh and Vedelsby [1994]: 

ERR joint = ERR ind − DIV . (4) The joint loss equals the mean loss of the individual members minus a diversity term that captures how much each member’s prediction deviates from the ensemble mean. However, from Equation (4) it is clear that any movement of base learner predictions in opposing directions that leaves the joint loss unchanged must increase the mean individual loss accordingly. The diversity term therefore does not offer a “free lunch” to a jointly trained model on the training set: any apparent inflation of diversity under fixed joint prediction is paid for by a corresponding degradation of individual member performance. The same decomposition admits a different reading. Equation (4) can equally be rearranged as 

ERR ind = ERR joint + DIV . Under this reading, it is the per-member objective that imposes the additional constraint, penalising diversity at fixed joint loss. Per-member training therefore systematically discourages individual members from making more extreme predictions, even in cases where their average would better match the target. Whether this constraint is desirable depends on the inductive bias of the model and training procedure. Our experiments are consistent with this framing. Models trained with logit averaging produce per-trial predictions that are more extreme than would be optimal under per-trial training, mirroring the prediction-spreading behaviour reported by Jeffares et al. [2023] for joint ensembles. Yet logit averaging succeeds in our setting. Our results discussed in Section 5 show that the diversity expressed at training time generalises to held-out data. The success of logit averaging in our setting demonstrates that diversity learned under a joint objective can generalise. We suggest instead that the failure of joint ensemble training observed by Jeffares et al. [2023] is more plausibly a consequence of the specific inductive biases of deep ensembles or of the loss landscape of the problem settings studied rather than an inherent issue with the joint training objective. Understanding why joint training succeeds in our multi-trial setting but fails in the ensemble setting is a useful direction for future work. 

5.4 Limitations 

The experimental findings reported in this paper come with several limitations. First, our experiments are restricted to MEG recordings; we have not evaluated whether our findings transfer to EEG. Second, MegNIST is not yet publicly available, which limits the reproducibility of one of our seven experimental settings. We will release MegNIST in future work. Third, our analysis is limited to classifiers trained with cross-entropy loss. Whether our findings generalise to other loss functions, including contrastive losses such as SigLIP Zhai et al. [2023], is an open question for future work. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We have shown that aggregation choice matters substantially in non-invasive neural decoding, and that a simple change to the training objective, training-time logit averaging, is the most effective aggregation strategy across the settings we evaluate. On the phoneme classification task of the 2025 PNPL competition, it sets a new state of the art, outperforming the competition winner by 17.4 absolute percentage points. We further connect logit averaging to recent theoretical analyses of joint ensemble training, which has been shown to fail in the deep learning literature, and argue that our results call into question recent explanations of why this failure occurs. Understanding why joint training succeeds in our multi-trial setting but fails for ensembles remains a useful direction for future work. Together, these findings establish multi-trial aggregation as a key architectural choice in non-invasive neural decoding and identify training-time logit averaging as a simple, effective default. 9References

# C Learning rate tuning 

For each combination of dataset, paradigm, and group size, we tune the learning rate in two stages. We first evaluate three learning rates ( 7 × 10 −5, 1 × 10 −4, 3 × 10 −4) with a single seed. Where the upper or lower edge of this initial grid performs noticeably better, we extend the grid in the relevant direction: 5 × 10 −4 and 1 × 10 −3 above the upper edge, and 4 × 10 −5 below the lower edge. Post-hoc averaging is trained once per (dataset, learning rate) and evaluated at all group sizes, since its training does not depend on the group size. The learning rate yielding the highest validation balanced accuracy is then fixed for the final evaluation.

0.000 and 0.008 respectively; at g=100 , both are 0.000 . This collapse follows directly from the averaging rule. Post-hoc averaging combines bounded per-trial probabilities pn ∈ [0 , 1] C , so a class with low marginal probability across all g trials cannot accumulate enough probability mass to overtake a more frequent alternative, even when it is the correct answer. 

# G Calibration analysis 

This appendix reports a more detailed analysis of model calibration referenced in Section 5.1. Calibration is assessed for models trained with training-time logit averaging and with post-hoc averaging, evaluated both on single ungrouped trials (Table 7) and at the group level used during evaluation (Table 8). All calibration metrics use expected calibration error (ECE), where lower values indicate better agreement between predicted probabilities and empirical correctness rates. 14 G.1 Single-trial calibration 

When applied to ungrouped trials, models trained with logit averaging exhibit substantially worse calibration than models trained with per-trial cross-entropy (Table 7). ECE values for logit averaging range from 0.058 on phoneme voicing to 0.899 on phoneme classification, while post-hoc models maintain ECE below 0.16 on every dataset. This is consistent with the loss-landscape argument in Section 5.1: training-time logit averaging does not optimise for honest per-trial probabilities, and the resulting models produce single-trial logits whose magnitudes substantially exceed what per-trial calibration would warrant. Per-trial balanced accuracy is not uniformly worse for logit averaging, however. On phoneme classification and phoneme voicing, single-trial balanced accuracy is comparable or higher for logit averaging despite the calibration gap. On MegNIST and word top-10, post-hoc retains a per-trial advantage. The per-trial accuracy results are not directly tied to the calibration story — they reflect what the per-trial features have learned, not how confident the model is about them. G.4 Interpretation 

The single-trial and group-level calibration results together suggest that the per-trial logits produced by logit averaging are not independently calibrated, but combine constructively under averaging in a way that single-trial training does not produce. A model trained with per-trial cross-entropy can produce per-trial probabilities that are individually well-calibrated and yet, when averaged, fail to provide useful discrimination between confident and uncertain predictions at the group level. 16
