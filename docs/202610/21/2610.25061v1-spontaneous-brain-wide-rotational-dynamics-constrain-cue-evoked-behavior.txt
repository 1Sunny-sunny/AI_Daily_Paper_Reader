(Dated: October 20, 2026) Uncovering low-dimensional representations of brain-wide neural dynamics and their relationship to behavior remains a central challenge. Here, we show that spontaneous brain-wide rotational dy-namics constrain cue-evoked behavior and learning. To systematically establish these findings, we developed a dimensionality-reduction technique called Rotational Component Analysis (RCA) that optimizes the detection of low-variance rotational modes even within individual trials. We applied RCA to spike train data from motor cortices of monkeys and humans, mouse cortex-wide calcium imaging and in human functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and magnetoencephalogra-phy (MEG). In mice, a rotational mode identified from activity recorded at rest predicts cue-evoked behavior: the phase of rotation when a randomly timed cue arrives predicts whether the animal subsequently pulls the lever. We explain this surprisingly strong correlation using both a recurrent neural network (RNN) and a minimal two-dimensional model of response learning, in which the intrinsic rotational mode serves as a dynamical constraint on response and learning. We further identify double-rotation modes containing two centers of rotation that separate preparatory from task dynamics in both mouse and human data. Remarkably, corresponding modes also organize neural dynamics during a non-motor human task, and rotational irreversibility captures early psy-chosis even when a principal component analysis shows no group difference. Our work establishes rotational dynamics as low-dimensional coordinates that constrain how ongoing brain states are con-verted into behavior. More broadly, RCA provides a versatile tool for detecting hidden nonreciprocal dynamics in biological and physical datasets. 

Although neural activity is high-dimensional, the be-haviorally relevant part can often be captured by low-dimensional latent dynamics [1–6]. Rotation is a recur-rent motif in such low-dimensional neural dynamics. In this context, rotation arises in cyclic activities produced by central pattern generators and rhythmic motor con-trol [7], but also in non-cyclic actions such as individ-ual reaches [8–13]. At the brain-wide scale, oscillations are believed to play a central role in cognitive processes [14–16]. Similarly, rotating and spiral waves propagat-ing through physical brain space have been suggested to coordinate long-range information flows [17, 18]. These elements suggest rotation as a potential organizing prin-ciple for brain-wide neural dynamics. Rotations in ongoing spontaneous neural activity have received far less attention. Spontaneous activity refers to intrinsic neural activity that is not a direct response to external cues [19–24]. By definition, it cannot be temporally aligned with known causes, making it more challenging to study. Whether rotational dynamics can be robustly identified in spontaneous activity, and if so, whether they can be linked to future behavior, remain open questions.

To address these questions, we introduce rota-tional component analysis (RCA), an unsupervised dimensionality-reduction method that explicitly prior-itizes rotational dynamics. RCA quantifies rotation through the breaking of mirror symmetry in the dis-tribution of neural trajectories and searches for a low-dimensional projection that maximizes a measure of this asymmetry, rather than maximizing explained variance or reconstruction of the full neural activity [25–29]. Be-cause of its prioritization, RCA can reveal low-variance rotational modes that may be underrepresented by other methods. This feature is particularly important for spon-taneous activity, in which uninstructed behavior and other ongoing processes generate large rotation-irrelevant fluctuations that cannot be suppressed by trial averag-ing. RCA can therefore be applied directly to continu-ous recordings or pooled individual trials, without event alignment, trial averaging or behavioral labels. Here, we first benchmark RCA using human and macaque motor-cortical spiking activity during reaching, a setting in which rotational population dynamics are well established. We then apply RCA to mouse cortex-wide calcium imaging, human functional magnetic reso-nance imaging (fMRI) and human magnetoencephalog-raphy (MEG), and succeed in detecting spontaneous ro-tational dynamics in all three datasets. In mice, the rota-tional mode identified during rest exhibits an unusually strong association with cue-evoked behavior during task arXiv:2610.25061v1 [q-bio.NC] 18 Oct 2026 2sessions. We propose that the intrinsic rotation serves as a dynamical constraint on both response and learn-ing: cue-evoked responses are more likely, and more read-ily learned, when they follow the pre-existing rotational flow. We reproduce this relationship in a recurrent neural network (RNN) and a minimal two-dimensional model. In human fMRI, we find that rotational dynamics are weaker in participants with early psychosis than in con-trols. We further find that double-rotation modes, de-fined by circulation around two distinct centers, separate preparatory and task dynamics in both mouse and hu-man data. Altogether, our results suggest that rotation provides a brain-wide dynamical coordinate associated with both behavior and learning.

> A brain-wide rotational mode predicts mouse responses to a random cue

We next applied APR to wide-field calcium imaging of mouse brain activity. We analyzed the BraiDyn-BC dataset, which contains longitudinal recordings from 25 head-fixed mice expressing GCaMP6s in cortical exci-tatory neurons during rest and during acquisition of a tone-triggered lever-pull task over 15 training days [30]. We used hemodynamics-corrected fluorescence sampled at 30 Hz from 44 regions (22 per hemisphere) registered to the Allen Common Coordinate Framework (Fig. 2A, left). The regions span the mouse dorsal cortex, includ-ing motor, somatosensory, olfactory, visual, auditory, and retrosplenial areas (Extended Data Fig. 2). We first compared resting-state probability flux in the PCA and RCA planes (Fig. 2B,C). In the PCA plane, where x1 and x2 are the first two principal compo-nents, the probability flux showed only weak circulation (Fig. 2B). By contrast, the RCA plane revealed a strong clockwise probability current (Fig. 2C). This circulation was identified from spontaneous activity and therefore did not depend on sensory stimulation or task events. Notably, the RCA plane accounted for only 0.6% of the total variance, compared with 87% for the PCA plane, highlighting how a rotational mode can be overlooked by conventional dimensionality-reduction methods. The regional composition of this mode and the minimal an-tisymmetric coupling pattern show loadings across the cortex, with large values in motor and somatosensory ar-eas (Extended Data Fig. 2). This latent rotation coor-dinates distributed brain regions that need not be spa-tially adjacent, distinguishing it from topographic rotat-ing waves [18]. We next asked whether the rotational mode identi-fied at rest was related to behavior during task sessions. An auditory cue initiated a response window in which a successful lever pull was followed by a water reward (Fig. 2D). Each cue occurred after a random delay of 3–4 s following the preceding lever pull. This random-ized timing sampled different states of the ongoing cor-tical dynamics across trials, allowing us to test whether the cue-onset state predicted task outcome. We defined 

P rsp (xcue ) as the probability that the mouse responds to a cue, conditioned on its state xcue in the reduced plane at cue onset. Response probability varied only weakly and irregularly across the PCA plane (Fig. 2E), but showed a strong directional dependence in the RCA plane: cues that occurred when cortical activity occu-pied the upper-right portion of the plane were followed by responses more often than cues that occurred when activity occupied the lower-left portion (Fig. 2F). We de-scribed this pattern as a radially symmetric baseline plus a directional linear component, 

P rsp (xcue ) = P0(1 − c0∥xcue ∥) + g·xcue , (5) where xcue is the cue-onset state and P0, c0 and g were fitted to the empirical data. The fit captured both the directional increase and the weaker radial dependence of response probability (Fig. 2G). Here, g is the response gradient and points in the direction of increasing response probability. To determine whether this predictive relationship was unusual among two-dimensional projections, we com-pared the magnitude of the response gradient with those obtained from 2,000 random planes (Fig. 2H). The RCA gradient lay near the extreme upper tail of the random-plane distribution and was substantially larger than the PCA and jPCA gradients. Notably, the RCA plane was selected using only resting-state activity, without ac-cess to task or behavioral information. Thus, a sponta-neous rotational mode identified without behavioral su-pervision nevertheless provided a strong neural correlate of subsequent cue-evoked behavior. We further asked whether this predictive relationship could be explained by arousal, for which pupil diameter provides a widely used proxy. On held-out sessions, the cue-onset RCA state predicted trial outcome better than pupil-derived arousal (Extended Data Fig. 3).

> Learning strengthens the cue-evoked response drift

The lever-pull response is a learned rather than an in-nate mouse behavior. We therefore asked how the re-sponse drift changed as the behavior was acquired. We inferred f pre and f rsp in five nonoverlapping three-day windows spanning the 15 training days. The intrinsic drift f pre was largely unchanged, whereas the cue-evoked field f rsp showed a general increase in magnitude (Ex-tended Data Fig. 4). This is evident in the amplitude parameter f rsp 0 , which shows an overall increasing trend across training (Fig. 4A). In parallel, the distribution of f post  

> ∥

shifted toward the response-generating regime during learning (Fig. 4B), whereas the conditional drift-to-response mapping P rsp (f post  

> ∥

) remained similar across training windows (Fig. 4C). A cross-validated model that held this response rule fixed while allowing the inferred response drift to vary captured most of the improvement in observed performance (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that learning primarily alters the cue-evoked dynamics, and hence the distribution of f post  

> ∥

, while preserving the mapping from this dynamical variable to response prob-ability. During learning, the direction of the transverse gradi-ent of f rsp remained fixed, which is consistent with the intrinsic rotational dynamics. This gradient was not cap-tured by the RNN (Fig. 3I). This difference may reflect the distinct learning signals available to the two systems. The RNN was trained by backpropagation using an ex-plicit, differentiable objective, whereas the mice presum-ably received sparse trial-level reward feedback. To ex-plain how sparse reward feedback could produce this, we constructed a minimal reinforcement model. Before a learned response drift develops, the intrin-sic rotation alone occasionally carries activity through states whose velocity points along the rewarded response direction (Fig. 4E). Because cues occur at random times, they sample both favorable and unfavorable phases of the rotation, producing rewarded and unrewarded tri-als. The states and drifts associated with reward there-fore form a biased subset of all samples in the rotational plane (Fig. 4F). Rotational currents generalize to humans and detect early psychosis 

We next tested whether rotational modes can be gen-eralized beyond mouse cortex and calcium imaging. We analyzed resting-state recordings from a large cohort of healthy young adults in the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) dataset [31], which includes whole-brain fMRI and MEG data. Applying RCA to the HCP-YA fMRI recordings revealed a rotational cur-rent in a projection of cortical activity (Fig. 5A,B). HCP-YA MEG recordings likewise contained a coherent rota-tional current (Fig. 5C,D), despite MEG’s distinct spatial resolution and much faster sampling rate. The recur-rence of organized currents in mouse calcium imaging, human fMRI and human MEG indicates that symmetry-guided projection is not restricted to one species, record-ing modality or timescale. The inferred rotation peri-ods differed markedly, from 51 s in fMRI to 4.4 s in MEG, consistent with the distinct temporal resolutions and characteristic signal dynamics of the two modalities. Despite this difference, the rotational planes accounted for only 0.2% of the variance in fMRI and 2.6% in MEG, in agreement with the low-variance rotational mode iden-tified in mouse cortex. DISCUSSION 

Our results identify broken mirror symmetry as auseful principle for extracting behaviorally meaning-ful dynamics from brain-wide recordings. RCA and DRCA consistently recovered rotational modes across species and recording modalities. These rotations are defined in a latent phase space, complementing previ-ous observations of brain-wide rotating waves in physi-cal space [17, 18]. Although these rotational dynamics do not rely on external signals, their associations with behavior and learning, together with their alteration in early psychosis, suggest that rotational flow is a preva-lent low-dimensional motif of brain-wide dynamics with functional and clinical relevance. An important distinction is the space in which rota-tion is defined. Ye et al. [18] detected topographic waves in which oscillatory phase winds around a center in the physical cortical sheet, allowing the location, radius, di-rection and speed of individual wave events to be mea-sured. By contrast, RCA detects directed circulation of the brain-wide activity trajectory in a learned latent state space whose axes are weighted combinations of recorded regions or sensors. It therefore does not require dense spatial sampling, a predefined frequency band or a spa-tial wave template, and can be applied across calcium imaging, fMRI and MEG datasets. A loop in this latent space does not by itself imply that activity propagates as a physical wave. The two forms of rotation are neverthe-less compatible: a coherent rotating wave could generate a loop in population state space, whereas latent circula-tion could also arise from sequential interactions among distributed regions without forming a spatial vortex. Why might rotation recur so broadly? Here we con-sider two possibilities. First, cortical circuits may reuse cyclic dynamical motifs that support central pattern gen-erators and rhythmic movement [8, 33]. Such motifs could have been adapted to organize more complex com-putations, including discrete, non-rhythmic actions. Sec-ond, rotation is among the simplest flows capable of sus-taining a nonzero steady-state current and breaking de-tailed balance. In a linear system, nonreciprocal cou-pling between only two macroscopic degrees of freedom can generate rotational drift, making rotation a natural outcome of high-dimensional recurrent dynamics. Con-sistent with this possibility, the trained RNN developed a spontaneous rotation even though no rotational structure was explicitly imposed. 11 The analysis of mouse cortical activity suggests how continuous rotational dynamics can shape a discrete, non-rhythmic behavioral response. Rotations in mo-tor cortex are well established during individual reaches, where they have been interpreted as structured popula-tion dynamics for generating movement [8, 12, 13]. In our analysis, the rotation was already present in sponta-neous cortex-wide activity before the cue. Random cue timing therefore sampled different states and directions of this ongoing flow. Because a dynamical state speci-fies not only the current activity pattern but also its di-rection of evolution, the cue-evoked drive could combine with the intrinsic flow to make a response-generating tra-jectory more or less accessible. According to this view, the cue gates an event-triggered readout of a continu-ously evolving latent process: neural activity may cy-cle, whereas behavior occurs only when the cue and on-going flow jointly carry the system toward a response-producing regime. The rotational state thus provides a coordinate of dynamical readiness, converting structured ongoing dynamics into trial-to-trial variation in a discrete outcome without requiring rhythmic behavior. The orga-nization of the cue-evoked drift field further suggests that the intrinsic rotation may constrain how the response is learned: reinforcement could preferentially strengthen cue-evoked drives that cooperate with the existing flow, rather than requiring the cortex to construct a new tra-jectory that opposes it. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that animals more readily learn activity patterns within existing neural manifolds and have diffi-culty producing trajectories that violate intrinsic dynam-ics, suggesting that learning preferentially follows exist-ing neural structures [34, 35]. Our findings extend this idea from constraints on producing neural trajectories to constraints on learning an input–response mapping: anew behavior may be acquired by reshaping cue-evoked inputs within an existing dynamical scaffold rather than by rewriting the intrinsic dynamics. The reaching benchmarks illustrate where RCA dif-fers from conventional dimensionality-reduction methods (Fig. 1). In the macaque data, coherent circulation was evident only in the RCA plane. Both RCA and jPCA revealed rotation in condition-averaged human activity, but RCA produced a more consistent individual-trial current. This advantage is expected when trial-specific or other rotation-independent variation obscures a low-variance rotational mode. Whereas PCA prioritizes vari-ance and jPCA first restricts the analysis to leading prin-cipal components, RCA directly optimizes the strength and consistency of angular current. APR is related to the thermodynamic language of irreversibility but should not be equated with the entropy production rate (EPR). EPR quantifies all detectable time-reversal asymmetry. Current-based APR isolates the component expressed through a current that is odd under both time rever-sal and the chosen spatial transformation. This con-struction targets rotational handedness while retaining a quantitative connection to nonequilibrium probability currents [25–27]. Neuronal activity recorded immediately before trial on-set (i.e., spontaneous) has been shown to nonetheless influence post-trial activity [24, 36], and even bias be-havioral performance [37]. As discussed above, RCA is a dimensionality reduction technique that can identify within-trial rotational dynamics. Analysis of the mouse cortical activity during the tone-detection/lever-pulling task shows that the position of rotational dynamics be-fore cue onset determined (in part) behavioral response (Fig. 2F). The simple two-dimensional latent description that RCA provides greatly simplifies the connection from spontaneous to trial-evoked neural activity, compared to past analyses [24, 37]. Together, our results support a view in which ongo-ing brain-wide activity is not merely background vari-ability around an evoked response. Its directed flow defines coordinates that shape how an input is con-verted into action, how rewarded dynamics are reinforced during learning and how preparatory and task-related states are organized. By optimizing for broken dynam-ical symmetry rather than variance, RCA and DRCA provide a route from high-dimensional recordings to low-dimensional flows with direct behavioral meaning. Al-though developed here for brain activity, the framework is broadly applicable to high-dimensional data and could uncover hidden nonreciprocal dynamics across diverse physical and biological systems [38, 39] such as epige-netic landscapes [40]. 12 (A) (B)

Normalization and orientation of two-dimensional modes 

Across all datasets, we treated a two-dimensional linear mode first as a subspace, independent of the scaling of its two weight vectors. Human fMRI 

We analyzed cortical fMRI data from the HCP Young Adult S1200 release using the cohort and preprocessed time series described previously [27, 47]. Cortical data were registered to fsLR-32k space using MSM-All and parcellated into 100 regions. Five component-based noise-correction (CompCor) components derived from ventricular and white-matter masks and 12 detrended HCP motion regressors were removed. The mean global signal was regressed out, after which the time series were band-pass filtered between 0.009 and 0.08 Hz. Frames with framewise displacement greater than 0.2 mm or 18 DVARS greater than 75 were treated as outliers, and sessions in which more than 50% of frames were outliers were excluded. Participants were retained only if all se-lected scans survived quality control, yielding 590 healthy adults. For the resting-state analysis, we used the REST1 LR and RL runs (repetition time, 0.72 s) and divided each run into nonoverlapping 50-frame trajectories. For the social task analysis, we used the complete LR and RL runs (274 frames per run), yielding 1,180 participant-run trajectories from 590 participants for mode estimation. HCP task labels were not used to identify the projection and were introduced only afterward to align and aver-age task and fixation trajectories. For this block-aligned analysis, participants whose block order differed from the majority order were excluded separately for each phase-encoding direction (3 from LR and 2 from RL), leaving 587 LR runs and 588 RL runs from 589 unique partici-pants.

We analyzed resting-state MEG recordings from the HCP Young Adult S1200 release [48]. > HCP Early Psychosis fMRI

We analyzed the derived AAL116-parcellated resting-state fMRI time series distributed with the HCP Early Psychosis (HCP-EP) study [32]. The dataset comprised 53 healthy controls and 82 participants with non-affective psychosis. Each participant completed four resting-state runs over two consecutive days, with 410 frames per run and a repetition time of 0.72 s. The source images were processed using the HCP minimal preprocessing pipeline and denoised using ICA-FIX; regional time series were band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.08 Hz. We re-tained all four runs, yielding 212 control runs and 328 non-affective-psychosis runs. In the primary analysis, RCA and PCA projec-tions were learned exclusively from healthy-control data. The projections were fitted using all 53 controls, and participant-level APR and PCA variability were calcu-lated by combining the four runs from each participant; PCA variability was defined as the standard deviation along the leading principal component. In the control-trained split-sample analysis, 26 randomly selected con-trols were used for projection learning, while the remain-ing 27 controls and all 82 participants with non-affective psychosis were held out for evaluation. Group differences were assessed using two-sided Welch’s t-tests, with partic-ipants as the independent analysis units. In this control-trained analysis, PCA variability did not differ between groups ( P = 0 .592 ), whereas APR remained lower in par-ticipants with non-affective psychosis ( P = 2 .52 × 10 −4;Extended Data Fig. 7A,B). We performed a complementary disease-trained anal-ysis to test whether this result depended on learning the projections from control data. We randomly divided the 82 participants with non-affective psychosis into two equal groups, learned the RCA and PCA projections from 41 participants, and evaluated them in the remain-ing 41 participants and all 53 controls. In this analysis, PCA variability was only modestly lower in the held-out psychosis group ( P = 0 .0275 ; one asterisk), whereas APR showed a more significant reduction ( P = 8 .29 × 10 −5;Extended Data Fig. 7C,D). Thus, reduced APR was de-tected irrespective of which group supplied the data used to learn the rotational plane.

> Comparison with alternative dimensionality-reduction methods

We evaluated the detected rotational modes by com-paring the current-based scores obtained with RCA, DyCA, jPCA, an unconstrained linear dynamical sys-tem (LDS), PCA and UMAP across the datasets ana-lyzed in this study (Extended Data Fig. 8). For the six single-rotation analyses, each method was evaluated us-ing the surrogate APR bσ in Eq. 2; for the mouse task and HCP-YA social task, the corresponding projection was evaluated using the double-rotation score bσdouble in Eq. 13. RCA yielded the largest single-rotation score in every dataset, with DyCA providing the closest al-ternative in several cases. This similarity is expected because DyCA favors components whose derivatives are captured by low-dimensional linear dynamics, and asym-metric linear coupling between two variables can generate rotation. Its performance was much weaker for macaque reaching, however, where the rotational trajectories may not be adequately described by a single linear flow. RCA showed a particularly large advantage in this dataset, for which the other projections produced little net angular current. DRCA likewise produced substantially larger double-rotation scores than the comparison methods in both mouse and human task data. Because RCA and DRCA directly optimize the re-spective scores, these comparisons do not provide an independent test of predictive performance. Neverthe-less, bσ is a physically motivated, current-based measure of the strength and consistency of directed circulation; hence, the comparison shows that RCA and DRCA de-tect stronger and more consistent rotational dynamics.

> Held-out prediction from rotational states and pupil-indexed arousal

For the receiver operating characteristic analysis in Fig. 6D, we randomly divided recording sessions into training (75%) and test (25%) sets. The RCA and DRCA projection vectors were fixed before this super-vised analysis. Using only the training sessions, we sep-arately fitted the response-probability model P (r, θ ) = 

P0 + [ f0 + c sin( θ − θ0)] r to the cue-onset RCA state and to the DRCA state 80 frames (2.7 s) pre-cue. Each fitted function assigned a scalar response score to every trial. We then fitted three logistic-regression classifiers using the training sessions: one based on the RCA score, one based on the DRCA score and one based on both scores. Predictors were standardized using the training data, and the classifiers used L2 regularization with C = 1 . The response-probability fits, feature scaling and classifier pa-rameters were applied without refitting to trials from the held-out test sessions. The receiver operating character-istic curves and AUC values in Fig. 6D were calculated exclusively from these held-out predictions. For the comparison with pupil-indexed arousal (Ex-tended Data Fig. 3), we used the continuous right-eye pupil-diameter trace d(t), measured in pixels. Pupil di-ameter was converted separately for each session to a robust arousal score, 

zarousal (t) = d(t) − median[ d]1.4826 median[ |d − median[ d]|] , (28) where the median and median absolute deviation were calculated over all finite, positive pupil-diameter sam-ples in that session; the factor 1.4826 makes the scaled median absolute deviation consistent with the standard deviation for Gaussian data [50]. Nonfinite or nonpos-itive samples, as well as samples with |zarousal | > 20 ,were treated as tracking artifacts and set to missing. For each trial, we used the arousal score from the nearest time point with a finite value, without temporal interpo-lation. We retained trials with finite arousal values and finite cue-onset RCA coordinates and applied the same 75:25 session-level splitting procedure described above. We also divided pupil arousal into 20 equally spaced bins, retained bins con-taining at least 25 trials and fitted the binned response probabilities with a cubic polynomial weighted by the square root of the bin count. Predicted probabilities were clipped to the interval [0 , 1] .The RCA-only model was an L2-regularized logistic regression using the fitted RCA response score. The arousal-only model used the cubic fit directly, and the combined model was an L2-regularized logistic regression using both the RCA score and the cubic arousal pre-diction. Logistic-regression predictors were standardized using the training sessions only.
