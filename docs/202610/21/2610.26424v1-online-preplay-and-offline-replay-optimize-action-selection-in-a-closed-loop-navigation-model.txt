# Online Preplay and Offline Replay Optimize Action Selection in a Closed-Loop Navigation Model 

Masud Ehsani and Sen Cheng 

Institut für Neuroinformatik, Ruhr-Universität Bochum, Germany 

Masud.Ehsani@ruhr-uni-bochum.de , sen.cheng@ruhr-uni-bochum.de 

Abstract 

Hippocampal neurons not only encode the animal’s current position, but also generate sequences representing potential future states and past locations. During goal-directed navigation, the represented location sweeps ahead of the animal’s location (online forward replay). After such tasks, during rest, neuronal sequences occurring during sharp-wave ripples replay previously explored trajectories (offline forward replay). Following random foraging, however, neuronal sequences resemble Brownian motion, which does not correlate with behavior (diffusive replay). The mechanisms producing such diverse sequences, and their distinct effects on spatial coding and navigation, remain unclear. Here, we address these questions using a closed-loop model of navigation in a two-dimensional open-field environment based on a spiking neuronal network. In our framework, neuronal sequences are generated by a common mechanism driven by background noise and the balance of excitation and inhibition. Different replay modes arise solely from task-dependent synaptic modifications and serve different functions: Online forward replay facilitates shortcut discovery, offline forward replay consolidates optimal sequences of actions, and diffusive replay helps in generalizing the action and state map and homogenizing internal connectivity. Together, these results show that a single network architecture can flexibly generate various replay types and clarify how each contributes to optimizing goal-directed navigation behavior. 

# 1 Introduction 

The hippocampal formation supports navigation and memory through place cells, neurons that fire at specific environmental coordinates to encode an animal’s position [O’Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971]. During active exploration, these cells organize into theta sweeps—rapid sequences nested within each 4–12 Hz theta cycle that "sweep" toward the animal’s immediate future path [Foster and Wilson, 2007, Johnson and Redish, 2007]. During periods of quiet wakefulness and sleep, these same cells are reactivated within high-frequency sharp-wave ripples (SWRs) [Buzsáki, 2015]. Neural decod-ing [Zhang et al., 1998] reveals that this SWR-bound activity represents structured spatial se-quences that manifest in distinct modes depending on behavioral context [Diba and Buzsáki, 2007, Stella et al., 2019]. Crucially, the recurrent CA3 network is thought to be the primary generator of these diverse sequences. Its extensive recurrent architecture, characterized by sparse but powerful excitatory connections, allows for the rapid retrieval of stored spatial associations, which are then paced by theta-frequency inhibition to form ordered sweeps during navigation [Lisman and Redish, 2009, Skaggs et al., 1996]. The same network also drives hippocampal activity during immobility; specif-ically, population burst activity in CA3 has been shown to be a prerequisite for the sequence 1arXiv:2610.26424v1 [physics.bio-ph] 19 Oct 2026 replay observed in CA1 during sharp-wave ripples [Sullivan et al., 2011, Nakashiba et al., 2009]. These bursts are highly skewed in size [Buzsáki, 2015] and typically last tens to a few hundred milliseconds, with statistics appearing log-normal or power-law depending on brain state and mea-surement scale [Priesemann et al., 2013]. Computational studies indicate that log-normal synaptic weight distributions in recurrent networks can produce log-normal bursts [Omura et al., 2015], whereas power-law bursting emerges when networks operate near a critical phase transition [Beggs and Plenz, 2003, Scarpetta et al., 2023, Poil et al., 2012]. Together, these findings suggest that replay during sharp-wave ripples arises intrinsically from the dynamical regime of the CA3 recurrent network, highlighting the importance of a CA3 model in which population bursts serve as the primary mode of activity and the stored spatial map provides the replayed content. To understand how such a map is physically instantiated, models of CA3 have historically framed the network either as a point attractor [Treves and Rolls, 1994], a continuous attractor [Colgin et al., 2010, Tsodyks and Sejnowski, 1995, Azizi et al., 2013], or a hybrid supporting dis-crete memories and continuous spatial maps [Rolls and Treves, 2024, Rolls, 2018]. In continuous attractor networks, activity evolves on a manifold whose topology reflects the encoded variable, a principle extensively studied in grid cell networks both experimentally [Gardner et al., 2022] and theoretically [Guanella et al., 2007]. However, whether CA3 implements a true two-dimensional plane attractor remains debated [Knierim and Zhang, 2012]. Experimental and computational evidence instead suggests that CA3 may operate as a high-dimensional recurrent system with metastable, quasi-continuous attractors [Sasaki et al., 2007], producing an energy landscape with shallow basins that enables memory recall and replay but does not sustain ideal continuous attractor dynamics. This metastability, together with tight excitation–inhibition balance, provides a potential mechanism for generating spontaneous population bursts capable of activating specific assemblies. Beyond these internal dynamics, experimental studies investigate the specific contents and behavioral relevance of such sequence replay. In goal-directed tasks, the network produces "online" forward replay during pauses to depict future paths toward goals [Pfeiffer and Foster, 2013], as well as backward (or reverse) replay to evaluate recently traveled routes and propagate reward signals [Foster and Wilson, 2006, Diba and Buzsáki, 2007]. During subsequent rest or sleep, the network continues to replay these paths to consolidate task-relevant memories and stabilize the internal map [Dupret et al., 2010, Ji and Wilson, 2007]. Conversely, after random foraging, the network generates diffusive replay, where sequences resemble random-walk dynamics rather than directed trajectories [Stella et al., 2019]. In parallel with these offline functions, the real-time "look-ahead" mechanism observed during theta sweeps is considered essential for maintaining spatial orientation and coordinating goal-directed movement [Lisman and Redish, 2009]. These considerations highlight a gap in the literature on hippocampal replay: existing models rarely combine (i) intrinsic generation of burst dynamics, (ii) spontaneous replay of spatially encoded content embedded in the network as metastable states, and (iii) the behavioral relevance of this replay on action selection. To fill this gap, we implement a closed-loop spatial navigation model composed of place cells and action-selection neurons [Brzosko et al., 2017, Ghazinouri et al., 2024, Ghazinouri and Cheng, 2025]. Place cells emerge from spatially tuned inputs within a recurrent excitatory and inhibitory network and project onto an action-selection ring attractor that determines the agent’s movements. Plasticity in both recurrent and place-to-action pathways allows the agent to learn optimal trajectories and reach reward locations. We evaluate this system in two navigation tasks. In a goal-directed task analogous to the Morris watermaze [Morris, 1984, Morris et al., 1982], online and offline forward replay activate the appropriate action cells for each place cell, enabling shortcut discovery and flexible strategy adjustment. In a random foraging task, diffusive replay produces local sequences resembling random walks [Stella et al., 2019], which promote a more uniform spatial representation, strengthen recurrent 2connectivity, and generalize place-to-action mappings across the environment, ultimately improving subsequent goal-directed navigation. Altogether, these findings show that neuronal sequences, shaped jointly by learning and intrinsic network dynamics, can flexibly guide behavior by influencing action selection. 

# 2 Methods 

We use a modified version of the closed-loop spatial navigation model implemented in [Brzosko et al., 2017, Ghazinouri et al., 2024], extending it to include a recurrent network capable of embedding and replaying sequences. Brzosko et al. [Brzosko et al., 2017] introduced a simplified action selection network with dopaminergic modulation of the connections from place cells to action neurons via an eligibility trace, enabling delayed STDP-based synaptic changes [Gerstner et al., 2018]. This framework has been used to investigate place cell properties [Ghazinouri et al., 2024], neu-romodulation [Wert-Carvajal et al., 2022], and the effects of STDP rules on reversal learning [Ghazinouri and Cheng, 2025]. 

2.1 Model Architecture 

Our modified model comprises three layers: a spatially tuned input layer received from the envi-ronment, a recurrent layer with place cells consisting of excitatory and inhibitory neurons, and an action-selection layer modeled as a ring attractor with effective Mexican hat connectivity, arising from local excitation and broader inhibition (Fig. 1a). The model operates in two distinct states: an online phase (exploration) and an offline phase (rest/replay). During exploration, the recurrent network receives spatially tuned input from the environment, and activity in the action-selection layer determines the agent’s movement direction and velocity, computed as a population vector average (Appendix A.2). In contrast, during the rest phase, the spatially tuned input is removed and replaced by spatially homogeneous excitatory and inhibitory noise modeled as Poisson spike trains. Neurons in the recurrent and action-selection layers are modeled as leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) units with conductance-based synapses. Excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs are implemented as transient conductance changes with distinct reversal potentials. During rest, network activity operates in a low firing-rate regime driven by spatially homogeneous external noise as well as internally generated fluctuations. An analytical approximation of the single-neuron gain function in this regime is provided in Appendix A.3.1. All neuron, synapse, connectivity, and simulation parameters are summarized in Appendix A.1. The core of our model lies in the dynamics and plasticity of the recurrent layer. This layer receives environmental inputs, encodes trajectories to the reward location via its recurrent connections, and drives behavior through forward projections to the action-selection layer (the place-to-action pathway). Synapses in both the recurrent and place-to-action pathways employ dopaminergically modulated plasticity governed by eligibility traces. The recurrent layer represents a spatial map of the environment as a 2D neural manifold. One can think of the cells as being arranged on this manifold such that neural assemblies encoding adjacent places lie closer to each other. These assemblies receive spatially tuned input from nearby locations and exhibit higher effective connectivity in both number and strength. More specifically, excitatory projections to both excitatory and inhibitory neurons follow a distance-dependent Gaussian connectivity kernel,

3.1 Spatially Balanced E–I Network at the Edge of Instability Reproduces Burst Dynamics 

We reproduce the main characteristics of place cell activity during active exploration and rest within our low-dimensional network. Specifically, this includes the emergence of a localized activity bump in response to spatial input at a given location, as well as population bursts of varying sizes during rest [Buzsáki, 2015]. Although CA3 bursts tend to coincide with Down–Up transitions in the dentate gyrus [Kajikawa et al., 2022], intracellular recordings show that many CA3 pyramidal neurons become hyperpolarized or show weak modulation during these transitions, consistent with strong recurrent or feedforward inhibition within the CA3 network [Hahn et al., 2007]. This suggests that DG-driven input can transiently excite subsets of CA3 neurons at Up-state onset, but that during sustained Up states or replay, CA3 operates under powerful inhibitory control, resulting in sparse, state-dependent firing [Kajikawa et al., 2022, Hahn et al., 2007]. Thus, experimental evidence suggests that the network operates in a slightly inhibition-dominated regime. In our model, we tune the external homogeneous inputs so that interneurons are slightly more depolarized than pyramidal cells, thereby maintaining inhibitory control. In Appendix A.3.1, we analyze the firing of neurons receiving balanced subthreshold input, identify the appropriate balanced input rate that leads to low background firing of around 0.1 Hz, and approximate a low-firing regime gain function. Furthermore, for individual neurons to be able to trigger activity through recurrent connections, the synaptic weights should be relatively large, while for the system to support low-firing solutions, the average excitation and inhibition should approximately cancel out [Van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky, 1996, Brunel, 2000]. To achieve this balance, while maintaining an adequate level of fluctuations, we scale the weights as w ∼ 1/√k, where k is the number of connections per neuron. This spatially balanced network can exhibit multiple dynamic states depending on the input and recurrent weight kernels. In Appendix A.3.2, we derive the conditions on the recurrent weights for the system to operate near a critical phase transition. This regime gives rise to bursts with highly variable sizes and durations as well as irregular inter-burst intervals. By analyzing the bifurcations of the homogeneous mode ( k = 0), we find that the spatially connected network displays scale-free avalanches near the Bogdanov–Takens (BT) bifurcation point (Fig. 2a). Similar behavior has been reported in sparse and all-to-all homogeneous networks [Benayoun et al., 2010, Ehsani and Jost, 2023]. We focus on the network operating near the Hopf bifurcation line specifically in the vicinity of the Bogdanov–Takens point. At the edge of this bifurcation, unstable fixed points characterized by eigenvalues close to zero exist within the low-firing regime (Fig. 2b2). Recurrent connections amplify fluctuations in external input to trigger neuronal avalanches. These events maintain a tight balance between inhibition and excitation. This precise balance allows activity to propagate with highly variable avalanche sizes and durations, following power-law distributions. Through a subcritical Hopf bifurcation, a low-firing fixed point surrounded by an unstable limit cycle emerges (Fig. 2b3). At the boundary between regimes 2 and 3 in the bifurcation map, stochastic fluctuations drive the system to switch intermittently between the quiescent and low-firing states. It is in this transition region that neuronal avalanches emerge, as recurrent interactions amplify noise-driven excursions while preserving overall excitation–inhibition balance. In the same network, when spatially tuned input is applied, strong local inhibition gives rise to fast ( ≈ 50 Hz) oscillations (Fig. 2c). Under conditions of near-balanced excitation and inhibition, network activity instead decays slowly following stimulus offset. 3.2 Learning an Optimal Path through Online Replay 

The recurrent connectivity is organized such that a localized bump of activity emerges in response to spatially tuned input and moves continuously across the manifold as the agent navigates the environment. In this framework, learning a path toward a goal corresponds to encoding a sequence of place cells together with the actions that transition the agent from a start location to the goal. When the goal location is invisible or distant from the agent’s starting position, navigation cannot rely on a direct spatial representation of the goal. Instead, the agent must depend on memory of previously experienced trajectories, represented as sequences of locations and associated actions. We assume a pre-established spatial map, such that place cells are already formed and local state transitions are partially learned through established synaptic weight kernels. However, constructing goal-directed navigation beyond local transitions requires embedding a one-dimensional trajectory within the two-dimensional recurrent manifold. In this section, we examine how the agent refines an initially suboptimal navigation strategy during exploration. Specifically, we consider a network in which the recurrent weights and place-to-action connections encode an indirect path to the goal, and we analyze how forward replay of upcoming place-cell and action representations influences action selection and supports path optimization. 94

3.3 Learning during Offline Replay 

Here, we discuss the effect of spontaneous activity on the network connections within the 2D manifold, particularly focusing on the potential line attractors that are formed during the early exploration phase.

3.4 Diffusion Dynamics on the 2D Attractor Manifold

# 4 Discussion 

The results presented here establish a dynamical framework for understanding how hippocampal CA3 can facilitate memory consolidation through scale-free population bursting. By situating the network near a Bogdanov–Takens bifurcation, we show that CA3-like dynamics can operate at the edge of both activation and synchronization thresholds, enabling fluid transitions between quiescent states and coordinated activity. Operating in this regime gives rise to power-law burst distributions that satisfy scaling relations consistent with those observed near second-order phase transitions. Unlike traditional models that rely on homogeneous irregular firing, this critical regime maximizes the network’s susceptibility to internal memory traces: because the system is balanced near an instability, even weak synaptic inputs can trigger large-scale recruitment. Crucially, the long spatial correlation lengths that emerge near this transition allow the network to bridge distant neural ensembles, facilitating the activation of sequences that are distributed across the spatial extent of the network rather than confined to local clusters. The stability of this sensitive operating point is maintained through homeostatic regulation via synaptic normalization, ensuring that the network remains responsive to specific synaptic modifications without descending into runaway excitation. Our results further indicate that the propagation of activity is governed by the architecture of the synaptic kernels. In particular, broader local inhibition favors the emergence of wandering activity bumps in the form of a random walk, which prevent global synchronization while allowing activity to sample the learned manifold across multiple spatial and temporal scales. An important open question concerns the statistical nature of burst dynamics in CA3. While power-law distributed bursts are well documented across cortical areas ([Beggs and Plenz, 2003], [Bellay et al., 2015]), CA3 bursting activity has been less extensively characterized. Some studies 21 suggest power-law scaling ([Timme et al., 2016]), whereas others report log-normal size distributions that deviate from strict criticality ([Buzsáki, 2015]). This variability highlights the need for further experimental recordings and systematic analyses of the spatiotemporal structure of spontaneous CA3 activity to clarify the mechanisms underlying its burst statistics. Our framework contributes to this discussion by demonstrating how scale-free population bursts can emerge as a direct consequence of excitation–inhibition balance in a network of leaky integrate-and-fire neurons with initially normally distributed synaptic weights. At the same time, the model provides a foundation for further biological refinement. Incorporating log-normal synaptic weight distributions consistent with experimental observations ([Buzsáki and Mizuseki, 2014]) together with intrinsic bursting neuron models could enrich the network’s capacity to generate heavy-tailed dynamics and allow investigation of single-neuron stochastic resonance coordinated by population events. Notably, such skewed weight distributions may themselves arise as emergent properties of ongoing synaptic plasticity interacting with spontaneous activity, suggesting a self-organizing pathway toward the connectivity profiles observed in hippocampal circuits. A related theoretical question concerns the universality class of the observed critical behavior. Although the critical exponents in our network align with several experimental spike-recording studies [Fontenele et al., 2019, Hahn et al., 2010, Jones et al., 2023] and share features with oscillatory criticality models [Poil et al., 2012], the specific exponent values do not coincide with those of standard universality classes typically associated with neural avalanches. Directed percolation, for example, predicts mean-field exponents of τ = 1 .5 for burst size and α = 2 .0 for duration and has been widely proposed as an effective model following the landmark results of Beggs and Plenz ([Beggs and Plenz, 2003]). The distinct exponents emerging along the subcritical Hopf bifurcation line in our model suggest that the corresponding critical regime may belong to a different universality class. Clarifying the effective description of this regime will require systematic analyses of finite-size scaling, perturbations, and noise structure, as both internally generated variability and the specific neuronal model can influence the universality class of the dynamics. Beyond consolidation, our results indicate that sequential memory replay reshapes behaviorally relevant information processing through homogenization and local generalization of the state–action map. In a closed-loop navigation model where the two-dimensional recurrent network serves as a topological embedding of the environment, place-cell sequences and place-to-action mappings acquired during exploration are transformed during both online and offline replay. Replay integrates actions across neurons encoding adjacent spatial locations, enabling the combination of information about past and future trajectories within compressed temporal windows. This mechanism supports path optimization and shortcut discovery by recombining previously experienced trajectories into more efficient routes. Diffusive replay smooths and generalizes the action policy to neighboring locations, effectively interpolating between discrete experiences. In behavioral simulations, this refined action map enables shortcut selection and near-optimal navigation, indicating that diffusive replay constitutes a functional mechanism for transforming episodic traces into a continuous, generalized representation. The navigation component of the model, however, relies on specific structural assumptions. In particular, the environmental spatial map is pre-embedded in a low-dimensional two-dimensional manifold via state-dependent connectivity, with synaptic strength scaling according to spatial proximity. An important extension would be to examine how such a topological embedding might emerge within a large, sparse recurrent network lacking an initial spatial organization, for example through exploration along one-dimensional trajectories. Integrating grid-cell modules and Dentate Gyrus inputs into an excitation–inhibition balanced CA3 network may prove essential for generating structured spatial representations in this setting. Within a high-dimensional sparse regime, one could then analyze how bursting dynamics influence the efficiency and stability of map formation, 22 and how spontaneous activity primes the network for future navigation. Moreover, the phenomenological model of action selection assumes that preferred actions are encoded directly in synaptic weights from purely spatial place cells to action-selection neurons, whose activity immediately generates movement commands. While this architecture suffices for goal-directed learning with local state transitions, it becomes restrictive in tasks involving multiple overlapping trajectories, varying starting points, or multiple goal locations. Alternative navigation frameworks using biologically plausible local learning rules that align with temporal difference (TD) learning of a global value function have been shown to implement successor representations [Bono et al., 2023, Fang et al., 2023], as also formalized in hippocampal predictive-map models [Stachenfeld et al., 2017]. These frameworks decouple reward values from state transitions, allowing more flexible adaptation to changing goals. While the interaction between spontaneous replay and the updating of successor representations has been suggested in both spiking models [Bono et al., 2023] and reinforcement learning models [Russek et al., 2017], a fully closed-loop spiking network inte-grating replay dynamics with an explicit action-selection mechanism has yet to be developed. Such a model could clarify how replay contributes to policy generalization and optimization in more elaborate navigation scenarios. Finally, the present formulation does not explicitly incorporate the mixed selectivity widely observed in hippocampal neurons [Bittner et al., 2015]. Many cells encode conjunctive combi-nations of location, direction, context, and goal-related information [Ormond and O’Keefe, 2022, Howard et al., 2014, Bittner et al., 2015], indicating that hippocampal representations are inherently high dimensional [Leutgeb et al., 2005]. Extending the model to include conjunctive state–action coding or goal-modulated place representations would provide a more biologically grounded sub-strate for action selection and may enhance the capacity for flexible generalization across tasks and environments. Together, these considerations outline several directions for future work, aimed at refining the biological realism of the model and deepening our understanding of how critical bursting dynamics and spontaneous replay interact to shape memory consolidation and adaptive navigation.

A.3 Dynamics of Balanced Spatial Network Close to Phase Transition 

The emergence of power-law scaling in neural activity, often characterized by neuronal avalanches and heavy-tailed burst distributions, suggests that brain might operate near a critical phase transition. To investigate the biophysical conditions under which these power-law bursts arise, we 33 must bridge the gap between microscopic membrane dynamics and macroscopic population stability. In this section, we develop a theoretical framework to analyze the stability of a spatially extended, conductance-based excitatory-inhibitory (EI) network. We begin by approximating the single-neuron gain function to derive a linearized population model. This allow us to identify the critical points, specifically the Bogdanov-Takens and Turing-Hopf bifurcations, where the system transitions from stable asynchronous firing to structured, scale-free bursting. By examining the stability conditions in Fourier space, we seek to determine how synaptic weights, reversal potentials, and spatial connectivity kernels converge to satisfy the conditions for critical dynamics and robust power-law scaling.

A.4 Scale-Free Bursts in Random and Strictly Local Networks 

In our model, we observe scale-free behavior and critical scaling near a subcritical Hopf bifurcation. In this regime, stochastic fluctuations induce intermittent transitions between a quiescent state and a balanced active state, which may take the form of a high-activity fixed point or a limit cycle. As the system approaches the non-equilibrium critical point (Λ ∗ → 0), the distributions of burst sizes 

S and durations τ follow power laws, 

P (S) ∝ S−τs , P (τ ) ∝ τ −αt . (73) The critical exponents in the oscillatory regime depend on the network dimensionality. Figure A.3 illustrates avalanche dynamics in both random and locally connected networks. Our recurrent network typically operates in the under-damped neighborhood of the Bogdanov-Takens (BT) point, where the lead eigenvalues acquire an imaginary component. For comparison, in the vicinity of a pure saddle-node bifurcation ( ℑ[λ] = 0), the return dynamics are monotonic and effectively one-dimensional. In this limit, the network reduces to a critical branching process belonging to the mean-field directed percolation universality class, characterized by the universal exponents τs = 1 .5and αt = 2 .0 [Ehsani and Jost, 2023, Ehsani and Jost, 2022]. This transition from monotonic to oscillatory return induces a unique universality class where the exponents shift significantly from the standard 2D Directed Percolation (2D-DP) (Table 2). This indicates that critical dynamics at the edge of oscillation/synchronization place the system outside the standard directed percolation universality class [Munoz, 2018]. In oscillatory criticality, the scaling exponent γ ≈ 1.1–1 .2 is markedly lower than in 2D directed percolation ( γ ≈ 1.67), indicating an approximately linear relationship between size and duration, which is characteristic of stochastic hovering around a limit cycle or fixed-point solution. E–I coupling limits burst volume through inhibitory feedback, while spiral dynamics stretch their duration, producing temporally long but volumetrically thin bursts. This regime preserves scale-free flexibility and supports rhythmic transients. A candidate mechanism is provided by [Liang et al., 2020], who show that in mean-field excitation– inhibition networks tuned to a Hopf bifurcation, the squared oscillation amplitude X = r2 obeys a first-passage-time problem with demographic (shot) noise, dX/dt = aX − X3 + h + √X ξ (t), with a

tuned to the marginal point a = 0 and h a small effective drive. Because the noise scales as √X,this maps onto an exactly solvable Bessel-type diffusion whose absorbing-boundary statistics give a continuously tunable duration exponent αt = 3 /2 − 2h, rather than a fixed universality-class value – the same near-marginality mechanism underlying the crackling-noise relation used elsewhere in this work. The authors note this applies to marginal bifurcations generally, not uniquely to the Hopf case. Their derivation is for a supercritical Hopf normal form, whereas our network operates near a Bogdanov–Takens point, where the relevant line is a subcritical Hopf bifurcation terminating on a saddle-node of limit cycles. The corresponding quintic-stabilized normal form, dr/dt = ar + br 3 − r5

(b > 0), gives dX/dt = 2 aX + 2 bX 2 − 2X3. For excursions well below the unstable limit-cycle 42 amplitude, the cubic and quintic terms are subleading and short avalanches should inherit the same relation, αt → 3/2 − 2h as a → 0−. Longer avalanches are instead governed by the nearby saddle-node fold where the stable and unstable limit-cycle branches annihilate and are expected to cross over to a second regime with αt → 2 for generic (uniform) reinjection, or to be exponentially suppressed if the network is not tuned close enough to the fold. The 3 /2 asymptote is not specific to the Bessel construction: a purely diffusive escape with no deterministic drift, dr = √2D dW , gives the classical inverse-Gaussian first-passage density

Cross Corelation in Spatial Domain: Spontaneous Replay of Spatially Disjoint Sequences in a Balanced Network 

In a spatially extended excitatory–inhibitory (E–I) network, the spatial structure of neuronal activity can be characterized by correlations between neurons, either in their spike trains or in the total synaptic input they receive. These correlations reveal how collective activity propagates across the network and how it is shaped by network interactions. At the critical point , corresponding to the onset of bursting or avalanche-like activity, spatial correlations decay slowly with distance and are often well described by a power law, 

C(r) ∼ r−κ, (76) reflecting long-range coordination of neuronal activity (Fig. A.4-c). The correlation length becomes large, indicating that fluctuations are dominated by collective network dynamics rather than by local connectivity alone. In the middle and right panels of Fig. A.4-c, one can see that increasing the spatial extent of the interaction kernel further enhances these correlations, effectively promoting coordinated activity across spatially disjoint neuronal assemblies. In contrast, in the asynchronous regime away from the critical point, correlations decay rapidly with distance, typically approximately exponentially, 

Cspk (r; L) ∼ e−r/ξ , (77) as shown in Fig. A.4-d. Here, activity is largely uncorrelated beyond the local neighborhood, and the network exhibits only short-range coherence.

47 A.6 Effect of Different Spontaneous Firing Regimes on Behavioral Performance 

To test how the spontaneous firing regime affects the behavioral benefit of replay, we started from 15 non-optimal trajectories learned during exploration and measured the change in trial time after a replay period in each of three regimes (Fig. A.6a). The balanced regime produced the largest improvement, reducing trial time by 15% on average (Fig. A.6b). Both imbalanced regimes led to much smaller improvements, but for different reasons. In the hyperexcitable regime, weak inhibition leads to widespread over-activation during replay. Many place cells are co-active at once, so plasticity strengthens associations between locations and actions that are not causally related. These excessive, incorrect associations overwrite parts of the useful policy, resulting in only a small mean improvement (5%) and a high variability across trajectories (variance 10) due to the spurious associations. In the overinhibited regime, strong inhibition confines excitation to local neighbourhoods. Replay events cannot propagate across the environment, so no long-range associations between distant locations are formed and the policy is updated only locally. This yielded the smallest improvement (3%) and the lowest variability (variance 2): the behavior changes consistently, but very little. Together, these results indicate that a balanced spontaneous firing regime is needed for replay to improve behavior. It provides enough excitation for replay to link distant locations, and enough inhibition to keep the resulting associations selective.
