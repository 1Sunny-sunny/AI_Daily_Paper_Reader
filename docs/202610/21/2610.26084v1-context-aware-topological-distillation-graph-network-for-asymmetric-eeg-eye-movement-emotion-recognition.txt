# Context-Aware Topological Distillation Graph Network for Asymmetric EEG–Eye Movement Emotion Recognition 

Peijun Shi ∗, Xiujin Zhu ∗, Zhenguo Lao, Chee-Onn Chow, Senior Member, IEEE , Wei Ru Wong, Zhen Liu, and Xiaobing Jiang 

Abstract —EEG–eye movement emotion recognition can support mental-health monitoring and adaptive interfaces, but most fusion models treat the two signals as parallel features, classify each window alone and degrade when eye data are lost. We propose the Context-Aware Topological Distillation Graph Network (CTD-GNet), in which EEG features form the nodes of an electrode graph, and eye movements and preceding windows modulate its edges, nodes and readout, the edges through electrode-specific prompts. Bidirectional distillation of the graph, node relations and predictions couples this multimodal teacher with a single-window EEG student, improving the teacher and aligning the two graphs 35-fold. In leave-one-subject-out tests on SEED and SEED-IV against 17 baselines, CTD-GNet has the highest mean accuracy, 84.91% and 76.17%, significantly above 9 and 6 of 13 eye-based baselines after Holm correction, the smallest cross-subject spread among multimodal models and the highest worst-subject accuracy. It is the most accurate model at every rate of missing eye data on SEED-IV, keeps 66.1% on SEED without eye data, more than any EEG-only model, and stays 9.3 and 11.9 points above all single-window fusion models under eye-movement noise of one standard deviation. On film clips unseen in training, it leads on SEED with 77.1%. 

Index Terms —Electroencephalography, emotion recognition, eye movements, graph neural networks, knowledge distillation, missing modalities, multimodal fusion.

1 INTRODUCTION 

# EMOTION recognition from physiological signals [1] is moving from the laboratory into mental-health moni-toring, adaptive interfaces and driver monitoring. Electroen-cephalography (EEG) measures cortical activity, and eye tracking measures fixations, saccades, blinks and pupil size, which reflect visual attention and arousal [2]. On SEED [3] and SEED-IV [4], combining the two signals outperforms either alone [4]–[6]. Three problems keep this gain from reaching practice. First, fusion models treat the two signals alike. Concate-nation, bimodal autoencoders [7], canonical correlation [6], tensor and low-rank fusion [8], [9] and cross-modal at-tention [10] encode each signal as a vector and merge the vectors. Yet eye movements indicate where attention is directed, and attention changes which cortical regions exchange information [11]: eye movements describe how regions interact, whereas EEG describes what each region does. Merging the two as parallel vectors discards this difference. Second, emotions induced by a film clip unfold over tens of seconds [12], but most models classify each window

Missing-modality training [15] and reconstruction [16] let a fused model tolerate absent inputs, but the eye data still enter the fused representation directly, and multi-window models need a buffer of past windows on the device. The three problems share one cause: eye movements and temporal context are treated as additional features rather than as signals that shape cortical interaction. We therefore build an electrode graph from EEG and let the eye movements and the context modulate its edges, nodes and readout. This design addresses the three problems together. The two signals take different roles; the preceding windows change the graph of the current window; and because the EEG evidence enters every graph computation, missing or corrupted eye data weaken the prediction instead of corrupting it. The effect of eye movements and context on connectivity is concentrated in the adjacency matrix, which a model that sees only EEG can learn to approximate. We propose the Context-Aware Topological Distillation Graph Network (CTD-GNet), shown in Fig. 1. Its teacher computes the edges by an attention over the 62 electrodes, with a recurrent context of the preceding windows in the queries and the eye movements in the keys; a gated node pathway and a gated multimodal unit [17] add the eye movements to the nodes and the readout. Because eye movements and past windows are available during training but not always at deployment, we treat them as privileged information [18], [19] and train an EEG-only student that sees only the current window. The two networks are trained jointly by bidirectional distillation of the graph, the node relations and the predictions. The main contributions of this work are as follows.  

> •

We propose an asymmetric fusion paradigm in which EEG forms the nodes of an electrode graph and eye movements and temporal context modulate its edges, nodes and readout, so that the EEG evi-dence enters every computation; the resulting model has the highest worst-subject accuracy of all models, 74.02% on SEED.  

> •

We introduce bidirectional distillation between the multimodal teacher and a single-window EEG stu-dent at the levels of the graph, the node relations and the predictions, which aligns their graphs 35-fold, so that both networks can be read on one connectivity, and also improves the teacher.  

> •

We design and evaluate the framework for unreliable sensors, with missing and noisy eye data and film clips unseen in training; with 80% of the eye data missing on SEED-IV, the teacher is 4.1 points more accurate than the strongest baseline. The code will be released at https://github.com/ Peijun1122/CTD-GNet. The remainder of this paper is orga-nized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 presents CTD-GNet. Section 4 describes the experimental setup and results. Section 5 discusses the findings, limita-tions and future work, and Section 6 concludes the paper. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 EEG-based emotion recognition 

Differential entropy (DE) features [20] are standard on SEED and SEED-IV, and Zheng and Lu [3] identified the β and γ

bands and the lateral temporal electrodes as the most dis-criminative. Convolutional models [21] learn from raw sig-nals or feature maps, and hemispheric models [22], [23] use the difference between the hemispheres. Cross-subject mod-els align the features of different subjects, for example by adversarial training [22] or with class prototypes [24]. Graph models treat electrodes as nodes: DGCNN [25] learns one adjacency matrix, RGNN [26] initializes it from electrode distances and hemispheric pairs, and instance-adaptive and self-organized graphs [27], [28] compute it from the EEG of each sample, as graph attention [29] computes edge weights from node features. In all of them, the graph depends on EEG only. 

2.2 Multimodal fusion and temporal context 

EEG and eye movements have been fused at feature and de-cision level [5], by a bimodal deep autoencoder (BDAE) [7], a bimodal LSTM [14], and deep canonical correlation anal-ysis (DCCA) [30] with its attention variant DCCA-AM [6]. Recent models let one modality guide the encoding of the other [31], align the two modalities across subjects [32]– [34] or adapt the fused features to the unlabeled data of a new subject [35]. General multimodal models use tensor products [8], low-rank factors [9], invariant and specific sub-spaces [36], gated units [17] or cross-modal attention [10]. Most of these models fuse or align feature vectors of the two signals, so the eye movements do not shape the rela-tions between electrodes, and a corrupted eye input alters the fused representation directly. Temporal context enters through smoothing of the features [3], [13] or recurrent [37] and temporal convolutional [38] networks over the window sequence; it changes the features or the output, but not the graph. 

2.3 Distillation, privileged information and missing modalities 

Distillation transfers outputs [39], features [40], sample re-lations [41], [42] or the local structure of graph embed-dings [43], and deep mutual learning [44] distills between peers in both directions; across modalities, a visual teacher has been distilled into an EEG student for continuous emotion recognition [45]. Learning using privileged infor-mation [18] and generalized distillation [19] exploit data available only in training. Missing-modality methods drop modalities during training [15], reconstruct them [16], [46], model their shared and specific features [47] or learn a prompt for each pattern of missing inputs [48], so that one fused model tolerates absent inputs; when the eye data are present, they still enter the fused features di-rectly. Prompts [49] and FiLM [50] condition networks with learned or input-dependent vectors, an idea that CTD-GNet applies to the edges of a graph. None of these methods transfers the graph through which a privileged modality acts, or couples the teacher to that graph in the opposite direction.

The distillation losses update both networks: the student learns the graph, relations and predictions of the teacher, and the teacher receives the gradients of the same losses. Deep mutual learning [44] couples peers at the output only; here the coupling also acts on the graph through which the privileged information enters, so that the two networks can be read on one connectivity. The validation protocol selects between this bidirectional form and a one-directional variant with sg( ·) on all teacher terms. Training is a single stage. At deployment, systems with an eye tracker use the teacher. The tracker’s validity check marks each window; windows without valid data, including those in the TCB history, enter with their eye features set to the training mean. Systems without an eye tracker can run the teacher in the same way, with all eye features at the training mean, or the student, which needs a quarter of the computation and no buffer of past windows. For comparison, Section 4.4 also evaluates a dual-mode variant that uses the student for the windows without valid eye data. 

4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 Experimental setup 

SEED [3] and SEED-IV [4] provide synchronized 62-channel EEG (ESI NeuroScan) and eye movements (SMI glasses) recorded during emotional film clips. SEED has 15 subjects with three sessions of the same 15 clips, labeled positive, neutral or negative; eye movements exist for 12 subjects (1– 5 and 8–14), on which all methods are evaluated. SEED-IV has 15 subjects with three sessions of 24 different clips each, labeled happy, sad, fear or neutral. The official eye-movement features cover pupil diameter, dispersion, fixa-tions, saccades, blinks and events ( de = 33 on SEED, 31 on SEED-IV). DE features [20] are computed from the 200-Hz EEG on 4-s non-overlapping windows in the δ, θ, α, β and γ bands (2526 windows per subject on SEED, 2505 on SEED-IV). No smoothing across windows is applied, so on the EEG side the context bank is the only source of temporal informa-tion. The official eye-movement features, computed on the same windows and smoothed by the providers, are used identically by all methods. Because EEG and eye-movement features shift between subjects and sessions, all methods use a session-wise calibration: each feature is standardized with the mean and standard deviation of its recording session, computed over all windows of the session without labels. The calibration is transductive, since it uses the unlabeled windows of the test session, and it is applied identically to every method. Published cross-subject accuracies on these datasets, of-ten above 85% for EEG with eye movements [32], [35], are obtained under protocols that differ in feature smoothing, session splits, domain adaptation to the test subject and model selection. Under a strict protocol with selection on validation subjects, the benchmarks LibEER [54] and LibE-MER [55] report far lower accuracies, for example 56.89% for the best EEG–eye model on SEED in LibEMER. re-implement all baselines under one protocol instead of quoting published numbers. All experiments use leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation with 12 folds on SEED and 15 on SEED-IV. In each fold, two training subjects form the validation set for early stopping and all selection; the labels of the test subject are never used. For CTD-GNet, K, τ and the loss weights are selected by the mean validation macro-F1 over the folds in a base configuration, which uses a temporal convolutional network (TCN) as context encoder and one-directional distillation; the context encoder (GRU or TCN) and the direction of distillation are then selected in the same way. The selected final configuration uses a GRU and bidirectional distillation. Each fold is run with three seeds; we report the mean and standard deviation over folds and test differences with the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-fold accuracies, with Holm correction over the baselines of each comparison [56] at the 0.05 level. All 17 baselines are re-implemented with the same folds, features, calibration and validation subjects. The EEG-only baselines are an SVM with an RBF kernel on the 310-dimensional DE vector, EEGNet [21] on raw 4-s segments, and DGCNN [25] and RGNN [26] without domain adapta-tion; the eye-only baselines are an MLP and a random forest. The fusion baselines are feature concatenation, BDAE [4], [7], DCCA [6], [30], DCCA-AM [6], TFN [8], LMF [9], MISA [36], cross-modal attention [10] over 62 electrode tokens and one eye token, and Bimodal-LSTM [14] with the same K = 5 preceding windows as the teacher. The missing-modality baselines are cross-attention with Mod-Drop [15] at a drop rate of 0.5 and MMIN [16]. For EEG-only inference, the CTD-GNet student is also trained with vanilla KD [39], FitNets [40], RKD [41], SP [42] and LSP [43]. On the validation subjects, the SVM [57] selects C ∈ { 0.1, 1, 10 }

and the random forest [58] of 500 trees its maximum depth from {unlimited, 10, 20 }, and each distillation baseline its loss weight from {0.1, 0.5, 1, 2}. The neural baselines follow the architectures of their original papers and share the optimizer, schedule, early stopping and validation subjects with CTD-GNet. The search of CTD-GNet covers K, τ and three loss weights, over which its validation accuracy varies by less than half a point, as Section 4.3 shows. dk = 32 , ds = 32 and dc = dr = 64 ; the student has two layers of width 32 with dk = 16 , and both use a dropout rate of 0.3. 4.2 Comparison with existing methods 

Table 1 reports the results with complete eye data. The CTD-GNet teacher has the highest mean accuracy on both datasets, 84.91% on SEED and 76.17% on SEED-IV, and the highest macro-F1. With Holm correction for multiple comparisons, it is significantly more accurate than 9 of the 13 eye-only, fusion and missing-modality baselines on SEED and 6 of them on SEED-IV, while its margin over Bimodal-LSTM and MMIN is not significant. Its advantage is largest where the baselines are weakest: on its most difficult test subject it reaches 74.02% on SEED and 54.81% on SEED-IV, SUBMITTED TO IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AFFECTIVE COMPUTING 8

5.8 and 2.7 points above the worst-subject accuracy of any baseline, and it has the smallest standard deviation across subjects among multimodal models, 5.60 and 7.68 points. Fig. 5 shows where this consistency comes from. On SEED, the teacher has the highest lower quartile and the highest worst-subject accuracy of all models; on SEED-IV, its two most difficult test subjects reach 54.8% and 66.6%, against 52.1% and 58.7% for Bimodal-LSTM and 51.5% and 58.5% for MMIN. Table 2 gives the F1 score of each class: the teacher is the best model for all three emotions on SEED and for neutral and happy on SEED-IV. The strongest baseline, Bimodal-LSTM, also reads the preceding windows, which points to the value of temporal context; CTD-GNet uses this context to shape the electrode graph and is more consistent, with a worst-subject accuracy 6.9 and 2.7 points higher. The teacher exceeds the eye-only MLP by 9.8 and 4.7 points, the largest multimodal gain of all models, so the graph extracts EEG information that complements the eye movements. The student, which sees only the current EEG window, reaches 64.10% on SEED and 42.04% on SEED-IV, on par with DGCNN, and needs neither an eye tracker nor a window buffer. Table 3 compares predictions without eye movements; each distillation baseline is listed with the loss weight se-lected on the validation subjects. Topology distillation gives the most accurate distilled student on SEED-IV; its main benefits, the graph alignment and, in the bidirectional form, the gain of the teacher, are analyzed in Sections 4.3 and 4.5. The student outperforms ModDrop and, unlike MMIN, needs no reconstruction network. Without its eye input, Bimodal-LSTM, the strongest baseline with complete data, falls below every student, to 61.04% on SEED and 37.22% on SEED-IV, whereas the CTD-GNet teacher, whose graph and context are computed from EEG, keeps 66.10% and 43.77%, the highest accuracy in Table 3, although its macro-F1 on SEED-IV is below that of all students and MMIN. A device without an eye tracker can therefore run the teacher with mean eye features or, at a quarter of the computation and without a buffer of past windows, the student. Table 4 compares the computational cost. The student needs 24% of the teacher’s FLOPs and processes a window in 0.21 ms on one CPU core, three times faster than the teacher. The teacher has 59% of the parameters of Bimodal-LSTM, 39% of those of MMIN and 39% of the FLOPs of cross-attention, and all models run in under 1 ms per window, far within the 4-s update interval. A recognizer in daily use meets new people and new stimuli at the same time. Because every subject of SEED and SEED-IV watches the same film clips [62], and the pupil responds to the luminance of a clip as well as to arousal [2], we also evaluate a stimulus-disjoint protocol that tests both kinds of generalization together. In each fold, one clip per emotion, on SEED-IV one per emotion and session, is removed from all training and validation subjects, and the test subject is evaluated on these clips only; the held-out clips rotate over the folds. Table 5 reports the accuracy on these unseen clips. On SEED, the CTD-GNet teacher is the most accurate model, 77.1%, and its lead widens under this protocol: over Bimodal-LSTM from 1.2 points in Table 1 to 4.5 points, and over MMIN from 2.3 to 5.9 points. With Holm correction, it is significantly more accurate than the eye-only MLP and DGCNN, p = 0 .017 for both, whereas its lead over the fusion models is not significant, p ≥ 0.19 .On SEED-IV, it matches the best model, Bimodal-LSTM, with 62.1% against 62.3%, and is 3.2 points above MMIN. All fusion models stay far above EEG alone, by about 20 points on SEED-IV, so the eye movements carry emotion information that transfers to new stimuli. 

4.3 Ablation study 

The upper part of Table 6 varies the final configuration. Replacing the GRU by a TCN, making the distillation one-directional or removing it lowers the teacher on both datasets, by 1.0 to 1.2 points on SEED, with uncorrected p

between 0.027 and 0.044, and by 0.3 to 0.5 points on SEED-IV. One-directional distillation matches no distillation, be-cause the stop-gradient isolates the teacher. Coupling only the predictions in both directions gives 84.57% and 76.25%, close to the full objective, whereas coupling only the graphs leaves the teacher at the level without distillation: the gain of the teacher is consistent with mutual learning through the predictions [44], and the topology coupling provides the 35-fold graph alignment of Section 4.5, which is its direct target. The eye movements can reach the teacher through either route. With the eye movements only in the graph, through the edge prompts, the node pathway and the context bank, or only in the readout, the teacher stays within 0.5 points of the full model on both datasets. Modulating the EEG graph thus carries the information of the eye movements without any eye features in the readout, which is the premise of CTD-GNet. The lower part removes components from the base con-figuration, and every removal lowers the accuracy on both datasets. The largest effects come from the components that make the graph change with every window: without the temporal context the teacher loses 1.1 and 1.9 points, a static adjacency shared by all windows, as in DGCNN, costs 1.7 and 1.4 points, and removing both prompts costs 0.5 and 2.0 points, so the graph should follow both the current window and its history. Mixup and weight averaging add 0.1 to 0.8 points. Fig. 6 shows that on SEED the accuracy rises during the first minutes of a trial, for the teacher as for the student, as the induced emotion builds up. The hyperparameters have small effects, as Fig. 7 shows: over histories of three to nine windows, over τ and over all 27 combinations of the loss weights, the validation accuracy varies by less than half a point, so CTD-GNet needs no careful tuning.

5 DISCUSSION 

5.1 Eye movements as modulators of cortical connec-tivity 

CTD-GNet tests the hypothesis that the emotion information in eye movements and temporal context can be expressed as a modulation of how cortical regions interact [11]. The results are consistent with it. The components that make the graph depend on the window and its context have the largest ablation effects, and the teacher keeps its accuracy when the eye movements only modulate the EEG graph and never enter the readout, so modulating the graph is a sufficient route for the eye information. The eye movements shift the attention of the graph from the temporal electrodes toward frontal electrodes for every emotion and toward occipital electrodes in six of seven classes, which may reflect the role of these regions in eye movements and vision. The model that keeps EEG as the substrate is the most consistent across subjects, has the highest mean accuracy, degrades gracefully without eye data and is the most accurate on film clips unseen in training on SEED. Eye movements are the stronger single modality on both datasets, yet this model gains the most over eye-only recognition: the role of a signal in a model need not follow its standalone accuracy. 5.2 What bidirectional distillation provides 

Learning using privileged information usually passes out-puts or features from teacher to student [19]. CTD-GNet couples the two networks in both directions, at the predic-tions, the node relations and the adjacency matrix, and the coupling serves two purposes. It improves the teacher: one-directional distillation leaves the teacher at the level without distillation, whereas the bidirectional form raises it by 1.2 points on SEED, and coupling the predictions alone gives most of this gain, in line with deep mutual learning [44], where networks trained together improve each other. The coupling of the graphs reduces the teacher–student graph divergence 35-fold, so that the EEG-only student, which runs without an eye tracker, approximates the graph of the multimodal teacher and both networks can be read on one graph. 5.4 Limitations and future work 

The evaluation covers two film-clip datasets from the same laboratory, with the officially smoothed eye-movement fea-tures and a session-wise calibration that uses the unlabeled windows of the test session, which an online system would replace by a calibration recording or running statistics. On film clips unseen in training, all models that use eye movements lose 10 to 14 points, against 1.6 to 3.2 points for EEG alone, so part of the eye-movement accuracy is specific to the clips. The margins over Bimodal-LSTM and MMIN are not significant, the student gains little accuracy from distillation, and the frontal gain in Table 9 may partly reflect electro-oculographic (EOG) activity, for which we ran no artifact-removal control. Future work will extend CTD-GNet to further datasets with more stimuli per emotion, to recordings outside the laboratory, to raw gaze features and to EOG-corrected EEG, and will give the student a longer EEG context. 

6 CONCLUSION 

We presented CTD-GNet, in which EEG forms the nodes of an electrode graph and eye movements and temporal context modulate its edges, nodes and readout. Coupling the multimodal teacher with a single-window EEG student in both directions improves the teacher and aligns the two graphs 35-fold, so that the EEG-only student approximates the teacher graph. Against 17 baselines on SEED and SEED-IV, CTD-GNet achieves the highest mean accuracy, 84.91% and 76.17%, with the smallest spread across subjects among multimodal models and the highest worst-subject accuracy, 74.02% and 54.81%. It is the most accurate model at every rate of missing eye data on SEED-IV, keeps 66.1% on SEED without eye data, more than any EEG-only model, and is the most accurate on film clips unseen in training on SEED. Assigning each signal its physiological role thus yields multimodal recognizers that stay consistent across users and robust to sensor failure.
