# ABSTRACT 

Encoding models that predict brain activation responses to various stimuli, includ-ing images, have traditionally been trained to maximize accuracy when predicting the observed brain data. However, in doing so, their internal representations may not reflect individual human perception. For example, two images that have very different predicted brain responses may be perceptually similar to a specific per-son. In this work, we investigate whether we can fine-tune brain encoding models to better align their representations with specific individuals’ similarity judgments. This improved perceptual alignment may be useful in downstream tasks using the brain encoding model, particularly decoding of seen or imagined images. Us-ing measured similarity judgments of 100 images from the 8 participants in the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), we adapt a generic human similarity judgment model (DreamSim) separately for each NSD participant to obtain their personal-ized perceptual similarity models. These personalized perceptual models are then used to obtain subject-specific similarity structure across the images in their en-coding model training set. We fine-tune each participant’s baseline encoder in two separate conditions, aligning its representations with either the generic or the personalized similarity judgments, with the same initialization and objective. We find that baseline brain encoders reflect within-subject perceptual similarity more strongly than measured fMRI responses and strongly preserve intersubject corre-lations present in measured fMRI, but fail to reflect the intersubject correlations of human perceptual similarity judgments. Fine-tuning brain encoding models with generic, population-level perceptual similarity information results in even better alignment with human perceptual judgments compared to baseline encoders, but these models still fail to capture intersubject correlation of human perceptual judg-ments. However, fine-tuning brain encoding models with personalized perceptual similarity information results in models that are overall the most aligned with hu-man perceptual similarity judgments out of all the brain encoders, and also reflect intersubject correlation of human perceptual similarity judgments. Our framework provides a robust approach for bootstrapping human similarity judgment data into creating more individualized, human-aligned brain encoding models of vision. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Visual brain encoding models aiming to predict brain responses elicited by images and video clips have become increasingly accurate as new vision models have been developed in adjacent fields. These visual brain encoding models are typically optimized to accurately predict measured brain activity (Mitchell et al., 2008; Huth et al., 2016; Gu et al., 2022; Gifford et al., 2025; Conwell et al., 2024; Naselaris et al., 2011). However, a visual brain encoding model that accurately predicts measured brain activity may not necessarily have internal representations that reflect an individ-1arXiv:2610.22569v1 [q-bio.NC] 15 Oct 2026 Figure 1: Overview of the human image similarity judgment-brain encoding model alignment frame-work. (b) A generic model of human perception (here called Population DreamSim) was adapted to each partici-pant’s observed similarity judgments to create perceptual similarity models that reflect that person’s judgments (here called personalized DreamSim). Such alignment of brain activity models and human perceptual similarity would be particularly useful for downstream tasks - for example decoding, i.e. reconstructing seen or imagined stimuli from brain activity patterns. In decoding, having a model whose brain activity representations for any two images reflect those images’ perceived similarity would allow reconstructing images with better fidelity to human similarity judgments (Scotti et al., 2023; Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Rajabi et al., 2025; Takagi & Nishimoto, 2023). How a person perceives the similarity of two images, or human perceptual similarity, is a popular area of study in the broader field of psychophysics. Behavioral similarity experiments in these areas ask individuals to organize stimuli according to their perceived relationships. Representational sim-ilarity analysis (RSA) is a statistical approach that provides a framework for comparing similarity structures across various , multi-modal spaces (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008; Sucholutsky et al., 2024; Nili et al., 2014; Kriegeskorte & Kievit, 2013; Simkova et al., 2026). RSA can be applied in this con-text to perceptual similarity, measured brain activity via fMRI, and encoding model predictions of brain activity to enable comparison of how image similarity spaces are conserved across the modal-ities. Analysis of these representational spaces can also allow quantification of either the similarity of representations across modalities within an individual or how the across-subject correlations of similarity spaces are preserved. A brain encoding model trained on individual participant data may be subject-specific in the sense that it predicts that participant’s neural activity in an identifiable way (compared to other subjects’ data), while still failing to reflect that person’s perceptual relationships between stimuli. Incorporating human similarity judgments into brain encoding models has several practical chal-lenges. Human similarity measurements are onerous to collect for the thousands of image stimuli usually contained in high-quality vision neuroscience fMRI datasets (Allen et al., 2022; Zerbe et al., 2026; Hebart et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2019). Therefore, when they are collected, it is only for a small subset of the images drawn from the stimuli set. Machine learning models have been used to estimate human similarity judgments, but are normally trained on data drawn across a cohort of many individuals and thus represent population-level averages rather than individual-level percep-tual judgments (Marjieh et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2023; Muttenthaler et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2018). These limitations motivate us to test if a small amount of individual behavioral data can be used to personalize a scalable perceptual model, and, subsequently, can the resulting estimated perceptual 2similarities be used to further personalize a brain encoder to increase alignment with that individual human’s perception? We investigate this question using the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), which contains fMRI re-sponses to tens of thousands of images from each of 8 participants, including a set of 1000 images that were seen by all participants - usually called the “shared 1000” (Allen et al., 2022). Similar-ity judgments were collected from these 8 participants for a subset of 100 images drawn from the shared 1000 set. We begin by adapting DreamSim separately for each participant using a feature-reweighting function (Fu et al., 2023) based on their similarity judgments, resulting in a Personal-ized DreamSim model for each participant (see Fig. 1a–b). We then apply both the personalized DreamSim model and the population (original) DreamSim to the 9,000 images in each of the 8 NSD participants’ image training sets. We then use these two types of predicted similarities (from personalized or population-level DreamSim) to fine-tune subject-specific Neural Response Function (NRF) brain encoding models (Chen et al., 2026). During fine-tuning, a representational similarity objective encourages the encoder’s predicted voxel-response patterns to reflect either population-level or personalized DreamSim perceptual similarity, while a frozen copy of the original encoder serves as a teacher to constrain changes to its optimized brain activity predictions. This design al-lows us to distinguish the effect of generic population-level perceptual alignment from the effect of individualized perceptual alignment. This work contains a few novel contributions, including • introduction of a framework for bootstrapping small amounts of subject-specific similar-ity judgments to adapt a pretrained, population-level perceptual similarity model to each individual. • introduction of a method to fine-tune brain visual encoding models to increase alignment with both population-level and individual-specific human perception of images. • an assessment of how increasing alignment of brain encoding models with human judg-ments changes their accuracy, and how these aligned models reflect within-subject percep-tion and intersubject correlations in measured brain activity and perception of images. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Visual brain encoding models and personalization. Visual brain encoding models aim to accu-rately predict neural responses elicited by visual stimuli. (Naselaris et al., 2015; G¨ uc ¸l¨ u & Gerven, 2015; Kay et al., 2008; Adeli et al., 2026; St-Yves & Naselaris, 2018) Response-optimized encod-ing models have also been used to compare brain activity representations to human ratings of visual similarity (Khosla et al., 2022). Personalized visual encoding models for a specific individual can preserve inter-individual variability while reducing the amount of subject-specific data required for accurate prediction (Gu et al., 2022). These approaches personalize brain encoders according to fMRI-based measures of brain activity responses. Our work asks a complementary question: can a brain encoder already tuned to an individual’s brain responses also be aligned with that same individual’s perceptual similarity structure? 

Human similarity judgment models and personalization. Human perceptual similarity judg-ments and measured brain responses may not possess the same representational structure for identi-cal visual stimuli (King et al., 2019), and brain representations can contain stable individual-specific structure related to differences in perceived similarity (Charest et al., 2014). The work Brotherwood et al. (2022) modeled subject-specific perceptual structure using the same NSD dataset and showed that individual similarity structure can be recovered even with a relatively small 100-image task. Our work takes this a step further by showing that observed subject-specific similarity judgments are correlated with an individuals’ brain activity and encoder, and, further, these observed judgments can be used to finetune a personalized both a perceptual judgment model and a brain encoder. 

Human perception aligned computer vision models. Progress has been made in aligning com-puter vision model representations with human perception. ML models designed to learn a percep-tual similarity and reflect human similarity judgments continue to improve as vision models improve (Fu et al., 2023; Muttenthaler et al., 2025; Roads & Love, 2021; Peterson et al., 2018). Pretrained 3visual representations can also be transformed to improve their correspondence with (population-level) human similarity judgments (Attarian et al., 2021; Muttenthaler et al., 2023), and modern computer vision models can be fine-tuned using perceptual similarity supervision (Sundaram et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). Because collecting human similarity judgments is costly, discovering ways to use a limited number of human judgments is fundamental for making progress in this field. (Marjieh et al., 2023; Sucholutsky et al., 2023). Muttenthaler et al. (2025) uses limited (population-level) human similarity judgments to construct human-aligned supervision that can be generated over much larger image sets and transferred into vision foundation models. Our framework applies this general principle of alignment of human perception for the first time to brain encoders, doing so at an individual subject level rather than using population-level perceptual data. 

# 3 METHODS 

3.1 DATA , H UMAN SIMILARITY JUDGMENTS , AND BASELINE BRAIN ENCODERS 

In this paper we used the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) (Allen et al., 2022) that contains whole-brain 7T fMRI measurements of activity responses in 8 individuals while viewing tens of thousands of natural scene images drawn from MS COCO (Lin et al., 2015). FMRI data was processed and brain responses were estimated using GLMsingle (Prince et al., 2022), normalized within each session, and averaged across repeated presentations of the same image. Each subject viewed approximately 9,000 unique images in addition to 1,000 images shared across every subject. We apply the official nsdgeneral region-of-interest (ROI) mask to this data to work only with the voxels found in the 7 lower-level and 12 higher-level visual cortical regions common to all participants. Individual perceptual similarity judgments were obtained from participants using the NSD-Meadows multiple-arrangement task (Kriegeskorte & Mur, 2012). In this task, the 8 NSD participants evalu-ated the same set of 100 images, drawn from the shared 1,000 images. They were asked to arrange the images inside a circle, placing more similar images nearer to one another; they were given 45 minutes to complete the task. In the end, the distance between the circle positions was calculated to obtain image-pair distances, yielding a 100 × 100 matrix. We negated the distance in order to have a metric that represented human similarity, where larger values indicate greater perceived sim-ilarity. We use pretrained subject-specific Neural Response Function (NRF) models as the baseline brain encoding models. An NRF model was trained for each individual in their unique 9K image response pairs to predict voxel-wise fMRI responses jointly from visual features and voxel loca-tion (Chen et al., 2026). All subsequent encoding model fine-tuning initialize training with these individual-specific baseline brain encoding models. 3.2 PERSONALIZED HUMAN SIMILARITY JUDGMENT MODELS 

Measured human similarity judgments are available for only 100 images per participant, while each brain encoder is trained on approximately 9,000 images. If we are to fine-tune our encoding models to better align them with an individual’s similarity judgments, it would be better if we had similarity judgments for all 9,000 training images. As a result, we adapt DreamSim separately to each par-ticipant in order to obtain subject specific similarity predictions for all pairs of images in the entire 9,000 unique image set. We begin with the pretrained generic (population-level) DreamSim model, which for each image produces a 1,792-dimensional image embedding vector in a space that was optimized to reproduce population-level human similarity judgments, i.e. vectors closer to one an-other in this space are perceived as more similar by people. During personalization, the DreamSim model remains frozen, but for each k subject we learn a feature-reweighting vector wk ∈ R1792 to optimize the match to that particular person’s similarity judgments. For each participant and representational source (measured fMRI brain responses, human similarity judgments, or brain encoding models), we construct a representational similarity matrix (RSM), where larger values indicate greater image similarity. Brain-encoder and measured fMRI RSMs are defined using Pearson correlation between predicted or measured voxel-response patterns, calculated using the mask of 19 visual ROIs present across all 8 individuals in the NSD. A view of the 19 ROIs used is provided in Appendix Fig. 6. DreamSim RSMs use cosine similarity, and human RSMs use the negated pairwise dissimilarity estimates derived from the NSD-Meadows Multiple Arrangements Task. Within-subject human similarity alignment is measured as Spearman correlation between a data- or model-derived RSM and the corresponding participant’s human similarity judgment RSM. Paired model comparisons of within-subject human similarity alignment use two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. To quantify intersubject structure, we compute Spearman correlation between RSMs of each pair of participants across different modalities, yielding an 8 × 8 intersubject similarity matrix 5for each representational source that reflects how similar two people’s RSMs are across modali-ties. Within-modality, across subject similarities were calculated, and their intersubject correlational spaces compared across modalities using Spearman rank correlation to test if across-subject relation-ships in RSMs were preserved across modalities. Correlations between intersubject similarity ma-trices across various modality pairs are evaluated using a Mantel-style subject-label permutation test with 10,000 permutations to account for dependence among the 28 subject pairs. Fold-dependent quantities are averaged across the 10 cross-validation repeats before statistical testing. 

# 4 RESULTS 

4.1 WITHIN AND ACROSS SUBJECT COMPARISONS OF MULTI -MODAL REPRESENTATIONAL SIMILARITY MATRICES 

We begin by computing the 8 individuals’ representational similarity matrices for 1) human simi-larity judgments, 2) measured fMRI brain responses, and 3) baseline brain encoders, using the 100 images contained in the NSD’s perceptual similarity task. We wanted to test if images that were rated as more similar by a specific individual had more similar measured or predicted brain activity. To do this, we correlated via Spearman the RSMs for human judgment with both the i) measured fMRI and ii) baseline brain encoder. Fig. 2a shows the 8 subjects’ correlations of these RSMs. In all individuals, both the correlations between the RSMs for human judgment and measured fMRI brain activity were significant. However, interestingly, the baseline brain encoding model had sig-nificantly better alignment ( W = 0 , p = 0 .008 ) with human similarity judgments ( ρ = 0 .198 , all 8 subjects p < 0.001 ) compared to the measured fMRI brain response alignment ( ρ = 0 .146 , all 8 subjects p ≤ 0.018 ). We posit that this may be due to the boost in SNR that the encoding model has over the raw fMRI data.

Intersubject baseline encoder structure strongly matches measured fMRI (ρ = 0 .82 , p = 0 .008 ), but neither measured fMRI nor the baseline encoder matches behavioral intersubject similarity judgment structure ( ρ = −0.19 and −0.03 , both p > 0.05 ). We then test if the intersubject correlations of multi-modal RSMs were preserved across modalities, i.e. if two people had more similar perceptual similarity judgments did they also have more similar measured and/or predicted brain activity. We characterized intersubject structure by correlating the three RSMs (measured fMRI, baseline brain encoder, and human similarity judgments) for each pair of subjects, see Fig. 2b. As expected, we found that the intersubject structure of the Baseline Brain Encoders closely matches the structure of measured fMRI ( ρ = 0 .819 , p = 0 .008 ), see Fig. 2c. In contrast, human similarity judgments’ intersubject structure was not significantly correlated with 6either measured fMRI ( ρ = −0.193 , p = 0 .345 ) or the Baseline Brain Encoders ( ρ = −0.028 , p =0.890 ), see Fig. 2c. Improved within-subject, across image-pair human similarity judgment alignment of encoding mod-els does not mean that these models capture the across subject relationships of the various modal-ities. We therefore asked whether the intersubject organization of each model corresponded to the intersubject structure observed in human similarity judgments. As expected, Personalized DreamSim reproduced the intersubject structure of the human similarity judgments ( ρ = 0 .766 , p < 0.001 ). Brain Encoder + Personalized DreamSim also recovered this structure ( ρ = 0 .508 , p = 0 .002 ). Neither the Baseline Brain Encoder ( ρ = −0.026 ) nor Brain Encoder + Population DreamSim ( ρ = −0.099 ) showed significant correspondence with intersub-ject correlations of human similarity judgments, see Fig. 3c. The personalized-encoder correla-tion was positive across all 10 cross-validation repeats (range across repeats was ρ = 0 .38 –0.57 ), showing that this effect was robust to the assignment of images to cross-validation test folds. The comparison with measured fMRI showed a different pattern. The Baseline Brain Encoder strongly preserved measured-fMRI intersubject human similarity judgment structure ( ρ = 0 .84 , p = 0 .007 )as expected, and this correlation remained high following population-level alignment ( ρ = 0 .77 ,

p = 0 .014 ). After personalized alignment, correlation with measured-fMRI intersubject structure was not significant. These results show a contrast in the effect of population perceptual finetuning vs personalized per-ceptual finetuning on brain encoders. Population DreamSim finetuning reshaped the brain encoder to be better aligned with human similarity judgments overall (compared to the baseline brain encoder) but did not reflect across-subject similarity judgment correlations; further, this model still reflected intersubject correlations of measured fMRI responses. In contrast, Personalized DreamSim finetun-ing reshaped the brain encoder to better reflect within-subject human similarity judgments as well as across-subject similarity judgment correlations, but no longer reflected intersubject similarity of measured fMRI data. 4.4 BRAIN ENCODER ACCURACY VS . Some subjects showed large gains in behavioral alignment with relatively modest changes in neural-prediction accuracy, and oth-ers showed larger reductions in voxel-prediction performance. Overall, however, the relative change in voxel accuracy was modest (mean -9.2% for the personalized encoder, -7.1% for the population one) compared to the gains in human similarity alignment (mean +190% and +119% respectively). # 5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Our results show that baseline brain encoders can be highly subject-specific in their predictions while still failing to reflect how that individual perceives relationships between visual stimuli. Population-level perceptual fine-tuning improved brain encoders’ perceptual alignment, yet still failed to re-cover individually-specific perception. Personalized perceptual models were able to capture both within-subject perceptual judgments and intersubject similarity judgment structure, in turn transfer-ring these properties to the personalized brain encoders. Our approach is limited by DreamSim’s performance as a proxy for human judgment. With con-tinued advances in vision and vision-language foundation models, modernizing DreamSim using newer perceptual representations could provide a stronger population-level similarity baseline. Fu-ture directions for this work can focus on evaluating whether different perceptual models can act as stronger generalizable targets for personalized brain encoder fine-tuning. 9These findings establish a framework for incorporating a small number of human similarity judg-ment measurements into brain encoding models to make them more aligned to an individual’s per-ception. Doing so moves visual brain encoding models toward internal representations that reflect not only how a person’s brain activates in response to an image, but also that person’s perception of image similarity.
