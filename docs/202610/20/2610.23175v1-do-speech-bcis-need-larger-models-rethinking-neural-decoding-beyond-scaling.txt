## Abstract 

Recent speech brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) increasingly rely on large mod-els and complex multi-stage pipelines for neural decoding. However, in speech BCI, where training data are scarce, noisy, and expensive to collect, the bene-fits of model scaling remain unclear. This work revisits a fundamental question: 

are large models truly necessary for accurate neural decoding? We propose 

BEST (Brain-Encoding-Speech-Text), a two-stage framework for efficient neural-to-text decoding. Instead of relying on large generative models or phoneme-centric pipelines, BEST introduces hierarchical intermediate representations and a com-pact speech-oriented decoder that directly maps neural activity to text. This design preserves essential structure while significantly reducing computational cost, en-abling robust generalization in low-resource settings and practical deployment under constrained resources. On the Brain-to-Text ’24 and ’25 benchmarks, BEST 

achieves word error rates (WERs) of 6.19% and 1.62%, respectively, using only 3.85% of the parameters required by comparable methods. Beyond these results, we provide three key insights: (1) decoder scaling yields non-monotonic gains; (2) cross-modal alignment is more critical than model size; and (3) overfitting degards sequence-level performance. Representation analysis further reveals a consistent neural →audio →text alignment pathway.

Speech brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) offer a promising pathway for restoring communication in individuals with severe motor impairments [47, 43]. By directly mapping neural activity to language, these systems bypass damaged neuromuscular pathways and enable brain-to-text communication [7]. Recent advances in neural decoding[52] have substantially improved transcription quality, bringing speech BCIs closer to practical deployment [58, 11, 23]. With the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs)[54, 17, 26, 49], most existing approaches adopt a multi-stage pipeline: neural signals are first decoded into phoneme sequences via supervised classification [23], and then converted into text using LLMs, often with additional rescoring [36, 55]. While effective due to the increasingly strong linguistic priors provided by rapidly evolving LLMs, this paradigm suffers from two fundamental limitations. First, it is poorly aligned with real-world deployment constraints. Practical BCI systems require low-latency and resource-efficient inference [32, 38], yet reliance on large pretrained models and memory-intensive rescoring introduces substantial computational overhead, limiting feasibility on edge or clinical hardware. Second, the pipeline imposes a discrete phoneme bottleneck that discards rich neural information. In data-scarce and noisy BCI settings, collapsing high-dimensional neural signals into hard phoneme labels amplifies overfitting and suppresses fine-grained linguistic structure [11]. Moreover, the strict separation between intermediate prediction and sentence generation prevents effective use of multi-level neural-linguistic representations during decoding. These challenges point to a central requirement: an effective speech BCI must achieve high accuracy while remaining lightweight, data-efficient, and robust under limited supervision. To this end, we propose BEST (Brain-Encoding-Speech-Text), a two-stage framework that replaces stage-wise symbolic pipelines with hierarchical feature-level integration. In Stage 1, multi-level neural-linguistic representations are fused through a lightweight integration module, allowing phonological and lexical information to interact before discrete decoding and mitigating overfitting induced by phoneme-level supervision. In Stage 2, the fused representations are translated by a compact speech-oriented decoder, followed by beam search with a lightweight n-gram language model for efficient hypothesis scoring. This design preserves informative neural structure while introducing linguistic regularization without the computational burden of large-scale reranking. We evaluate BEST on the Brain-to-Text ’24 and ’25 benchmarks, achieving word error rates (WERs) of 6.19% and 1.62%, respectively. Despite using only 3.85% of the parameters of comparable methods, BEST consistently improves decoding performance while remaining suitable for edge-class deployment. Representation analyses further reveal a coherent neural → linguistic → text alignment process underlying the model’s predictions. In summary, our contributions are threefold: 1. We propose BEST, a lightweight and practical framework for speech BCI that achieves strong performance with only 3.7% of the parameters of existing approaches. 2. We identify the discrete phoneme bottleneck and introduce a hierarchical feature-level decoding paradigm that preserves and integrates multi-level neural-linguistic representations. 3. We systematically analyze the role of decoder scale in speech BCI, showing that performance gains depend more on structured alignment than on model size under realistic constraints. 

## 2 Related Work 

Speech brain-computer interfaces. Speech BCIs decode neural activity from auditory and motor cortices into text. Early systems use RNN-based models to map neural signals to intermediate units (e.g., phonemes or characters), followed by statistical language models for text generation [5, 2, 48, 9, 28, 20]. Later work incorporates context-dependent modeling (e.g., diphones) to capture local phonetic transitions [23]. More recent approaches adopt transformer architectures for long-range dependency modeling and streaming or end-to-end decoding, often combined with LLMs [11, 28, 12]. LLMs have recently enabled neural-to-text decoding, semantic reconstruction, and cross-modal alignment[6, 57, 10]. Prior work explores LLM fine-tuning for neural decoding [34, 44, 8, 37] and autoregressive reconstruction from fMRI signals [51]. LLMs have also been used to align neural and linguistic representations [29], model semantic organization [33, 14], and support applications such as spellers and rehabilitation systems [4, 18, 16, 24, 25]. However, current approaches face key limitations: (1) end-to-end LLM decoding ignores structural mismatch between neural signals and linguistic embeddings; (2) large-scale inference incurs high computational and memory cost [58, 42]; and (3) weakly constrained decoding reduces robustness under noisy neural inputs. 

Real-world BCI deployment. Practical BCI systems require efficient, robust, and accurate decoding under resource constraints [32, 38, 21, 53]. Existing lightweight models mainly target motor or intention decoding and do not generalize well to speech [30, 56]. Classical methods (e.g., random forests) are efficient but cannot model the sequential dependencies required for speech [27, 13]. Lightweight neural models face a core trade-off: limited capacity weakens representation learning, while increased complexity leads to overfitting and unstable optimization in low-resource settings [1, 15]. This highlights the need for architectures that balance efficiency with structurally aligned neural-linguistic modeling. 

## 3 Method 

In this section, we present a lightweight speech BCI framework with a hybrid decoding strategy. The overall architecture comprises a hierarchical transformer-based neural encoder, a lightweight phoneme-character fusion block, a speech translator, and a custom language model (Figure 2). The neural encoder is pretrained to map neural signals into both phoneme-level and character-level representations, providing robust linguistic features. At inference time, encoder representations are 3fed into a Whisper-based decoder to generate an n-best list of hypotheses, which are subsequently rescored with a 4-gram KenLM for final selection. 

3.1 Architecture Stage 1: hierarchical transformer encoder. We introduce a hierarchical Transformer encoder [46] tailored for neural speech decoding, combining frequency-aware channel modulation with structured temporal attention. Neural signals are partitioned into temporal patches and embedded as token sequences. To incorporate spectral structure, we apply a multi-frequency channel attention mechanism (Appendix J) that projects inputs onto predefined frequency bases, aggregates channel-wise statistics, and produces adaptive reweighting coefficients, enabling frequency-selective feature refinement. The resulting embeddings are first processed by an iTransformer [31] to capture global dependencies and stabilize latent representations, followed by a hierarchical Transformer with structured attention (Appendix J). Temporal dependencies are regulated through three complementary masking schemes: past-oriented masking for historical context, local bidirectional masking for short-range interactions, and future-oriented masking to incorporate forward cues. These masks are applied sequentially to progressively refine the temporal receptive field, enabling the model to capture localized temporal patterns while maintaining global consistency. The final representations are projected to both phoneme-level and character-level outputs via CTC heads, providing complementary supervision at different linguistic granularities. 

Stage 2: phoneme–character fusion. To integrate multi-level linguistic representations, we design a lightweight multi-scale convolutional fusion module that aggregates features across encoder depths. Phoneme and character streams are processed through parallel convolutional branches with different receptive fields, capturing complementary temporal patterns. Within each stream, residual aggregation enables iterative refinement and cross-level information flow while preserving temporal structure. The fused features are then concatenated and projected into a unified representation through lightweight convolutions. Compared to additional attention layers, this design is more computationally efficient and better suited to low-resource, high-noise BCI settings. Multi-scale convolutions capture both local and longer-range dependencies without the cost of full self-attention. The fusion module also serves as a projection interface, aligning neural representations with the acoustic embedding space and facilitating downstream linguistic decoding. 

Stage 2: lightweight speech translator. To enable efficient neural-to-text mapping without large language models, we adopt a compact speech translation module based on Whisper [40]. The fused representations are projected into the acoustic embedding space and processed by the upper layers of the Whisper encoder, aligning them with pretrained speech representations and introducing implicit acoustic priors without requiring explicit audio input. The transformed embeddings are then decoded autoregressively by the Whisper decoder. The model is trained using next-token prediction with teacher forcing. During inference, text is generated conditioned on neural-derived embeddings, optionally with beam search to improve sequence quality. This design replaces LLM-based decoding with a pretrained speech model, enabling direct neural-to-text transcription with substantially lower computational cost. 

Stage 2: lightweight language model reranking. Neural-derived embeddings are inherently noisy, leading to ambiguous candidate sequences. To improve robustness, we apply lightweight n-gram rescoring using a custom 4-gram KenLM [19]. Compared to standard large-scale n-gram models (e.g., 5-gram models), this approach provides effective linguistic regularization with minimal overhead. It improves disambiguation and transcription accuracy while preserving real-time feasibility for BCI deployment. 

3.2 Training objective Pretraining stage. In contrast to masked reconstruction approaches such as BIT [58], we do not employ masked autoencoding. While masking promotes contextual inference, neural speech decoding depends more on preserving temporal–spectral structure and higher-level phonetic and linguistic information (Appendix G). To improve generalization, we adopt extensive data augmentation and hierarchical pretraining at both phoneme and word levels, enabling the encoder to learn compact 4representations that capture both fine-grained acoustic structure and higher-level linguistic information from neural signals. 

Fine-tuning stage. For sentence-level decoding, we freeze the neural encoder and Whisper encoder to preserve stable representations. Phoneme- and character-level features are injected into the upper layers of the Whisper encoder, while only the first layer of the Whisper decoder and the fusion module are updated. This targeted optimization restricts learning to lightweight components, reducing overfitting and training instability while enabling effective adaptation to neural-derived representations for accurate transcription. 

## 4 Evaluation 

4.1 Dataset 

We evaluate our method on the Brain-to-Text ’24 [48] and ’25 [3] benchmarks for attempted speech decoding. The ’24 dataset contains 12,100 sentences from participant T12 collected over 25 sessions using intracortical microelectrode arrays implanted in speech-related regions (Appendix A). The ’25 dataset includes 10,948 sentences from participant T15 across 45 sessions with 256 electrodes in the speech motor cortex. In both datasets, participants were visually prompted with sentences and asked to attempt speech following a cue, with decoding performed on the corresponding neural activity. The ’24 dataset consists of conversational sentences with attempted vocalized speech at approximately 62 words per minute. In contrast, the ’25 dataset spans more diverse corpora, and custom sets, and includes both attempted vocalized and silent speech. Neural features include threshold crossing spike counts and spike-band power, extracted at 20 ms resolution and normalized across sessions to reduce non-stationarity. 

4.2 Baselines 

We benchmark our method against 4 state-of-the-art baselines in neural speech BCIs: 

1. RNN Baseline [48] employs a 5-layer bidirectional GRU to map neural activity to phonemes. It generates text via a 5-gram language model with OPT rescoring. 

2. Transformer Baseline [11] adopts a causal transformer enhanced with time-masking augmentation to improve generalization. It operates on non-overlapping neural patches with relative positional encoding and supports real-time streaming inference. 

3. DCoND [23] improves phoneme decoding by modeling diphone contexts rather than individual phonemes. It uses a GRU encoder to estimate diphone distributions and leverages an ensemble of decoders with an LLM for correction. 

4. BIT [58] is an end-to-end speech decoding framework built on a cross-species, cross-task pretrained transformer. The encoder is first self-supervised pretrained via masked temporal patch reconstruction, then fine-tuned for phoneme decoding with CTC loss. 

4.3 Results Performance comparison on speech BCI decoding: We evaluate BEST on the Brain-to-Text ’24 and ’25 benchmarks (Table 1), reporting model size, memory consumption, phoneme error rate (PER), character error rate (CER), and WER on both validation and held-out test sets. Following prior work [58], we consider both end-to-end decoding and cascaded LM pipelines. BEST is instantiated in three configurations reflecting different accuracy-efficiency trade-offs: 

1. Lightweight BEST is an end-to-end pipeline without external language models that minimizes computational cost while preserving robutsness; 

2. Practical BEST incorporates a 4-gram LM to balance linguistic regularization and efficiency; 

3. Robust BEST employs a 5-gram LM to maximize accuracy under higher memory budgets. The lightweight configuration consistently outperforms the end-to-end baseline of BIT despite using substantially fewer parameters and eliminating reliance on large pretrained language models, demon-strating the effectiveness of hierarchical feature-level decoding. Incorporating lightweight n-gram LMs further reduces WER, with the practical and robust variants achieving 2.92% and 1.62% WER on the ’25 benchmark, respectively. LLM decoder scaling and adaptation analysis: We systematically study the effect of decoder scale and adaptation strategy in end-to-end speech BCI (Table 2). Across all settings, we find that decoder scaling exhibits a distinctly non-monotonic trend. Small decoders such as Whisper-Tiny [39] and DistilGPT2 [41] are capacity-limited, while larger text-based LLMs fail to deliver consistent gains with increasing scale. Instead, mid-scale decoders such as Qwen2-1.5B [50] achieve the best performance, revealing a non-monotonic scaling trend. This suggests that, in speech BCI, increasing model capacity can amplify overfitting to noisy neural signals rather than improve generalization, making capacity-efficient decoders more effective than maximally scaled models. A second key observation is that pretraining modality is decisive. Audio-oriented models (e.g., Aero1-Audio [22], Whisper [39]) consistently outperform text-only LLMs of similar or larger size, indicating that representation alignment with neural inputs, rather than model scale, dominates decoder performance. Even with additional projection layers for adaptation, text-only LLMs [45, 50] remain mismatched to the distribution of neural features. In contrast, speech-aligned pretraining produces representations inherently compatible with neural-derived embeddings. We further observe a scale-dependent adaptation pattern. Full fine-tuning is consistently suboptimal, reflecting instability under data-limited and high-noise conditions. For compact decoders, partial fine-tuning is sufficient to align representations, whereas larger models favor fully frozen decoding, as pretrained priors are degraded by task-specific adaptation. Decoder dimensionality (768) has a limited effect on performance. The encoder dimension is selected via hyperparameter tuning (Appendix G), as increasing dimensionality exacerbates overfitting in the neural encoding stage. 6Encoder–decoder factorization. To disentangle the contributions of neural representation quality and downstream decoding, we factorize the encoder and decoder choices while keeping the remaining pipeline fixed (Table 3). The BEST encoder consistently outperforms Transformer and GRU encoders under both Whisper-small and Aero, showing that its gain is not specific to a particular downstream decoder. Conversely, Whisper-small consistently outperforms the substantially larger Aero decoder for every encoder, with WER gaps of 0.0308–0.0408. Thus, increasing decoder capacity does not compensate for weak neural representations; both the quality of the neural encoder and its compatibility with the downstream speech decoder determine sequence-level performance. Notably, the decoder gap decreases from 0.0408 with GRU to 0.0308 with BEST, suggesting that better-structured neural representations are also less sensitive to the choice of downstream decoder. These results further support our central finding that representation quality and cross-stage compatibility, rather than decoder scale alone, dominate speech BCI decoding.

Overfitting analysis : The overfit-ting model achieves lower PER and CER but degrades WER (Table 5), exposing a misalignment between lo-cal phoneme accuracy and sequence-level decoding. We attribute to rep-resentation collapse induced by over-fitting. The visualization of out-put probability heatmap (Appendix F) shows that the overfitting model ex-hibits highly concentrated and frag-mented activations, reflecting over-confident and poorly calibrated pre-dictions. These representations lack temporal coherence and fail to cap-ture structured phoneme transitions, resulting in inconsistent sequence re-construction. In contrast, the well-fitting model produces smoother and more distributed activations, preserv-ing coherent neural-linguistic structure. This reveals a fundamental property of speech BCI: overfitting increases lo-cal discriminability while degrading global consistency. Reduced phoneme error does not translate to better de-coding, as the resulting representa-tions become less compatible with downstream inference. This explains why stronger phoneme supervision can harm end-to-end performance and sup-ports the design of BEST in preserv-ing structured, multi-level representa-tions rather than relying on discrete phoneme predictions.

Representation evidence analysis. We provide representation-level evidence for the staged decoding mechanism of BEST (Fig. 3, Appendix B). Self-RSA reveals that character, phoneme, and fusion embeddings all exhibit clear structure, indicating that neural activity is organized into stable linguistic manifolds rather than diffuse features. Notably, the fusion embedding remains structured but less locally redundant, suggesting a more compact representation after cross-level integration. Alignment analysis shows that phoneme embeddings weakly match the audio embedding space, whereas fusion embeddings achieve substantially stronger alignment, indicating that the fusion module transforms neural features into an acoustic-compatible manifold suitable for Whisper decoding. This alignment further shifts toward the text embedding space after the Whisper encoder, forming a coherent progression from neural to audio-aligned to text-aligned representations. Temporal activation patterns support this view: BEST produces smoother trajectories with shorter effective support, reflecting stronger temporal consistency and reduced redundancy. In contrast, GRU and vanilla Transformer exhibit fragmented activations, indicating weaker temporal organization. Overall, these results reveal a concrete mechanism: BEST performs decoding via progressive cross-modal alignment. Rather than committing early to discrete phoneme predictions, it first constructs an acoustic-compatible latent space and then maps it to text, providing evidence for a neural-to-audio-to-text decoding pathway.

Deployment analysis of end-to-end inference : We evaluate deployment efficiency on Jetson Orin Nano (Table 8). BEST reduces computation from 1736.26 to 123.17 GFLOPS, lowers inference latency from 1189.71 ms to 215.67 ms, and cuts peak memory from 7.23 GiB to 2.79 GiB, with training time reduced by over a factor of two. End-to-end decoding is desirable in speech BCI as it enables direct neural-to-text inference without external pipelines, reducing system complexity and latency. However, existing end-to-end frameworks incur prohibitive computational cost. BEST

By constructing structured representations before decoding, BEST reduces optimization and inference costs, achieving a comparable accuracy-efficiency trade-off for deployment. 

## 5 Discussion 

Rethinking scaling in speech BCI : A central insight of this work is that model scaling does not directly translate to performance gains in speech BCI. Unlike conventional NLP or ASR, speech BCI 9operates in a low-resource, high-noise regime where neural observations are weakly aligned with linguistic outputs. Under such conditions, increasing model capacity amplifies overfitting rather than improving generalization. Our results show a clear non-monotonic scaling behavior, where compact, modality-aligned decoders outperform larger text-based LLMs. This suggests that the dominant factor in speech BCI is not model size, but the alignment between representation and neural space. 

Representation, not prediction, is the bottleneck : We identify the discrete phoneme interface as a key limitation in existing pipelines. Collapsing neural activity into hard phoneme labels discards structured information and leads to brittle decoding. BEST avoids this bottleneck by preserving continuous, multi-level representations and performing feature-level integration prior to decoding. Both quantitative and representation-level analyses show that this design improves temporal consistency, reduces representation collapse, and enables more stable sequence reconstruction. This highlights a broader principle: in speech BCI, decoding quality is governed by representation structure rather than intermediate prediction accuracy. 

Implications for practical deployment : From a systems perspective, BEST demonstrates that efficient neural decoding does not require large-scale generative models. By replacing LLM-based decoding with a compact speech-aligned translator, BEST significantly reduces computation, latency, and memory footprint while maintaining strong performance. This is critical for clinical deploy-ment, where real-time inference and on-device constraints are fundamental requirements rather than optimization targets. 

Limitations and future directions : Despite these advances, several limitations remain. First, our evaluation is conducted on single-subject datasets, and cross-subject generalization remains an open challenge due to variability in neural representations. Second, while BEST improves robustness to cross-day distribution shift, long-term non-stationarity and adaptive decoding are not explicitly addressed. Third, our framework relies on pretrained speech models (e.g., Whisper), whose inductive biases may not fully capture the neural-to-linguistic mapping. Future work may explore jointly learned neural-speech representations or fully neural-native decoding architectures. Finally, while we show that large models are not necessary for current speech BCI settings, this conclusion may evolve with larger-scale neural datasets. Understanding how representation learning and model scaling interact under improved data regimes remains an important direction. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We present BEST , a lightweight and practical framework for speech BCI that replaces discrete phoneme-based pipelines with hierarchical feature-level decoding. BEST achieves strong performance with a fraction of the computational cost, while providing improved robustness to overfitting and cross-day distribution shift. Our findings challenge the prevailing assumption that larger models are inherently beneficial for speech BCI. Instead, we show that performance is governed by representation alignment, structured decoding, and controlled capacity. These results suggest a shift in focus from model scaling to representation design, offering a more efficient and robust pathway toward real-world neural speech decoding.

## A Implementation details 

Data preprocessing. We follow the preprocessing protocols of T12 [48] and T15 [3]. For T12 , 256 neural features are extracted (two features per electrode, including −4.5 RMS threshold crossings and spike band power across 128 electrodes), with White Noise Standard Deviation = 0 .8, Gaussian smoothing width = 2 .0, and Constant Offset Standard Deviation = 0 .2. For T15 , 512 neural features are used (two features per electrode across 256 electrodes), with White Noise Standard Deviation =1.0, Gaussian smoothing width = 2 .0, and Constant Offset Standard Deviation = 0 .2.

Training details: Stage 1 is trained from random initialization on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GiB). Convergence is typically achieved within 80 epochs (under 1 hour for T12 and under 2 hours for T15). Beyond this point, performance gradually degrades due to overfitting, with negligible improvements after 400 epochs as indicated by PER/CER. Stage 2 is trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GiB) for 50 epochs (under 1 hour for T12 and under 2 hours for T15). Overfitting is observed beyond 50 epochs, with no significant improvement in WER. The Whisper model is initialized from pretrained weights, with only the first Transformer decoder layer and the fusion block updated during training. Peak system memory usage remains below 32 GiB. Detailed hyperparameter configurations are provided in Table 9. 

## B Audio/Text embedding extraction for RSA 

Audio embedding : The audio embedding is obtained from the Whisper encoder. We input ground-truth speech audio into the pretrained Whisper encoder and extract the hidden states from the top encoder layer. These representations serve as the reference acoustic embedding space. For consistency with neural-derived features, we use the time-aligned encoder outputs after positional encoding, without additional pooling unless otherwise specified. In experiments involving neural inputs, the fusion embeddings are projected into the same latent space and passed through the upper layers of the Whisper encoder, ensuring that both embeddings lie in a shared acoustic representation. 

Text embedding : The text embedding is obtained from the Whisper decoder. Given the text, we first tokenize the text via Whisper’s tokenizer and feed it into the decoder with teacher forcing. We then extract the decoder hidden states corresponding to each token from the final decoder layer. These hidden states are used as the text embedding space. No logits or output projections are used; instead, we rely on the internal decoder representations prior to the vocabulary projection layer. 

Alignment for RSA : For cross-modal RSA, neural-derived embeddings (phoneme, character, and fusion representations) are compared against audio and text embeddings after temporal alignment. We apply linear interpolation or average pooling to match sequence lengths across modalities. ## E Validation CTC loss and CER analysis 

The validation curves (Figure. 5) show a consistent optimization advantage of BEST over the vanilla Transformer in both phoneme- and character-level pretraining. BEST reaches lower CTC loss minima and maintains lower CER throughout training, indicating more stable and more effective neural representation learning. The Transformer, by contrast, converges to a higher loss floor and consistently worse CER, reflecting earlier overfitting and weaker generalization. A second observation is the divergence between validation CTC loss and CER, which holds across all models. After the early training stage, CTC loss rises while CER continues to decrease or remains stable. This is a property of the CTC objective rather than a model-specific effect. CTC loss depends on the full alignment distribution, whereas CER depends only on the final decoded sequence. A worse alignment distribution therefore does not necessarily imply worse decoding. This highlights a limitation of validation CTC loss as a direct proxy for sequence-level quality. The advantage of BEST remains consistent under both phoneme and character supervision, indicating that the gain is not target-specific. It arises from the encoder design, which improves both local discrimination and sequence consistency. Figure 6 compares frame-wise phoneme decoding probabilities under well-fitted and overfitted training regimes. ## J Method implementation 

Multi-Frequency Attention : We introduce a lightweight multi-frequency channel attention module to capture informative temporal-spectral patterns in neural signals. Given input x ∈ RB×C×T , we first align the temporal dimension to a fixed length via adaptive average pooling. The signal is then projected onto a set of fixed discrete cosine transform (DCT) bases to extract frequency-specific responses. Specifically, we employ 16 fixed low-frequency DCT branches corresponding to indices 

1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 , which are selected by the top-K strategy. For each frequency branch, we compute three complementary channel-wise statistics: mean, max, and min. We aggregate these statistics and feed them into a shared bottleneck MLP to produce attention weights.The fixed DCT bases are stored as non-trainable buffers, and the input is reweighted by the sigmoid-activated attention to form final output.This design imposes negligible overhead while boosting robustness to noise and temporal distribution shifts in neural signals. 

Pseudo-code: 

Input: x ∈ RB×C×T

xpool = AdaptiveAvgPool (x, L )

feat = 0 

for each DCT basis fi :

s = xpool ∗ fi

feat + = Mean (s) + Max (s) + Min (s)

feat = feat /K 

attn = Sigmoid (MLP (feat )) 

Output: x ∗ attn.unsqueeze (−1) 

Hierarchical Transformer : To capture temporal dependencies at multiple scales, we employ a hierarchical Transformer with progressive masking. A shallow initial Transformer layer models global context, followed by stacked Transformer blocks with distinct temporal constraints, including causal (past), local-window, and forward (future) attention. This staged design separates long- and short-range temporal dependencies, improving stability and robustness under noisy neural inputs. 

Pseudo-code: 

Input: xh = iTransformer (x)

h = Transformer1 (h, mask=past_mask )

h = Transformer2 (h, mask=local_mask )

h = Transformer3 (h, mask=future_mask )

Output: Projection (h)

## K Ethical considerations 

This work presents a lightweight speech brain-computer interface (BCI) for neural-to-text decoding, with careful consideration of privacy, ethics, and responsible deployment. All datasets (Brain-to-Text ’24/’25) are publicly available and collected under IRB approval with informed consent. Data are fully anonymized, and no new human experiments are conducted. Speech BCIs raise risks of neural privacy leakage and unintended cognitive inference. The proposed BEST framework mitigates these risks via a compact architecture that avoids reliance on large third-party models, reducing external data transmission. It is explicitly designed to decode only speech-related neural features, adhering to minimal and task-specific information use. Clinically, this work targets communication restoration for patients with severe motor impairments. Its lightweight design enables edge deployment, promoting accessibility. However, further clinical validation, safety standardization, and long-term evaluation are required before real-world use. We further advocate efficient model design over unnecessary scaling, reducing computational cost and 24 environmental impact. Limitations, including single-subject evaluation and limited cross-subject gen-eralization, are clearly acknowledged to avoid overstatement. Future work will follow medical ethics standards and incorporate secure data governance (e.g., encryption and access control), alongside interdisciplinary collaboration to ensure BCI deployment aligns with privacy, and human dignity.
