# Deep LLM layers expose passage-level context in EEG during silent reading 

Ingo Marquardt ∗

, Anthilia Alchanat, Priyanka Jain nubrain October 2026 

Abstract 

In a previous report we showed that a decoder can recover open-vocabulary word-level information from 19-channel EEG recorded during silent reading, by aligning short EEG windows with hidden-state embeddings of the presented word taken from a large language model (LLM). That analysis contrasted two of the model’s 33 layers: the context-free input embedding and one mid-depth layer. Here we ask how EEG-decodable structure is distributed over the full depth of the language model. We trained one decoder per layer of Llama-3.1-8B, with and without a causal transformer over the sequence of EEG windows. We distinguish word-level decodability from a context-tracking component. Word-level decodability peaks in early layers and declines steadily towards the last layer. In contrast, the context-tracking component of the retrieval gain rises with depth, both with and without a causal transformer. Unexpectedly, a decoder that sees a single 0.5-s window at a time tracks the text passage too. Two new controls dissect this component. A target-geometry control, a synthetic predictor made of the true target plus noise calibrated to each decoder’s word-level accuracy, shows that context tracking is not a by-product of word decoding and target geometry. A control that permutes predictions across runs from either the same or from different sessions shows that most of the observed context-tracking is not caused by an EEG recording session signature. Instead, context tracking mostly follows the text. A single EEG window therefore carries passage-level information that a contextual target can absorb. The results locate word identity in the early layers of the language model, discourse state in the deeper layers, and make the choice of target layer a design dial for EEG language decoders. 

# 1 Introduction 

Language models and human readers process text in the same order: one token, or one word, at a time. A causal language model updates a layered internal state with every token, and layer-wise analyses of such models show how that state changes with depth. A token’s vector becomes more context-specific and less predictable from the token’s identity with every layer (Ethayarajh, 2019). Lexical and local syntactic information is read out from the lower layers and context-dependent structure from the upper layers (Tenney et al., 2019). In a causal model that reads text left-to-right, information about the current token fades with depth while information about the next token accumulates (Voita et al., 2019). A human reader updates an internal state with every word too. When brain activity during language comprehension is aligned with the internal states of a language model, the alignment depends on which layer the states are taken from, and the depth profile of

Most of that work uses linear encoding models, fitted to fMRI or MEG and in some cases to intracranial recordings (Goldstein et al., 2022; Schrimpf et al., 2021). The closest precedent to our paradigm aligned MEG recorded during word-by-word reading with the word embedding and the context state of a recurrent language model (Wehbe et al., 2014). But whereas many previous reports predict brain recordings from text embeddings, we attempt to predict text embeddings from EEG data. Specifically, we train a contrastive decoder to retrieve the presented word from 19-channel dry-electrode EEG. In a previous report (Marquardt et al., 2026, henceforth report 1) we established the paradigm and the decoder, and showed that word-level information is recoverable from EEG during silent reading of naturalistic text, that it extends to rare and mid-frequency words, that it scales log-linearly with data volume, and that it is not reducible to narrative topic tracking or to a positional prior. That analysis used two target layers of Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024): layer 0, the context-free input embedding, and layer 20, chosen on the assumption that a mid-to-deep layer would carry rich semantic representations without being dominated by next-token prediction. The present report systematically tests that assumption. We trained one decoder for every layer, 0 to 32, with and without a sequence model over the EEG, on five train/validation splits, and evaluated every fit with the full set of controls of report 1. We ask two questions. First, how is EEG-decodable structure distributed over the depth of the language model, along each axis report 1 used to characterise decoding: word-level versus passage-level retrieval, the position probes, and the word-frequency bins? Second, a question raised by our initial results. A decoder that sees one 0.5-s EEG window at a time has no access to neighbouring windows, yet if the target carries context, it tracks the passage being read. What does a single window share with its passage? We introduce two controls to answer it, a lag-dependent version of report 1’s run-mate swap with a calibrated target-geometry control, and a comparison of narrative neighbours recorded in the same versus different sessions. 

# 2 Methods 

2.1 Data, decoder and metrics 

Paradigm, acquisition, preprocessing, decoder and evaluation are those of report 1 (Marquardt et al., 2026). Here we summarise the essentials and refer to report 1 for details. 

Data. One participant read continuous narrative text (detective stories, sections from a fantasy novel and a science-fiction novel, and two newspaper articles) presented one word at a time by rapid serial visual presentation, with typography randomised on every trial, while EEG was recorded from 19 dry electrodes at 600 Hz. The complete dataset comprises 409 recording runs of about 600 words each, recorded in 52 sessions on 42 days spanning 146 days (a session is one sitting with the cap on), 249,753 word presentations, about 51 h of reading, of which 232,028 (93%) pass the amplitude inclusion criterion. Report 1 analysed the first 393 runs; the present sweep uses all 409, so absolute values differ slightly from report 1. 

Decoder. A convolutional encoder maps a 0.5-s onset-locked EEG window (19 channels, 300 samples) to a 256-dimensional feature vector. Optionally, a four-layer causal transformer over the sequence of feature vectors within a recording run (the “sequence model”) lets the prediction for word i depend on the EEG of words j ≤ i; a learned positional embedding is its only non-neural, trial-varying input. Encoder (or transformer) outputs and linearly projected LLM targets meet in a shared, L2-normalised 256-dimensional space, trained with the symmetric CLIP objective (Radford 2et al., 2021) over batches of eight complete runs (about 4,400 in-batch negatives). Fits ran for 100 epochs with AdamW; checkpoints were selected on the within-run gain. 

Targets. Each word is represented by the hidden state of the last sub-word token of its punctuation-stripped form at a chosen layer of Llama-3.1-8B, after a causal forward pass over the run’s full text. Layer 0 is the input embedding, which depends only on the word’s identity; layers 1 to 32 are the outputs of the 32 decoder blocks, each incorporating all preceding words through causal attention; layer 32 is the final hidden state. The targets are these 4,096-dimensional hidden states. The decoder’s bias-free linear projection maps them into the shared, L2-normalised 256-dimensional space, in which all similarities of this report are computed. The controls of Sections 2.3 and 2.4 likewise operate on the projected targets and predictions. 

Metrics. All metrics are word-grouped top-10 retrieval within pools of 512 validation trials (probability mass summed across trials sharing a core word before ranking), averaged over five pool draws, and reported as the gain over an empirical permutation baseline (10 permutations), in percentage points (pp), so that chance is 0 pp. We rely on the following metrics to distinguish between different aspects of decoding and to account for possible confounds: 1. The within-run gain , the primary metric, with pools of consecutive trials from one run and a within-run permutation null, so that topic tracking cannot contribute. 2. The overall gain , with random cross-run pools 3. The context-tracking gain , the overall gain recomputed after replacing every trial’s EEG prediction with that of a random other trial of the same run, which measures the passage-level component of the overall gain. 4. The context-independent gain is the overall minus the context-tracking gain. 5. Two position probes re-run a transformer fit with the EEG replaced by the learnable mask token, for every trial (position-only) or for the current trial only (mask-current), and decompose the within-run gain into a non-neural position-only term, a preceding-EEG term and a current-trial term. 6. The position-corrected within-run gain (within-run minus position-only) is the quotable word-level figure for transformer fits, and coincides with the within-run gain for fits without a sequence model, where both probes are zero by construction. Gains are also computed per word-frequency tertile (rare, mid, frequent), each against its own baseline. 

2.2 The layer sweep 

One decoder was trained for every target layer ℓ = 0 , . . . , 32 and for each architecture (without and with the sequence model), with every other hyperparameter fixed at a configuration that performed well in report 1’s sweeps: EEG windows locked to word onset, 0.5-s window length, no temporal-shift augmentation, amplitude scaling only, no masking of within-run negatives, trainable temperature, batch size 8, learning rate 10 −4, 100 epochs. The 33 × 2 grid was repeated for five randomisation seeds, 330 fits in total. The seed fixes the content-aware train/validation split (about 20% of the corpus by word count, 85–86 validation runs per seed, no 10-gram overlap between the two sides); model initialisation and batch order are not seeded and differ between every pair of fits. The five seeds are therefore independent fits on different held-out text, and “mean ± SD over seeds” throughout this report is a conservative error bar that includes both training stochasticity and the choice of held-out passages. 32.3 Lag-dependent run-mate swap and the target-geometry control 

In order to quantify to what extent our EEG model decodes textual context (as opposed to individual word identity), we previously calculated a context-tracking gain by replacing each trial’s prediction with that of a randomly sampled trial from the same run (report 1). For contextual targets that gain is positive even without a sequence model. That is not necessarily expected; without a sequence model, the EEG model only sees a single EEG segment (corresponding to a single word) at a time, so the contextual information has to come from this single EEG segment. We propose three potential mechanisms: 1. Word decoding plus target geometry : To calculate the context-tracking gain, a trial’s prediction is swapped with another trial’s prediction from the same run. The true target vectors (from the LLM) of words from the same text passage are expected to be more similar to each other than to targets from other text passages, and the EEG model might learn this pattern. Hence, a decoded run-mate still ranks the true target above candidates from other runs; no context in the EEG is required. 2. Context in the single window : The evoked response to a word depends on the discourse state (Kutas & Federmeier, 2011), and the decoder maps it to the run’s contextual component. This mechanism would be genuine decoding of discourse context from the neural response. 3. A recording-session signature : Slow non-language EEG variance (electrode impedance, cap placement, vigilance) that correlates with progress through the corpus because the narrative continues across runs and sessions, and that generalises to a held-out run because validation runs are interleaved in time with training runs. We repeated the swap with the run-mate at a fixed signed lag of k included trials (roughly 

k words) earlier or later, k ∈ { 1, 2, 4, . . . , 256 }, reflecting at run boundaries so that |k| is exact wherever the run is long enough (Figure 1b). Mechanisms 1 and 2 should decay with the lag, because the contextual similarity of two words falls off with their distance in the text, whereas a session signature is constant within a run. To bound mechanism 1 we built a target-geometry control :a synthetic predictor that has word-level information and nothing else. Its prediction for a trial is the trial’s true projected target plus isotropic Gaussian noise in the shared 256-dimensional space, re-normalised, with the noise standard deviation calibrated per checkpoint by a 14-step bisection search so that the control’s within-run gain equals the checkpoint’s (Supplement, Section S0). The control is then swapped at the same lags. It contains no information from the EEG, so any cross-run gain it shows arises from the geometry of the targets alone, and only the excess of the decoder over the control requires context in the EEG. It is distinct from report 1’s context-tracking control (the random-run-mate swap), which is applied to the decoder’s own predictions. 

2.4 Narrative neighbours across session boundaries 

The lag swap cannot separate confound mechanism 3 (a potential recording-session EEG signature) from mechanism 2 (a successfully decoded run-level neural discourse state), because both are constant within a run. But they can be distinguished across runs. As a complementary view, we asked what makes the decoder’s predictions for two runs alike. For each validation run we averaged its predictions into one mean prediction vector and its projected targets into one mean target vector. For every pair of validation runs, the cosine similarity of the two mean predictions was regressed, by ordinary least squares with all variables standardised, on three candidate sources: the cosine similarity of the two mean targets (the text), a same-session indicator (a session signature) and the log of one plus the gap in days between the two recordings (slow drift). A session signature would appear as a positive same-session coefficient. Pairs share runs and are not independent, so the coefficients are descriptive. Because only 2–3% of the pairs come from the same session, the standardised same-session coefficient is small by construction; the Supplement therefore also reports the term on the indicator’s own 0/1 scale, compares same-session pairs directly with same-day pairs from different sessions, and tests it against a permutation null (Section S1). 

2.5 Restricted candidate set 

The retrieval metrics above rank the true word among the words that occur in a pool of 512 validation trials, about 250–300 distinct words of an open vocabulary, and quote the gain over a permutation baseline. Closed-vocabulary speech neuroprostheses report instead the word error rate of sentences decoded under a fixed vocabulary, typically of 50 words (Card et al., 2024; Moses et al., 2021; Willett et al., 2023), and the accuracy of classifying one isolated word of that set (Moses et al., 2021; Willett et al., 2023). To make our numbers legible in the latter form, we re-scored the predictions of every fit on trials whose word belongs to a fixed list of 50 words. The model was not retrained; the restriction is applied at inference only. Two lists were defined once on the full corpus, so that they are identical for every split (Supplement, Section S2, Table S11): the 50 most frequent content words, and the 50 most frequent words of any kind, almost all of them function words. Pools held exactly one validation trial of every list word, so that a pool is a 50-way identification task with uniform chance (2% top-1, 20% top-10). The candidates of such a pool come from different passages, so a contextual target can be identified through the discourse state rather than through the word. Hence, two controls were scored on the same 50-word pools. In the first, every prediction is replaced by that of the run-mate one trial earlier, which carries the local discourse state and the position but not the current word. In the second, every prediction is replaced by that of a random run-mate (the context-tracking control). Finally, the within-run gain was recomputed on the list words alone, with pools made of the list-word trials of one run, where candidates from other passages are absent.

2.6 Reading the curves 

Every value is read at the epoch that maximised the within-run gain, on the validation set that also drove that selection, so absolute levels are optimistic. Cross-layer orderings are reliable (report 1, Section 2.8). Adjacent layers of a language model carry highly similar representations, so a real effect is expected to be a smooth function of the layer index. Where we report a peak, we report the peak of the seed-mean curve, each seed’s own peak, and the mean pairwise Spearman correlation between the per-seed curves as a measure of how well the seeds agree on the shape of the profile.

3.2 Deeper layers buy the passage, not the word 

The overall gain in mixed pools (Figure 3a) leaps from 11 .0 pp at layer 0 to 33 .4 ± 1.5 pp at layer 1 with the sequence model, peaks at 38 .5 ± 1.9 pp at layer 9, and falls to 27 .9 ± 2.8 pp at the last layer; without a sequence model it rises from 10 .3 to about 17–20 pp and stays there. The composition of this gain shifts with depth. Its context-tracking component (Figure 3b) grows with depth: without a sequence model almost monotonically, from 1 .0 to 7 .2 ± 0.6 pp (Spearman ρ with layer index 0 .84; every seed peaks at the last layer), and with the sequence model from 1 .6 to about 8–9 pp by the middle layers, where it stays (8 .9 ± 1.4 pp at layer 32; ρ = 0 .52), while the context-independent remainder (Figure 3c) follows the slow decline of the within-run gain. The share of the overall gain 7that is passage tracking (Figure 3d) rises from 19% at layer 1 to 32% at layer 32 with the sequence model and from 10% at layer 0 to 42% at layer 32 without. Deeper targets therefore make the decoder better at recognising which passage is being read, not which word. The orange curve of Figure 3b is the observation that motivated the second half of this report. The decoder without a sequence model sees one 0.5-s window and nothing else, yet from layer 4 onwards it tracks the passage at 5–7 pp. Section 3.5 asks what a single window can share with its passage. 

3.3 Rare words are decoded best against mid-depth targets 

Frequent words drive the early peak (Figure 4). Their within-run gain peaks at layers 1–2 in both decoders (12 .5 ± 0.3 and 17 .5 ± 0.2 pp) and declines from there. Rare and mid-frequency words behave differently: their curves keep rising past the frequent-word peak and plateau across the middle of the network. With the sequence model the rare-word gain rises from 3 .3 pp at layer 0 to 12 .6 ± 0.6 pp at layer 8 and stays above 10 pp through layer 20 (10.3 pp) before falling to 6 .9 pp at layer 32; without a sequence model it rises from 3 .6 to 6 .6 ± 0.4 pp at layer 15. The rare-to-frequent ratio, 0 .32–0 .33 at layer 0 for both decoders, rises to 0.7–0.8 across the middle layers with the sequence model (peak 0 .80 ± 0.07 at layer 21) and to about 0.6 without (peak 0 .62 ± 0.05 at layer 9), and eases off in the last few layers (Figure 4c). For the words a communication device needs most, the context-free target is the worst choice. 

3.4 Position probes and what the sequence model adds 

The transformer’s within-run gain decomposes into the three nested probe terms at every layer (Figure 5a). The non-neural position-only term is 0 .0 ± 0.2 pp at layer 0 and between 2.3 and 3.6 pp everywhere else, without a trend across depth (2 .9 ± 0.6 pp at layer 2, 2 .4 ± 0.3 pp at layer 20), the depth profile of Figure 2 is explained by the neural terms. The preceding-EEG term is largest at the early layers (4 .0 ± 0.4 pp at layer 2) and 2–3 pp in the deep layers; the current-trial term falls from 10 .0 ± 0.3 pp at layer 2 to 5 .9 ± 0.4 pp at layer 32. What the sequence model adds depends on the metric (Figure 5b; differences paired within seed). On the overall gain its advantage is large and depth-dependent, from 17 .6 ± 1.3 pp at layer 2 through 20 .1 ± 1.7 pp at layer 8 to 10 .6 ± 2.1 pp at layer 32. The sequence model’s larger overall gain is mostly passage tracking. On the within-run gain the sequence model’s advantage is 4–5 .5 pp across most of the depth, and once the positional shortcut is removed it shrinks to 2 .3 ± 0.6 pp at layer 2, 1 .9 ± 0.4 pp at layer 20 and 0 .4 ± 0.6 pp at layer 32. At layer 0, where the target carries no context, the sequence model is slightly harmful ( −0.4 ± 0.3 pp), reproducing report 1’s interaction between target type and architecture along the whole depth axis. 

3.5 What a single window shares with its passage 

Word decoding alone does not produce the single window’s context-tracking gain. 

Section 3.2 left open whether the context-tracking gain of the decoder without a sequence model could be a by-product of word decoding and target geometry (mechanism 1 of Section 2.3). Supplementary Table S2 and Figure 6 compare each checkpoint with its target-geometry control, a pure word decoder calibrated to the same within-run gain. Without a sequence model the control’s random-swap gain is 0.4–0.7 pp at every layer above 0, against 3.8–7.2 pp for the real decoder. Roughly nine tenths of the context-tracking gain therefore comes from something in the predictions themselves that the geometry of the targets does not explain, a component that the predictions for the trials of one run share. At layer 0 the control gives 0.1 pp: target geometry contributes nothing there, as expected for context-free targets, whose projected vectors are barely more similar within a run than between runs (mean cosine similarity 0.123 against 0.116 in the decoder’s projected space). The decoder nevertheless gives 1.0 pp, because its predictions cluster by run far more than the targets do (0.09 within against 0.03 between runs). Hence, a run-level component is present in the predictions even when the target has no context, and it earns a small gain through the weak vocabulary clustering of the targets. 

Without a sequence model, the shared component is mostly constant within a run. 

The swap gain decays only gently with the lag (Figure 6a): at layer 8 it is 7 .3 ± 0.3 pp for a run-mate four trials away and 4 .9 ± 0.4 pp for one 256 trials away, against 5 .4 ± 0.4 pp for a random run-mate and 0 .6 ± 0.1 pp for the target-geometry control. We read the value for the farthest run-mate as a run-level part that all trials of the run share, and the excess of the near over the far run-mate, 7.3 − 4.9 ≈ 2.4 pp at layer 8 and 1.7–2.7 pp across layers 1–32, as a local part. The control shows the same kind of decay at only 0.3–0.9 pp, so most of the local part, too, goes beyond target geometry. The full set of profiles is in Supplementary Figure S3. 

With the sequence model, the shared component is local. The transformer’s swap gain is large for close run-mates (26 .8 ± 1.4 pp at layer 8 and lag 4, against an overall gain of 38 .3 ± 1.9 pp) and falls steeply beyond a lag of about 64 trials, to 0 .8 ± 0.3 pp at lag 256 (Figure 6b, Supplementary Figure S4). The sequence model’s context has a characteristic length of a few dozen words. The random swap, which averages over all lags, understates how local it is. 

Most of the run-level component follows the text, not the session. The lag swap cannot tell a decoded discourse state from a recording-session signature, because both are constant within a run; narrative neighbours recorded in different sessions can (Section 2.4). When a validation run takes the predictions of a narrative neighbour two to eight runs away, the fraction of its cross-run gain that is retained is 0 .32 ± 0.03 for neighbours recorded in the same session and 0 .24 ± 0.02 for neighbours recorded in a different session without a sequence model, and 0 .47 ± 0.04 versus 0.36 ± 0.02 with it (mean ± SD over layers 1–32 of the split means; Supplementary Table S3, Figure 7a,c). A recording-session signature would collapse at the boundary; instead, neighbours recorded in a different session retain about three quarters of what neighbours recorded in the same session retain, with either decoder (0.75 without and 0.77 with the sequence model). So about a quarter of the context tracking component may be session-linked, and the rest follows the text. Scrambling the partner run’s trial positions removes about a fifth of the retained fraction without a sequence model (0 .32 → 0.25) and about two thirds of it with the transformer (0 .47 → 0.15), whose positional embedding carries a within-run position component that the probes of Section 3.4 already measure (Figure 7b,d). The regression over pairs of validation runs gives the same picture (Figure 7e,f). It asks what makes the decoder’s mean predictions for two runs alike, and weighs three candidate sources against each other: the similarity of the two runs’ mean targets (the text), whether the runs were recorded in the same session, and how far apart in time they were recorded. Three results follow; see Supplement for details (Section S1, Supplementary Table S3). First, the text carries most of the weight: its standardised coefficient is 0 .32 ± 0.01 without and 0 .44 ± 0.02 with the sequence model (mean ± SD over layers 1–32 of the split means). Second, the session contributes little. Without a sequence model, two runs from the same session are slightly more alike than their text and their recording-time gap predict, by about 0.09 in cosine similarity (on its scale from −1 to 1); the effect is absent at layers 0–2, grows with depth, and exceeds a permutation null in about half of the checkpoints, mostly at layers 9–32. With the sequence model there is no such effect. Third, the time gap carries a negative weight of about −0.3 for both decoders: predictions for runs recorded months apart are less alike than their text similarity predicts. This drift is present even at layer 0, where the 11 targets are context-free, so it is not passage tracking through the targets. Because the participant read the corpus in recording order, recording time and position in the corpus are nearly the same axis, and the size of the drift term, though not its sign, depends on how the text is controlled for (Supplement, Section S1). The drift is a slow change of the prediction geometry along the recording axis, whether of the recordings themselves or of a state that follows the reader’s progress through the corpus, and it is not the carrier of the context-tracking gain, most of which survives the session boundary. In sum, the decoder without a sequence model carries a small session-linked component at the deeper layers and the transformer does not, and in both cases it is small next to the text and the drift. 

3.6 A restricted candidate set 

For comparison with closed-vocabulary speech neuroprostheses, the Supplement (Section S2, Fig-ure S5, Table S10) re-scores every fit on a fixed list of 50 words, as a 50-way identification task with uniform chance of 2% (top-1) and 20% (top-10), together with two controls that measure how much of the closed-set accuracy comes from the passage rather than from the word. Against the context-free layer-0 target, where only the word can be read, a single 0.5-s window identifies one of 50 content words at 5 .1 ± 0.7% top-1 and 33 .8 ± 1.2% top-10 without a sequence model, and at 4.8 ± 0.3 and 34 .0 ± 1.3% with it, and both controls sit near chance. Against contextual targets the closed-set accuracy jumps, to 12 .8 ± 0.5% top-1 at layer 1 without a sequence model and to 33 .0 ± 2.0% at layer 8 with it, but so does the control that replaces each prediction with that of the run-mate one trial earlier, which carries the local discourse state but not the current word: at layer 8 it recovers 27 .9 ± 1.3 of the transformer’s 33.0%. The word-level part of the closed-set accuracy, the excess over that control, is largest at layers 1–2 (5 .8 ± 1.1 pp without and 7 .7 ± 0.7 pp with the sequence model, top-1) and declines to about 2.5 pp at the last layer: the profile of Figure 2, in closed-set form. The 50 most frequent words give the same picture. 

3.7 Sanity checks 

Every fit converged within the 100-epoch budget (Supplementary Figure S1); checkpoints were selected at epoch 58–92 on average per layer, and no fit approached the temperature clamp (Supplementary Figure S2). The lag-swap reconstruction reproduced the logged overall and context-tracking gains of every checkpoint to within 0.07 pp. 

# 4 Discussion 

4.1 Summary 

Across the full depth of Llama-3.1-8B, the EEG-decodable structure of silent reading has a simple distribution. Word identity is easiest to read out against the early layers, a few blocks in, and fades steadily with depth. What deeper layers add is predicting the text passage. The context-tracking component of the retrieval gain and its share of the total rise with depth for both decoders. Rare and mid-frequency words are decoded best against mid-depth targets, where the frequent words have already begun to decline. The profile replicates across five train/validation splits, holds after subtracting the positional shortcut, and is the same in shape for a decoder that integrates over the sequence of EEG windows and for one that sees one window at a time. The second result we would like to highlight concerns that single window. The passage tracking is not a by-product of word decoding and target geometry: a pure word decoder calibrated to the same word-level accuracy produces a tenth of it. It is largely constant within a run, with a small local part. And most of it follows the text rather than the recording session: narrative neighbours recorded in a different session retain about three quarters of what neighbours recorded in the same session retain. The regression over run pairs attributes the missing quarter to a slow drift along the recording axis and, for the decoder without a sequence model only, in part to a small session-linked component at the deeper layers; for the transformer it finds no session-linked component at all. The half-second EEG response to a word therefore carries information about the passage being read, which a contextual target can absorb. 

4.2 Early layers hold the word, deep layers hold the discourse 

The depth profile is what one would expect if the EEG response to a word carries the word’s identity together with a weaker trace of its context. That the context-tracking gain approaches the word-level gain in percentage points at the deeper layers does not contradict this: telling apart the roughly 85 validation runs that feed a cross-run pool is a far easier discrimination than telling apart the 250–300 words of one passage, so a weak contextual signal buys a large gain. Layer-wise dissections of language models find that the lowest hidden layers are the least context-specific and the top layer of a causal model the most (Ethayarajh, 2019), that lexical information is read out early and context-dependent structure late (Tenney et al., 2019), and that under a next-token objective information about the current token fades with depth while information about the next token builds up (Voita et al., 2019). On that reading, a layer-1 or layer-2 hidden state is still dominated by the input token, mixed with a small amount of the preceding text. A mid-depth state carries a substantial share of the preceding passage. The final state is specialised for next-token prediction. The word-level metric, which by construction cannot be earned from passage identity, tracks the word’s identity and therefore peaks where the hidden state is still mostly the input token. The context-tracking metric rises as the hidden state absorbs more of the preceding text. The profiles differ from those typically reported for fMRI and MEG encoding models, where alignment follows an inverted U across depth and peaks in the middle layers, at least for contexts longer than about 15 words (Caucheteux & King, 2022; Toneva & Wehbe, 2019), but the quantities differ too: an encoding model predicts a slow haemodynamic signal, or an MEG response averaged over time, from the target, whereas our primary metric asks how well a half-second EEG window discriminates among the words of one passage, a question about the word rather than about the brain state. Consistent with this, the word-by-word ECoG dataset analysed by Schrimpf et al. (2021) was better predicted by earlier layers of GPT-2 XL than their fMRI datasets were, a difference the authors suggested reflects the prediction of individual word representations rather than whole-sentence representations. Three implications follow for the design of EEG language decoders. First, the target layer is a dial between “which word” and “which passage”. Early-to-mid layers are the place for word identity, deeper layers for discourse state, and a deployed decoder probably wants both, in the same way that invasive speech neuroprostheses combine the neural evidence for each word with a language-model prior over the words already decoded in the sentence (Card et al., 2024; Metzger et al., 2023; Moses et al., 2021). Training against an early-layer target for the word and a deeper-layer target for the context is an obvious next step. Second, the context-free layer 0, the cleanest choice for a control analysis, is the worst choice for rare content words, which prefer context-rich targets. Third, the sequence model’s advantage is mostly passage tracking; its contribution to word-level decoding, once the positional shortcut is removed, is 1–2 pp and largest at the early layers. 15 4.3 Context in a single window 

That the evoked response to a word depends on its context is well established at the level of single EEG components (Kutas & Federmeier, 2011); the present result is that a decoder can read enough of that dependence from a 0.5-s window to recognise the passage in a held-out run, and that this is largely not an artefact of a recording-session signature. Two loopholes remain. First, the target-geometry control assumes isotropic errors around the true target, whereas a real decoder’s errors are structured, so it is one instance of the word-decoding mechanism rather than a reproduction of it. Errors that fall towards semantically or contextually similar words rather than in random directions would give a pure word decoder a larger cross-run gain than isotropic errors do, and the decoder’s error structure cannot be measured independently of the run-level component we are trying to isolate, so the control is an estimate of the mechanism’s contribution, not a bound on it. Second, the session-boundary test limits slow recording artefacts to a minority of the component, and even that shortfall is not necessarily explained by an EEG session signature, since a discourse state may simply have cooled over a break of hours or days. The regression over run pairs puts a number on the part that is tied to the session itself: about 0.1 in cosine similarity between run centroids (on its scale from −1 to 1) for the decoder without a sequence model at the deeper layers, and nothing detectable for the transformer. What the test does not exclude is a potential feature of the stimulus stream that is linked to the text, visible in a single window, and non-semantic. Randomised typography attenuates the obvious visual candidate, and position scrambling removes progress through the run, but a residual feature of that kind cannot be excluded within a reading paradigm. The passive-listening data planned for the next stage of this project, in which no such visual feature exists, is the natural test. 

4.4 Closed-set accuracy, and what it measures 

Imposing the vocabulary at the language-model stage, downstream of a vocabulary-agnostic phoneme decoder, is how the invasive literature obtains its 50-word numbers (Card et al., 2024; Willett et al., 2023), and the Supplement restricts the candidate set at inference in the same spirit (Section S2). The restricted numbers are modest where they are clean. Against the context-free target, a single EEG window identifies one of 50 words at a few times chance in top-1. They become large as soon as the target carries context, and the controls show why. In a cross-run pool the competitors come from other passages, so the discourse state identifies the true candidate, and the earlier-neighbour swap, which keeps that state and removes the word, recovers most of the closed-set accuracy of the deeper layers. The depth profile of Section 3 thus reappears in closed-set form, with early layers for the word and deeper layers for the passage. It also carries a warning for closed-set evaluations that align brain data with contextual language-model targets: without a control that preserves the local context and removes the word, a 50-way accuracy conflates the two. 

4.5 Limitations 

All results come from one densely sampled participant. Five seeds give error bars but not a large sample; the smoothness of the curves across adjacent layers is the stronger evidence for the profile’s shape. All values are computed on the validation set that also drove checkpoint selection, so levels are upper bounds and orderings are the reliable output. The validation runs are also interleaved in time with the training runs, and the time-gap term of Section 2.4 shows that the prediction geometry drifts over the months of recording, so the levels are optimistic for use on a later point in time. The layer index is confounded with the geometry of the hidden states as the decoder sees them through a linear projection (their anisotropy changes with depth, and the final layer sits after 16 the model’s last normalisation), so a layer effect is an effect of the representation as presented to the decoder, not a direct statement about the language model’s semantics. The dataset is slightly larger than report 1’s (409 versus 393 runs), which is why the values at layers 0 and 20 differ slightly from those reported there. 

4.6 Conclusion 

Word-level information in EEG during silent reading aligns best with the early layers of a language model and passage-level information with its deeper layers, rare words prefer the middle. A single half-second window already carries a trace of the passage that mostly follows the text rather than the recording session. Even a decoder without a sequence model that sees only a 0.5-s EEG window performs some context tracking. Adding a sequence model to the decoder, so that it can see preceding EEG segments, substantially increases the overall gain, but most of that advantage is due to the sequence model’s passage tracking ability. That is not to say that context tracking is undesirable; on the contrary, explicitly leveraging both word-level decoding and context tracking might prove useful for (non-invasive) language decoding.

# S0 Implementation notes 

The lag-dependent run-mate swap and the target-geometry control of Section 2.3 answer two different questions. The swap asks how far along a run the information shared between the decoder’s predictions reaches: each trial’s prediction is replaced by that of a run-mate a fixed number of trials earlier or later, and the cross-run gain that survives the swap is read against that lag, so that a component shared by the whole run (a discourse state or a recording-session signature) shows as a flat profile, whereas a local component decays with the lag. The target-geometry control asks how much of that swap gain word decoding alone would produce: it is a synthetic predictor made of each trial’s true target plus noise, with the noise calibrated so that its word-level accuracy matches the decoder’s, and it contains no information from the EEG, so any swap gain it shows comes from the similarity between the targets of neighbouring words, and only the decoder’s excess over it requires context in the EEG. The notes below give implementation details of both, and of the narrative-neighbour analysis of Section 2.4. 

Lag swap at run boundaries A trial whose partner at lag k would fall outside the run takes the partner in the other direction, so that |k| is exact wherever the run is longer than 2 k; validation runs contain about 550 included trials, so at k = 256 roughly half of the trials are reflected and none falls back to a shorter lag. Directions were also analysed separately, and the figures average the two. Without a sequence model the earlier and later profiles differ by at most 1.1 pp at any lag and layer. With the sequence model the later partner is the better one from a lag of about 4 trials on, by up to 6.8 pp at lags 32–64, because its prediction has already integrated the current trial’s EEG window through the transformer. 

Short lags The swap profiles are not monotone at the shortest lags. Without a sequence model the gain for the immediate neighbour is lower than for a run-mate four trials away at every layer, by 0.7 pp on average, and with the sequence model at 32 of the 33 layers, by 0.3 pp; the target-geometry control, which shares the targets’ geometry but was never trained, is highest at lag 1 at nearly every layer above 0. We attribute the dip to the training objective: run-mates are in-batch negatives, a trial’s immediate neighbours have the most similar targets and are therefore its hardest negatives, and the decoder is thus trained to discriminate a trial from its neighbours in particular. At layer 0 even the control is below zero at lag 1, because adjacent words are less similar in the input embedding than two words drawn at random from the same run (consecutive words tend to differ in word class). 

Calibration of the target-geometry control The control’s prediction for a trial is the trial’s true projected target plus isotropic Gaussian noise with standard deviation σ per dimension, re-normalised to unit length; the same noise seed is used throughout, so that the control’s within-run gain is a deterministic function of σ. That function decreases monotonically with σ, from perfect retrieval at σ = 0 to chance at large σ, so a unique σ matches any attainable gain, and σ was found by bisection on the interval [0 , 8]. At each of 14 steps the control was built at the midpoint of the current interval and scored on the within-run gain with the same pool draws, permutations and learned temperature as the checkpoint; if its gain exceeded the checkpoint’s, the noise was too small and the upper half of the interval was kept, otherwise the lower half. After 14 halvings the interval is 8 /214 ≈ 5 × 10 −4 wide, and the calibrated within-run gain matches the checkpoint’s to within 19 0.04 pp in every case (Supplementary Table S7). The calibrated σ is then used for every swap of the control. 

Run index The 409 runs were ordered by recording time, which is the narrative order. There were 52 sessions on 42 days, about 8 runs per session, 146 days between the first and last; the longest break between two sessions was 23 days. Each split’s validation set contained 85–86 runs spread over 38–40 sessions, yielding 66–104 same-session and 60–120 cross-session neighbour pairs at shifts 2–8 per split (each unordered pair counted once in each direction). 

Retained fraction The retained fraction measures how much of a validation run’s cross-run gain survives when its trials take the predictions of a narrative neighbour. It is built from the per-trial quantities behind the overall gain of the main text. For every trial, the top-10 hit is 1 if the trial’s true word is among the ten highest-ranked candidate words of the trial’s random cross-run pool (word-grouped, as in the main text) and 0 otherwise; averaged over trials, this is the top-10 accuracy. The permutation-baseline hit rate of a trial is the fraction of the 10 within-pool permutations of the predictions in which the true word still lands in the top 10, that is, the chance level of that trial. The top-10 hit minus the permutation-baseline hit rate, averaged over a set of trials, is the gain over the permutation baseline in which all metrics of this report are expressed. For a run r

with partner p, the swapped gain is this difference averaged over r’s trials after every prediction of r has been replaced by a prediction of p (position-matched: trial m of r takes trial m of p,starting again from p’s first trial when p is the shorter run; scrambled: a random trial of p), and the 

unswapped gain is the same difference averaged over the same trials with r’s own predictions. Both are scored in the random cross-run pools of the overall gain, with the same pool draws and the same permutations, so that the two differ only in the predictions. The retained fraction is the swapped gain divided by the unswapped gain: a value of 1 means that the partner’s predictions earn the run as much gain as its own, a value of 0 that they earn nothing. Within a checkpoint, the swapped and the unswapped gains were each averaged over all run pairs of the same kind (shifts 2–8 pooled, separately for same-session and different-session partners and for position-matched and scrambled trials), with each pair weighted by the number of trials in r, and the ratio of these two averages is the checkpoint’s retained fraction; the figures and tables report its mean and SD over seeds. 

# S1 Run-to-run similarity of the predictions 

Section 2.4 summarises a regression that attributes the similarity between two runs’ predictions to the text, the session and the recording-time gap. 

Quantities For each validation run r of a checkpoint, the mean of its trials’ predictions in the shared 256-dimensional space, re-normalised to unit length, is the run’s prediction centroid ¯ pr, and the mean of its trials’ projected targets, likewise normalised, is its target centroid ¯ tr. For every pair of validation runs ( a, b ) the prediction similarity is cos(¯ pa, ¯pb) and the target similarity is cos(¯ ta, ¯tb). With 85–86 validation runs per split there are 3,570–3,655 pairs, of which 79–97 (depending on the split) are pairs of runs recorded in the same session. 

Why centroids Averaging over the trials of a run removes the word-level content of the predictions, which changes from trial to trial, and leaves what the predictions of a run have in common: the run-level component that the lag swap identified. The target centroid plays the same role for the 20 text: two runs whose target centroids are similar are about similar things in the language model’s space. 

Model The prediction similarity is regressed by ordinary least squares, with an intercept, on three predictors: the target similarity, an indicator that is 1 when the two runs were recorded in the same session and 0 otherwise, and log(1 + ∆), where ∆ is the number of days between the two recordings (a fraction of a day for same-session pairs, up to 146). The logarithm compresses the strongly skewed distribution of gaps. Response and predictors are standardised to zero mean and unit variance, so each coefficient is the change in prediction similarity, in standard deviations, per standard deviation of the predictor with the other two held fixed. The model is fitted separately for every checkpoint (layer, decoder and split); Figure 7e,f shows the mean and SD over splits per layer, and Supplementary Table S3 the mean and SD over layers 1–32. 

Reading the coefficients The target-similarity coefficient measures how much of the run-to-run similarity of the predictions follows the text. It is expected to be positive for any decoder that recovers word identity at all, because the mean of noisy word predictions tracks the run’s mean target; its role here is to let the other two terms be read net of the text. A recording-session signature would make two runs from the same session more alike than their text and time gap predict, and would appear as a positive same-session coefficient. A slow drift of the recordings, or of the decoder’s mapping of them, would make runs recorded far apart less alike and would appear as a negative time-gap coefficient. The same-session indicator and the time gap are related, since a same-session pair has a gap of a few hours at most, but they are not collinear, because the cross-session pairs span the full range of gaps. The same-session coefficient is the deviation of the same-session pairs from the log-gap line extrapolated from the cross-session pairs, and therefore isolates a boundary step over and above the smooth drift, to the extent that the logarithmic form describes the drift at short gaps. The drift term is largest at layer 0 (about −0.5), where the targets carry no context, and about −0.3 at the deeper layers. 

The same-session term on its own scale The standardised coefficient of a binary predictor is its per-pair effect times the predictor’s SD divided by the response’s SD. The same-session indicator marks 79–97 of 3,570–3,655 pairs and has an SD of 0.15, so its standardised coefficient is six to seven times smaller than the extra similarity of a same-session pair in SD of the response, and the common scale of Figure 7e,f understates it next to the two continuous predictors. Supplementary Table S3 therefore adds three readings. First, the same model with the indicator left on its 0/1 scale, whose coefficient is the extra similarity of a same-session pair in SD of the response. Second, a direct contrast that assumes no drift curve: same-session pairs minus different-session pairs recorded on the same day (29–65 per split; the cap was taken off and put back between them), after regressing the response on the target similarity alone. Third, a block-permutation null for the standardised same-session term, which asks whether a coefficient of the observed size could arise from any division of the time-ordered runs into contiguous blocks, that is, from temporal contiguity alone rather than from the actual session boundaries. The 52 session sizes are kept and their order is shuffled over the time-ordered runs, so that a permuted session is still a contiguous block of runs, with 1,000 draws per checkpoint and a two-sided p. Permuted and real partitions overlap, so the test is conservative; the null’s mean is not zero (about +0 .02 without and −0.02 with the sequence model), which shows that part of a raw same-session coefficient is generic contiguity rather than a boundary step. Without a sequence model the three readings agree: 0 .31 ± 0.15 SD on the 0/1 scale (SD over layers; about 0.20 over splits), rising with depth from near zero at layers 0–2 to about 0.4 SD at 21 layers 16–32; a same-day contrast of 0 .60 ± 0.10 SD, larger than the model-based reading because the logarithmic drift absorbs part of the step; and p < 0.05 in 52% of the checkpoints, 23% at layers 1–8 and 62% at layers 9–32, in about four fifths of the layers in three of the five splits and in at most a sixth in the other two. With the sequence model the three readings are −0.11 ± 0.06 SD on the 0/1 scale, a same-day contrast of 0 .19 ± 0.08 SD, and p < 0.05 in 6% of the checkpoints, the chance rate. The decoder without a sequence model therefore carries a small session-linked component at the deeper layers, of the order of 0.1 in cosine between run centroids, and the transformer does not. 

Caveats Each run enters 84–85 pairs, so the pairs are not independent observations, and no confidence intervals or tests are reported; the across-split SD is the only error bar. Target similarity and time gap are themselves correlated (Spearman ρ between −0.4 and −0.7, depending on layer and decoder), because the text of runs recorded far apart differs more, and within a passage the log time gap and the log narrative distance in runs correlate at 0.88, because the corpus was read in recording order. The division of weight between the text and the time gap is therefore only as good as the linear model, and the size of the time-gap term depends on what else is in it. To test how much of it is carried by narrative proximity rather than by recording time, two further text controls were added: a same-passage indicator, which is 1 when both runs lie in the same continuous stretch of text (no change of source text between them), and the log of the two runs’ distance in the narrative, counted in runs within that stretch. The time-gap coefficient is −0.33 and −0.31 in the three-predictor model, −0.25 ± 0.05 and −0.28 ± 0.03 with the two text controls added, and −0.10 ± 0.04 and −0.12 ± 0.05 when the model is fitted to within-passage pairs only, with the narrative distance alongside (Supplementary Table S3). Its sign is robust, its size is not, and the regression cannot separate a drift of the recordings from a slowly varying component that follows the reader’s progress through the corpus; the layer-0 result excludes only passage tracking through the targets. The same-day contrast and the permutation null of the same-session term do not depend on this division. 

# S2 Restricted candidate set 

The retrieval metrics of the main text rank the true word among the 250–300 distinct words of an open-vocabulary pool, which cannot be compared directly with the accuracies reported for closed-vocabulary speech neuroprostheses, where one word of a fixed set of typically 50 is identified. The purpose of this analysis is to re-score the same predictions, without retraining, as a 50-way identification of a word from a fixed list, so that our numbers can be read in that form, and to add two controls that measure how much of the closed-set accuracy comes from the passage rather than from the word, because the candidates of such a pool come from different passages and a contextual target can therefore be identified through the discourse state alone. Section 2.5 summarises the design; this section gives the rules, the scoring and the results (Figure S5, Tables S10 and S11). 

Word lists Both lists were built from the full corpus of the participant, i.e. every presentation of every run, with words reduced to the punctuation-stripped, lower-cased form used for word grouping throughout. The content list holds the 50 most frequent words that are alphabetic, not in the English stop-word list of scikit-learn, and not proper names, a word being counted as a proper name when it was capitalised in more than 90% of its presentations (which removes, for example, the characters’ names and the title “Mr”). The frequent list holds the 50 most frequent words of any kind. Table S11 lists both with their corpus counts. The content words account for about 5% of all presentations and the frequent words for about 44%. 22 Pools and scoring For a given fit, the trials of the validation split whose word belongs to the list were selected, and pools were drawn that hold exactly one trial of every list word present in the split. Within a draw, the trials of each list word were put in random order and dealt out to the pools in turn: the first pool took the first trial of every word, the second pool the second trial, and so on. The number of pools per draw equals the largest trial count among the list words, and a word with fewer trials than that starts again from its first trial once its trials are used up. Every trial therefore serves as a query at least once per draw, every pool holds exactly one trial of every word, and every word contributes the same number of queries, so the accuracy is a macro-average over words. Five draws were scored and averaged. Within a pool, the prediction of every trial was compared with the projected target of every trial, with the learned temperature, and the rank of the trial’s own target was read; ties count against the model. Because every candidate is a different word, no word grouping is needed and uniform chance is k/V for top-k, with V the number of list words present in the split. The empirical permutation baseline of the main text (predictions permuted within the pool, 10 permutations) was computed as well and reproduces k/V . The two controls replace every trial’s prediction, over the full validation set and before the restriction to the list, by that of the run-mate one trial earlier (reflected at run boundaries as in Section 2.3) or of a random run-mate (one draw per pool seed), and score the result on the same pools. The within-run and random-pool variants call the main text’s pooled evaluation on the list trials alone (within-run: the list trials of one run per pool, within-run permutation null; random: cross-run pools of up to 512 list trials, word-grouped). 

Results All 330 fits were scored, and the recomputed full-set within-run gain agreed with the logged value to within 0.01 pp in every case. Every list word was present in every validation split (V = 50 throughout), with 2,464–2,698 content-word trials and 21,150–21,585 frequent-word trials per split, and a median of 29 content-word and 246 frequent-word trials per run. Figure S5 and Table S10 give the results, Table S11 the lists. Against the context-free layer-0 target, the 50-way identification of a content word from one 0.5-s window reaches 5 .1 ± 0.7% top-1 and 33 .8 ± 1.2% top-10 without a sequence model, and 4 .8 ± 0.3% and 34 .0 ± 1.3% with it (chance 2% and 20%), and both controls lie within 1.5 points of chance in top-1 (earlier-neighbour swap 2 .7 ± 0.2 and 3 .2 ± 0.4%, random run-mate 2 .9 ± 0.1 and 3 .3 ± 0.2%). In top-10 they sit further above chance, at 23 .8 ± 1.5 and 26 .1 ± 0.9% for the earlier-neighbour swap and 23 .5 ± 0.4 and 25 .6 ± 0.4% for the random run-mate (content list; 21.7–23.2% for the frequent list), as Figure S5b, e shows. This excess is not a property of the layer-0 targets, which are the same vector for every presentation of a word, but of the predictions. The decoder’s predictions carry a run-level component even at layer 0 (Section 3.5: cosine similarity 0.09 within against 0.03 between runs), which is tilted towards the words that are frequent in the run; a trial’s own word is by construction one of the words of its run, and is on average more frequent there than in a random run, so a run-mate’s prediction ranks it slightly above chance. Three features of the data fit this account. The random and the earlier-neighbour control are equal within error at layer 0, so the effect is not specific to the neighbour (the neighbour’s EEG window cannot contain the current word: words were shown for at least 500 ms, report 1, and the window is 0.5 s long). The content list is further above chance than the frequent list, because content words are passage-specific whereas function words occur in every passage. And the transformer’s controls lie above the other decoder’s, because its prediction for a run-mate has integrated the preceding EEG and carries more of the run state. The bias lifts a word into the top 10 of 50 more easily than to rank 1, which is why it shows more in top-10 than in top-1. The frequent list gives 5 .3 ± 0.1% top-1 and about 34.5% top-10 for both decoders. At layer 0, then, either decoder identifies one of 50 words at about two and a half 23 times chance in top-1 and 1.7 times chance in top-10, and the sequence model has nothing to add when the target carries no context. A single layer of context changes the closed-set numbers far more than it changes the word-level metric of the main text. Without a sequence model, content-word top-1 rises from 5.1% at layer 0 to 12 .8 ± 0.5% at layer 1 and stays between 13 and 16% through the depth (peak 15 .7 ± 0.6% at layer 18); with the sequence model it rises to 29 .6 ± 1.9% at layer 1, peaks at 33 .0 ± 2.0% at layer 8 and declines to 24 .1 ± 2.4% at layer 32, with top-10 above 80% at every contextual layer. The controls show where this comes from. The earlier-neighbour swap, which keeps the local discourse state and the position and removes the current word, rises with the accuracy itself: for the transformer it recovers 21 .9 ± 1.5 of the 29.6% at layer 1 and 27 .9 ± 1.3 of the 33.0% at layer 8; without a sequence model it recovers 7 .2 ± 0.5 of the 12.8% at layer 1 and 11 .2 ± 0.8 of the 15.4% at layer 20. The random run-mate swap, which keeps only the run-level component, stays at 6–10% for both decoders, so what the earlier neighbour recovers and a random run-mate does not is local context, consistent with the lag profiles of Section 3.5. The word-level part, the excess of the accuracy over the earlier-neighbour control, has the depth profile of Figure 2: in top-1 it peaks at layers 1–2 (5 .8 ± 1.1 pp without and 7 .7 ± 0.7 pp with the sequence model for the content list, 5 .8 ± 0.3 and 7.1 ± 0.3 pp for the frequent list) and declines to 2 .5 ± 0.2 and 2 .4 ± 1.0 pp at the last layer. In top-10 the excess is largest at layers 0–1 without a sequence model (10 .0 ± 0.8 to 14 .1 ± 1.4 pp) and is only 2–3 pp for the transformer from layer 8 on, whose top-10 accuracy is near its ceiling there. The restricted within-run gain (Figure S5c, f) peaks at layers 1–2 as well (13 .3 ± 0.9 pp without and 26 .5 ± 1.1 pp with the sequence model for the content list) and declines with depth. For the transformer this variant is not position-corrected, and the list trials of a run are spread over the whole run, so the positional embedding separates them more easily than it separates the consecutive trials of the main text’s pools; the transformer’s values in panels c and f are therefore upper bounds on word-level decoding, and the earlier-neighbour control on the balanced pools is the cleaner control for that decoder. Per word, the best-identified content words at the word-level peak are those with characteristic local contexts, the dialogue attributions “thought”, “told” and “said” and the forms of address “sir” and “yes” (top-1 at layer 1 without a sequence model: about 25, 24, 17, 18 and 18%, against a median of 12% over the 50 words), and the least identified are short frequent nouns and verbs such as “way”, “hand” and “say” (6–7%). 

Reading Three points follow. First, the numbers that are comparable to closed-vocabulary classification of attempted speech are the layer-0 ones, and they are modest: one of 50 words from a single scalp-EEG window at a few times chance. Second, closed-set accuracy against a contextual target is not word identification unless a control removes the local context; without one, the transformer’s 50-way accuracy at layer 8 would be quoted at 33% where about 5 points are the word. Third, the restriction to a candidate set is a property of the evaluation, not of the model: the same checkpoints, selected on the open-vocabulary within-run gain, produced every number here. 

# S3 Number of trainable parameters 

Supplementary Table S12 gives the number of trainable parameters of the two decoders, by component. Both use the architecture of report 1 (Marquardt et al., 2026) with 19 channels; the counts were obtained by instantiating the model with the sweep configuration and summing the sizes of all parameter tensors that receive gradients, including the affine parameters of the normalisation layers. They are the same for every target layer, because the layer changes which hidden state of the language model serves as the target, not its dimension. The decoder without a sequence model 24 has 3.03 million trainable parameters, the decoder with the transformer 6.55 million. Three points help to read the table. First, the EEG encoder holds 1.92 million parameters, most of them in the fully-connected head, which maps the 1,536 concatenated pathway features (384 attention-pooled global features and 192 × 6 pooled local features) into 512 units; the convolutional blocks of the global and local pathways together hold 0.73 million. Second, the target projection, the bias-free linear map from the 4,096-dimensional hidden state into the 256-dimensional shared space, is trained jointly with the rest of the model and accounts for 1.05 million parameters, about a third of the total without a sequence model; it acts on the targets, not on the EEG. Third, the sequence model adds 3.52 million parameters: four pre-norm transformer encoder layers of 0.79 million each, and the learned positional embedding of 1 ,400 × 256 entries (0.36 million), whose contribution the position probes of Section 3.4 measure. The counts are not logged as a metric; the training script prints them at start-up, and the table was produced by instantiating the model directly. 25 S4 Supplementary figures
