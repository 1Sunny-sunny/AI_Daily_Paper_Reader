# TOWARDS SEMANTIC RECONSTRUCTION OF INDIVIDUAL WORDS FROM fNIRS USING CLIP LOSS 

## S. Posso-Murillo †, N. Palladino ⋆, B. Pyykkonen ⋆, DY Han ‡,LG Sanchez-Giraldo †, and J. Bae † ∗

## October 7, 2026 

Abstract 

Semantic reconstruction maps neural activity to a word-embedding space, recovering the meaning of a perceived word instead of selecting it from a fixed vocabulary. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) carries semantic information suitable for this mapping. However, most fNIRS decoders are trained with a squared-error objective that fits each word independently and ignores the geometry of the embedding space. To address this limitation, we evaluate a contrastive loss based on the contrastive-language-image-pretraining (CLIP) loss, as an alternative to mean-squared-error (MSE) for reconstructing perceived words from fNIRS. We compare the two objectives by training a bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) decoder to map fNIRS signals to word embeddings. We use GloVe-50 and T5 word embeddings as targets, across three fNIRS datasets recorded under a shared paradigm pairing each word image with its spoken name. Performance is measured with a pairwise matching score and open-vocabulary top-k retrieval. The Bi-LSTM trained with CLIP is the most consistent decoder across experiments. T5 produces higher matching scores, whereas every significant retrieval result uses GloVe-50. These results support the use of contrastive objectives as a promising di-rection for fNIRS semantic decoding and motivate validation on larger datasets.

While fMRI offers excellent spatial resolution, it is constrained by the scanner environment. fNIRS is a portable, motion-tolerant alternative suited for clinical use [6]. Like fMRI, it measures the hemodynamic response to neural activity, but through the differential absorption of near-infrared light by oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated (HbR) hemoglobin. Current fNIRS studies show that the signal carries information useful for de-coding perceived speech. [7] used representational similarity analysis to demon-strate that semantic information elicited by words is encoded in fNIRS. On the classification side, [8] decoded four phonemes from a hybrid EEG-fNIRS setup over imagined and perceived speech, [9] classified overt and imagined words from a similar hybrid setup, and [10] classified imagined words from two seman-tic categories using fNIRS alone. Because these classifiers predict only words seen in training, adding a word requires retraining with a new output class. Word embeddings avoid this prob-lem by representing each word as a vector in a continuous space where semanti-cally related words lie close together, so a decoder can predict a vector and name the word by its nearest embedding without a fixed set of classes. [11] mapped fNIRS into the GloVe space [12] with ridge regression, and [13] replaced ridge with recurrent networks, removing the dependence on hand-crafted features. Both [11] and [13] regress onto word embeddings under a squared-error ob-jective. MSE only requires each prediction to stay close to its own target, with no term coupling it to the embeddings of other semantically similar words. Therefore, the structure of the embedding space is ignored. Additionally, in the recurrent model of [13], a regularization term that spreads the outputs of the neural network was necessary to avoid predictions to collapse. The contrastive CLIP objective [14], designed to align images and text by pulling matched pairs together and pushing mismatched pairs apart, has become a common tool for aligning brain activity to pretrained representations. It has been used to align fMRI with image embeddings [15], MEG and EEG with self-supervised speech audio embeddings, with substantial gains over MSE [3], and used to align word embeddings to EEg and MEG signals [2]. To our knowledge, CLIP has not been evaluated for fNIRS semantic decoding. Based on these observations, we evaluate the CLIP objective as an alter-native to MSE for fNIRS semantic decoding. We also compare two decoding targets: GloVe-50 word vectors [12], learned from word co-occurrence statis-tics, and embeddings from the pretrained transformer language model T5 [16]. Language model representations map onto fMRI responses to language [17] and have been used directly as decoding targets for EEG and MEG [2]. 1

22 METHODOLOGY 

## 2.1 fNIRS Datasets 

We evaluate on three fNIRS datasets recorded under the same word-perception paradigms. 

Cao Dataset. Seven healthy subjects viewed 50 words drawn from 10 semantic categories, each presented as a photograph of the object together with its spoken name [11]. Every word was shown 7 times for 3 s, followed by a 10 s rest period. fNIRS was recorded with 22 channels over Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas at 7.8 Hz, and the released data is preprocessed. 2

Nathan Dataset. Five healthy subjects perceived 6 words from 3 categories (vegetables, animals, and clothing). Each word was shown 20 times for 5 s, with a 10 s rest. fNIRS was recorded with 44 channels at approximately 7 .63 Hz, with bilateral coverage over the frontocentral, central, and temporoparietal areas. We convert the raw intensities to optical density, apply the modified Beer–Lambert law to recover hemoglobin concentrations, and apply a band-pass filter between 0.01 and 1 Hz for consistency with the Zinszer dataset. 

Zinszer Dataset. This dataset [7] contains 20 healthy subjects who viewed 8 words drawn from 2 semantic categories. Every word was shown 12 times for 3 s, followed by a 6 to 9 s rest period. fNIRS was recorded at 10 Hz with 24 channels in a posterior array over the occipital lobe and 18 channels in a lateral array over the left temporal, parietal, and prefrontal cortex, and we use the available preprocessed data. 3

32.3 Word Embeddings 

We use two pretrained word-embedding spaces as decoding targets. GloVe-50 

provides 50-dimensional vectors learned from global word co-occurrence statis-tics [12]. We employ it following [11] for its widespread use and adequate vo-cabulary coverage. T5 [16] embeddings are obtained by passing each stimulus word, in isolation and without context, through the t5-large encoder and ex-tracting the 1024-dimensional representation from its middle layer, as in [2].For 

T5 , words split into multiple tokens are averaged over their token embeddings. 

## 2.4 Evaluation Metrics 

2.4.1 Matching Score 

Given two test trials with ground-truth embeddings Ya, Yb and predictions ˆYa, ˆYb, we compare the correct pairing, cos( ˆYa, Ya) + cos( ˆYb, Yb), against the cross-pairing, cos( ˆYa, Yb) + cos( ˆYb, Ya). A pair is “correct” when the first sum is larger, and the subject-level score is the fraction of correct pairs [11]. 

2.4.2 Open-Vocabulary Retrieval 

Since the matching score only discriminates within a held-out pair, we also report top-k retrieval [2]. For each test trial, we rank the each w ∈ V ” by the cosine similarity between their embedding Yw and the prediction ˆY, and count the trial as “correct” if the true word is in the top k. We avoid top-1, which penalizes predicting a synonym, and set k to the number of words per category (k = 5 for Cao, k = 2 for Nathan). Zinszer has only two categories of 4 words, so we use k = 2 instead of 4. 

2.4.3 Statistical Analysis 

Given the small number of subjects (5 to 20), significance is assessed with a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test of per-subject scores against chance. We mark p ≤ 0.05 as significant ( ∗) and 0 .05 < p < 0.10 as marginal ( †). 

## 2.5 Experimental Settings 

Input. Following [11, 13], we use the 1 s HbO decoding window spanning 6 .5to 7 .5 s after stimulus onset, on all three datasets. Although the Zinszer and Nathan datasets also include the HbR signal, we only use HbO to match Cao’s data and to keep the input signal identical across datasets. 

Ridge-regression baseline. Following [2], we fit one ridge regressor per em-bedding dimension, with α chosen by leave-one-out cross-validation over a log grid from 10 −2 to 10 8.

Bi-LSTM decoder. We evaluate the Bi-LSTM network, which obtained the best matching scores across all experiments and subjects in [13]. For training, we use Adam [18] under a plateau scheduler that reduces the learning rate by 410% after 10 epochs without validation improvement, with early stopping after 20 epochs. The architecture and remaining hyperparameters depend on the pipeline ( §2.6, §2.7). 

Training objectives. We compare two objectives on the Bi-LSTM. The MSE objective is regularized with an uncentered-covariance term weighted by γ fol-lowing [13]. The CLIP objective uses the same architecture and hyperparame-ters, so the two variants differ only in the loss. 

## 2.6 Average-Trial Pipeline 

We first reproduce the neural-decoding tests from [11, 13] on the Cao dataset as a benchmark for the CLIP loss and the T5 embedding, using the Bi-LSTM configuration of [13]: 5 layers, hidden size 100, learning rate 4 .74 × 10 −3, weight decay 2 .72 × 10 −2, and γ = 2 .59 for MSE. The 7 trials of each Cao word are averaged into 50 word-vectors per subject. # 4 Conclusion 

This work compares the entropy-regularized-MSE and CLIP losses for recon-structing perceived words from fNIRS signals across three datasets. Bi-LSTM (CLIP) is the most robust decoder. It is the only decoder above chance on the single-trial matching score, it is significant on the retrieval task for two of the three datasets, and it matches or exceeds the best single-subject retrieval score on every dataset. The contrastive objective suits the reconstruction task, since word decoding depends on whether a prediction lies closer to its own target than to the alternatives, which is what the CLIP loss optimizes directly. These results are based on small datasets, and confirming them will require larger datasets. The CLIP objective is based on the InfoNCE loss [19], which motivates future work on other learning objec-tives that maximize mutual information between word embeddings and neural decoder mappings. These results are a step toward accessible non-invasive BMIs that could support communication for people who have lost the ability to speak. This study has three main limitations. First, the datasets were collected from healthy subjects, which limits clinical translation. Second, the experimental paradigm targets language perception rather than production. Third, the stimuli are isolated words rather than natural speech.
