# ABSTRACT 

Electrophysiological recordings provide powerful tools for characterizing the dynamics of neuronal networks. In vitro neuronal cultures offer a controlled setting for investigating network responses to diverse interventions, including pharmacological and optogenetic stimulation. However, conven-tional analyses often reduce electrophysiological activity to aggregate measures calculated over fixed temporal windows, providing only a static representation of the network state. Here, we introduce a comprehensive, multiparametric analytical framework designed to characterize both the temporal evo-lution of network activity and the transitional states induced by external interventions. The framework combines multiple network discriminators with a compositional analysis that resolves electrode-level responses into excited, inhibited, and unchanged states over successive temporal segments. We apply the framework to electrophysiological recordings obtained under pharmacological interventions with 4-aminopyridine (4-AP), optogenetic stimulation, and additional benchmark conditions. The results demonstrate that the proposed analysis can reveal subtle and transient responses that may be difficult to detect using conventional aggregate measures, including responses to low-dose pharmacological interventions. These findings highlight the value of temporally resolved and compositional analyses for characterizing heterogeneous neuronal network responses in vitro. 

Keywords In-vitro · Electrophysiology · Neuronal Networks 

# 1 Introduction 

In-vitro platforms offer a highly controlled experimental environment that permits precise manipulation of biological variables, thereby providing distinct advantages over in-vivo and clinical studies for isolating the mechanisms underlying neurological and neurodegenerative disorders [1]. These systems offer a powerful framework for unraveling the complex cellular and molecular mechanisms that govern disease initiation and progression. In-vitro models, in particular, enable precise interrogation of neuroinflammatory signaling networks that contribute to epileptogenesis and provide mechanistic insight into how tau pathology drives neuronal dysfunction and disease progression in Alzheimer’s disease [2, 3, 4]. Furthermore, in-vitro approaches enable real-time monitoring of how these pathogenic factors alter the functionality of the neuronal network [5]. These approaches are instrumental not only in the development and preclinical evaluation of targeted therapies, but also in assessing drug safety, toxicity, and dose–response relations prior to progression to in-vivo or clinical studies [8]. Electrophysiological approaches, such as microelectrode arrays (MEAs), provide a powerful means of interrogating the functional dynamics of neuronal cultures by capturing features such as firing rates, network synchrony, rhythmic activity, and excessive bursting, thereby enabling longitudinal assessment of neuronal network activity and organization [9]. Despite these capabilities, conventional electrophysiological metrics such as mean firing rate (MFR) or burst indices provide only static snapshots of network activity, often obscuring the temporal dynamics of neuronal excitability. Firing rate, for instance, may indicate hyperexcitability but cannot reveal whether this state is transient, sustained, or oscillatory dynamics that are critical for understanding epilepsy models or synaptic plasticity [10]. For instance, kainic acid, a widely used agent for inducing seizures in animal models, reliably produces well-characterized hyperexcitability in-vivo [11]. Interestingly, in-vitro studies of neuronal cultures have reported a reduction in firing rate following the same treatment, which could indicate the decreased neuronal excitability [12, 13]. This apparent discrepancy may arise from several factors. First, heterogeneity in the cellular composition of cultured populations may shape network-level responses and contribute to variability in the observed effects [14]. Second, transient increases in neuronal activity immediately following drug application may be masked when analyses are based solely on average firing rates. In particular, an initial surge in activity occurring within the first few seconds may be diluted by subsequent reductions in neuronal firing, resulting in an overall mean that fails to capture these rapid and potentially biologically relevant dynamics. In addition to the limitations of summary metrics such as MFR, which can obscure temporal changes in network behavior, another critical factor in in-vitro studies is the cellular type composition of the culture [9, 15]. Evoked responses in neuronal culture networks are critically shaped by factors including neuronal subtype composition, astrocytic support, developmental stage, receptor distribution, and external stimuli [15, 16]. Existing approaches rarely integrate these temporal and compositional dimensions, constraining mechanistic interpretation and predictive power in preclinical studies. These limitations are particularly consequential when stimulus-evoked changes are modest, as subtle but biologically meaningful reorganization of activity patterns may occur without substantial changes in conventional aggregate measures. Resolving how network dynamics evolve in response to perturbation therefore requires analytical frameworks capable of capturing not only the magnitude, but also the temporal structure and patterning of the response. To address these limitations, we introduce a comprehensive analytical framework that captures not only the magnitude of changes in network activity, but also their temporal evolution and persistence. We hypothesize that transient network dynamics are jointly shaped by the nature of the intervention and the cellular composition of neuronal populations—features that are poorly resolved by conventional aggregate measures. This limitation is particularly relevant for mild or subtle intervention-induced effects, for which changes in overall activity may be modest, while meaningful alterations in the temporal organization and persistence of network responses may nevertheless emerge. Beyond the cumulative spiking characteristics of the cultures, our analysis examines the statistics of inter-spike intervals (ISIs) within distinct temporal windows. This approach provides additional information on changes in the temporal organization and dynamics of network activity, enabling the identification of intervention-induced effects that may not be apparent from aggregate measures alone. A central component of the framework is the characterization of the response composition of the culture, by quantifying the relative fractions of neuronal populations exhibiting increased activity, decreased activity, or no appreciable change in response to the intervention. This population-level characterization captures the heterogeneity of network responses and provides a means of resolving subtle effects that may be masked when activity is considered only at the level of the culture as a whole. In this study, we applied the proposed framework to quantify the responses of neuronal cultures to diverse interventions and to characterize the subtle, transient dynamics of their activity over time. Our results demonstrate that these responses are influenced not only by the type of intervention, but also by the specific neuronal subtypes represented in the cultures. These findings highlight the importance of temporally resolved and cell-type-aware analyses for elucidating intervention-induced network dynamics and for improving the interpretation of in-vitro models in mechanistic studies and preclinical evaluation.

2.1 Neuron Isolation: Techniques and Protocols for Neuronal Culture Preparation 

All procedures involving neuronal isolation were conducted in strict accordance with Italian Animal Welfare legislation (D.Lgs. 26/2014), which implements the Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council on the 2A Time-Resolved Framework for Quantifying Neuronal Network State Transitions (v1: Draft) protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the competent local veterinary authorities and authorized by the Italian Ministry of Health. 

Primary Neuronal Culture Pregnant mice at embryonic day 17–18 (E17–E18) were euthanized by CO 2 inhalation. Embryos were rapidly harvested, decapitated, and transferred to ice-cold dissection buffer composed of Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, 1×; Gibco) supplemented with D-(+)-glucose (1 mM; Sigma) and HEPES (1 M; Gibco). Following careful removal of the skin and skull, whole brains were promptly isolated. The meninges were removed and the cerebral cortices dissected under clean conditions. Cortical tissue was enzymatically dissociated in 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA (Gibco) for 20 min at 37 ◦C. Digestion was quenched with high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin–streptomycin (10,000 U/mL; all from Gibco). The suspension was gently triturated, filtered to remove debris, and cells were counted. Cells were pelleted (1,900 rpm, 5 min), resuspended in Neurobasal medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin–streptomycin, and plated at 1.36 × 10 5 cells in 80 μL ( 1.7 × 10 3 cells/μL) per poly-D-lysine/laminin-coated microelectrode array (MEA) chip. Cultures were allowed to adhere for 2 h under standard incubation conditions prior to further medium addition and subsequent experiments. Following this incubation, the feeding medium was added, consisting of Neurobasal (Gibco), B27 Supplement (50 ×,Gibco), GlutaMAX Supplement (100 ×, Gibco), and Penicillin-Streptomycin. Half of the medium was replaced every 3–4 days. To ectopically express light-sensitive ion channels in neurons, cultures were transduced at 3 days in-vitro (DIV) using a viral vector encoding the candidate protein (pAAV-CaMKII-ArchT-GFP, Addgene #PV2527). The viral titer was adjusted accordingly (Titer: 8.4 × 10 9 GC per chip). Cultures were incubated overnight, and GFP or YFP expression was evaluated approximately 7 days later to confirm successful expression of the optogenetic construct. 

Mouse Embryonic Stem Cells We further applied our computational framework to additional datasets generated by an independent laboratory employing different recording systems. In these datasets, cells were prepared and characterized as previously described by Crocco et. al. [15]. Neurons were derived from mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) and differentiated into dorsal telencephalic (DT) or ventral telencephalic (VT) lineages. The final experimental groups included pure DT, pure VT, and mixed cultures at DT:VT ratios of 80:20 and 50:50 [15]. 

2.2 Electrophysiological Monitoring 

Electrical activity of neuronal cultures was recorded using the MEA2100-MiniSystem (Multi Channel Systems GmbH) at a sampling rate of 10–20 kHz. Neurons were cultured on titanium nitride (TiN) microelectrodes integrated on glass substrates with polyimide insulation and an internal reference electrode. The microelectrode array comprised an 8 × 8 configuration with 60 active electrodes (30 μm diameter, 200 μm interelectrode spacing). Spontaneous network activity typically emerged between 9–11 days in-vitro (DIV), and recordings were performed between DIV 12 and 23, when stable and robust activity was observed [17, 18]. Prior to data acquisition, cultures were allowed to equilibrate for 30 min to ensure signal stability [18]. For each recording across the experimental groups, electrophysiological data were acquired at a sampling frequency of either 20 kHz or 10 kHz, independent of the recording platform employed. Action potentials (spikes) were identified using the Precise Timing–Spike Detection (PTSD) algorithm described by Maccione et. al. [19]. This approach detects candidate events by identifying relative local extrema within a predefined peak-lifetime window and subsequently evaluating their peak-to-peak amplitude against an electrode-specific, noise-adaptive differential threshold. The detection threshold is computed individually for each electrode to account for variability in baseline noise levels. A refractory period constraint was imposed to prevent multiple detections of the same spike event, thereby preserving temporal precision and enhancing discrimination from background activity. For all mESC-derived culture groups [15], electrophysiological activity was recorded using high-density CMOS-based 4096-channel microelectrode arrays (Accura, 3Brain). This platform enabled high-resolution characterization of network-wide spiking and bursting dynamics. Spike detection was carried out using the previously established PTSD protocol [19], ensuring analytical consistency across experimental conditions and datasets. To avoid unreliable estimates arising from insufficient statistical sampling, electrodes exhibiting a firing rate below 

0.1 s −1 were excluded from further analysis and classified as inactive. 2.4 Analytical Approach 

The method presented in this work is based on quantifying changes in neuronal network activity by comparing a measured or derived observable (hereafter referred to as a discriminator) between two defined time windows. The framework requires the electrophysiological dataset to provide the following elements: 1. Defined baseline window: The baseline window corresponds to the period of recording preceding the application of the external intervention or stimulation. Changes in the activity of the culture are quantified relative to the baseline, providing a reference for assessing intervention-induced effects. 2. Control group: A control condition in which no intervention-induced changes are expected a priori , apart from the intrinsic fluctuations of network activity. These fluctuations are used to characterize the background variability, or noise, against which intervention-induced changes are estimated. 3. Reference data: A dataset or experimental condition providing a reference against which the magnitude of an observed effect can be interpreted. For example, in a dose–response experiment, measurements obtained at different drug concentrations can serve as reference conditions for evaluating the relative magnitude of the response. Ideally, these three elements should be provided by independent datasets or experimental conditions. However, de-pending on the experimental design, some of them may be derived from the same dataset or shared across multiple conditions. The framework is therefore designed to accommodate different experimental configurations while maintain-ing a consistent basis for quantifying and interpreting changes in network activity. See Table 1 for details regarding datasets roles in the experiments of this work.

In mature cultured neuronal networks, the ISI distribution frequently exhibits a bimodal structure reflecting two distinct temporal scales: an intra-burst interval, typically ranging from a few milliseconds to a few tens of milliseconds, and an inter-burst interval, typically on the order of hundreds of milliseconds. Such behavior is commonly associated with synchronized network dynamics. Under different experimental conditions, however, alternative distributional profiles may emerge. 2.5.3 Kullback–Leibler Divergence 

The Kullback–Leibler divergence, DKL , is a statistical metric that quantifies the difference between two probability density functions. Since the mean or the mode of an ISI distribution can remain unchanged while its modality or overall shape changes, DKL provides a robust measure of distributional changes over time [27]. captures the extent of these changes, it does not reveal their direction or physiological interpretation, such as whether they reflect faster or slower neuronal dynamics. It is worth noting that DKL inherently quantifies the difference between two probability distributions and therefore provides an alternative measure of change between datasets. In this sense, 

DKL can serve as an alternative to ∆ξ when the discriminator is represented by a probability distribution rather than a scalar quantity.

# 4 Discussion 

In this study, we introduce a quantitative analytical framework for characterizing intervention-induced changes in neuronal network activity. Rather than reducing neuronal responses to changes in aggregate activity alone, the proposed EIU framework quantifies alterations in the underlying organization of network activity. We evaluated its performance across neuronal cultures subjected to distinct perturbation paradigms, including acute pharmacological stimulation with 4-aminopyridine (4-AP), prolonged optogenetic inhibition, and an independent inter-laboratory dataset used as a benchmark [15]. Together, these experimental conditions provided a range of response magnitudes and temporal profiles against which the sensitivity and interpretability of the framework could be assessed. A central limitation of conventional electrophysiological metrics, particularly MFR, is that they compress complex temporal responses into a single measure of activity magnitude [33, 34]. Although such metrics remain highly informative for identifying pronounced changes in network excitability, averaging can obscure biologically meaningful differences in the temporal structure of the response [33, 34]. For example, a strong but transient initial response may be substantially attenuated when firing activity is averaged over a longer observation window, potentially obscuring short-lived changes in neuronal activity [35]. In contrast, the EIU framework retains information regarding both the temporal organization and persistence of intervention-induced changes, thereby distinguishing response profiles that may produce similar aggregate activity levels (Figure 7). This distinction is particularly relevant when interventions induce dynamic or nonstationary responses rather than sustained monotonic changes in activity (Figure 8). A second limitation of conventional summary metrics becomes apparent when intervention effects are small or moderate. Subtle alterations in spike timing or temporal organization may occur in the absence of substantial changes in average firing rate, making such effects difficult to resolve using rate-based measures alone [36]. Under such conditions, averaging can reduce the separation between experimental groups, limiting the ability of conventional statistical tests to detect intervention-induced effects [37, 38]. This behavior was particularly evident in the 4-AP intervention groups, where relatively modest changes were not consistently resolved using conventional metrics Figure 6 . We therefore investigated whether distribution-derived descriptors could increase sensitivity while retaining a direct relationship to established electrophysiological quantities. The shift in the peak of the ISI distribution, for example, provided a more sensitive representation of intervention-induced changes than conventional averaging and produced a modest improvement in effect size, as quantified by Cohen’s (d) (Figure 9). These findings indicate that incorporating information from the underlying activity distribution can improve the detectability of subtle network responses. The strongest discrimination, however, was obtained using ( DKL ), which captures changes in the overall shape and modality of the ISI distribution rather than changes in a single summary statistic Figure 9. Consequently, ( DKL ) was particularly sensitive to small-to-moderate perturbations that were difficult to distinguish using conventional metrics Figure 9. This enhanced sensitivity comes at the cost of directional interpretability: A divergence measure quantifies the magnitude of the difference between two activity distributions but does not, by itself, indicate the physiological direction of the underlying change [39]. Thus, a large divergence establishes that the network state has changed substantially, but does not directly reveal whether neuronal activity has shifted toward faster or slower firing dynamics. To overcome this limitation, we developed EIU compositional analysis , which integrates the sensitivity of distribution-based divergence with information about the direction and structure of the underlying electrophysiological change. This representation enabled not only discrimination between experimental groups exposed to different intervention strengths or doses, but also characterization of how network activity changed (Figure 6). Importantly, this improvement was supported quantitatively by effect-size analysis. Cohen’s (d) increased substantially for several metrics when the EIU framework was applied, with the largest improvements observed for mean ISI and MFR, whereas ( DKL ), which already exhibited relatively strong discrimination, showed no further increase in effect size (Figure 9). The framework therefore does not simply generate an alternative visualization of neuronal activity; rather, it increases the statistical separability of network states, particularly when the underlying intervention produces subtle or moderate effects. Notably, the largest gain in effect size was observed when the EIU framework was applied to MFR (Figure 9). This result is important because it indicates that the improvement does not depend exclusively on the choice of a particular electrophysiological feature, such as ISI. Instead, the framework can enhance the information extracted even from a conventional metric such as MFR by changing how intervention-induced deviations are represented and compared. The relative performance of individual input metrics may therefore depend on the biological question and perturbation under investigation, whereas the principal advantage of the EIU framework lies in its ability to amplify otherwise subtle differences between network states. 16 A Time-Resolved Framework for Quantifying Neuronal Network State Transitions (v1: Draft) An important consideration is that the gain in statistical separability provided by the EIU framework was not uniform across all input metrics or effect magnitudes. In particular, when the underlying electrophysiological descriptor already exhibited relatively strong discrimination between experimental conditions, as observed for ( DKL ), transformation into the EIU representation did not further increase the effect size and could produce a comparable or slightly reduced Cohen’s (d) (Figure 9). One possible explanation is the bounded nature of the compositional representation. Because (E+I+U=1) and the responsiveness metric is defined as (R=E+I), (R) is constrained between 0 and 1. As the proportion of time points classified as responsive approaches unity, increasingly large changes in the underlying electrophysiological signal cannot produce proportional increases in (R). This potential saturation should therefore be considered when interpreting strongly separated conditions. The EIU framework should consequently be viewed primarily as a complementary approach for enhancing the detection and characterization of subtle or moderate, temporally persistent responses, rather than as a transformation expected to increase effect size under all experimental conditions. Collectively, these findings suggest that the EIU framework occupies a complementary position between conventional summary statistics and purely distribution-based divergence measures. Conventional metrics such as MFR provide intuitive directional information but can lose temporal and distributional structure through averaging, whereas ( DKL )

offers greater sensitivity to changes in network-state distributions but lacks an intrinsic indication of physiological direction [33, 34, 39]. EIU compositional analysis combines these complementary properties, providing both enhanced sensitivity and interpretable characterization of intervention-induced changes. Across the perturbation paradigms examined here, this advantage was most evident for small-to-moderate effects, for which conventional approaches showed limited statistical separation. The framework may therefore provide a broadly applicable quantitative strategy for detecting and characterizing subtle changes in neuronal network dynamics that would otherwise remain concealed within conventional aggregate measures.

In addition, the framework can be used to optimize stimulation parameters in optogenetic experiments. For example, in the present study we employed prolonged light-on windows to evaluate ArchT-mediated responses in neuronal cultures. Interestingly, the faster-spiking state observed in the ArchT-LS condition may be compatible with post-illumination rebound firing previously reported in ArchT-expressing neurons; however, identifying the underlying mechanism was beyond the scope of the present computational study[40, 41]. Depending on the experimental objective, stimulation duration and frequency can be systematically adjusted while continuously tracking culture trajectories, thereby enabling optimization of the desired functional outcome. The benchmark dataset provided an important complementary validation of the proposed framework. In contrast to the more subtle responses observed in the other experimental paradigms, bicuculline induced pronounced changes across all culture compositions [15]. Under these conditions, where the experimental groups were already well separated by conventional statistical measures, a substantial further increase in effect size following transformation was not necessarily expected. Nevertheless, the benchmark dataset revealed an important feature of the network response that extended beyond statistical separability. Across μ, DKL , and the other evaluated metrics, the responses increased rapidly following bicuculline exposure and subsequently approached relatively stable plateaus Figure 6. Importantly, although all culture groups responded strongly to the same pharmacological perturbation, they converged toward different response plateaus, with the magnitude of these responses varying according to culture composition Figure 6. This observation indicates that the effect of a common perturbation cannot be characterized solely by whether a response occurs or by its statistical significance; the resulting response state also reflects differences among the underlying neuronal populations [15]. Thus, the benchmark analysis demonstrates that the value of the proposed framework is not restricted to enhancing the detection of subtle effects, but also extends to characterizing distinct network response states when intervention-induced changes are already pronounced. In this study, 4-AP was expected to increase neuronal activity; however, the observed response was characterized by a reduction in activity, contrary to the anticipated excitatory effect [42, 43]. Only a modest initial change in activity was detected, followed by a reduction in MFR during continued 4-AP exposure see Figure 6. One possible interpretation is that sustained exposure to 4-AP induced a progressive reduction in network responsiveness, producing a desensitization-like pattern of activity rather than persistent excitation [44, 45]. In this scenario, prolonged 4-AP-induced hyperexcitability may recruit homeostatic mechanisms that progressively suppress or stabilize neuronal firing, thereby contributing to the observed reduction in MFR [46]. However, because the underlying cellular mechanisms were not directly investigated, our data cannot distinguish pharmacological desensitization from other activity-dependent or homeostatic adaptations. To determine whether this unexpected reduction reflected a persistent alteration in network function, cultures were reassessed 24 h after 4-AP removal (Fig. 10). MFR was no longer significantly different from pre-exposure levels, indicating recovery of network activity and suggesting that the effect was reversible. This recovery is consistent with a transient adaptive or desensitization-like response to 4-AP rather than persistent impairment of neuronal function [46]. 

# Conclusion 

In conclusion, we developed a time-resolved EIU compositional framework for characterizing intervention-induced changes in neuronal network activity beyond conventional aggregate electrophysiological measures. By representing activity as excited, inhibited, and unchanged states relative to baseline, the framework provides complementary information on the magnitude, direction, and temporal evolution of network responses. Across pharmacological, optogenetic, and independent benchmark datasets, the greatest advantage of the EIU framework was observed for small-to-moderate responses, where conventional measures showed limited separation between experimental conditions and the compositional transformation enhanced statistical discriminability. In contrast, when the underlying response was already strongly distinguishishable, the framework provided additional value by characterizing response direction, temporal evolution, and differences in the resulting network states rather than necessarily further increasing effect size.
