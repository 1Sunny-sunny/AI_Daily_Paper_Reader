## ABSTRACT 

Electroencephalography (EEG) recorded for motor-imagery (MI) brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) carries subject-identifying structure that persists across sessions, allowing separately released sessions from the same person to be linked. Exist-ing EEG identity protections use learned or additive perturbations and operate on representations other than the covariance geometry underlying MI pipelines. We ask whether cross-session identity leakage can be suppressed directly in this covariance representation without learning a privacy model. We introduce a Rie-mannian transform that equalizes two subject-conditioned geometric statistics: the Riemannian mean covariance and the tangent-space scatter around it, with two op-tional band-specific refinements. Ablations show that mean alignment and scatter whitening carry nearly all of the suppression, while the optional stages contribute small, dataset-dependent refinements. Across two public datasets and five attack-ers spanning four covariance-space representations, the worst attacker falls from 90.8% to 15.6% on BCI Competition IV-2a and from 90.8% to 9.9% on OpenBMI, at a cost of 7.0 and 2.1 points of MI decoding accuracy relative to the 8–30 Hz in-put, and no aggregation rule exceeds chance by more than two points at any batch size. The transform requires no learned privacy model, surrogate network, or gradient-based training, and is fitted on one session and applied unchanged to a held-out later session. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Motor-imagery (MI) brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) support communication and control for peo-ple with severe motor impairment (Wolpaw et al., 2002), as well as neurological rehabilitation (Daly & Wolpaw, 2008), robotic device control (Edelman et al., 2019), and awareness assessment in disor-ders of consciousness (Li et al., 2016). Advancing the field requires shared data, and the benchmarks supply it by releasing every subject and session as recorded: BCI Competition IV-2a (Tangermann et al., 2012) publishes two sessions from each of nine subjects, OpenBMI (Lee et al., 2019) from each of fifty-four. Cross-dataset transfer learning depends on pooling them (Wu et al., 2022), and recent EEG foundation models aggregate at far greater scale still (Jiang et al., 2024). This creates a risk distinct from the task the data was collected for: EEG carries private information beyond the intended signal (Ienca et al., 2018; Xia et al., 2022). Resting-state EEG alone supports subject identification at high accuracy (Choi et al., 2018), deep sequence models identify users from task recordings (Sun et al., 2019), and side-channel work has shown that credentials and personal asso-ciations can be inferred from BCI signals (Martinovic et al., 2012). The specific risk we address is cross-session linkage . Given two separately released sessions, an adversary can determine which recordings belong to the same individual, and thereby aggregate a person’s data across releases, sites, or time. Meng et al. (2023) make the consequence concrete: linked sessions make inference about health status or study participation far more reliable than any single release permits, which discourages participation in data collection. Existing EEG privacy work addresses adjacent problems. Cryptographic and secure-computation methods fit a model without any party seeing raw signals but never produce a shareable dataset. Perturbation-based methods do defend a release, but target a different representation and release model (Section 2). Our setting differs on three counts. The transform requires no learned privacy model or gradient-based training; its subject-specific transforms are estimated directly from covari-1arXiv:2610.15626v1 [cs.CR] 11 Oct 2026 ance statistics of a calibration session. Every released session is defended, so the adversary compares defended data against defended data rather than a perturbed training set against a clean test set: the protection is a property of each released recording, not of what a classifier can be made to learn. And the representation is spatial covariance, which MI decoding is built on and identity attackers read, yet no existing defense targets. The core difficulty is that identity and task occupy the same signal. MI decoding relies on event-related desynchronisation in the μ (8–13 Hz) and β (13–30 Hz) rhythms, expressed through spatial covariance, while identity is encoded in the structure of that same covariance. A defense that re-moves one may therefore degrade the other. We hypothesize that persistent cross-session identity is carried by complementary subject-specific statistics, manifold location and tangent-space scat-ter, and ask whether jointly equalizing them can suppress identity while preserving task-relevant structure. 

Contributions. We identify subject-specific covariance mean and tangent-space scatter as com-plementary sources of cross-session identity leakage, and jointly equalize them with a Riemannian transform. Ablations show they account for nearly all suppression, with band-specific stages provid-ing only dataset-dependent refinements (Section 7). The pipeline substantially reduces five attackers spanning four covariance-space representations on two public datasets, from over 90% to 15.6% and 9.9%, at a measurable utility cost (Section 6). At the session level, mean-tangent aggregation strengthens undefended linkage to 100% and 90.7%, but does not materially exceed chance after defense, no rule by more than two points (Section 6.2). Geometric alignment and prior perturbation-based protection address a different regime (Section 8). 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Resting-state EEG supports subject identification at high accuracy (Choi et al., 2018), and identity persists in task-recorded EEG across paradigms (Sun et al., 2019). Meng et al. (2023) state the consequence directly: identity is learned easily enough that separate sessions from one user can be associated, making private inference more reliable than any single release permits. Aggregation makes this worse, since LaBraM pre-trains on roughly 2,500 hours from around twenty datasets (Jiang et al., 2024) and the larger the pool, the more releases an adversary can cross-reference. Ex-isting protections divide three ways. The closest prior work makes EEG “identity-unlearnable” by perturbing the training release, sample-wise or user-wise, so a user-classifier trained on it fails to generalise (Meng et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2021); Chen et al. (2025) extend this to four pertur-bation types and Meng et al. (2024) to gender and BCI-experience, while Singh et al. (2023) train an autoencoder that outputs an anonymised signal and ID-RemovalNet (Wang et al., 2025) strips identity from learned features before decoding. All are evaluated against deep time-domain classi-fiers, and the perturbation methods pair a perturbed training release with clean test data, a different attacker family and release model from ours. A parallel line makes the data unreadable rather than unidentifying: secure multiparty computation fits a model without any party observing raw signals (Agarwal et al., 2019), and non-invertible neural encoding obscures feature values while preserv-ing class separability (Khanam et al., 2026); both target confidentiality within a single session, and neither measures re-identification or cross-session linkage. Third, Euclidean Alignment (He & Wu, 2020) and related per-subject covariance normalisations, standard for cross-subject MI transfer, are sometimes assumed to confer privacy by construction. That assumption is only partly correct: EA suppresses re-identification substantially but stops far short of chance (Section 8), so a practitioner running it has protection and no way to know how much. None of these targets the representation the attackers read. MI decoding is built on spatial co-variance: Common Spatial Patterns and its filter-bank extension (Ang et al., 2008) estimate spatial filters from class covariances, Riemannian tangent-space classification (Barachant et al., 2012) op-erates on the SPD manifold of trial covariances, and the alignment techniques behind cross-subject transfer (Zanini et al., 2018; He & Wu, 2020) are covariance transforms. A defense built elsewhere in the pipeline does not directly target these covariance-space attacks. Second-order and tangent-space alignment methods (Sun et al., 2016; Bleuz´ e et al., 2022) match distributions by whitening and recolouring, but were developed for domain adaptation rather than cross-session EEG identity suppression. ## 3 THREAT MODEL 

A data owner holds recordings from U subjects across two or more sessions, and every released session is passed through the defense. There is no clean copy behind it: the defended data is the data. Membership inference, and the poisoned-training-set setting of Meng et al. (2023) where only the training release is perturbed, are different release models. The adversary sees only defended trials plus subject labels for a reference session, trains a re-identification classifier from scratch on it, then receives defended trials from a different session and links them to a subject. It never receives the fitted transform, and may preprocess the release however it wishes. Our metric is re-identification accuracy on the held-out session, with chance at 100 /U ; utility is MI decoding accuracy on the same defended data. Our privacy metric reports the worst case over the evaluated attackers, and therefore provides a lower bound on leakage. We measure it at two levels: trial-level identification, the metric used throughout the covariance-attacker literature, and session-level linkage, which aggregates the trials a subject contributes into one decision. The second is what the threat model concerns, since evidence weak per trial may be decisive once pooled.

Configuration. RMA and SCW form the core transform, while BCA and cleanup are optional refinements whose strengths may be set to zero. BCI-IV-2a uses BCA only, while OpenBMI uses both. Section 7 shows that RMA and SCW account for nearly all suppression, with the refinements shifting the worst attacker by less than one point. The privacy stages use fifth-order Butterworth band-pass filters in second-order-section form, applied with zero phase, while the MI decoding baselines use the reference FBCSP toolbox Chebyshev II filter bank; Appendix N shows the two are equivalent across the 8–30 Hz band and records which design each component uses. 

## 5 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets and protocol. BCI Competition IV-2a : 9 subjects, 22 channels, 4-class MI (left hand, right hand, feet, tongue), 2 sessions, 288 trials each, 250 Hz. Chance re-identification 11.1%; chance MI 25%. OpenBMI : 54 subjects, 20 channels (motor montage), 2 sessions, resampled to 250 Hz. Chance re-identification 1.85%. The transform is fit on Session 1, the attacker trained on defended Session 1 and tested on defended Session 2. All primary results are cross-session; the single-session baseline of Appendix F is separate. We evaluate five covariance-space attackers across four represen-tations: SVM (broadband covariance), XDAWN+SVM (evoked-augmented covariance), SPDNet-ID (deep SPD-manifold network), CSP-SVM and CSP-LDA (narrow-band spatial patterns). 

Attacker configuration. Attacker configurations are fixed independently of Session 2 except for XDAWN, reporting an oracle maximum over a predefined set of filter counts, nfilter ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8}.This oracle selection provides a conservative upper bound on XDAWN attacker performance by giving the attacker the best preprocessing configuration on the held-out session; the selected count is 8 undefended and 1 defended on BCI-IV-2a. Tangent-space SVM: OAS covariance, Riemannian tangent space, RBF SVC at scikit-learn defaults. XDAWN+SVM: OAS-estimated 

XdawnCovariances with the evoked filters applied. CSP-SVM and CSP-LDA: one-versus-rest CSP on trace-normalised covariances, then an RBF SVC and an LDA respectively on the same fea-tures. SPDNet-ID: BiMap dimensions c → c/ 2 → c/ 4, 400 epochs, Adam at 5 × 10 −4, batch 64, early stopping at patience 40 on a 10% stratified split, seeded to 42. SPDNet-ID tracks label-space size rather than signal, falling from 87.0% on 9 OpenBMI subjects to 66.1% on all 54 (Appendix B). 

Utility decoders. FBCSP (Ang et al., 2008), EEGNet (Lawhern et al., 2018), DeepConvNet (Schirrmeister et al., 2017), and FBCNet (Mane et al., 2021), trained and evaluated on the same defended data. One utility decoder was fixed per dataset before operating-point selection and held fixed throughout: FBCNet on BCI-IV-2a, EEGNet on OpenBMI. The admissibility floor and every reported cost therefore use the same architecture, measured against that decoder’s own undefended accuracy. All four decoders are reported in Table 1. 

Operating-point selection. Session 1 is split per subject by trial order into an inner enrolment and query set. The transform is fitted on enrolment data, and privacy and utility are evaluated on the query split, leaving Session 2 untouched. Let u0 be the decoder’s undefended query accuracy. A grid cell is admissible if its decoder accuracy is at least u0 − s. Among admissible cells, we select the one with the lowest worst-case attacker accuracy. The grid is γrma ∈ { 0.8, 0.9, 1.0},

γscw ∈ { 0.35 , 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8}, α ∈ { 0.3, 0.6} and β ∈ { 0, 0.2}. The selected setting is refit on full Session 1 and evaluated once on Session 2. The utility slack s is the one free choice in the procedure. We use s = 0 .5 for BCI-IV-2a and s = 2 .5 for OpenBMI. Appendix J gives the full sensitivity analysis.

An adversary holding a released session holds every trial in it, and evidence weak per trial may become decisive once pooled. We therefore build subject templates from defended Session 1 and aggregate N defended Session 2 trials into one decision under four rules (Appendix H). Aggregation does help an undefended adversary: under the mean-tangent rule, accuracy rises from 88.3% at one trial to 100% at fifty on BCI-IV-2a, and from 82.5% to 90.7% on OpenBMI, so trial-level accuracy understates undefended risk. Under defense no aggregation rule exceeds chance by more than two percentage points at any batch size. The mean-tangent rule in fact declines as more trials are added, because the residual variation the transform leaves is centred on the population mean rather than a subject-specific mean. The geometry says the same without a classifier: same-subject cross-session distance relative to cross-subject distance rises from 0.47 to 1.05 and from 0.37 to 1.00, so after the transform two sessions from one person are no closer than two from different people. Aggregation therefore reaches chance where trial-level identification does not, which is where the linkage threat is defined. 6.3 IDENTITY AND TASK , MEASURED THE SAME WAY 

Identity and task can be compared with one instrument: the squared deviation of a group mean log band power from its reference, in units of the relevant within-group trial variance. Under the trans-form, subject deviation retains 13.8% on BCI-IV-2a against 55.8% for class deviation, and 20.9% against 44.3% on OpenBMI. Under this matched band-power measure, identity-related structure is therefore attenuated more strongly than task-related structure (Figure 3; Appendix I). 6.4 WHERE THE TRANSFORM STOPS 

All attackers above read spatial covariance, the object the transform manipulates, so we also evaluate two that do not: per-channel band power over 8–30 Hz with an RBF SVM, which discards cross-channel structure, and EEGNet trained for subject identification. Both fall on both datasets. On BCI-IV-2a bandpower drops from 84.3% to 72.7% and EEGNet-ID from 94.4% to 83.3%, on OpenBMI from 69.0% to 33.6% and 79.1% to 38.1%. These attacks remain far above chance, so we scope the claim to covariance-space protection. The deep time-domain case is complementary to Meng et al. (2023), as examined in Section 8.

Tying the two broadband strengths is worse on BCI-IV-2a, but on OpenBMI the tied setting at 0.9 reaches 6.7%, below 9.9%, at 2.3 points of EEGNet accuracy; adopting it on a cross-session measurement would be the test-set selection the protocol exists to avoid. Appendix J sweeps all four strengths on both datasets, and a 44 sweep of 256 configurations bounds what blind selection gives up. 

## 8 BASELINE COMPARISONS 

Geometric alignment is a weak de-identifier. Under an identical protocol, Euclidean Align-ment reduces the worst attacker from 90.8% to 69.0% on BCI-IV-2a and from 90.8% to 48.4% on OpenBMI while raising decoding accuracy (Table 3). That combination is the signature of a transfer normalisation: it removes between-subject variation, where identity partly lives. A recentring-and-scaling baseline after Riemannian Procrustes Analysis (RPA) (Rodrigues et al., 2019) goes further, recentring each subject to the identity and rescaling their dispersion by a scalar where SCW whitens the tangent space. It is not RPA as published: we omit the rotation step, which Rodrigues et al. (2019) report barely improves MI classification, and generalise from the pairwise form to a cohort target. We write RPA-RS for this variant. It leaves the worst attacker at 46.3% and 54.5%, bound by XDAWN or tangent-space SVM rather than CSP attackers it suppresses (Table 3). On BCI-IV-2a it buys that at a smaller decoding cost than ours, so the two sit on one frontier; on OpenBMI it does not, reading 63.6% EEGNet against our 63.1% (Table 3) while leaving five times the leakage. Neither is adequate: six and twenty-six times chance for EA, four and twenty-nine for RPA-RS. A practitioner running either for transfer has protection they did not ask for and cannot quantify. 

A published attack, on its own terms. The five attackers of Section 5 are constructed for this paper. Appendix F reproduces the single-session identification method of Bak & Jeong (2023) on OpenBMI at 99.78% against their reported 98.97%, and drives it to HTER 0.494 on the 20-channel montage, approximately chance-level, and 0.430 on a 36-channel reconstruction. 8Perturbation-based protection covers a different representation. Meng et al. (2023) add a learned user-wise perturbation to each subject’s trials so that a user-classifier trained on the per-turbed release fails to generalise. We reproduce it and run each method against the other’s attackers (Appendix E). Their perturbation reduces deep time-domain identification sharply, EEGNet from 68.7% to 8.5% and ShallowConvNet to 19.9%, and leaves the covariance attackers untouched, the worst of our five staying at 90.8% and none moving by more than 0.3 points. Ours is the mirror im-age, reducing all five covariance attackers to 15.6% while leaving EEGNet identification at 83.3% (Section 6.4). The two are therefore complementary rather than competing, and built for differ-ent release models: ours defends every released session, theirs perturbs a training release evaluated against clean data. Whether a single release can be protected in both representations at once is open. 

Differential privacy. To our knowledge, existing DP work in BCI has focused on privacy during model training rather than release-time protection against cross-session EEG re-identification (Xia et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2025). Under our trial-covariance release formulation, standard Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms (Dwork & Roth, 2014) suppress covariance-space attackers but reduce MI decoding to near-chance performance: worst-case re-identification falls to 21.7–34.2% in central mode and 28.4–29.6% in local mode, while decoder accuracy remains only 29%–36% against 25% chance, compared with 64.9% under our transform. Under the stated adjacency, the calibrated noise dominates the covariance signal ( ∆1 = 44 versus entries of order 1/22 ), and a ten-fold increase in ε changes neither axis materially. Thus, under this direct covariance-release formulation, the calibrated DP mechanisms do not retain useful MI accuracy. Appendix M gives the formulation and full results. Central mode was evaluated in full on BCI-IV-2a and, given the resulting utility collapse, was not repeated on OpenBMI. 

## 9 STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE 

Appendix K reports subject-level paired differences with bootstrap 95% CIs, Cohen’s dz , and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against the band-limited baseline. All five covariance-space attack-ers decrease significantly on both datasets, by 57.6–88.4 points, with large effects. Utility losses are much smaller and less consistent: on BCI-IV-2a, FBCSP and FBCNet decrease significantly (p = 0 .039 and 0.004 ), whereas EEGNet and DeepConvNet do not ( p = 0 .16 and 0.20 ). 

## 10 CONCLUSION 

Subject identity in motor-imagery EEG survives across recording sessions in the spatial covari-ance that classical BCI pipelines consume. Our results show that this persistent identity is strongly expressed through two geometric properties of this representation: the subject-specific covariance mean and the tangent-space scatter around it. A Riemannian transform that jointly equalizes these statistics reduces five attackers spanning four covariance-space representations to 15.6% and 9.9% on two public datasets, at costs of 7.0 and 2.1 points for the fixed utility decoder on each dataset. Ablations show that mean alignment and scatter whitening are the load-bearing components, with the remaining stages providing only small dataset-dependent refinements. The transform is fitted on one session and applied unchanged to a held-out later session, and suppresses covariance-space identity substantially more strongly than standard alignment baselines. The result is deliberately scoped. The transform does not provide universal EEG anonymization: attackers operating directly on time-domain signals or per-channel power retain substantial identity information. Rather, it establishes that cross-session covariance-space identity can be strongly sup-pressed with a fixed geometric map while preserving considerably more task information. Future work should determine whether complementary mechanisms can jointly suppress identity across covariance and time-domain representations, and evaluate linkage between independently held-out future sessions and under stronger adversarial knowledge of the fitted transform.

## G SUB -BAND OPERATION 

Stages 1–2 equalise the 8–30 Hz covariance as one object, and Section 7 shows that a stage which re-aligns a narrower slice too aggressively can re-introduce structure CSP reads. That raises the question of whether an attacker gains by the transform being applied within a narrow band rather than across the whole release. We therefore repeat the defense with the transform fitted and applied inside each of three narrow bands, adding the residual 8–30 Hz component back untouched, and read the released signal with the same CSP attackers at the matching band. The release is band-limited to 8–30 Hz in every condition, so only the band the transform operates on varies. “Stacked” is the strongest of the three narrow-band attackers, the choice an adversary free to pick a band would make. At the reported operating points the attacker does not gain. CSP-SVM, which binds the worst case on BCI-IV-2a, is lower in every narrow band than at full band, 12.8–13.4 against 15.6, and on OpenBMI it falls from 2.8 to 2.1–2.5. CSP-LDA rises on BCI-IV-2a, from 3.5% to at most 6.8%, and is flat on OpenBMI within two points. No band, and no choice among bands, lifts either attacker above the full-band worst case of 15.6% and 9.9%. The mechanism Section 7 identifies is therefore reachable only by mis-setting a strength, not by the adversary’s choice of band: it is α = 1 .0 and β = 1 .0 that let CSP recover (Tables 12 and 13), and both lie outside the operating-point selection grid of Section 5.

Subject templates are built from defended Session 1; N defended trials from Session 2 are drawn at random and aggregated into a single decision. ## M FULL DIFFERENTIAL PRIVACY RESULTS 

Formulation. Adjacency : two releases differ in the covariance of a single trial. Released object :the trial covariance, trace-normalised to unit trace, so ∥C∥F ≤ tr( C) = 1 and ∥C∥1 ≤ c; under replacement this gives ∆2 ≤ 2 and ∆1 ≤ 2c with c the channel count. Calibration : Laplace noise of scale ∆1/ε for ε-DP, and Gaussian noise of standard deviation ∆2

p2 ln(1 .25 /δ )/ε for (ε, δ )-DP at δ = 10 −5 (Dwork et al., 2006; Dwork & Roth, 2014). The noise matrix is drawn symmetrically, as when releasing a covariance (Dwork et al., 2014), and eigenvalues are clipped to keep the result positive semi-definite, which is post-processing and consumes no budget. The adjacency relation and the sensitivity bound are ours: neither work releases a per-trial covariance, and the local mode is a local-DP model rather than the aggregate release those papers analyse. Both modes suppress re-identification, and both destroy the task. Every DP condition leaves the decoders between 29% and 36% against 25% chance, where the proposed transform leaves 64.9%. Raising ε from 1 to 10 changes nothing measurable on either axis, because at the sensitivity these bounds require the noise dominates the signal at every setting in the sweep: at ε = 1 the Laplace scale is ∆1 = 44 against covariance entries of order 1/22 .FBCSP could not be fitted under central mode on any subject, and the reason is informative. ## N FILTER DESIGN : B UTTERWORTH VS . C HEBYSHEV II 

The toolbox Chebyshev II design and the Butterworth design are interchangeable over the band that matters here: both are within 3 dB at the edges and flat in between. They differ only in roll-off outside the band, where Chebyshev II is sharper at the cost of stopband ripple. The 60 dB figure that motivates the apparent conflict comes from supplying [8 , 30] Hz as the stop-band rather than the passband, which attenuates both edges by 60 dB and removes 80–87% of μ-band power. That is a specification error rather than a property of the filter family, and Figure 5 shows the three curves together so the distinction is visible.
