## ABSTRACT 

Contrastive learning for time series depends on deciding which pairs of inputs should be treated as similar and dissimilar. Because few augmentations reli-ably preserve meaning in time series, prior methods have relied on two disjoint notions of similarity between windows: structural similarity, which relates win-dows with similar morphology, and temporal similarity, which relates windows that occur close together in time. We introduce TEX-MEX, to our knowledge the first approach to unify the two into a single relationship. Given a random batch of windows drawn across the dataset, TEX-MEX uses the encoder to iden-tify which windows are structurally similar or dissimilar to each other. It then pulls each window toward the temporally shifted versions of similar windows and pushes it away from those of dissimilar ones, learning what typically accom-panies similar windows in time. Across PPG, EEG, ECG, accelerometry, and continuous glucose monitoring, TEX-MEX broadly achieves state-of-the-art per-formance against contrastive, reconstructive, JEPA-based, and modality-specific approaches, establishing it as a strong general SSL objective. We further train a 560K-parameter PPG foundation model that achieves state-of-the-art performance across a broad range of downstream benchmarks while being roughly 50 × smaller than the next-best unimodally pretrained PPG foundation model in our evaluation. We will release this foundation model open-source and open-weight to the com-munity to support future work and practical applications. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as the dominant paradigm for learning generalizable representations from large-scale unlabeled data (Chen et al., 2020; He et al., 2022). This approach is particularly attractive for modeling physiological signals, such as photoplethysmography (PPG) or electrocardiography (ECG), which are routinely captured as time series data by wearables (e.g. smart watches) or medical devices. Labeling clinically-important events in such data (such as atrial fibrillation in an ECG recording) typically requires expert interpretation, invasive assays, or con-trolled clinical settings (Xu et al., 2024), and is very difficult to perform at scale. At the same time, the increasing adoption of smart watches by the general public and the large-scale aggregation of waveform data within electronic health records has created an opportunity to develop foundation models for such physiological signals (Xu et al., 2025; Saha et al., 2025; Ansari et al., 2025). Such models could enable new data-driven approaches to modeling states of health and disease. Time series representation learning is challenging due to the need to capture statistical dependencies across widely-varying temporal scales (Goldberger et al., 2002). For example, heart rate, which is measurable via PPG, is correlated at the level of minutes in responding to physiological demands such as exercise, but is also correlated at the level of hours due to the diurnal cycle. Weekly patterns of stress or physical activity, such as regular commute times or exercise routines, can induce even longer-range correlational structure. A key step in developing foundation models for such data is the identification of effective pretext tasks. Two common approaches are contrastive learning,

Contrastive learning is a particularly useful lens for examining time series representation learning as it provides a way to explicitly reason about similarity and dissimilarity. Unfortunately, time series recordings typically lack a rich set of well-defined invariances from which positive pairs can be constructed (Xu et al., 2024). As a consequence, prior contrastive learning works have adopted one of two disjoint approaches to defining similarity between windows: struc-tural similarity approaches that index on signal morphology or frequency content (i.e., windows are similar if they contain similar waveform shapes, like two heart beats in an ECG waveform) (Xu et al., 2025; 2024), and temporal similarity measures that index on temporal proximity (i.e., two windows are similar if they occur close together in time, such as when a cool-down period follows a period of vigorous exercise) (Tonekaboni et al., 2021; Oord et al., 2018). In contrast, this paper proposes, to our knowledge, the first contrastive learning approach which unifies structural and temporal sim-ilarity measures into a single framework. We demonstrate that the standard approach of generating positive pairs by mining morphologically-similar patterns from time series recordings (Xu et al., 2024) can be extended by composing it with temporal similarity, drawing positive and negative pairs from the temporal context of similar and dissimilar windows. This unifies the two primary sources of similarity in time series modeling: windows that correspond to similar physiological processes (e.g., two heartbeats) will have similar morphology, while windows which map onto stable patterns of human behavior (e.g., cool-down following exercise) will be temporally co-occurring. An exer-cise window, for instance, can learn from the cool-downs that follow exercise in other recordings, capturing what typically follows exercise rather than what followed it once. We introduce TEX-MEX: for each anchor window, it first identifies similar and dissimilar windows within a random batch drawn across the dataset. Every window is then shifted by the same random offset, forward or backward in time, redrawn in each batch to span many timescales. TEX-MEX pulls the anchor toward the shifted versions of similar windows and pushes it away from those of dissimilar windows. We find that the encoder’s own representations suffice for measuring this similarity, eliminating the costly signal distance measures of prior methods (Xu et al., 2024). Unlike those fixed measures, this similarity improves as the encoder trains, ultimately surpassing them. Because it is graded rather than binary, TEX-MEX is naturally a soft contrastive method (Lee et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025), weighting each shifted window by its source window’s similarity to the anchor, which captures degrees of relatedness and adds robustness to false negatives. The main contributions of our work are as follows: 1. We introduce TEX-MEX, a contrastive objective that unifies structural and temporal sim-ilarity by contrasting the shifted versions of similar windows against those of dissimilar windows, with similarity derived from the encoder’s own representations. 2. We show that this representation-space similarity improves throughout training, surpassing existing signal distance approaches as a predictor of mutual class membership. 2Preprint. 3. We demonstrate the generality of TEX-MEX across PPG, ECG, accelerometry, EEG, and CGM, where it broadly achieves state-of-the-art performance against leading contrastive, reconstructive, JEPA-based, and modality-specific methods. 4. Leveraging its efficiency, we train and will release an open-source, open-weight PPG foun-dation model that achieves state-of-the-art performance across a range of downstream tasks with just 560K parameters, roughly 50 × smaller than the next-best unimodally pretrained PPG foundation model in our evaluation. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Time-Series Self-Supervised Learning. Temporal contrastive methods use proximity in time as supervision: CPC (Oord et al., 2018) contrasts a window’s future against other samples, and TNC (Tonekaboni et al., 2021) treats windows within a locally stationary neighborhood as positives, reweighting others as unlabeled. A single neighborhood, however, ties relatedness to one timescale and one recording. Structural methods instead relate windows by learned distances, such as RE-BAR’s reconstruction-based motif similarity (Xu et al., 2024) and RelCon’s learnable distance (Xu et al., 2025), which RelCon also uses to grade relationships for soft contrastive learning, as in our work. These, however, miss temporal relationships, relating only windows that look alike. TS2Vec (Yue et al., 2022) and SoftCLT (Lee et al., 2024) apply instance-wise and temporal contrast as losses independent of one another, and their temporal contrast stays within a single input. SoftCLT soft-ens both, using DTW distance and a monotonically decaying timestamp distance, and reports that embedding-space similarity, as in NNCLR (Dwibedi et al., 2021), degrades performance; in our formulation, where it determines how temporal context is contrasted rather than serving directly as positives, it instead surpasses data-space distances. Masked modeling approaches such as TimeMAE (Cheng et al., 2026) and PatchTST (Nie et al., 2023) instead reconstruct masked portions of an input. 

PPG Foundation Models. Recent PPG foundation models span contrastive, reconstructive, and au-toregressive pretraining. Contrastive approaches differ mainly in how they relate windows: through shared subject identity (Abbaspourazad et al., 2024; Pillai et al., 2025), morphology-based simi-larity (Pillai et al., 2025; Saha et al., 2025), quality-aware temporal proximity (Ding et al., 2024), or synchronization with paired ECG (Nie et al., 2026). xMAE (Zhou et al., 2026) instead learns through cross-modal reconstruction of masked ECG, and GPT-PPG (Chen et al., 2025) models PPG autoregressively through next-patch prediction. We build on Pulse-PPG (Saha et al., 2025), adopting its pretraining corpus and downstream evaluation.

## 4 TEX-MEX AS A GENERAL TIME -S ERIES SSL O BJECTIVE 

To evaluate TEX-MEX as a general time-series SSL objective, we adopt two recently proposed evaluation settings spanning five signal domains: first, following PULSE (Chen et al., 2026) (PPG, EEG, ECG, and HAR) and second, following CGM-JEPA (Muhammad et al., 2026) (for CGM). 4.1 MULTI -D OMAIN SELF -S UPERVISED LEARNING : PPG, EEG, ECG, AND HAR 

Setup The core evaluation setting of PULSE (Chen et al., 2026) utilizes four datasets spanning four time-series domains. For PPG, WESAD (Schmidt et al., 2018) is used for four-class affective-state classification across baseline, stress, amusement, and meditation states. For EEG, Sleep-EDF (Kemp et al., 2000) is used for five-class sleep-stage classification. For ECG, MIT-BIH Atrial Fib-rillation (Moody, 1983) is used for binary classification of atrial fibrillation versus normal rhythm. Finally, the accelerometry dataset from Reyes-Ortiz et al. (2015) is used for six-class human activity recognition. We retain preprocessing, windowing, and predefined 70/15/15 inter-subject splits. The PULSE evaluation protocol provides a standardized setup for introducing new SSL objectives while preserving the encoder architecture and downstream evaluation. We use the same 10-layer dilated-convolution encoder with 320-dimensional representations. Each method is tuned through 30 random-search trials over shared training hyperparameters, while allowing method-specific hy-perparameters to vary independently. The best hyperparameter configuration is selected by vali-dation performance, and the lowest-validation-loss checkpoint within each training run is retained. Frozen representations are evaluated using a fixed logistic-regression linear probe, with results av-eraged across five model initialization seeds (0–4) on the predefined split. We reuse results for existing baselines from Chen et al. (2026), which cover representative modern approaches spanning contrastive learning, masked autoencoding, and sequential variational autoencoding. Alongside TEX-MEX, we implement TNC (Tonekaboni et al., 2021) and SoftCLT (Lee et al., 2024) as additional baselines representing prior temporal contrastive approaches. For each, we sweep method-specific hyperparameters following the search spaces of their original implementations. Fur-ther details on hyperparameter sweeps and final choices can be found in Appendix A.

Results Table 1 summarizes linear-probe performance across the four domains. For PPG, EEG, and ECG, TEX-MEX achieves the highest (or, in one case, tied-highest) performance on both AU-ROC and PRAUC, with PULSE generally providing the closest baseline performance. Further, TEX-MEX retains its computational efficiency: on a Quadro RTX 6000, a controlled runtime bench-mark found that TEX-MEX reduced peak GPU memory utilization by 65.4% and pretraining wall-clock time by 49.6% on average relative to PULSE. On HAR, however, TEX-MEX is outperformed by SoftCLT, LFADS, and REBAR, ranking 4/11. Nevertheless, performance on this task is highly saturated: all but one baseline exceeds 99% AUROC, with TEX-MEX reaching 99.44% compared with 99.69% for SoftCLT, the best-performing method. A similar, though somewhat larger, gap is observed for PRAUC, where TEX-MEX reaches 98.11% compared with 98.93%. The advantage of TEX-MEX is especially clear in comparison with contrastive methods that simi-larly exploit temporal structure. Across the benchmark, TEX-MEX is the only such approach that remains consistently competitive with the strongest SSL methods, with particularly large gains on PPG and ECG over TNC, SoftCLT, and the SimCLR implementation of Chen et al. (2026), which uses temporal shifting as an augmentation. Each of these methods prescribes similarity in advance, whether by temporal proximity (TNC, SimCLR) or by DTW and timestamp distance (SoftCLT). TEX-MEX instead learns similarity through the encoder itself, contrasting the temporal shifts of similar windows against those of dissimilar ones. These learned relationships carry most of the 6Preprint. positive weight, with only 30.2% of w+ (averaged across domains and epochs) falling on the an-chor’s own shift. Because similarity comes from representations already computed in the forward pass, TEX-MEX also avoids the highly costly dataset-wide preprocessing required by TNC (ADF stationarity testing) and SoftCLT (DTW distances), making it substantially more amenable to large-scale pretraining. Setup We follow the experimental setting of CGM-JEPA (Muhammad et al., 2026), preserving its transformer-based encoder, pretraining data, downstream tasks, seeds, and evaluation protocol. Pretraining uses unlabeled continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) recordings from a Stanford study of metabolic health (Metwally et al., 2025) together with a public CGM dataset (Col´ as et al., 2019). Downstream evaluation uses two labeled groups from the Stanford study. One group contains glu-cose responses measured from venous blood draws during an in-clinic oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), while a separate held-out group contains both venous OGTT measurements and wearable CGM collected at home. Each participant is clinically labeled for insulin resistance and β-cell dys-function, yielding two binary prediction tasks. We evaluate frozen representations in three settings: training on venous measurements from the first group and testing on venous measurements from the held-out group, training and testing on home CGM within the held-out group, and training on venous measurements and testing on home CGM within the held-out group. The logistic-regression probe and average of 20 seeds of subject-level 2-fold cross validation are unchanged, and all baseline results (other than TNC and SoftCLT) are derived from Muhammad et al. (2026). Incorporating TEX-MEX requires a few tweaks in this setup. First, the corpus does not support temporal shifts at CGM-JEPA’s 24-hour (288-reading) window: 391 of its 413 recordings are exactly one day long, leaving only 22 long enough to hold both an anchor and a displaced partner. We therefore rewindow and pretrain on 39-reading (3.25-hour) windows, matching the 39-point curves that form every downstream input in their setup. Second, we z-score inputs using corpus statistics; this is applied to TS2Vec in their setup. We derive all TEX-MEX-specific hyperparameters without modification from defaults derived in Section 5, with an exception for σ = 1 chosen beforehand as a more plausible timescale for the domain. We also expand the baselines list by including TNC and SoftCLT. For the former, we use the TEX-MEX window size of 39, while for the latter, we preserve the window size of 288 as SoftCLT only performs within-window temporal contrast. For both, we use defaults for method-specific hyperparameters. 

Results Table 2 summarizes linear-probe performance for metabolic subphenotyping. TEX-MEX achieves the best or tied-best performance on 11 of 12 metric-task combinations, spanning both β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance across the three evaluation settings. The sole exception is venous insulin resistance AUROC, where TNC achieves the highest performance. The largest gains emerge on at-home CGM, particularly for insulin resistance. TEX-MEX exceeds the strongest competing baseline by 6.3 AUROC points for in-domain home evaluation and by 8.6 points under venous →home transfer. The latter is the largest improvement across the evaluated settings and is particularly relevant for practical deployment, as it tests whether a probe learned from clinically collected venous OGTT curves transfers to CGM measurements obtained during an at-home glucose challenge. This setting reflects a plausible path toward scalable metabolic screening, where models can leverage high-quality clinical measurements during development while operating on substantially more accessible wearable measurements at deployment (Metwally et al., 2025).

## 5 SCALING TEX-MEX: A B ITE -S IZED , SOTA PPG F OUNDATION MODEL 

Setup We broadly derive our setup from Pulse-PPG (Saha et al., 2025), a prior state-of-the-art open PPG foundation model pretrained on field-collected wearable data. We inherit its Mobile Open Observation of Daily Stressors (MOODS) pretraining dataset (Neupane et al., 2024), a large-scale collection of raw smartwatch PPG recorded during participants’ everyday lives, as well as its diverse downstream evaluation: PPG-BP (Liang et al., 2018), WESAD (Schmidt et al., 2018), PPG-DaLiA (Reiss et al., 2019), MOODS (Neupane et al., 2024), and SDB (Garde et al., 2014). The downstream tasks span wearable field, wearable lab, and clinical settings, cover both classification and regression problems such as stress, activity, heart rate, sleep disturbance, blood pressure, and hypertension, and use input windows at a variety of temporal scales. We strengthen the downstream evaluation protocol for all models. Rather than evaluating on a single subject split with one ran-dom seed, we use subject-level five-fold cross-validation repeated across 15 random seeds for every downstream task. We also expand the set of foundation-model baselines to include more recent general time-series and PPG-specific models. These include MOMENT (Goswami et al., 2024), Chronos 1 & 2 (Ansari et al., 2024; 2025), PaPaGei (both S and P variants) (Pillai et al., 2025), AnyPPG (Nie et al., 2026), and Pulse-PPG (Saha et al., 2025). For the TEX-MEX PPG foundation model, we apply a pretraining window length of 1 minute. This contrasts with Saha et al. (2025), where longer, four-minute windows were specifically chosen to capture the multi-minute physiological dynamics associated with stress. TEX-MEX, however, ex-plicitly learns temporal relationships across shifted signal windows during pretraining. We therefore use a shorter window intended to provide a more general representation across tasks operating at different temporal scales. Similar to Saha et al. (2025) we use a ResNet backbone, however, we sub-stantially reduce the size of the encoder to 560K parameters, yielding a 96-dim representation. Results Table 4 summarizes linear-probe performance across 11 downstream classification and regression tasks, spanning five datasets and three settings: wearable field, wearable lab, and clinical. Overall, TEX-MEX is the strongest-performing approach, achieving the best performance on 7 of 11 tasks and outperforming all unimodally pretrained PPG foundation models on 10 of 11. The gains are particularly pronounced for hypertension prediction, where TEX-MEX exceeds the next-best models by roughly 6–7 percentage points in both AUROC and PRAUC. While TEX-MEX is the best-performing model overall, it is also by far the smallest in our evalua-tion: the next-smallest, AnyPPG, is over 7 × larger and additionally requires paired PPG and ECG recordings during pretraining. The comparison with Pulse-PPG, the strongest unimodally pretrained baseline (outperforming both PaPaGei variants on 6 of 11 tasks), is especially stark. It is roughly 50 × larger and uses the same pretraining dataset and evaluation splits, yet TEX-MEX outperforms it on 10 of 11 tasks; on the sole exception, sleep, the gap is under one percentage point on both AUROC and PRAUC. Further, attempts at downscaling by Saha et al. (2025) show that reducing Pulse-PPG even to ∼4.73M parameters causes AUROC losses up to 0.139 in downstream tasks. On the four tasks where TEX-MEX is not the top performer, two involve marginal differences from the best result in the table, with gaps to the best competing model averaging 1.5 percentage points. The other two cases (DaLiA heart-rate estimation and sleep classification on SDB) are more sub-stantial. For the former, TEX-MEX nevertheless remains the best-performing unimodally pretrained PPG model, with only general time-series foundation models and multimodally pretrained AnyPPG performing better. On sleep, however, TEX-MEX underperforms by roughly six percentage points relative to the best performer, PaPaGei-P. We suspect this reflects a limitation of the MOODS pre-training dataset, which contains no sleep recordings. Consistent with this explanation, Pulse-PPG, a generally strong model trained on the same corpus, also performs poorly on SDB. PaPaGei, by contrast, is pretrained with sleep data and performs best in this setting though even it is hardly better than random. This limitation could be addressed in future work by expanding MOODS to include sleep recordings. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduce TEX-MEX, an efficient, scalable self-supervised objective for time series that uni-fies structural and temporal similarity by contrasting the temporal shifts of windows the encoder 9Preprint. deems similar against those it deems dissimilar. Across PPG, ECG, EEG, accelerometry, and CGM, TEX-MEX broadly achieves state-of-the-art performance. We further demonstrate it at scale with a 560K-parameter PPG foundation model that achieves state-of-the-art downstream performance while having significantly fewer parameters than the PPG foundation models in our evaluation.
