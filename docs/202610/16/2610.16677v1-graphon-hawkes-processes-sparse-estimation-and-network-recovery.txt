GRAPHON HAWKES PROCESSES: SPARSE ESTIMATION AND NETWORK RECOVERY 

JUSTIN R. BAARS, ROGER J. A. LAEVEN, AND MICHEL R. H. MANDJES Abstract. Learning interaction structures from spatiotemporal event data is a key statistical problem in fields such as neuroscience, epidemiology, and seismology. We study this problem for Hawkes processes on a con-tinuous spatial domain, where events may trigger future events at different locations. Following the framework of [5], we model the spatial interaction mechanism using an underlying graphon that characterizes how the intensities’ excitation strength varies across locations. By expanding the baseline intensity, temporal excitation kernel and graphon in orthogonal bases, we represent the problem as a high-dimensional parametric infer-ence problem. We propose a sparse penalized least-squares estimator that recovers the effective interaction structure while controlling model complexity. Our analysis establishes non-asymptotic error bounds in a high-dimensional regime where the number of parameters may grow with the observation horizon, without requiring the conditional intensity to be almost surely bounded. We further establish sparsistency results, showing that the latent interaction network can be consistently recovered. Simulation studies demonstrate the good finite sample performance of the proposed method and an application to large-scale neuronal spike data, comprising 1.81 million events, showcases its practical effectiveness. 1. Introduction Large-scale point process data arise in many scientific applications where events occur in spatiotemporal domains. In such settings, events are seldomly stochastically independent: the occurrence of one event may affect the likelihood of future events along the temporal and spatial dimensions. Identifying these interaction mechanisms, and the latent network structure that governs them, has become an important statistical task. A widely used framework for modeling such interactions is the Hawkes process ([28, 29]). In a multivariate Hawkes process, each component point process represents a node in a network, and past events in one component may influence the future intensities of other components through a system of interaction kernels. Because of its flexibility and interpretability, the multivariate Hawkes process has been applied in numerous areas, including neuronal spike recordings ([9, 10, 56]), financial (transaction) data ([1, 11, 6, 40]), seismic activity ([46, 32]), and the spread of infectious diseases ([16]). In these applications, the key object of interest is often the interaction structure itself: A directed edge between two nodes in the network indicates that events in one component influence the future occurrence rate in another.

Despite the success of multivariate Hawkes processes, most existing models rely on a finite collection of component processes and therefore implicitly assume that the spatial domain has been discretized. However, in many applications, such as neuroscience, epidemiology, and seismology, the events occur in a genuinely continuous spatial domain and the interaction strength between two locations varies smoothly with their distance or other spatial characteristics. Discretizing space in such situations introduces modeling artifacts and may obscure important structural features of the interaction mechanism. Moreover, increasing the spatial resolution in order to reduce discretization error quickly leads to high-dimensional models. In this work, we study the estimation of the latent interaction structure in spatiotemporal point processes defined on a continuous spatial domain. The number of parameters grows rapidly with the spatial resolution, in particular through the 𝑀 2 interaction coefficients associated with the graphon. Such high-dimensional parameterizations require regularization in order to obtain stable and interpretable estimates. In many applications, the effective interaction structure is expected to be (approximately) sparse, meaning that only a small fraction of the multiplicity of all possible interactions play a substantial role (see, 3

e.g., [12]). Motivated by this observation, we estimate the model parameters using a penalized least-squares approach with an ℓ1-type penalty on the interaction coefficients. This penalization promotes sparsity in the effective excitation mechanism in order to control model complexity. Penalization methods have become a central tool in high-dimensional inference for point processes. An influential early contribution is [27], which develops ℓ1-penalized estimators for multivariate counting processes whose intensities depend linearly on an unknown function expanded over a dictionary. Their analysis establishes oracle inequalities and introduces data-driven choices of penalty weights using martingale concentration bounds. Subsequent work has extended these ideas in various directions: [56] incorporates structural information through structured sparsity penalties, whereas [19] studies Bayesian nonparametric inference for Hawkes processes. Concentration inequalities for Hawkes processes derived in [15] have also played an important role in subsequent developments, including high-dimensional inference procedures such as the decorrelated score test of [53]. Related problems such as the classification of Hawkes process trajectories ([22]), sparsity testing ([43]), and models with covariates ([23]) have been investigated as well. Among these contributions, the work of [13] considers high-dimensional multivariate Hawkes process estimation using penalized least squares with spline basis expansions. Their framework allows for both excitatory and inhibitory interactions and accommodates time-varying baseline intensities. Importantly, they establish non-asymptotic error bounds and network recovery guarantees in regimes where the dimension of the multivariate process may grow faster than the observation horizon. Our approach shares several methodological aspects with this line of work, in particular the use of basis expansions and sparsity-inducing penalties to control high-dimensional interaction structures. However, the setting we consider differs fundamentally from [13] in that our spatial domain X is continuous and infinite-dimensional, and that the connectivity structure is modeled through a graphon rather than a finite adjacency matrix. These differences remain after basis expansion and truncation: whereas these yield a finite-dimensional parameterization, the underlying interaction structure is still described by a graphon on a continuous, infinite-dimensional spatial domain. In our setting, the finite network structure is replaced by a continuous-space interaction operator, requiring non-asymptotic control of a random Gram matrix that inherits the temporal and spatial dependences induced by the continuous-space graphon Hawkes process. Moreover, unlike [13], we do not impose an almost-sure upper bound on the conditional intensity. This necessitates an intrinsically different treatment of the stochastic terms arising in the estimation problem and leads to additional technical challenges in establishing non-asymptotic error bounds. We summarize the main contributions of this paper. In Section 2, we introduce the statistical framework and formally define the estimation problem together with the assumptions required for our analysis. Although the model allows for a continuum of spatial locations X , the assumptions imposed are in several respects weaker than those used in related high-dimensional Hawkes process literature. In particular, unlike [13], we do not assume that the conditional intensity is almost surely bounded from above, thus allowing for linear Hawkes dynamics. Section 3 establishes non-asymptotic error bounds for the estimated intensity functions obtained from the truncated basis representation. The analysis is carried out in a high-dimensional regime where the number of basis coefficients may grow with the observation horizon. We overcome the technical difficulties resulting from our possibly unbounded conditional intensity through a truncation argument combined with probabilistic control of the relevant stochastic terms. In order to establish the non-asymptotic error bound, we require concentration inequalities for first- and second-order statistics of the spatiotemporal Hawkes 4

process. These bounds are obtained using a thinning-based representation of the process and are of potential independent interest for the study of spatiotemporal point process models. In Section 4, we study recovery of the latent interaction structure and establish sparsistency of the proposed estimator: under suitable regularity and sparsity assumptions, we show that it identifies the true network edges with probability tending to one as the observation horizon grows. Thus, the procedure consistently recovers the underlying connectivity structure despite the high-dimensional parameterization induced by the basis expansion. Section 5 presents a simulation study assessing the finite-sample performance and robustness of the proposed estimator across a range of sparsity levels, interaction structures, and forms of misspecification. The results demonstrate accurate coefficient recovery, support recovery, and decreasing estimation error with increasing observation horizon, with performance persisting under substantial departures from the specified model class. In Section 6, we apply our method to a Neuropixels extracellular data set maintained by the Allen Institute for Brain Science [3], consisting of time-stamped neuronal spike events with associated probe-depth information from an awake mouse exposed to controlled visual stimuli. The estimated graphons are highly consistent across substantially different spatial bases, with Legendre-polynomial and Haar-wavelet specifications re-covering the same dominant connectivity structure despite their different representations. Together, these results provide evidence that the proposed procedure is able to recover interpretable and basis-robust spatial interaction patterns in both controlled simulations and real data. We finish in Section 7 with possible extensions and suggestions for further research.

6. Application to Allen Neuropixel data set In this section, we apply our penalized least-squares estimation procedure for the graphon Hawkes process to real data. We analyze an electrophysiological experiment that originates from session 715093703 of the Neuropixels extracellular recording collection maintained by the Allen Institute for Brain Science [3]. The dataset comprises time-stamped spike events and corresponding probe depths. Let {( 𝑡 𝑖 , 𝑑 𝑖 )} 𝑁 𝑖 =1 denote the set of spike events, where 𝑡 𝑖 is the spike time (in seconds) and 𝑑 𝑖 is the probe depth (in micrometers). The continuous spatial structure of the recording makes the graphon Hawkes framework particularly natural: unlike a multivariate Hawkes process, which requires assigning neurons to a finite collection of discrete dimensions, the graphon formulation allows neuronal interactions to vary continuously with spatial location. This avoids an arbitrary discretization of the neuronal population while providing a natural framework for capturing spatially structured network interactions. The recording was obtained from a head-fixed laboratory mouse ( Mus musculus ) in an awake state while the animal was presented with controlled visual stimuli on a monitor. Neuropixels probes were inserted into cortical and subcortical regions, enabling high-density measurements of extracellular voltage deflections associated with neuronal action potentials. These signals were subsequently spike-sorted to infer single-unit activity, yielding time-stamped spike events and depth annotations for each unit. The experimental paradigm is designed to study population-level responses to sensory input and to provide benchmark data for computational models of neural dynamics. Fitting multivariate Hawkes processes to neuronal spike trains is by now standard practice in the analysis of neural population dynamics; see, for example, [9, 10, 13, 15, 19, 23, 27, 53, 56]. A spatiotemporal Hawkes process with a graphon-induced network structure was introduced in [5] as a high-dimensional limit of multivariate Hawkes processes. In many neuroscience applications, the spatial domain is discretized so that each coordinate of a multivariate Hawkes process corresponds to a predefined spatial region or recording site. By contrast, the present dataset provides inherently continuous spatial information via probe depth. This motivates estimating the graphon Hawkes process directly to the non-discretized neuronal data, thereby avoiding an artificial partition of space. To control computational complexity and for illustrative purposes, we restrict the analysis to event times in the 250-second interval [5000 , 5250 ], comprising approximately 1 .81 million events. The spatial coordinate is observed on the discrete grid {20 , 40 , . . . , 3280 }, corresponding to probe-depth bins measured in microm-eters. To obtain a continuous spatial coordinate, we apply a standard jittering procedure (cf. [51], Section 7) by adding independent Uniform (− 10 , 10 ) perturbations to the recorded depths and linearly rescaling the resulting range (10 , 3290 ) to the unit interval X = [0, 1]. This preserves the bin frequencies while avoiding artificial ties induced by the discretization and yields a continuous representation suitable for kernel-based estimation of the graphon connectivity function. We fit the graphon Hawkes process to the data using a truncated spatial basis (𝜑 𝑘 )𝑘 ∈ [ 𝑀 ] consisting of the first 𝑀 = 16 Legendre polynomials on X = [0, 1], as given in (31). The choice 𝑀 = 16 provides sufficient spatial resolution while keeping the resulting optimization problem computationally tractable; as a robustness check, we also considered 𝑀 = 8, for which the estimated patterns are qualitatively similar but somewhat less detailed. Concluding remarks In this paper we studied statistical inference for linear graphon Hawkes processes observed on a continuous spatial domain. We proposed a penalized least-squares estimation procedure based on truncated orthogonal basis expansions for the baseline intensity, interaction graphon, and excitation kernel. The resulting estimator allows the number of parameters to grow with the observation horizon while promoting sparsity in the effective interaction structure. We established non-asymptotic error bounds for the estimated intensity, proved consistent recovery of the latent interaction network under suitable sparsity conditions, and illustrated the method through simulation experiments and an application to neuronal spike train data. Several extensions and follow-up questions arise. 

• In this paper we considered graphon Hawkes processes starting from an empty history. The analysis can be extended in a straightforward way to the case where the process has a nonempty history on 

(− 𝑏, 0). Under Assumption B(vi), the excitation kernel has bounded support, so that allowing a nonempty history on (− 𝑏, 0) effectively accommodates arbitrary past histories. 

• The proof of Theorem 2 indicates that several variations of the proposed estimation procedure can be accommodated with only minor modifications. In particular, as noted in Remark 6, the baseline coefficients 𝛼 may be included in the regularization term without affecting the resulting probabilistic error bounds. Moreover, the ℓ1 penalty used in the present work can be replaced by other regularization schemes, such as ridge, elastic net, or standardized group lasso penalties [52]. Since the key arguments rely primarily on the quadratic structure of the least-squares contrast and the bounds derived from the Gram matrix representation, extending the analysis to these alternative penalties is straightforward. 

• The present work focuses on linear graphon Hawkes processes defined on a continuous spatial domain with a stationary baseline intensity. Our contribution lies in extending the framework to infinite-dimensional, spatiotemporal processes and removing the assumption that the conditional intensity is almost surely bounded. While frameworks such as [13] can accommodate nonstationary and nonlinear intensities, we restricted attention to the linear and stationary setting to keep the scope manageable. Investigating estimation and recovery in more general nonlinear or nonstationary spatiotemporal Hawkes models is a natural direction for future work, but is beyond the scope of the present study. 

• Another natural extension concerns statistical inference based on the estimated interaction structure. In particular, it would be interesting to develop formal hypothesis tests for the presence or absence of 22 

interactions, or for structural properties of the graphon 𝑊 . Such procedures would rely on suitable limit theorems for the proposed estimator or for related score-type statistics. Developing these results would require a substantially more detailed asymptotic analysis and is therefore left for future work. 

• Finally, while the present work focuses on theoretical guarantees and proof-of-concept applications, further work is needed to study the computational aspects of the proposed estimator in large-scale settings. In particular, developing scalable optimization algorithms and data-driven procedures for selecting the truncation levels and regularization parameters would be important for applying the methodology to very large datasets. Proof of Theorem 2 . The proof proceeds by comparing the estimator ˆ 𝜃 with the approximating parameter ˜𝜃 from Assumption A through their penalized least-squares criteria. In Step 1, we decompose the resulting excess risk into an approximation term, a martingale term, and a penalty term. In Step 2, the martingale term is controlled via a truncation argument: on a high-probability event, local event counts are logarithmically bounded, which yields uniform bounds on the jumps and predictable quadratic variation of the associated martingale; an exponential martingale inequality then gives the required deviation bound. In Step 3, the penalty term is controlled through the empirical Gram matrix and its minimum eigenvalue. Combining these bounds reduces the analysis to a deterministic comparison inequality, which we resolve in Step 4 by considering the three possible relative magnitudes of the estimation, approximation, and estimator– approximant errors. In each case, this yields the stated rate.
