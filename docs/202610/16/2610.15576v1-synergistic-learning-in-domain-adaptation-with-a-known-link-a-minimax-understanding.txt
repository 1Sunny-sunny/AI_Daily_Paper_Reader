Abstract 

When can combining source and target data yield a much better performance than using either one alone? Statistically, we address this issue from a minimax viewpoint: writing R(n, n T ) for the minimax risk attainable from n source and nT target observations, we ask when R(n, n T ) decays strictly faster than both R(n, 0) and R(0 , n T ). When this happens, we call it the Synergistic Learning Phenomenon (SLP) and quantify it by the 

Synergistic Advantage Ratio SAR = min( R(n, 0) , R (0 , n T )) /R (n, n T ). In this work, we focus on domain adaptation with a known link : the source regression function is f , the target regression function is g = Af for a known operator A, and the two domains may also differ in their designs. Three links—the identity, integration, and differentiation—together with the behaviour of the density ratio ρ = hT /h S determine whether synergy is possible, at what rate, and in which sample-size window. For the identity link (covariate shift) with a Beta( a, 1) source and a uniform target, SLP is possible only if a ≥ 2 + 1 /(2 β), where β

is the smoothness of f (Result I; Zhou and Yang 2025). An integral link g(x) = ∫ x

for 2 < a < 3, and covariate-shift-type spatial SLP for a > 3 (Result III). A derivative link g = ˙f synergizes only under a stronger singularity (Result IV), giving a sharp integration–differentiation asymmetry under a bounded ratio. Across all three links, synergy arises from role separation : each dataset resolves a distinct bottleneck associated with the link and the designs. We characterize the sample-size windows in which combining source and target data improves on both individual minimax rates, and how the SAR varies with the source and target sample sizes. Simulations support the predicted phase structure, and a mirrored integration–differentiation analysis of real vehicle and smartphone signals illustrates the integration–differentiation asymmetry. 

# 1 Introduction 

Can two datasets together do better than either alone? Labeled target observations are often scarce while a related source domain supplies a far larger sample at lower cost (Pan and Yang, 2009; Weiss et al., 2016; Torrey and Shavlik, 2010). We ask whether the two samples together can achieve a strictly better rate of convergence than either achieves alone—a “1 + 1 > 2” effect rather than a substitution effect. In this work, we consider a domain adaptation setting where the covariate distribution may differ and the two regression functions are related via a known link. We take the source regression to be f and the target to be g = Af for a known operator 

A. The two designs—the covariate distributions of the source and the target—may differ with an unbounded density ratio. Most existing rate improvements in transfer learning arise by imposing structure on an 

unknown discrepancy between the source and target problems, for example through sparse or low-complexity parameter shifts, function proximity, shared representations, or a smoother 1arXiv:2610.15576v1 [math.ST] 11 Oct 2026 additive bias; see Section 2 for details. The gain comes from a residual that is easier to estimate than either regression function. Our setting removes this source of gain because the known relation g = Af determines g from f , leaving no additional regression residual to estimate. Any “1 + 1 > 2” effect must instead come from how the known operator interacts with the two designs: the two samples provide complementary information, rather than an easier unknown offset. Closest to this known-link regime are covariate shift (the identity operator) and classical inverse problems (the same operators observed through a single experiment); both are discussed below. Under the identity link with a bounded density ratio—plain covariate shift—combining the two samples merely matches the faster of the two individual rates (Pathak et al., 2022; Kpotufe and Martinet, 2021). An earlier work (Zhou and Yang, 2025) showed that this limitation can be overcome for suitably singular designs with an unbounded density ratio ( ρ(x) → ∞ as 

x → x0 for some x0): the combined estimator then strictly outperforms either dataset alone in a sample-size window, the Synergistic Learning Phenomenon (SLP), quantified here by the 

Synergistic Advantage Ratio (SAR). The two non-identity links have not been studied at all to the best of our knowledge. For every x ∈ [0 , 1], they take the form

Here f has Hölder smoothness β, and the goal is to estimate g under the sampling and Gaussian noise assumptions of Section 3. Both experiments are informative about g, but through different channels and at different smoothness levels, which is what makes the combined rate nonobvious. The classical inverse-problem literature (Cavalier, 2008; Efromovich and Koltchinskii, 2001) studies the same operators with a single dataset, observing either the source or the target regression. We compare the minimax risk of estimating g from both samples with the risks based on either sample alone. An SLP occurs when the combined risk has a strictly faster rate than both individual risks. As the source and target sample sizes n and nT grow jointly, their relative scaling determines which sample alone provides the faster minimax rate and whether combining the samples yields a further rate improvement. We distinguish four types of sample-size regions according to these comparisons. In the target dominating (TD) region, the joint risk has the target-only rate; in the target leading (TL) region, the target provides the better individual rate and gains from source data; in the source leading (SL) region, the source provides the better individual rate and gains from target data; and in the source dominating (SD) region, the joint risk has the source-only rate. At equality, SLP remains possible in a subpolynomial boundary layer (Appendix C.6). These results establish an asymmetry between integration and differentiation: in the bounded-density setting, the integral link admits synergy for suitable relative sample sizes even when the source and target covariate distributions coincide, whereas the derivative link does not. Under Beta-source designs, the functional link determines the singularity requirements, sample-size windows, and attainable SAR orders. We characterize the joint minimax risks up to multiplicative constants for these functional links and design distributions. This identifies both the gains available from combining the samples and the regimes in which no estimator can improve on the better individual minimax rate. We also restate the identity-link rates of Zhou and Yang (2025) as Result I, with an improved understanding at a = 2 + 1 /(2 β), so the three links can be compared in one table. # 2 Related Work 

The identity link: covariate shift. Under a bounded ratio, importance-weighted ERM (Cortes et al., 2010; Mansour et al., 2009; Sugiyama et al., 2007; Shimodaira, 2000) and kernel methods (Kpotufe and Martinet, 2021) attain the minimax rate, which equals the faster of the two individual rates (Pathak et al., 2022): no synergy. Under an unbounded ratio, Zhou and Yang (2025) showed that a density singularity can produce an SLP, the first rigorous instance of SAR → ∞ . Result I restates their results for the identity link under covariate shift. 

Non-identity links and inverse problems. Classical inverse-problem theory studies 

g = Af for a compact operator (Cavalier, 2008; Engl et al., 1996), with known single-dataset minimax rates (Cavalier, 2008; Efromovich and Koltchinskii, 2001) and derivative estimation strictly harder than function estimation (Rice, 1984; Hall and Marron, 1990), but observes either 

f or g, never both; whether a second dataset on the other side of a known link gives a faster combined rate is what Results II–IV answer. 

Unknown source–target relations. A broad transfer-learning literature allows the relation between source and target problems to be unknown but restricts the discrepancy to have additional structure. Examples include sparse coefficient differences (Li et al., 2022), aligned GLM coefficients (Tian and Feng, 2021), shared representations (Tripuraneni et al., 2021; Du et al., 2021), source–target function proximity (Reeve et al., 2021), close class posteriors (Cai and Wei, 2021), small transfer bias (Cai and Pu, 2024), and smoother additive offsets (Lin and Reimherr, 2024). Although the structural assumptions differ, the common mechanism is that an unknown cross-domain discrepancy is restricted to a simpler class, making transfer statistically beneficial. In our model, the target regression function is determined by the source regression function through the known relation g = Af . Hence any improvement from combining the two samples must arise from how the known operator interacts with the two designs, rather than from estimating a simpler unknown source–target discrepancy. Mechanisms underlying the SLP. Each gain above is a division of labor, not a pooling of samples. Under bounded density conditions, integration averages noise and permits a parametric source-only risk. The target observes g directly and contributes information about its local structure, yielding the joint rate ( nn T )−1/2 in Result II. A density singularity adds a second division. For a > 2, source estimation of the cumulative coefficients of g is limited by the sparse observations near zero; the target estimates this finite-dimensional component at rate n−1 

> T

. If the singularity is strong enough, the two samples also split space: the source carries the bulk and the target the neighborhood of the singularity (Result III). The derivative link reverses the first mechanism. Differentiation amplifies noise, and on a window of scale t a source observation of f

informs ˙f only after a factor t2. The source is then a weak channel for the target estimand. Only a much stronger singularity restores synergy (Result IV), and under a bounded ratio nothing does (Theorem 11). 

Necessary conditions for SLP. Three ingredients recur. (I) Difficulty asymmetry : the two problems differ in intrinsic difficulty, in effective sample size (Results I and III) or in smoothness class (Results II and IV). (II) Complementary information : each fills a gap the other cannot, spatially near and away from the singularity (Results I and III), or through source information about the running integral and direct target observations of g (Result II). (III) Sample-size window in which SAR diverges : the SLP holds on the middle wedges of Figure 1, always bracketed by SD and TD, with the SAR attaining its largest order at the equalizing scale in the displayed settings.

Real-data analysis. The public IO-VNBD benchmark (Onyekpe et al., 2021) records vehicle and smartphone measurements on public-road trajectories. We use vehicle yaw rate recorded through the controller area network (CAN) and smartphone GPS heading, synchronized at 10 Hz. Yaw rate measures the vehicle’s turning rate, while GPS heading records its direction of travel, providing a physical basis for the functional links studied here. After heading unwrapping, sign-and-scale calibration, and normalization, the heading change g is approximately the integral of the yaw-rate signal f on the audited windows, g(x) ≈ ∫ x 

> 0

f (u) du . Taking yaw rate as the source response and heading change as the target response gives the integral task; exchanging their roles gives the derivative task. The dataset therefore permits comparison of the two links using the same pair of observed signals, without constructing synthetic responses. Data selection and calibration are detailed in Appendix E.1. Across 21 audited windows from 11 signal-distinct tracks, the terminal track-geometric SAR is 1 .385 [1 .126 , 1.699] on the κ = 0 .8 integration path and 0 .954 [0 .930 , 0.976] for safeguarded differentiation (Figure 7(b) in Appendix E.1). These exploratory 95% track-bootstrap intervals condition on the selected windows and do not account for window-selection uncertainty. Finite-range rate discrepancies remain, and these illustrations do not establish minimax rates. 13 7 Discussion and Conclusion 

Under a known functional link, synergy arises when the source and target samples provide complementary information about the target regression function, allowing joint estimation to improve on both individual minimax rates for suitable relative sample sizes. When both design densities are bounded above and away from zero, integration admits SLP for suitable relative sample sizes, whereas differentiation does not. Under Beta-source designs, the two links have different singularity requirements and sample-size windows for SLP. The minimax characterizations identify the rate gains available from combining the samples, together with the regimes in which the joint risk has the better individual rate. 

Limitations. Only one-dimensional regression is studied. The rate-optimal estimators require knowledge of the source density and ( a, β ), so the adaptation issue remains open, as do extensions to higher-order links and multivariate singularities. Only asymptotic results on convergence rates are provided.

# B The Identity Link: Covariate Shift

# D Additional Simulation Results 

We illustrate selected transfer scenarios from Sections 4.1–4.3. The experiments examine rate mechanisms, sample-allocation effects, and the limitations of finite-range comparisons with minimax theory. 

Common experimental setting. Design points are sampled independently on the unit cube of the corresponding dimension. All regression experiments use independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0 .25. Every Monte Carlo configuration in this simulation section and its diagnostics uses 400 independent repetitions, including regression experiments, surrogate loss simulations, and separate Monte Carlo diagnostics. Analytic-risk calculations have no Monte Carlo repetition count. Unless a subsection specifies otherwise, we approximate integrated risk on a 4096-point grid and construct paired percentile-bootstrap 95% intervals from 5000 resamples; bootstrap resamples are distinct from simulation repetitions. Slopes use the largest four sample sizes unless another tail length is stated. For the synthetic regression experiments, β denotes the source-function class index used for tuning and rate references. The target class index is β + 1 for the integral link, β − 1 for the derivative link. Individual test functions may be smoother than these class requirements. The surrogate experiments specify their coefficient classes separately. 

Risk interpretation. We distinguish raw-regression Monte Carlo, surrogate-model Monte Carlo, and analytic-risk calculations. Monte Carlo is abbreviated as MC. Raw-regression Monte Carlo generates design points and responses and applies the regression estimators. Surrogate-model Monte Carlo samples losses induced by a separately specified observation model and estimator, possibly by exact aggregation of coefficient errors. Analytic-risk calculations evaluate expected losses without Monte Carlo sampling and may concern either a regression estimator or a surrogate. Each experiment evaluates specified estimators at fixed truths or over finite function libraries. The minimax formulas serve as asymptotic benchmarks for these risks and their SARs. Bootstrap bands quantify pointwise Monte Carlo precision under the stated model and risk definition. Discrepancy between the surrogate and regression models is outside their scope. Slopes, normalized risks, and scan maxima describe behavior over the displayed sample sizes, while conclusions about minimax optimality and asymptotic SLP boundaries rely on the theoretical analysis. A bounded SAR allows constant-factor gains while indicating no asymptotic rate improvement. 

Display conventions. Sample-size and SAR axes are logarithmic; normalized-risk and relative-risk diagnostics use linear vertical axes. We write κn = log n/ log nT for target-anchored scans and κT = log nT / log n for source-anchored scans. Markers identify Monte Carlo estimates. Colored dotted curves give expected risks or SAR formed from those risks for the specified estimators, without sampling bands. Rate references instead give theoretical order shapes. Unless stated otherwise, same-color dashed curves give longitudinal references and purple dash–dot curves give scan references. Rate curves are vertically aligned at the first fitted point because 20 the theorems determine rates only up to constants. Light-gray regions mark slope windows. Methods and risk calculation at a = 2 , 3. The analysis at a = 2 , 3 uses roots 1 and √3(2 x − 1) and two orthonormal piecewise-linear wavelets on each dyadic interval, each with zero zeroth and first moments. Their nested piecewise-polynomial spaces make integrated squared error additive without requiring independent coefficient errors. The implemented methods use Taylor-compensated IPW and integration-by-parts source statistics, direct target projection, and deterministic combination, shrinkage, and cutoff rules based on variance and squared-bias bounds. The bounds use conditional second moments at most one and the squared coefficient envelope ( H/ 8) 2b5 at width b, rather than truth-specific risks; the joint bias bound includes the source weight. At a = 3, the unregularized boundary wavelets have finite IPW variance but an infinite fourth moment. We therefore apply zeroth-order Taylor compensation below δ = ( H2n)−1/5, capped at half the wavelet’s support width. This rule was fixed before production Monte Carlo. Actual signed boundary bias and modified variance enter the expected risk, while combination and shrinkage use variance plus squared-bias bounds. The bounded weights of each finite-resolution estimator yield finite variance of the sampled losses. Expected risks include signal-induced design variance, observation noise, boundary and shrinkage bias, and the exact projection tail of the quadratic truths. Numerical resolution grows with the information range to approximate one fixed infinite system, rather than changing the truth class. The bound on the omitted tree contribution and projection tail is at most 0 .453% of the corresponding fixed-library risk over the 180 cells. Increasing resolution by two levels at seven prescribed configurations changes the actual risks by at most 0 .00478%. E.2 Semi-Synthetic Calibration under Unbounded Covariate Shift 

The observed time design cannot isolate an unbounded density ratio. The semi-synthetic benchmark therefore uses constructed covariates and latent functions, with observation errors calibrated from vehicle GPS heading and CAN yaw rate. 

Residual calibration. We form a separate residual library from audited 600-second windows. On the experiment portion of each window, a cubic spline with nine interior knots is fit to normalized heading. Heading minus this spline gives the level-residual pool, and calibrated yaw rate minus the spline derivative gives the derivative-residual pool. Each pool is centered and standardized to unit root-mean-square magnitude within window. Semi-synthetic responses add independent draws from the relevant pool, scaled by 0 .25. Within each window, the resulting errors retain the empirical marginal shapes and are independent across observations and channels, with covariate-independent variance.
