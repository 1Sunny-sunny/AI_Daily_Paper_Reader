# ABSTRACT 

More than 1.5 billion people live with hearing loss, and when several people talk at once, a hearing aid amplifies every voice alike. Auditory attention decoding (AAD) aims to solve this: it reads a listener’s brain signals, finds the talker they are attending to, and lets the device amplify that talker. A real device will not see the brain alone. Glasses and earbuds can also track the eyes, the head and the scene, and these signals help, because people tend to look at the person they listen to. But they show where a listener is looking, not whom they are listening to. A decoder that leans on them works when the two agree and fails when they differ — when a listener cannot move their eyes or head, or listens to someone they are not looking at — which is exactly when a brain-based device is needed. A multimodal decoder must therefore use these signals without letting them replace the electroencephalogram (EEG), and accuracy alone cannot show whether it does. We present M ERIT , a decoder and an evaluation method built around one question: how much of the accuracy does each input earn? M ERIT shuffles each input stream across test examples, measures how much accuracy drops, and splits the result exactly across the streams with Shapley values. It also blocks two shortcuts by design: the candidate audio is checked before training so that it cannot reveal the answer, and the scoring function cannot rise above chance if it ignores the brain signal. On a public dataset with EEG, eye tracking, head motion and scene video, M ERIT improves on the published benchmark, reaching 0.82 four-way accuracy within listeners and 0.74 on unseen listeners, where chance is 0.25 . The measurement also shows what accuracy hides: adding the behavioural streams raises accuracy but almost halves the brain’s share of it. On two other public datasets, the same tests find a label imbalance in a widely used benchmark and flag results that accuracy alone would report as brain decoding. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Why attention decoding matters. About 1.5 billion people live with some hearing loss, and the number is expected to reach 2.5 billion by 2050 (World Health Organization, 2021). The hardest situation for them is the one Cherry (1953) called the cocktail party problem: several people talking at once. A hearing aid can make sound louder, but it cannot tell which voice matters, so it amplifies all of them, including the ones the listener is trying to ignore. Auditory attention decoding (AAD) addresses this (O’Sullivan et al., 2015; Geirnaert et al., 2021). The brain’s response follows the speech a listener attends to (Ding & Simon, 2012; Lalor & Foxe, 2010), so a decoder can read that response from the electroencephalogram (EEG), find the attended talker, and steer a hearing device toward them (Pan et al., 2024). 

Why several streams matter. Such a device will not see the brain alone. Glasses and earbuds already carry motion sensors, and eye trackers and cameras are being added to them. These signals are useful. In everyday listening, people turn their head and eyes toward the talker they follow 1arXiv:2610.17507v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026 (Hendrikse et al., 2019; Best et al., 2023; Kidd Jr. et al., 2005), and a hearing aid steered by an eye tracker has already been built (Kidd Jr. et al., 2013). A realistic decoder should therefore combine these streams with the EEG, and doing so raises accuracy (Section 7.2). 

Why the EEG must still carry the decision. Gaze and head motion show where a listener is 

facing , not whom they are listening to , and the two often differ. A driver listens to a passenger while watching the road. At a dinner table, people listen to one person while looking at another. Some listeners cannot move their eyes or head freely at all. A decoder that leans on the behavioural streams does well when facing and listening agree, and fails in exactly these cases. Those are the cases where a brain-based device is needed: when facing and listening agree, an eye tracker is enough. Whatever the EEG adds, it adds through the brain signal. A multimodal decoder should therefore use the other streams as helpers, but it must not let them replace the EEG. Accuracy cannot tell us whether this holds. A multimodal model is reported with one number, and that number does not say which input produced it. A decoder that silently decides from gaze and head motion and a decoder that truly reads attention from the EEG can therefore report the same accuracy, even though only the second keeps working when facing and listening disagree. The risk is real: Rotaru et al. (2024) found that decoders believed to read spatial attention from EEG owed most of their accuracy to eye movements captured in the recording. The usual formulation is a matching task. The model sees a window of the recording and K candidate speech signals, one of which is the attended talker, and it must pick that one; accuracy is compared with chance, 1/K (Accou et al., 2021; Jalilpour Monesi et al., 2020; Puffay et al., 2023). We study it on MAESTRO (Hassan et al., 2026), a public dataset in which sixteen listeners each attend one of four talkers speaking at once while EEG, eye tracking, head motion and scene video are recorded together (Section 6). We show that this task can be solved without using the brain signal at all, in three ways (Section 4). (i) The audio gives the answer away. Scaling each candidate to the same loudness does not make the candidates look alike: statistics such as skew and sparsity do not change with scale. On this dataset they differ between attended and ignored talkers, and a simple classifier that sees only the audio reaches 0.5581 four-way accuracy, against a chance of 0.2500. (ii) The standard scoring function gives the model a path to the leak. Problem (i) says the answer is 

present in the audio; this one says the standard architecture can reach it without the brain. If the brain encoder outputs the same vector at every time step — the collapse an encoder falls into when its input stops helping — the usual score reduces to a classifier on the audio alone, and training often finds this solution because it is easier than the intended one. (iii) Easy streams crowd out the brain. When several streams are available, training leans on the easiest one (Wang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022). Here the easy streams measure where the listener is looking , not what they are listening to , so accuracy rises while the evidence for the claim shrinks. One test catches all three problems. We shuffle a recorded stream across the test windows, so each window gets another window’s recording but keeps its own candidates and its own label, and we measure how much accuracy drops. We call the drop the contribution of that stream. A decoder that does not use a stream loses nothing when it is shuffled. This paper turns that test from an occasional check into the centre of the method. MAESTRO was released with a benchmark, and the benchmark already reports a permutation test: it shuffles the EEG, gaze, head motion and video together , and its contributions are positive. That shows the listener’s signals are used. It cannot show which signal is used, and problem (iii) sits exactly there, because a joint test gives its largest value when a strong behavioural stream has pushed the brain aside. M ERIT shuffles each stream separately and splits the total exactly across streams. It also decodes better than the benchmark: 0.82 four-way accuracy within listeners and 0.74 on unseen listeners, against the benchmark’s 0.698 and 0.674 (Section 7.3). 

Contributions. (1) A per-stream measure of credit (Section 5). We compute each stream’s Shapley value over all 2M subsets of the M recorded streams ( M = 5 here), keeping the streams in a subset intact and shuffling the rest. The values add up exactly to the total contribution, and they are cheap enough to report for every run. (2) A decoder in which the shortcuts are blocked by design 

(Section 5). A time-centred correlation score gives every candidate the same score when the brain encoder is constant, so that shortcut lands exactly at chance. The classifier heads that read gaze and head motion receive no audio, so they cannot use audio cues. The candidate audio is tested before training. (3) Choosing checkpoints by contribution, not accuracy , which trades a little accuracy 2for a larger share of credit on the brain. (4) Findings. Adding behavioural streams cuts the brain’s share of the credit almost in half; at two-second windows the EEG earns almost none of it. On two other public datasets, the same tests find a label imbalance in a widely used benchmark and show that high accuracy on both cannot be confirmed as brain activity. We also tested adding the permutation test to the training loss (Section 5); it does not increase the brain’s share, and we report this in full. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Decoding auditory attention. Brain activity follows the speech a listener attends to (Ding & Simon, 2012; Lalor & Foxe, 2010). The oldest decoders are linear: they rebuild the speech envelope from the EEG and compare the reconstruction with each talker (O’Sullivan et al., 2015). They are simple and well understood, but they need windows of tens of seconds to decide reliably (Geirnaert et al., 2021), too slow for a hearing device. Deep models shorten the window in two ways. Match-mismatch 

models learn directly whether a window of EEG goes with a window of speech (Accou et al., 2021; Jalilpour Monesi et al., 2020; Puffay et al., 2023); our decoder belongs to this family. Direction 

decoders drop the audio and classify which side the listener attends (Vandecappelle et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2024a; Fan et al., 2025; Ni et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Both lines share a weakness: the reported accuracy often measures something other than attention. Rotaru et al. (2024) showed that direction decoding on several public datasets relies largely on eye movements captured in the EEG rather than on attention, and when within-trial leakage between training and test is removed, recent direction decoders lose 17 to 45 points and several fall to chance (Alavi & Williamson, 2026) (Table 8). We bring the concern of Rotaru et al. (2024) to the matching task and to several recorded streams at once, and make the check part of training and model choice rather than an after-the-fact test. 

Datasets. The public AAD corpora record EEG and little else, and their designs deliberately hold the listener still, which is good for isolating the brain response and rules out the multimodal question: DTU uses earphones that simulate a room (Fuglsang et al., 2018), KU Leuven a quiet booth (Biesmans et al., 2017; Das et al., 2019), and a recent four-speaker dataset a chin rest (Yan et al., 2024b). A stream that was never recorded, from a listener who was not free to move, cannot compete with the EEG for credit. KU Leuven has a further limitation we measure in Appendix H: one of its two talkers is attended in 256 of 320 trials, so the audio alone carries part of the answer. AV-GC-AAD (Geirnaert et al., 2024) records gaze and decouples it from attention on purpose — the right tool for asking 

whether a decoder reads gaze instead of the brain, but its other streams are designed to carry no information, so it cannot say how credit is shared among streams that do. MAESTRO (Hassan et al., 2026) records EEG, eye tracking, head motion and scene video from a listener who moves freely in a room of loudspeakers, which is why we work on it; its own limitation, an audio confound in the usual candidate construction, is the subject of Section 4. 

Shortcut learning and multimodal balance. The problem is a case of shortcut learning (Geirhos et al., 2020; Torralba & Efros, 2011; Gururangan et al., 2018). The usual fixes rebalance the data (Goyal et al., 2017) or train against a shortcut-only branch (Cadene et al., 2019); we add a scoring function in which the shortcut is impossible. Work on balancing modalities (Wang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022) evens out how fast each stream learns during training; we measure each stream’s effect on the decision at test time instead. The measurement is permutation importance (Breiman, 2001; Fisher et al., 2019) and the Shapley value (Shapley, 1953; Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Covert et al., 2020) applied to whole streams; what is new is using it to choose models and to report every result. Appendix B gives a longer discussion. 

# 3 CANDIDATE SETS AND SPLITS 

Candidate sets. In the matching task the decoder is shown K candidate speech signals and must pick the one the listener attended. The candidates are inputs, not labels: the decoder sees them, so anything in them that marks the correct one is a shortcut that solves the task without the recording. How the candidates are built therefore decides what the task measures, and we name each set by how it is built. UNMATCHED : the four talkers of the trial, each standardised to zero mean and unit variance. This is the usual choice in the literature. Standardising removes loudness, but the four 3candidates are still four different recordings, and any property beyond the mean and the variance — skew, sparsity, the balance between frequency bands — can still tell them apart; on this dataset it does (Section 4). MATCHED : the same four talkers after quantile matching . We sort each candidate’s values, average the sorted values across the four candidates, and give each candidate these averages back in its own time order. All four then contain exactly the same set of values, so no statistic of the values — loudness, skew, sparsity or any other — can tell them apart. Each keeps its own timing, and timing is what a brain response follows. This is the most we can remove from the audio without removing the signal the task is about. SAME -TALKER : the wrong candidates are the attended talker’s own speech, taken from other, non-overlapping parts of the same trial. All candidates then come from one voice and one loudspeaker, so neither the audio nor the listener’s orientation can say which is correct, and chance is exact by construction (Accou et al., 2021). Trials are long enough for K = 3 

and K = 2 . SAME -TALKER MATCHED applies both steps. We use MATCHED for the four-way task and SAME -TALKER MATCHED as the strictest check that the EEG follows the speech. 

Splits. Within-listener , five folds: trials are split by their audio content, so no stimulus heard in training is heard again at test; every listener appears on both sides. Leave-one-listener-out (LOSO), sixteen folds: one listener is held out, and 20% of the audio content is also held out, so the test listener is new in both identity and content. A validation set is always taken from the training part, and each test set is scored once. 

# 4 THREE WAYS TO BE RIGHT FOR THE WRONG REASON 

(i) The audio gives the answer away. The decoder sees the candidate audio, so anything in the audio that marks the attended talker solves the task without the brain. Holding out listeners or stimuli does not help, because the mark belongs to the role of attended talker, so it is present for every listener and every stimulus. Making all candidates equally loud does not help either. Loudness is removed exactly by standardising each candidate, but standardising only fixes the mean and the variance. Every statistic that does not change with scale survives: skew, kurtosis, sparsity and the balance between frequency bands. On this dataset the attended and ignored talkers differ in exactly these. The peak-to-average ratio of the envelope alone separates them with an effect size of d = −1.22 .The dataset’s authors trace this to the loudness adjustment, which clipped the attended talker’s peaks (Hassan et al., 2026). We therefore test each candidate set before training, with an acceptance probe : a logistic regression on eight statistics that do not depend on scale, evaluated on held-out content. A clean candidate set must score 1/K . The UNMATCHED set scores +0.3081 above chance. MATCHED brings this down to 0.2603 against 0.25 , and same-talker candidates to 0.5134 against 0.50 (Table 3). 

(ii) The standard scoring function has a shortcut. The standard score normalises the brain and audio embeddings at each time step, multiplies them, averages over time and applies a linear layer (Accou et al., 2021; Jalilpour Monesi et al., 2020). Suppose the brain encoder outputs the same unit vector ˆc at every step. This is not a hypothetical state: once the audio already carries the answer, the easiest way for gradient descent to lower the loss is to let the encoder ignore its noisy input and emit a constant, and we observe exactly this collapse when the standard model is trained on this data (Section 7.1). One test catches all three. Shuffling a recorded stream across test windows, while each window keeps its own candidates and label, costs accuracy only if the decoder uses that stream. We shuffle the recording, not the labels: shuffling the labels would also break the link between the audio and the answer, and would hide the very shortcut we are testing for (Appendix A.4). The probe bounds what a simple reader can take from the audio; the shuffled accuracy measures what this decoder actually takes. Appendix A gives all three problems in more detail. 

# 5 MERIT 

The name states the goal: every input should earn its share of the result. M ERIT has two branches that answer two different questions. The coupling branch asks whether the recording follows a candidate’s speech over time. Only EEG enters it, because only EEG has a timed relationship with a speech signal. The orientation branch asks which loudspeaker the listener was facing. It receives no audio, so it cannot use audio cues. Figure 1 shows the whole decoder. 

Encoders sized to the brain response. Each stream goes through five dilated temporal convolutions (Yu & Koltun, 2016) with dilations 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and GroupNorm (Wu & He, 2018) after each layer. This gives a window of 63 samples ( 0.98 s at 64 Hz), about the time over which the cortical response to speech unfolds (Lalor & Foxe, 2010). There is no activation on the last layer, because a rectified output has no negative values and squeezes all the correlations the score depends on toward one. Padding is symmetric, because the brain responds 100 –300 ms after the sound, so an encoder that looks only backward in time cannot see the response. Appendix D details both choices. One-sided versions restrict the model to a chosen range of delays (Section 7.4). 

A score that cannot use a constant brain signal. For each of the D = 16 embedding dimensions we compute the correlation over time between the EEG embedding e and the embedding ˆak that the shared audio encoder computes from the k-th candidate envelope. We combine the D values with a weight vector that has no bias, scale by a learned temperature, and subtract the mean over candidates:

Orientation heads with no audio, and an audio-only adversary. Gaze, head motion, scene motion and the foveal stream — a frozen V-JEPA 2 embedding of the patch of scene around the gaze point (Section 6) — show where the listener is facing. They have no timed relationship with speech, so asking them to match a speech envelope makes no sense — and doing so made them collapse in our first attempt. Instead, each one feeds a classifier head that predicts the loudspeaker index from the mean and standard deviation of its embedding over time. These classifier heads get no audio input , so shuffling them drops accuracy to chance, and anything above chance is earned by the recording. The EEG also gets such a classifier head, because attention shifts brain activity toward one side. A learned, non-negative weight per stream and a small fusion layer combine the loudspeaker scores, which are then added to the coupling scores. # 6 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Data and task. MAESTRO (Hassan et al., 2026) has sixteen listeners and one hundred trials each. In every trial four talkers speak at once from loudspeakers at fixed positions, and the listener is told beforehand which one to attend to. Trials last about 30 s. Each talker recording is used only once, so no audio is shared between training and test. The attended talker is about 15 dB louder than the others in every trial. We use five streams, all resampled to 64 Hz and standardised per channel: EEG (32 channels), gaze ( 6), head motion ( 6), optical flow from the scene camera ( 4), and one new stream, a frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L embedding (Assran et al., 2025) of the part of the scene the listener is looking at (one 1024 -d vector per window, never trained). Signals are cut into windows of W seconds with hop H (W = 10 , H = 5 unless stated). Each example holds the streams, K candidate speech envelopes of which one is correct, the index of the correct one, the loudspeaker each candidate came from, and the listener’s identity. The candidates are placed in random order in every window, so their position says nothing. At test time the order is fixed by the window’s index, so every model we compare sees exactly the same inputs (Appendix S). Every result is defined by four choices: the candidate set, the split, the decision window and the set of streams. Every accuracy comes with its own shuffled baseline; an accuracy without one is not a result. Besides accuracy, the baseline and the contribution, we report a permutation p-value 

(# {vnull ≥ v} + 1) /(n + 1) , whose smallest possible value with twenty shuffles is 0.048 ; the Shapley credit ϕm; the flip rate , the fraction of windows whose chosen candidate changes when the stream is shuffled; accuracy with the stream replaced by zeros; and the collapse , the mean cosine similarity between the time-centred embeddings of different windows, where a value near one means every window produces the same pattern. Training uses AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with learning rate 10 −3, batches of 32 windows from one listener, and at most 50 epochs. # 8 CONCLUSION 

A decoder can become more accurate because it has stopped using the input the claim is about. We showed three ways this happens and built a decoder in which two of them are impossible. For the third, we made each stream’s credit a number that is measured, used to choose models, and reported for every result. It shows that adding behavioural streams almost halves the brain’s share of the credit, and that on other public datasets, high accuracy can hide an imbalance in the labels or a signal that cannot be confirmed as neural. Training against the measurement did not raise the brain’s share. That the measurement resists this is the best evidence we have that it is measuring something real. AI USE STATEMENT

This section gives the steps behind Section 4 that the main text states briefly. A.1 WHY MAKING THE CANDIDATES EQUALLY LOUD IS NOT ENOUGH 

Every step of the envelope pipeline is linear, so scaling a recording by α scales its envelope by 

α: env( αx ) = α env( x). Standardising each candidate then subtracts its mean and divides by its standard deviation, which removes any change of the form x 7 → αx + β. Playing a recording 

15 dB louder or quieter therefore gives exactly the same standardised envelope. A pure difference in loudness cannot survive, so it cannot be what gives the answer away. What does survive is every property that scaling and shifting leave unchanged. Standardising fixes only two numbers per candidate, the mean and the variance. Skew, kurtosis, sparsity, ratios between percentiles and the balance between frequency bands all pass through untouched. If the attended and ignored recordings differ in any of these, no rescaling can help — and on this dataset they do: the envelope’s peak-to-average ratio alone separates attended and ignored talkers with an effect size of d = −1.22 . A fix must therefore either make the candidates’ whole distributions identical (MATCHED ) or remove the difference between attended and ignored recordings altogether (same-talker candidates). A.3 WHY A JOINT SHUFFLE CANNOT SEE CROWDING OUT 

Shuffling all recorded streams together measures how much the model uses the listener’s signals as a whole. Suppose EEG alone earns a contribution of 0.40 . Now add a gaze stream that is easier to read, and suppose the fused model earns 0.55 in total, but only 0.20 of it from the EEG. The joint contribution went up , from 0.40 to 0.55 , while the brain’s share fell by half. A joint shuffle reports the first change and hides the second. Only a split per stream, such as the Shapley values of Eq. 6, shows both. The numbers of Table 1 follow exactly this pattern. A.4 WHY WE SHUFFLE THE RECORDING , NOT THE LABELS 

A common check shuffles the labels and retrains or re-scores. That is the wrong test here. Shuffling the labels breaks the link between the audio and the answer as well as the link between the brain and the answer, so a decoder that reads only the audio also drops to chance, and the shortcut we are testing for is hidden. We instead shuffle the recording across test windows and keep each window’s own candidates and label. A decoder that reads the audio keeps its accuracy; only a decoder that uses the recording loses any. The accuracy that remains, v(∅), is what this model gets from the audio alone, measured through its own audio pathway. The linear probe bounds what a simple reader can take from the audio; v(∅) measures what this decoder actually takes. 14 B RELATED WORK IN MORE DETAIL 

Auditory attention decoding. Brain activity follows the envelope of the speech a listener attends to (Ding & Simon, 2012; Lalor & Foxe, 2010). Standard decoders either rebuild the envelope from EEG and correlate it with each candidate (O’Sullivan et al., 2015), or learn the match directly (Accou et al., 2021; Jalilpour Monesi et al., 2020; Accou et al., 2023; Puffay et al., 2023). A separate line decodes the direction of attention without any audio (Vandecappelle et al., 2021). The work closest to ours is Rotaru et al. (2024), who showed that direction decoding on several public datasets is driven largely by eye movements and by regularities in the trial design, not by attention. We bring the same concern to the matching task and to several recorded streams at once, and we make the check part of how models are chosen and reported rather than a test run afterwards. 

Shortcuts in datasets. Models use whatever solves the task (Geirhos et al., 2020), and benchmarks often contain cues that make the intended input unnecessary (Torralba & Efros, 2011; Gururangan et al., 2018; Goyal et al., 2017). The usual fixes are to rebalance the data (Goyal et al., 2017) or to train against a branch that sees only the shortcut (Cadene et al., 2019). M ERIT does both — it checks the candidate sets and it trains an audio-only adversary (Ganin & Lempitsky, 2015) — and adds one thing specific to the matching task: a scoring function in which the shortcut is impossible rather than discouraged (Section 5). 

Balancing modalities. When modalities are trained together, the easiest one dominates, and the fused network can end up worse than its best single part (Wang et al., 2020). This has been called modality competition (Huang et al., 2022) and a greedy learning process (Wu et al., 2022), and it is usually treated by evening out training — adjusting gradients as training runs (Peng et al., 2022), blending losses (Wang et al., 2020), or dropping modalities at random. M ERIT looks at something different. Those methods balance how fast each modality learns; a scientific claim instead rests on how much each modality affects the decision at test time , which is what Eq. 6 reports. The two are not the same: in Table 1, turning modality dropout on or off changes the EEG’s credit very little, while the EEG’s credit is still far below what it earns alone. 

Attribution. Permutation importance (Breiman, 2001; Fisher et al., 2019) measures how much a model depends on an input by breaking that input’s link to the rest of the example. Shapley values (Shapley, 1953; Lundberg & Lee, 2017) give the only way to split credit that adds up exactly, and SAGE (Covert et al., 2020) uses them for importance measured by performance over a whole dataset. We apply the same idea to whole streams, where there are few enough subsets ( 2M ) to compute it exactly; Hessel & Lee (2020) make a related point for multimodal models. What is new is not the measurement but how it is used: to choose checkpoints, and to report the brain’s share alongside every accuracy. We also tried adding it to the training loss, and report that this does not help (Section 7.2). 

Components. The contrastive term is InfoNCE (van den Oord et al., 2018) in the two-way arrange-ment of CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), with negatives from the same listener; the anti-collapse term follows VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022); and the foveal stream is a frozen V-JEPA 2 encoder (Assran et al., 2025).

A much larger window would make the deepest layers mostly see padding, which is the same in every window, so their output would stop depending on the input. There is no activation on the last layer. # E FURTHER LIMITATIONS 

Part of the four-way contribution can come from the trial. The attended talker is the same in every window of a trial, so a decoder that recognises which trial it is looking at already knows part of the answer. The same-talker results do not have this issue, because all candidates there come from the same trial and the same voice. 

One room, sixteen listeners, no tuning. The data come from one room, one language and sixteen listeners, and the frozen video stream in particular reads this room. We did not tune any setting. The numbers are therefore not tuned in our favour, but we also cannot claim they are the best this model can reach. 

The cause of the audio mark is not tested directly. Every recording is used in only one role, so the role and the recording cannot be separated. The dataset’s authors explain the mark by clipping during the loudness adjustment (Hassan et al., 2026), but the released data cannot test this directly. No result depends on it: the probe measures how readable the mark is, whatever its cause, and both fixes work either way. A dataset in which each recording is the attended talker in some trials and an ignored one in others would remove the mark at the source.

A match that depends on timing but not on its direction is what a signal at zero delay would give, spread equally into both directions by the slow changes of the speech envelope itself. Both datasets play the audio through in-ear phones close to the electrodes, and an electrical leak of the sound into the recording is the classic zero-delay artifact; MAESTRO plays it through loudspeakers across a room, and there the check passes. We think this is the most likely explanation, but we have not shown it, and we do not count the KU Leuven or DTU accuracies as brain decoding.

# N THE TRAINING OBJECTIVE , EXPLAINED 

Equation 2 gives the loss. This section explains two choices behind it. Why the contrastive term uses one listener at a time. Raw EEG identifies the listener with accuracy 0.90 in a sixteen-way test, against a chance of 0.0625 . In a batch that mixes listeners, the contrastive term could match EEG to speech by recognising the listener, which says nothing about attention. Every batch is therefore drawn from a single listener. Why the contrastive term and the anti-collapse term cannot be satisfied by collapse. If the EEG encoder outputs a vector that does not change over time, the time-centred score gives s(e, a ) = 0 for every pair, so every entry of S in Lcon is zero and the loss is exactly log B, its value at chance. And the first part of Lcol measures exactly the spread over time that such an encoder has lost. M ERIT trains without modality dropout; the dropout models of Table 1 drop each stream with probability 0.3, never all of them at once, and fill a dropped stream with zeros so the fusion layer always runs.

# R THREE PER -STREAM MEASURES 

The runs report three per-stream numbers, which answer different questions. The contribution 

v(M ) − v(M \ { m}) is the accuracy lost by shuffling stream m while all others stay intact; it is small for a stream whose information is also in another stream. The Shapley credit ϕm is the same quantity averaged over all subsets of the other streams, and it is the one that adds up to the total. 

Leave-one-out is the accuracy lost by zeroing stream m; it differs from shuffling when the model uses a stream’s overall level rather than its window-by-window values. All three are in the released result tables. 

# S DATASET 

Design. MAESTRO (Hassan et al., 2026) has sixteen listeners with one hundred trials each. In every trial four talkers speak at once from loudspeakers at fixed, known positions. The listener is told before the trial which talker to attend to and answers a question about it afterwards. Trials last about 30 s, and each loudspeaker is the attended one equally often. The talkers of a trial are different recordings and no recording is used twice, so no audio is shared between training and test. The attended talker is played about 15 dB louder than the others in every trial; this is why the difference between loudness and shape in Section 4(i) matters. 

Streams. Next to the EEG, the dataset records gaze, head motion and a head-mounted scene video. All signals are resampled to 64 Hz and standardised per channel over each trial. This gives four time-series streams — EEG ( 32 channels), gaze ( 6), head motion ( 6) and optical-flow summaries of the scene video ( 4) — and, for each talker, the speech envelope. We add a fifth stream that the released benchmark does not use: a frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L (Assran et al., 2025) embedding of the part of the scene video around the gaze point, one 1024 -d vector per window, never trained. 

Examples. Signals are cut into windows of W seconds with hop H; unless stated, W = 10 sand H = 5 s, which gives T = 640 samples and 7,990 windows. One example holds the streams 

xm ∈ RT ×Cm ; K candidate envelopes, exactly one of them correct; the index y of the correct one; the attended loudspeaker; the permutation π that records which loudspeaker is in which candidate 24 slot; and the listener’s identity. The candidates are put in a random order in every window, so their position says nothing. At test time that order is fixed by the window’s index, so it is the same for every model we compare. 

Two details of the candidate sets that matter. First, same-talker negatives must be drawn at random from windows of the same trial that do not overlap the target window. Taking the window furthest from the target seems natural, but it pushes the negatives toward the start and end of the trial, and the audio probe then learns the position: it reached 0.60 on a task with a chance of 0.50 .Second, a negative must never overlap the target window; when a trial is too short to provide enough non-overlapping windows, we lower K rather than allow overlap. For per-trial decisions, the training crops are drawn at random positions and the candidates are matched separately in each crop, and the same-talker negatives are random non-overlapping crops of the same trial. 25
