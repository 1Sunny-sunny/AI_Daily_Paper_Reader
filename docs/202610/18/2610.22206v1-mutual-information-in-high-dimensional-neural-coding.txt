(Dated: October 16, 2026) Estimating mutual information in large neural populations is limited by the exponential growth of possible neural response patterns. Here we apply a Monte Carlo framework to make this problem tractable by sampling from a conditionally independent population model fit from single-neuron responses. The estimator accurately recovers analytical ground truth, corrects finite-data bias, and scales to empirical recordings containing 10 2 to 10 4 neurons from visual cortex, hippocampus, and retina. Across systems, stimulus uncertainty decays exponentially with population size, revealing a simple scaling law for high-dimensional neural codes. 

A central goal of neuroscience is to understand how neu-ral activity encodes external variables [1–9]. The neural response to a stimulus can be formulated probabilistically: each stimulus s produces a neural response x = {xi}

drawn from a conditional distribution P (x|s), where xi is the response of neuron i. Mutual information I[x; s] mea-sures the strength of the relationship between stimulus and response: it quantifies how much the neural activity reduces our uncertainty about the stimulus [Fig. 1(a)] [10– 16]. Consequently, it provides the foundation for theories of neural coding, including coding efficiency [17–22], maxi-mizing entropy [23–25], feature representation [11, 26, 27], and the information bottleneck [28, 29]. Modern experiments now provide measurements of the activity of thousands of neurons simultaneously [30–35]. However, as recordings increase in size, the number of possible responses explodes exponentially, posing two challenges in the estimation of mutual information. First, one must define or estimate the distribution of neural responses for each stimulus P (x|s). Even in the simplest scenario where each neuron is binary ( xi = 1 for active or 

xi = 0 for silent), there are still K2N different stimulus-response pairs, where N is the size of the population and K is the number of stimuli. As a result, directly estimating the response probabilities P (x|s) is only pos-sible in small populations of N ∼ 10 cells. Second, one must compute the expectation of log[ P (x|s)/P (x)] over all stimuli and responses. While both challenges stem from the exponential growth of possible responses, we will see that only the former fundamentally limits our ability to reliably estimate mutual information. Existing methods for estimating mutual information in neural populations can be organized by how they confront the curse of dimensionality. Direct estimators compute mutual information from observed response counts, often with sparse-pattern approximations such as the singleton estimator [12, 36, 37]. These methods make few assump-tions about the structure of the population code, but are restricted to small populations or to regimes in which the relevant response patterns are sufficiently sampled. A second approach is to introduce explicit structure into the population distribution using maximum-entropy mod-els, latent-variable models, or other parametric stimulus-response models [24, 38–40]. Such models can reveal how correlations affect coding, but computing P (x|s) still re-quires normalization, which is typically only possible for small networks. A third strategy avoids the full response space by estimating information after dimensionality re-duction, or by training neural-network estimators to ap-proximate information-theoretic objectives [41–43]. These approaches are powerful in settings where the relevant dependence is low-dimensional or where large training sets are available. Here, we study high-dimensional mutual information in large neural populations under the assumption of conditional independence. As the natural first-order model for P (x|s), conditional independence reduces the intractable challenge of estimating probabilities for all 

K2N stimulus-response pairs to the manageable problem of estimating N × K single-neuron firing probabilities 

P (xi|s). The remaining obstacle in computing the expectation of log P (x|s)/P (x) can be overcome with a Monte Carlo estimator that samples stimulus-response pairs from the conditionally independent model and averages the information density directly [14, 44, 45]. We validate this approach using an analytically tractable model for which the ground-truth mutual information can be computed exactly, establishing reliable convergence with only a modest number of samples. Applying this method to large-scale recordings from mouse visual cortex, mouse hippocampus, and salamander retina, we find that stimulus uncertainty decays exponentially with the number of neurons. These results confirm existing theoretical predictions [46] and provide a starting point for estimating mutual information in neural populations far beyond the reach of direct enumeration. arXiv:2610.22206v1 [physics.bio-ph] 15 Oct 2026 2(a)

High-dimensional neural coding —We are now prepared to study mutual information in large populations of neu-rons. We first consider N = 8 , 991 neurons in the mouse visual cortex, recorded using calcium imaging in previous experiments as the animal observes K = 32 different ori-entation gratings [50]. After preprocessing, each neuron is classified as active ( xi = 1 ) or silent ( xi = 0 ) for each stimulus presentation (Appendix). In this population, we find that the conditionally independent model [Eq. (2)] quantitatively predicts the correlations between neurons [Fig. 3(a), inset ], suggesting that it provides an accurate description of the population responses. To understand how mutual information scales with population size, we randomly subsample neurons and, for each subpopulation, compute the finite-data-corrected mutual information. Just as for synthetic neurons, we find that the Monte Carlo estimator quickly converges with the number of samples Ts and that finite data corrections are small (Supplemental Material). The mutual information increases rapidly with the number of neurons N [Fig. 3(a)]. Only 63 neurons are needed to halve the uncertainty about the stimulus, quantified by the conditional entropy 

H[s|x] = H[s] − I[x; s]. With 613 neurons, the mutual information reaches within 1% of its maximum possible value, the stimulus entropy H[s].We next investigate N = 11 , 523 neurons in the mouse visual cortex, recorded in the same experiment as the animal observes K = 32 natural images [50]. Compared to the orientation gratings, the conditionally independent model captures less of the correlation structure in the population [Fig. 3(b), inset ]. However, we find that the mutual information grows at a similarly rapid rate with the number of neurons N [Fig. 3(b)]. With N = 71 neu-rons, uncertainty about the stimulus is reduced by half, and 513 neurons are sufficient to raise the mutual infor-mation to within 1% of the stimulus entropy [Fig. 3(b)]. While neurons in the visual cortex respond to visual stimuli, in the hippocampus, some neurons are tuned to fire when the animal is in a specific location [51, 52]. Exponential decay of conditional entropy — A clear fea-ture shared across all four populations, as well as simu-lated neurons, is the rapid saturation of mutual informa-tion with population size. Indeed, previous theoretical results predict that the conditional entropy H[s|x] decays exponentially for conditionally independent neurons [46]. Moreover, the exponent of this decay is the Chernoff dis-tance between response probabilities induced by different stimuli [46]. Plotting the estimated conditional entropy as a function of N , we find a clear exponential decay in all populations (Fig. 4). A larger decay exponent λ means that fewer neurons are required for the same decrease in stimulus uncertainty 

H[s|x], or, equivalently, for the same increase in mutual information I[x; s]. Intuitively, neurons whose responses vary significantly between stimuli should carry more in-formation. By contrast, neurons with low firing rates or whose responses vary only weakly between stimuli should contribute less to stimulus decoding. In each population, we can divide the neurons into those that respond sig-nificantly to a subset of stimuli and those that do not (Supplemental Material). For each group, we sample populations of increasing size N and fit the exponential scaling of the conditional entropy H[s|x]. Consistent with the above intuition, neurons with significant responses exhibit a steeper decay in stimulus uncertainty, character-ized by a larger exponent λ (Fig. 4). In the mouse visual cortex and hippocampus, non-significant neurons lead to conditional entropy that decays less than half as fast as significant cells [Fig. 4(a)-(c)]. Discussion —Quantifying how neural populations en-code stimuli is central to understanding sensory and cognitive computation [54–56]. However, estimating population-level mutual information is notoriously diffi-cult due to the exponential explosion of possible responses with population size. Here, we use a Monte Carlo frame-work to estimate high-dimensional mutual information between population responses and discrete stimulus vari-ables under conditional independence. We model the re-sponse distribution P (x|s) as the product of single-neuron firing probabilities, which can be reliably estimated from experimental data. We then sample stimulus-response pairs to estimate mutual information. While Monte Carlo methods have previously been used to study synthetic neurons [14, 44], here we directly infer conditionally in-dependent models from large-scale recordings of neural activity. We verify our mutual information estimates in a model of synthetic neurons, which we solve analytically. For empirical recordings of large populations, we apply finite-data corrections to account for sampling bias, demon-strating that high-dimensional mutual information can be reliably estimated with only a modest number of ex-perimental samples. Across mouse visual cortex, mouse hippocampus, and salamander retina, we find that mutual information saturates exponentially with population size. These results immediately open the door for future research in several directions. First, our Monte Carlo estimates can be generalized to any model of population responses P (x|s). As a natural starting point, here we focus on the conditionally independent model in Eq. (2). However, neural populations often contain shared variabil-ity, or noise correlations, that are not captured by condi-tional independence [50, 57–59]. Therefore, an important next step is to investigate richer models P (x|s), perhaps including recent maximum entropy models that scale to large populations [24, 25, 60]. Second, the framework is not tied to a specific brain region, species, recording modality, stimulus variable, or response state space. It can be applied to any population activity for which one can define an external variable of interest, including sen-sory stimuli, spatial position, behavioral state, decision variables, or internal latent states. More broadly, the same strategy could be used to estimate high-dimensional mutual information in other complex biological systems composed of many interacting units, where direct esti-mation of the full joint distribution is impossible. Thus, this work provides a path toward extending information-theoretic tools to the scale of modern neural and biological experiments. 6
