## ABSTRACT 

Reconstructing what a person perceives from their brain activity is a stringent test of whether neural recordings carry meaningful information. Naturalistic speech offers a rich testbed for this challenge: it unfolds over multiple timescales, but fMRI’s noisy, temporally smeared measurements make this structure hard to re-cover. We introduce NOEMA, a temporal convolutional decoder that aligns win-dows of consecutive fMRI measurements with multimodal stimulus embeddings. These embeddings combine complementary text, audio, and speech representa-tions of the same stimulus segment, enabling its retrieval directly from brain ac-tivity. On two naturalistic speech datasets, Cephalonauts One and LeBel , NOEMA retrieves the correct segment among 2,000 candidates within the top 10 for more than 42% of held-out queries. This substantially outperforms baseline decoders, an advantage that persists when the candidate pool is extended with thousands of distractors absent from training. Controlled ablations establish the contributions of temporal modeling and complementary stimulus representations. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Can one gain access to the cognitive experience of a living being from recordings of their brain activity? A growing body of work shows that machine learning models can reconstruct what a participant is viewing, hearing, or even imagining from brain activity alone (Horikawa and Kamitani, 2017). This decoding endeavor is tightly linked to representation learning: if a model can precisely predict cognitive states from brain activity, it should have extracted meaningful structures from the signals at hand, opening a window into a better understanding of the complexity of the living brain. Seminal work from Haxby et al. (2001) showed that fMRI measurements of the ventral visual stream contain sufficient information to distinguish the category of a viewed image from a small set of cate-gories. Recent data collection efforts like the Natural Scenes Dataset (Allen et al., 2022) have since scaled up the number of image categories, enabling more accurate decoders (Ozcelik and VanRullen, 2023). Yet, since these stimuli are static, these decoders mostly rely on spatial information - where the activations are happening on the cortex - to perform their task. Given the inherently dynamic nature of the brain, it is crucial to develop decoding approaches that can leverage, and therefore reveal, the temporal structure of the underlying neural processes.

To this end, naturalistic speech stimuli are particularly promising: unlike static images, they unfold over time, giving decoders temporal structure to exploit. Yet fMRI trades temporal precision for spatial resolution, while M/EEG offer the reverse trade-off. Pereira et al. (2018) showed that fMRI can reconstruct the semantic content of words and sentences, primarily by leveraging spatial pat-terns of brain activity, while D´ efossez et al. (2023) showed that M/EEG support speech decoding through their finer temporal dynamics. This suggests that spatial and temporal information may offer complementary routes to decoding naturalistic speech. Seeking to leverage both, we introduce Neu-ral Ordered Embeddings with Multimodal Alignment (NOEMA), a temporal convolutional decoder that maps windows of consecutive fMRI measurements to multimodal representations of the cor-responding speech segments (Figure 1). On two large, densely-sampled speech datasets, NOEMA substantially outperforms existing decoding baselines on held-out sessions and extended retrieval sets. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Natural language decoding from fMRI. Pereira et al. (2018) trained semantic decoders on individ-ual concepts and generalized to sentences, while Brain2Word (Affolter et al., 2020) decoded words from a fixed vocabulary. For naturalistic listening, Huth et al. (2016) modeled cortical responses to semantic features; Tang et al. (2023) combined an encoding model with language-model-guided beam search to reconstruct continuous text. BrainLLM (Ye et al., 2025) instead feeds fMRI-derived embeddings directly into a language model. 

Temporal integration of fMRI frames. Prior methods integrate temporal context in different ways. Jalouzot et al. (2025) average consecutive volumes before decoding text embeddings. UniCoRN (Xi et al., 2023) combines snapshot and sequence reconstruction pretraining with text generation. BP-GPT (Chen et al., 2025) encodes fMRI windows into language-model prompts, using contrastive alignment with text prompts alongside a generation loss. CogReader (Lu et al., 2025) combines spatiotemporal encoding with semantic summaries carried between generated segments. UniCoRN reports lower generation scores under stimulus-disjoint than random splits; BP-GPT evaluates on a held-out story, and CogReader uses stimulus-disjoint splits from naturalistic stories. These gener-ation results are not directly comparable to segment retrieval. Here, we compare learned temporal convolutions with frame-wise decoders and an MLP receiving the same context. 

Cross-modal alignment with contrastive learning. CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) aligns paired modalities for retrieval in a shared space. D´ efossez et al. (2023) aligned MEG and EEG with heard-audio representations, while MindEye (Scotti et al., 2023) used contrastive alignment with CLIP image representations for fMRI decoding. Language-model representations predict neural responses to language (Schrimpf et al., 2021; Caucheteux and King, 2022; Pasquiou et al., 2022), and can guide stimulus selection to modulate language-network responses (Tuckute et al., 2024). ## 3 METHOD 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION AND RETRIEVAL OBJECTIVE 

Decoding task. We frame naturalistic speech decoding as a retrieval problem: given fMRI measure-ments, the decoder must identify the corresponding perceived speech segment among candidate seg-ments. As illustrated in Figure 1, we learn a participant-specific decoder fθ that maps Xi ∈ RN ×V ,a window of N fMRI frames with V cortical features, to yi ∈ RD , the embedding of the corre-sponding speech segment. At evaluation, we rank the cosine similarities sij between the predicted embedding ˆyi ≜ fθ (Xi) and each of M candidate segment embeddings yj from a held-out session. In particular, at inference, the decoder receives only fMRI; candidate audio and transcripts are used solely to precompute the fixed stimulus embeddings.

Temporal window. NOEMA instantiates fθ as a temporal convolutional decoder that maps N =7 consecutive fMRI frames, each lasting ∆ seconds, to the stimulus embedding associated with the central frame (Figure 2A). For a given stimulus context ending at time t, the associated fMRI window is centered at t + δ (where δ = 2 .5 seconds), and lasts N × ∆ seconds (see Section 4.1 for values). We use an alignment offset of 2.5 s, which yields the highest accuracy for the MLP and near-peak accuracy for NOEMA (Appendix C). 

Spatial projection. A pointwise (1 ×1) convolution maps V cortical features to a hidden width of 

H = 4096 , followed by GELU and dropout with probability 0.7. More simply, this convolution ap-plies the same linear projection independently to each frame, preserving temporal order and yielding an N × H representation per sample. 

Temporal mixing. Two residual temporal convolutional blocks (Bai et al., 2018) integrate consec-utive frames using kernel size K = 3 and dilations d1 = 1 and d2 = 2 , measured in frames. Each TCN block applies a dilated convolution, GELU, and a pointwise convolution, followed by residual addition and layer normalization (Figure 2B). Together, the blocks give the central representation a seven-frame receptive field that includes both earlier and later measurements, making the decoder noncausal. 

Central-frame prediction. A final linear layer maps the central representation of dimension H to the D = 8704 -dimensional multimodal target space (Section 3.3). Only this prediction contributes to the training loss and retrieval score. Appendix B.1 summarizes the decoder architecture. 3.3 MULTIMODAL STIMULUS REPRESENTATIONS 

Stimulus encoders. We construct multimodal stimulus targets from three frozen encoders operating on text, audio, and speech, extracted at a sequence of time markers t spaced by ∆ seconds. Text and audio share a longer context (t − T, t ] with T = 10 seconds: transcript words are selected by their end times, and audio uses the corresponding waveform segment. Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025) embeds the transcripts and supplies 4096 text features from the last non-padding token of its final hidden layer. LCO-Embedding-Omni-7B (Xiao et al., 2025) supplies 3584 audio features from the audio waveform. Finally, the ASR encoder Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 (Sekoyan et al., 2025) uses a window of size ∆ seconds, (t − ∆, t ], producing 1024 speech features by averaging its penultimate encoder-layer states within that interval. This shorter context provides a temporally precise speech target that complements the broader text and audio contexts. Since this window matches the marker spacing, successive speech targets tile the stimulus without overlap, giving them a sequential, window-specific organization. We evaluate the effect of changing the speech context in Appendix D. The encoders are used only to precompute targets for training and evaluation; their parameters remain fixed while the participant-specific fMRI decoder is trained.

## 4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

4.1 DATASETS , PREPROCESSING , AND SPLITS 

Datasets. We evaluate NOEMA on the naturalistic speech decoding task across two datasets, train-ing and evaluating a separate decoder for each participant in each dataset independently: 4Decoding Naturalistic Speech from fMRI PREPRINT 

• Cephalonauts One (Collas et al., 2026): fMRI recordings from three participants listening to podcasts in their native language, French. Each participant completed approximately 30 hours across 24 sessions; sessions are shared across participants, but no stimulus is repeated within a participant. • LeBel (LeBel et al., 2023): fMRI recordings from eight participants listening to 6 hours of English stories. We focus on the three participants who completed an additional 10 hours, for sufficient training data, and exclude localizer runs and the repeatedly presented story 

Where There’s Smoke .Table 1 in Appendix A reports the number of sessions, runs, and acquisition hours for each partici-pant. 

Preprocessing. For both datasets, we use DeepPrep (Ren et al., 2025) to process fMRI images and reconstruct fsaverage5 cortical surfaces, concatenate vertex data from both hemispheres, and detrend and standardize each run’s vertex time series. Cephalonauts One recordings have a native repetition time (TR) of 0.52 seconds. Following the Cephalonauts One preprocessing protocol (Col-las et al., 2026), we apply a four-TR moving average and retain every fourth frame, yielding a frame spacing of ∆ = 2 .08 seconds. LeBel ’s native TR already matches the stimulus marker spacing (∆ = 2 .0 seconds), so we keep the original frame spacing without additional smoothing. Using this preprocessed data, we form the seven-frame fMRI windows described in Section 3.2. 

Dataset splits. Consecutive fMRI frames are temporally correlated and share stimulus context, so random splits would leak information between training and evaluation. We therefore split by recording session, which, given the datasets’ design, also ensures that no stimulus is shared across training, validation, and test sets. Each model is evaluated on five splits, each holding out one session for testing, one for validation, and using the rest for training. For Cephalonauts One , the five test sessions are those of the dataset benchmark (Collas et al., 2026). 4.2 BASELINES , TRAINING , AND EVALUATION 

Baselines. We compare NOEMA with four baselines: (1) Ridge , a center-frame Ridge regressor with L2 regularization; (2) MLP , a single-frame MLP with one hidden layer and GELU activation, from Collas et al. (2026); (3) MindEye MLP , a deeper frame-wise MLP with four residual blocks and GELU activation, adapted from Scotti et al. (2023); and (4) Context MLP , which applies MLP ’s architecture to concatenated fMRI frames, controlling for access to the same temporal input window. All baselines predict the same concatenated target embedding as NOEMA, using identical training and evaluation splits. Unless stated otherwise, NOEMA and Context MLP receive seven-frame windows while the remaining baselines receive only the central frame; Context MLP keeps its name even when this window size is varied in the temporal-context ablation (Section 5), to distinguish it from the single-frame MLP. 

Training. The Ridge baseline is fitted in closed form using α = 4 .5 × 10 3, selected by the hy-perparameter search described in Appendix B.2. All other baselines and NOEMA are trained with AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) for up to 200 epochs with an early stopping patience of 10 epochs, using hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay, batch size, temperature) tuned through a Bayesian search with Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019). For NOEMA, this yields a learning rate of 

1.1 × 10 −4, a weight decay of 6.8 × 10 −3, a batch size of 4096 , and a temperature of 6.2 × 10 −3;the complete search space and final settings for every model are given in Appendix B.2. Training uses a single NVIDIA H100 GPU (80GB) or AMD MI250X GPU. 

Retrieval settings. For model comparisons and ablations on both datasets, the retrieval set consists of all stimulus embeddings from the held-out test session. The retrieval-set extension experiment (Section 5.3) instead evaluates generalization beyond the original stimulus collection: for Cephalo-nauts One , we progressively augment each test-session retrieval set with up to 8,494 embeddings corresponding to ten-second segments from 50 additional episodes of the same radio podcast, absent from the original dataset, reaching a retrieval set roughly five times larger than the original at the largest extension.

## 5 RESULTS 

5.1 RETRIEVAL PERFORMANCE 

We first evaluate how accurately each decoder identifies the speech segment that elicited a given neural response. Figure 3 compares NOEMA with the baseline decoders on Cephalonauts One 

and LeBel datasets. All models predict the same multimodal (text, audio, speech) targets and are tuned according to the hyperparameter optimization procedure described in Appendix B.2. NOEMA consistently outperforms all baselines across subjects, retrieving the correct speech segment within the top 10 candidates for 42.7% of queries on Cephalonauts One and 43.6% on LeBel , far above the chance level of 0.5%. Notably, NOEMA also surpasses the Context MLP, even though both models receive the same input context and the Context MLP has more parameters (Appendix F.4). NOEMA’s advantage therefore does not appear to only arise from additional context or greater model capacity. One plausible explanation is its use of temporal convolutions, which explicitly model the temporal structure of the neural signal. Stimulus representations. To assess the contribution of each stimulus representation, we trained NOEMA, MLP, and Context MLP on targets built from cumulative combinations of text (T), audio (A), and speech (S) representations. On Cephalonauts One , adding audio to a text-only target raises NOEMA’s Top-10@2000 accuracy from 27.6% to 35.5% (Figure 4a), and further adding speech to the text-and-audio target raises it to 42.7% (Figure 4b). Adding audio and then speech improves retrieval for all three models in every subject and fold, with the largest mean gains for NOEMA. These gains exceed those of Context MLP, which receives the same seven-frame input, by 1.58 and 2.34 percentage points, respectively. The same pattern holds on LeBel , where adding speech has an even larger effect (Appendix F.1). Absolute accuracies for each model under the T, T+A, and T+A+S targets are reported in Appendix F.2. 

Temporal context. To assess how effectively each architecture exploits information spread across neighboring fMRI frames, we train Context MLP and NOEMA with 1, 3, 5, or 7 input frames. Adding frames increases Context MLP’s parameter count, while NOEMA’s remains fixed. Both models benefit from additional frames, with larger accuracy gains for NOEMA (Figure 4c). On 

Cephalonauts One , NOEMA improves steadily with each added frame, rising from 28.7% Top-10@2000 accuracy with one frame to 42.7% with seven. Context MLP, in contrast, rises from 25.4% to a peak of 34.5% at five frames and declines slightly to 33.7% at seven. On LeBel , both models im-prove at every window length, and again NOEMA benefits more (Appendix F.1). NOEMA achieves this higher accuracy with fewer parameters: 253.8M trainable parameters, compared with 623.0M for the seven-frame Context MLP (Appendix F.4). These results suggest that NOEMA’s temporal convolutions make better use of additional fMRI frames than the tested Context MLP. Absolute ac-curacies are reported in Appendix F.3. To probe the role of this temporal inductive bias, we retrain and evaluate both models with their input frames permuted. A permutation shared across samples can be offset by reordering the Context MLP’s input weights, but it disrupts the temporal neigh-borhoods on which NOEMA’s convolutions rely. Retrieval beyond the original dataset. Retrieval within a single test session uses a closed set of about 2,000 candidates. A practical decoder, however, must identify the correct segment within a much larger, open-ended pool. To test whether our conclusions hold in this setting, we extend each 

Cephalonauts One test-session retrieval set with up to 8,494 ten-second segments drawn from 50 episodes of the same radio podcast that are not part of the dataset (Appendix I). Drawing distrac-tors from the same show limits broad differences in recording conditions and stimulus domain; this reduces, but does not eliminate, acoustic and stylistic differences between original and added can-didates. All models are evaluated without retraining, and we track the median rank of the correct segment as the retrieval set grows. If the added distractors were exactly as confusable as the origi-nal candidates, the median rank would grow in proportion to the retrieval-set size, and the median relative rank would stay constant. 

Effect of retrieval-set size. NOEMA shows the smallest absolute increase in median rank as dis-tractors are added. At the largest extension, its median rank increases by 66 positions, compared with 112 and 191 for the Context MLP and MLP baselines (Figure 5a). Relative to their original median rank, the ranks of all three models grow by a similar factor (4.5-4.6 × for a 5.1 × larger set; Figure 5b). Because NOEMA already ranks the correct target within the top 0.9% of candidates, compared with 2.5% for the MLP, it moves fewer positions as the pool grows. Across all three models, median rank increases slightly less than proportionally to retrieval-set size, yielding a small decrease in median relative rank (Figure 5c). NOEMA maintains the lowest median relative rank throughout the expansion. 5.4 MODEL INTERPRETATION

## 6 CONCLUSION 

NOEMA combines temporal modeling of fMRI with alignment to text, audio, and speech represen-tations to improve naturalistic speech retrieval. On entirely held-out recording sessions containing speech stimuli absent from training, NOEMA outperforms all baselines across two naturalistic lis-tening datasets, demonstrating generalization to new sessions and stimuli within participants. This advantage is preserved when the retrieval set is extended with external distractors. Our ablations show that both temporal context and the choice of target representations contribute to retrieval performance, supporting the view that the temporal structure of brain measurements and the representations used for supervision are complementary design choices for speech decoders. NOEMA benefits more from additional fMRI frames than an MLP receiving the same context, despite having fewer parameters, and its performance depends on chronological frame order. 

Limitations and future work. Our evaluation is limited to six participants across two listening datasets, with a separate decoder trained for each participant; generalization to new individuals and to other tasks remains untested, and extending NOEMA across participants is a natural next step. Our analyses also do not establish what underlies the benefit of temporal context: it may reflect accommodation of the delayed hemodynamic response, stimulus-related temporal structure, or both. Moreover, the temporal window is bounded by the architecture’s seven-frame receptive field, and the current results do not establish an optimal context length. Finally, NOEMA retrieves from a predefined candidate set. Although its advantage persists with external distractors, retrieval from any finite set does not demonstrate unrestricted speech reconstruction; using NOEMA’s predicted embeddings to guide text generation is a promising direction toward this goal. 9Decoding Naturalistic Speech from fMRI PREPRINT

## D ASR CONTEXT WINDOW 

Section 3.3 sets the trailing context window of the ASR encoder equal to the marker spacing ∆

(2.08 seconds for Cephalonauts One , 2.0 seconds for LeBel ), so that successive speech targets tile the stimulus without overlap. Here we test whether a different window improves retrieval. We vary the Parakeet window over 1 second, ∆, and 3 to 10 seconds in 1-second steps, keeping the marker spacing ∆ and the text and audio context T = 10 seconds fixed. NOEMA and the MLP are retrained at each window with the hyperparameters of Appendix B.2 on five session folds, for one subject per dataset ( Cephalonauts One sub-01 and LeBel UTS01), for 200 fits in total. Accuracy follows an inverted-U shape for both models on both datasets (Figure 9). NOEMA’s Top-10@2000 accuracy peaks at 5 seconds on Cephalonauts One (48.19%, compared with 43.27% at 1 second and 42.55% at 10 seconds) and at 3 seconds on LeBel (39.84%, compared with 33.18% and 32.13%). The default window lies 1.70 and 2.52 percentage points below these peaks, respectively; for the MLP, the gaps are 1.03 and 0.82 points. Validation accuracy peaks at the same windows, so tuning the ASR window per dataset could bring further gains. ## G SENSITIVITY TO F MRI FRAME ORDER 

Figure 17 compares seven-frame Context MLP and NOEMA after retraining and testing with shuf-fled frame orders. Each input retains exactly the same frame values and prediction target; only the assignment of frames to input positions changes. Because the shuffling rule is applied during both training and evaluation, this experiment measures the benefit of the original temporal organization after adaptation, rather than robustness to a test-time perturbation. 

A shared permutation probes the architectural bias toward temporal order. Context MLP is nearly unchanged ( +0 .33 percentage points): its first dense layer operates on concatenated frames and can absorb any fixed input permutation by reordering its weights. Thus, the model can learn position-specific temporal information without requiring neighboring input positions to correspond to neighboring acquisition times. NOEMA instead loses 4.26 points despite retraining. Its local, shared temporal filters impose structure on how frames interact; arbitrary permutations alter tem-poral neighborhoods and their relation to the central readout. This result suggests that NOEMA benefits from the chronological organization of fMRI frames, which its temporal inductive bias is designed to exploit. 

Per-sample permutations probe the value of consistent temporal positions. Shuffling each sam-ple differently breaks the temporal ordering across samples. Accuracy falls by 10 .90 points for Context MLP and 18 .46 for NOEMA. Both models therefore benefit from consistent temporal posi-tions, with a larger observed dependence for NOEMA. ## I EXTENDED RETRIEVAL SETS 

The extended retrieval sets in Section 5.3 draw on 50 additional episodes of the podcast show used in 

Cephalonauts One , none of which appear in the dataset. We transcribe these episodes with the As-semblyAI universal-3-pro speech-to-text model, as for the original stimuli (Collas et al., 2026), and split them into non-overlapping ten-second segments. Treating the end of each segment as a time marker t, we compute its text, audio, and speech embeddings exactly as for the original stimuli (Section 3.3), yielding 8,494 additional candidates. For each test session, we add random subsets of 500, 1,000, 2,000, and 5,000 candidates, or all 8,494, to the original retrieval set of approximately 2,000 segments. We evaluate the MLP, Context MLP, and NOEMA, trained as in Section 4.2, on each extended set without retraining.
