# An FPGA-Based Spiking Neural Network for Spatiotemporal Pattern Learning and Classification 

Jingang Jin, Yue Ma, Zhenhang Zhang and Qinru Qiu 

Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science Syracuse University, Syracuse, New York, USA 

{jjin24, yma183, zzhan281, qiqiu }@syr.edu 

Abstract —Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) are promising for energy-efficient spatiotemporal pattern recognition at the edge, but efficient deployment and on-device adaptation re-quire specialized hardware. In this work, we present an FPGA implementation of multilayer SNNs for spatiotemporal pattern classification, in which both inference and training take place on the same hardware. Our design adopts a temporal-forward-only training algorithm that avoids the memory and latency overhead associated with traditional Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT). A mixed-precision scheme balances training sta-bility with resource efficiency, and architectural optimization maximizes hardware and memory utilization. We further identify and resolve hazards that arise when inference and learning share the same memory, including port conflicts between the forward and backward access patterns and read-after-write dependencies in floating-point potential accumulation. Experimental evalua-tions on MNIST and five multivariate time-series classification datasets demonstrate that, with fully on-chip training, our SNN model achieves accuracy comparable to SNN or LSTM trained with BPTT. Compared to CPU and GPU based implementations, our FPGA design delivers 2.5× to 6× higher throughput while consuming an order of magnitude less energy per sample. 

Index Terms —Neuromorphic Computing, Spiking Neural Net-work, In-hardware learning, FPGA 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Driven by the emergence of cyber-physical systems in recent years, a growing number of neural network models have been integrated into edge devices, such as smart vehicles and wearable sensors. Because user behavior, environmental conditions, and even sensor characteristics vary over time and across systems, it is highly desirable for neural networks to adapt to their specific hardware and application environment through in-hardware learning after deployment, in order to maintain performance. There is also increasing demand for edge devices to process temporal sensor readings not as static, frame-based input, but as a stream with spatiotemporal structure. Compared to buffering an entire sequence and classifying all temporal samples at once, a state-space model (e.g., a recurrent neural network (RNN)) that processes input sample-by-sample incurs much lower computational cost and latency, making it more suitable for edge applications. However, a key challenge is that RNNs are trained using Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), which unrolls the network over time, so its memory requirement grows linearly with sequence length. Moreover, training cannot begin until the entire sequence has been re-ceived, which again causes high latency and a bursty workload. These properties make it difficult to train RNNs on edge devices. Spiking neural networks (SNNs) [1] [2] present a promising alternative to conventional artificial neural networks. They are extremely energy efficient due to their event-driven dynamics and sparse computation. They are also well suited for temporal pattern recognition, since each neuron maintains a hidden state, making the network inherently an RNN. Finally, recent research on three-factor Hebbian learning [30] has made it possible to train such networks for spatiotemporal classifi-cation without unrolling the network in time [31]. Hence, unlike BPTT training, where memory requirements grow with the length of the input sequence, memory requirements under these algorithms remain constant throughout training. Given that their computational patterns differ significantly from those of conventional ANNs, SNNs are not well-suited to traditional platforms like CPUs and GPUs, whose architectures are not optimized for sparse, event-driven, fine-grained paral-lelism. To leverage the full advantages of SNNs, researchers have proposed several dedicated neuromorphic processors, no-tably IBM TrueNorth [3] and Intel Loihi [4]. Although highly optimized, these neuromorphic chips often impose limitations on data precision, neuron models, and supported learning rules. As an emerging research area, SNN structure and training algorithms are evolving rapidly. These new dataflows and computing models require specialized hardware circuit, which current neuromorphic processors cannot efficiently support without approximation and simplification. Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) occupy a strate-gic middle ground, offering a compelling balance of flexibility and efficiency. They also provide massive parallelism similar to GPUs, but at a more fine-grained level. Researchers have proposed numerous designs for SNN acceleration on FPGAs [5] [6] [7] [8], which exhibit significantly lower energy con-sumption than their CPU or GPU counterparts while offering greater flexibility than ASIC-based neuromorphic processors. However, existing work primarily focuses on accelerating the inference of pre-trained SNN models, largely overlooking on-line learning scenarios that are prevalent in edge applications. When online learning is taken into consideration, SNN implementation complexity increases substantially. Numerous variables, including parameters and neuron potentials, are shared between the forward and backward propagation stages and accessed in diverse patterns, introducing both data and re-arXiv:2610.17463v1 [cs.AI] 12 Oct 2026 source (e.g., memory port) dependencies. These dependencies hinder efficient hardware utilization on FPGAs. In this work, we present an FPGA implementation of multilayer SNNs with on-chip learning capability. Our design integrates a stateful synapse filter structure within the spiking neuron model, a temporal forward-only training algorithm that eliminates the need for BPTT, and a conservative mixed-precision scheme to achieve high energy efficiency and pro-cessing throughput. We further introduce optimization strate-gies, such as loop unrolling, memory partition and read/write scheduling, for computation and memory access concurrency to fully exploit FPGA hardware resources. Memory hazards that are unique to systems hosting both forward and backward propagation are also identified and resolved. Experimental evaluations on MNIST and five multivariate time-series classification datasets demonstrate that our imple-mentation achieves accuracy comparable to SNN or ANN trained using BPTT. Compared to CPU- and GPU-based implementations, our FPGA acceleration delivers 2.5× to 6× higher throughput at roughly one-tenth the energy per sample. The contributions of this work are summarized as follows:  

> •

We implement multilayer SNNs on FPGAs with in-hardware learning capability, performing both training and inference entirely on-chip for spatiotemporal stream classification.  

> •

We propose a resource-aware loop unrolling strategy guided by the empirical observation that both resource consumption and execution cycles scale linearly with the unroll factor, and a coarse-grained pipelining strategy that overlaps weight updates with backward passes.  

> •

We propose a BRAM partitioning and interleaving scheme to resolve memory access conflicts between for-ward and backward passes, and use buffer insertion to resolve Read-After-Write (RAW) hazards.  

> •

B. Online Training of SNNs 

Online learning [19] and continual learning [17] are active research areas, but the common method for training SNNs poses a challenge. SNNs are often treated as a special class of RNN due to their internal memory and temporal filter-ing, making BPTT the standard training practice. However, BPTT’s requirement to unroll the network across the entire input sequence results in computational and memory demands incompatible with resource-constrained edge devices. To over-come this limitation, learning algorithms such as SOLSA [15], OTTT [18], and e-prop learning [31] have been proposed for non-BPTT, gradient-based optimization of SNNs. These algorithms are based on similar principles that accumulate temporal gradients in a forward-only manner to avoid the need for unrolling the computation graph. C. FPGA implementations of SNNs 

Driven by the efficiency of SNNs and the flexibility of FPGAs, many works have accelerated SNN inference on FPGA hardware. SyncNN [6] restructures event-driven SNNs into a synchronous dataflow for regular, hardware-friendly computation. FireFly v2 [11], building on its predecessor [8], exploits parallelism across input channels, output channels, pixels, and timesteps, and adds native hardware support for non-spike operations. The work in [7] co-designs the input en-coding, neuron model, and architecture to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy and latency. While effective, these works deploy pre-trained SNNs on FPGAs without in-hardware learning. Only a limited number of studies have explored on-chip SNN learning on FPGAs, and most of them rely on biologi-cally inspired but relatively simple learning rules. CORDIC-SNN [22] implements STDP learning with Izhikevich neurons using CORDIC-based arithmetic to replace expensive multi-pliers with shift-and-add operations, but is limited to a two-layer topology with a single output neuron. TripleBrain [23] combines self-organizing maps with reinforcement-modulated STDP in a fully event-driven hardware core, achieving com-petitive accuracy on MNIST at low resource cost, yet remains restricted to a single trainable layer. These STDP-based ap-proaches, while hardware-friendly, are inherently unsupervised or require reward signals, hence have limited applications in multilayer supervised learning. Beyond STDP, the work in [26] takes a fundamentally different approach by employing evolutionary algorithms as a black-box training method for Izhikevich-neuron-based SNNs on a Zynq SoC, enabling continual in-field adaptation without gradient computation. However, evolutionary methods typi-cally suffer from slow convergence and scale poorly with network size, making them impractical for larger architectures. HaSiST [27] implements backpropagation on FPGA using a hard-sigmoid surrogate derivative, achieving high accuracy on MNIST, but the training procedure operates on a surrogate ANN that is subsequently converted to an SNN, rather than training the SNN directly. We instead train multilayer SNNs directly on FPGA with no ANN-to-SNN conversion. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Design Rationale and Architecture Overview 

Our proposed implementation comprises two core compo-nents: a datapath implemented using on-chip memory and programmable logic, and a workflow controller running on the embedded ARM processor. The controller streams in samples from the input time series and collects the classification results. It also provides labels for loss calculation during training. The datapath consists of per-layer forward propagation, backward propagation, and update modules, plus a loss calculation module. We adopted a High Level Synthesis (HLS) based design flow in this work. SOLSA learning performs both forward and backward propagation once per input sample, doubling the volume of memory accesses compared to an inference only system. On the other hand, since it does not require unrolling the network or storing the history of neuron activities, it has much lower storage requirements than conventional learning algorithms. This allows us to train networks of decent size while constraining the neuron states and weight parameters to fit within the FPGA’s on-chip Block RAM (BRAM), rather than relying on off-chip DRAM. 

B. Mixed Data Precision 

Prior FPGA acceleration of SNN inference exploited aggres-sive quantization, reducing weights to 4-bit fixed-point [6], ternary [28], or integer-only [29] representations. However, in-hardware learning imposes tighter precision requirements, as quantization errors accumulate through gradient compu-tation and weight updates, degrading training convergence. We therefore adopt a conservative mixed-precision scheme: FP32 for neuron potential ( Vj [t]) and FP16 for weight storage (wij ). This decision is to balance the precision and memory size. Since a fully connected layer with N inputs and M neurons requires N×M weights but only M potentials, weights dominate the memory footprint. Applying FP16 to them yields a substantial reduction in both storage and resource utilization, while FP32 for the comparatively few potentials ensures stable training. For the similar reason, we adopt FP32 for Fi, εi,j ,

μj and gi,j introduced in Section II-B. The binary nature of spikes enables additional simplifica-tion: using only 1 bit for the spike reduces the multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation on spike input to simple sum-mations, and the spike output of the activation function can be represented by the negation of the sign bit of the difference between membrane potential and threshold. C. Loop Unrolling 

SNN computation involves extensive vector and matrix operations, which are initially implemented as nested loops in HLS. While sequential execution of these loops induces high latency, unrolling all loops for maximum parallelism is infeasible due to limited hardware resources. The challenge is to determine the optimal unroll factor for each loop under a constrained resource budget. To address this, we profile each loop by synthesizing it at different unroll factors and recording the resulting execution time (measured in clock cycles) and resource utilization. For nested loop, only inner loop is unrolled due to hardware resource limitation. The results demonstrate a linear relationship between the unroll factor and both resource consumption and execution time. Based on this profiling, two linear estimation models, Cl(Ul)

and Rl(Ul), are trained for each loop l to predict its execution time and resource usage, respectively, under unroll factor Ul .Guided by these models, we search for unroll factors using the greedy strategy depicted in Algorithm 1. E. Memory Partitioning 

There are some unique hazards associated to running both inference and learning on the same hardware. First, the for-ward and backward passes access the same weight matrix, but in conflicting orders: the forward path reads it row-major, while the backward path reads it column-major. This conflict is exacerbated by loop unrolling. Each BRAM block has only two I/O ports, so with an unroll factor of k, the module must fetch k weights per cycle; if those k elements fall within the same block, the accesses are serialized, negating the benefit of unrolling. Because the two passes require opposing access patterns, no single memory layout can eliminate conflicts for both simultaneously. A layout optimized for the forward pass reintroduces conflicts for the backward pass, and vice versa. An inference-only design, which performs only the forward pass, never encounters this conflict. We resolve this hazard with memory partitioning and in-terleaving. The weight matrix is divided into k × k tiles, each mapped to a separate BRAM block with independent I/O ports; element wi,j is assigned to tile [ i mod k, j mod k ], as illustrated in Fig. 3. Any k consecutive elements along either a row or a column are thus distributed across k distinct blocks, Fig. 4. Resolution of RAW hazard with buffer

F. Buffer Insertion 

The second hazard unique to supporting on-chip learning is the Read-After-Write (RAW) dependency in the accumulating floating point values, such as Vj = P 

> i

wi,j Fi[t]. Learning on-chip requires high precision membrane potentials. Single precision (FP32) floating point adder on Xilinx FPGA typically has a pipeline latency of 3–5 cycles. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), every iteration updates the same accumulator, iteration k must read the value written by iteration k−1; and because the floating-point adder has a multi-cycle latency L,that value is not ready in the next L cycles. Such RAW hazard will not occur in inference only system where fixed-point precision is typically used. HLS automatically resolves such RAW hazards by raising the pipeline’s initiation interval (II) of the pipeline to L.However, this serializes the accumulation and negates the benefit of pipelining. We remove this dependency using an intermediate buffer that splits the accumulator into P partial sums: iteration k accumulates into line (k mod P ), so each line is updated only once every P iterations (Fig. 4 (b)). The dependency distance per line is then P cycles at II = 1 , and choosing P ≥ L sustains II = 1 regardless of the reduction length. Since the measured latency of the floating point adder is about four cycles, four buffer lines suffice. A final step sums the partial sums into the potential at negligible cost. IV. It also shows that the on-FPGA trained SNN performs comparable or even slightly better than the LSTM model.

As a result, it can be seamless migrated to smaller, cost-effective FPGAs for lower-throughput tasks without requiring architectural modifications. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented an FPGA implementation of a multilayer SNN that performs both inference and online learning entirely on-chip. By combining a temporal forward-only training algo-rithm with a conservative FP32/FP16 mixed-precision scheme and a set of architecture-level optimizations—resource-aware loop unrolling, coarse-grained pipelining, and BRAM par-titioning with hazard-resolving buffers—the design sustains gradient-based learning under tight edge resource budgets. On MNIST it reaches 95.92% accuracy with fully on-chip training, surpassing TripleBrain (95.10%) and approaching the off-chip-trained HaSiST (97.5%), and stays within 3% of BPTT-trained baselines on five temporal datasets. It delivers 2.5× to 6× the throughput of CPU and GPU platforms at roughly one-tenth the energy per sample, with headroom for real-time edge learning. The linear scaling of resource usage lets the same architecture migrate across FPGA capacities without modification.
