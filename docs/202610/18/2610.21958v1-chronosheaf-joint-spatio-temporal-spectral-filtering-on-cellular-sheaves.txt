ABSTRACT 

Sheaf neural networks generalize graph neural networks by assigning each edge a learned linear restriction map that specifies how the local representations of ad-jacent nodes relate to one another, thereby mitigating oversmoothing. Existing sheaf architectures, however, define the sheaf Laplacian as an instantaneous spa-tial operator and therefore cannot explicitly model how signals evolve over time on a graph. We first introduce a spectral sheaf Laplacian, defined jointly over space and time, such that a single operator regulates signal propagation across both the spatial graph and the temporal domain. Directly extending this opera-tor’s receptive field in space or time would require increasingly complex sheaf structures. Instead, we propose a spectral filter that expands the effective recep-tive field along both dimensions without enlarging the underlying sheaf. Building on this operator, we introduce ChronoSheaf , a spatio-temporal sheaf network with two key components. First, Transitional Restriction Maps (TRM) decom-pose each node’s restriction map into a spatial term and two temporal frames that independently control its relation to neighboring nodes at the preceding and following timestep, enabling asymmetric temporal influence. Second, the Dual Spectral Filter (DSF) applies a single bounded-degree polynomial filter with an adaptively estimated spectral radius to the joint spatio-temporal sheaf operator. We validate ChronoSheaf on a diverse set of spatio-temporal forecasting bench-marks, where it outperforms state-of-the-art baselines. The code is available at: 

https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ChronoSheaf-1D7D/ .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Sheaf neural networks generalize graph neural networks by assigning a vector space (stalk) to each node and edge and learning linear restriction maps between them, which lets them model hetero-geneous relationships. The ordinary graph Laplacian is recovered as a special case of the constant sheaf, where every stalk is identical, and every restriction map is the identity. By letting restric-tion maps be learned and non-trivial, Bodnar et al. (2022) showed that a node’s local frame need not agree with its neighbors’ outright, giving sheaf neural networks a proven advantage over con-ventional graph neural networks on heterophilic graphs and against oversmoothing. In every such construction, however, the sheaf is defined purely over the spatial graph. Even when restriction maps are learned from node features (Bodnar et al., 2022), the base space contains no notion of time, so the sheaf Laplacian only learns from nodes observed at the same instant, and the associated diffusion relaxes toward a static equilibrium, the space of harmonic cochains (Hansen & Ghrist, 2019). As a consequence, increasing depth or filter order does not let a spatial sheaf Laplacian reach across time. Hence, temporal context must come from a separate module outside the sheaf that does not benefit from its learned local frames. This restriction has a direct architectural consequence for spatio-temporal graph learning. The domi-nant paradigm thereby depends on conventional GNN architectures which are prone to oversmooth-ing and compose two separate operators, a spatial graph operator, and a recurrent, convolutional, or attention-based module for temporal dependence (Li et al., 2018b; Yu et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). Marisca et al. (2025) recently showed 1arXiv:2610.21958v1 [cs.LG] 15 Oct 2026 Under review preprint this composition has a measurable cost; because causal temporal convolutions propagate informa-tion only forward, deeper stacks accumulate disproportionately more paths from early timesteps than from recent ones, so convolutional spatio-temporal GNNs counterintuitively favor temporally distant information over nearby steps. We address this gap by defining a single spectral sheaf operator jointly over space and time, so that one filtering recursion controls both spatial and temporal dependence. This operator extends the sheaf domain from the spatial graph alone to a space-time graph, and edges connect spatial neighbors at a shared time as well as each node to its neighbors at the adjacent timestep. The network architecture built around this operator is ChronoSheaf, consisting of two components. Transitional restriction maps decompose each node’s restriction map into a learned spatial frame that determines how spatial neighbors are compared, plus two further frames, F in and F out , that independently weight a node’s interaction with its neighbors at the preceding and following timestep. The dual spectral filter then applies a single spectral filter to this joint operator, giving ChronoSheaf a receptive field that grows jointly in graph hops and temporal lag from one spectral recursion (Figure 1). Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce a new class of sheaf GNNs, called ChronoSheaf, in which we fundamentally rethink the three core components of sheaf GNNs: 1. The base space of the sheaf is a joint space-time graph, so that each node’s stalk is com-pared not only with its spatial neighbors but also with its own state and its neighbors’ states at adjacent timesteps. 2. The restriction maps allow each node to respond to its neighbors’ past and future states with distinct strengths to capture asymmetric temporal influence. 3. The sheaf Laplacian ’s spatial hops and temporal lags are controlled by a spectral filter that acts on the Laplacian’s frequency spectrum without needing eigendecomposition. We further provide a theoretical analysis of the resulting operator, characterizing its spectral properties and its joint spatio-temporal receptive field. • We conduct extensive experiments on six real-world datasets spanning diverse domains. The results show that ChronoSheaf resists oversmoothing, captures long-range dependencies more effectively, and outperforms state-of-the-art methods by a notable margin. k = 1  k = 2  k = 3

2 RELATED WORK 

Spectral graph convolution and its limits. Spectral filtering on graphs originates in Bruna et al. (2013) and is made scalable via polynomial truncation in Defferrard et al. (2016), whose first-order simplification underlies the method proposed in Kipf & Welling (2017). Veliˇ ckovi´ c et al. (2017) replace the fixed spectral filter with data-dependent attention weights. Known failure modes of this design, oversmoothing (Rusch et al., 2023; Li et al., 2018a) and oversquashing (Alon & Yahav, 2Under review preprint 2021), motivate architectures that enrich what a node can represent. This is the main gap targeted by sheaf neural architectures. 

Sheaf neural networks. Hansen & Ghrist (2019) generalize the graph Laplacian to a Hodge Lapla-cian over a cellular sheaf using linear restriction maps defined between per-node stalks. Bodnar et al. (2022) instantiate this as a neural network, with a fully parametric diffusion architecture and provable control over Dirichlet-energy decay, showing a learned non-trivial sheaf mitigates over-smoothing where the trivial sheaf cannot. Follow-up work restricts the space of restriction maps: with orthogonal Laplacians (Barbero et al., 2022) and O(d)-bundle maps with continuous transport (Bamberger et al., 2025). Most recently, Hajij et al. (2025) subsume sheaf diffusion, GNNs, and attention into a single copresheaf-on-combinatorial-complex formalism. Ribeiro et al. (2026) define cellular sheaves over directed graphs, allowing the diffusion operator to selectively listen to distant nodes and mitigate oversquashing. Fiorini et al. (2026) also propose a directed cellular sheaf and its associated directed sheaf Laplacian, which encodes both graph topology and edge orientation. These extensions, however, address spatial connectivity specifically. From a different angle, we address two questions: how time can be encoded within the sheaf and how to learn the sheaf Laplacian’s spectrum. 

Spatio-temporal graph learning. The dominant paradigm combines a spatial graph operator with a separate temporal mechanism, including recurrent units (Li et al., 2018b), gated temporal convo-lutions (Yu et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019), and self-attention (Zheng et al., 2020). Other approaches enrich the spatial or dynamical component through adaptive graph learning (Bai et al., 2020), zigzag-persistence representations (Chen et al., 2021), neural ODEs (Fang et al., 2021), or controlled differ-ential equations (Choi et al., 2022). Recent work continues to push the adaptive graph side toward efficiency: Ma et al. (2025) replace quadratic-cost adaptive graph learning with a linear, mixture-of-experts formulation. Across these methods, spatial and temporal dependence are usually modeled by two distinct operators, composed architecturally, so receptive fields grow additively with depth. A few works operate on a joint space-time domain; STSGCN (Song et al., 2020) connects each node to itself and its neighbors at adjacent timesteps within a localized space-time graph and graph-time convolutional networks (Sabbaqi & Isufi, 2023) filter over parametric product graphs. These methods demonstrate the value of a joint domain, but they weight its edges with scalars: every node shares a single feature space with its past, its future, and its neighbors, which is precisely the reason behind oversmoothing.

In Section 4.2, we include the results of traffic, weather, air quality forecasting on six bench-mark datasets compared to SOTA baselines. Setup. For traffic forecasting, we use PEMS04 and PEMS08 (Song et al., 2020), in addition to the San Diego (SD) and full California (CA) subsets of LargeST (Liu et al., 2023). All four datasets consist of traffic flow readings from loop detectors aggregated into 5-minute intervals, with sensors as nodes and edges derived from road-network connectivity. Graph sizes vary from 170 to 8,600 

nodes. Following the experimental setup of Liu et al. (2023), we use 12 historical timesteps as input to predict the next 12 steps. Further details and hyperparameter configurations are provided in Appendix C. Additionally, we use the Molene dataset (Girault, 2015) for weather forecasting, which contains hourly temperature readings from 32 stations in Brittany, France, with 10 historical timesteps as input to predict the next 5 steps. We further evaluate ChronoSheaf on the Air Quality dataset (Microsoft Research, 2026), which contains hourly PM 2.5 concentration readings from 437 monitoring stations across 43 Chinese cities. The input spans 48 hours and predicts the next 48 hours. Full dataset details are provided in Appendix C. 

Baselines. We compare ChronoSheaf to established GCN/RNN methods (STGCN (Yu et al., 2018), DCRNN (Li et al., 2018b)), adaptive graph learning (Graph WaveNet (Wu et al., 2019), Cross-GNN (Huang et al., 2023)), attention-based models (ASTGCN (Guo et al., 2019), GMAN (Zheng et al., 2020)), scalable architectures (SGP (Cini et al., 2023), SparseTSF (Lin et al., 2024), FITS (Xu et al., 2024)), spectral methods (CITRUS (Einizade et al., 2024)), and the most recent decomposition-based approaches (TimeBase (Huang et al., 2025), STDN (Cao et al., 2025)). Note on comparison with other Sheaf-based methods: to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no published literature on sheaf-based methods for spatio-temporal modeling; all identified sheaf networks operate exclusively in the spatial domain. Therefore, direct comparisons with analogous sheaf models are not applicable.

Real-world sensor networks rarely observe a single, homogeneous signal: traffic dynamics are modulated by exogenous factors, which are recorded by physically distinct stations on different scales and with different semantics. Standard graph diffusion treats all node features as residing in a common space and propagates them through a shared aggregation, which tends to wash out modality-specific information and over-smooth local representations when hetero-geneous signals are mixed. To test this, we augment PEMS04 and PEMS08 with meteoro-logical covariates from NOAA weather stations across the same regions (District 4 and District 8) during the same time intervals, and assess whether each model can exploit the additional modality. Baselines whose architectures do not support additional exogenous node features are omitted from this comparison. As shown in Table 4, ChronoSheaf achieves the lowest MAE and RMSE on both datasets, indicating that ChronoSheaf can effectively exploit heterogeneous input features without degrading forecasting performance. 

5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduced ChronoSheaf, a cellular sheaf defined jointly over space and time, con-sisting of two main components. First, transitional restriction maps decompose each node’s restric-tion map into a spatial frame and a pair of independently gated frames that map it to its neighbors at the preceding and following timestep, giving a node asymmetric sensitivity to past versus future 9Under review preprint influence. We proved the resulting joint Laplacian is real, symmetric, and positive semi-definite by construction. Second, the dual spectral filter applies a single polynomial recursion to this operator, with an adaptively estimated spectral radius, giving one filtering pass a receptive field that grows jointly in graph hops and temporal lag. Evaluated on several forecasting benchmarks, ChronoSheaf outperforms established spatio-temporal graph baselines.

C EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

This appendix describes the training protocol, evaluation metrics, and hyperparameters used for all experiments in Section 4. Unless stated otherwise, the same protocol is applied to ChronoSheaf and to all baselines. C.1 DATA PREPROCESSING 

Each dataset is split chronologically into training, validation, and test sets. Following the proto-cols of the original benchmarks, PEMS04 and PEMS08 (Song et al., 2020) and the two LargeST subsets (Liu et al., 2023) use a 6:2:2 ratio. Molene and Air Quality use a 7:1:2 ratio. Samples are extracted with a stride-one sliding window of Tin input steps followed by Tout target steps. Windows are extracted separately within each split, so that no window crosses a split boundary. All input channels are z-score standardized independently, using the mean and standard deviation computed on the training split only. The forecast target is standardized with its own training-split statistics. Model outputs are mapped back to the original scale before the training loss and the evaluation metrics are computed. The same normalization is applied to all baselines. 14 Under review preprint C.2 TRAINING 

We use four spatio-temporal blocks and set the rank of the restriction maps to 4. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 2.5 × 10 −3, ϵ = 10 −4, and weight decay of 10 −4, with the global gradient norm clipped at 5. Training windows are drawn from the training split with a stride of one, reshuffled at the start of every epoch, and grouped into mini-batches of 16. The maximum number of training epochs is set to 250. After each epoch, the model is evaluated on the validation split using the validation RMSE, computed on the original scale with missing entries masked. The learning rate is multiplied by 0.7 whenever the validation RMSE does not improve for 10 consecutive epochs, with a lower bound of 10 −5.C.3 EVALUATION
