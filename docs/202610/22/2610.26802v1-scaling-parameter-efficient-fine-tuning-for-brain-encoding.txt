# Scaling Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning for Brain Encoding 

Josh Ying 1,2, Guanglong Sun 3, Nolan Ho 2, Kieran Liu 2, Rui Yang 2,4, Andrew Chen 2,

Nikolaus Kriegeskorte 1, Pony Ma 2, Hossein Adeli 11Columbia University, 2Mind Lab, 3Tsinghua University, 4HKUST(GZ) Brain encoding models aim to predict neural responses to stimuli. The most widely used approach fits readouts from frozen pretrained backbones. Using neural data to train large models from scratch or fully fine-tune them after task-pretraining has proven difficult because neural data is costly and limited. We show that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) enables us to effectively adapt very large pretrained backbones for brain encoding with a small number of trainable parameters. To make it computationally practical, we exploit the backbone’s causal structure to predict multiple timepoints in a single pass instead of using sliding windows, significantly reducing feature-extraction cost. This enables efficient fine-tuning of multimodal backbones with up to 104B parameters, the largest model adapted for brain encoding to our knowledge. Trained with a low-rank adapter (LoRA) and a lightweight readout, our approach consistently improves brain encoding performance across nine diverse multimodal backbones. On the same Qwen3-Omni backbone as the prior state-of-the-art (SoTA), our method BrainLoRA achieves the best published performance on the Algonauts 2025 Challenge, using only about 5% as many trainable parameters. We also show the adapted multimodal backbones remain interpretable: modality attribution recovers the canonical sensory topography that prior work often obtains by construction from separate unimodal encoders, and neural adaptation increases the contribution of audio inputs and cross-modal mixing within the backbone. To scale out the number of adapted models, we develop a multi-LoRA kernel that accelerates joint training of multiple LoRAs on a shared backbone. This enables an ensemble of 200+ adapted models that ranks first on the public Algonauts post-challenge leaderboard at the time of writing. Together, our results highlight PEFT as an efficient and powerful paradigm for brain encoding.

# 1 Introduction 

Human brain encoding models predict neural responses to stimuli, providing computational insights into how the brain processes information. State-of-the-art (SoTA) encoding models mostly follow a goal-driven paradigm (Yamins and DiCarlo, 2016): a large model pretrained on behavioral tasks is used as a frozen 

feature extractor, and only a readout is learned from neural data (Adeli et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2025; Gokce et al., 2026a; d’Ascoli et al., 2026). This approach leverages strong pretrained priors, but misses the opportunity to exploit the backbone’s expressiveness to specialize for brain encoding. Adapting large pretrained backbones with neural data promises to scale models to the complexity required to capture human brain computation, and to best combine neural information and behavioral task training. In practice, however, such adaptation is difficult, because neural data are costly and scarce relative to the size of contemporary models. From frozen backbones to PEFT, and three directions to scale PEFT for brain encoding. 

(a) The dominant paradigm: a frozen pretrained backbone with readouts trained for brain encoding. The backbone prior is strong, but it is not specialized for brain encoding. (b) We advocate using PEFT to adapt strong pretrained backbones, retaining the powerful prior while allowing flexible neural adaptation. This suggests three complementary scaling directions: Scale up to stronger priors leveraging more capable backbones; Scale down the size of trainable adapters for brain encoding with limited data; and Scale out the number of adapters with different specializations. 

Training smaller models from scratch (St-Yves et al., 2023; Khosla and Wehbe, 2026) has not reached SoTA performance on whole-brain human benchmarks, and full fine-tuning of a pretrained backbone has been reported to underperform freezing it (Yang et al., 2023). Low-rank adaptation won Algonauts 2023 (Yang et al., 2023), but only with a relatively small ViT, and it has not been tested on large multimodal models. We argue that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) is a powerful yet underexplored regime for building strong brain encoding models. It retains the strong prior and expressive capacity of a large pretrained model and allows limited neural data to adapt its representations through a small set of parameters (§3). While the framework applies to any pretrained backbone, stimulus modality, or neural recording, we evaluate it in a particularly demanding setting: the CNeuroMod/Algonauts 2025 Challenge (Gifford et al., 2025), which requires predicting whole-brain fMRI responses to long, naturalistic audiovisual movies and tests generalization to held-out out-of-distribution stimuli. We show below that PEFT is powerful at scale, setting a new SoTA on this benchmark while remaining efficient. We first make large-scale adaptation for brain encoding computationally practical. Prior approaches typically rely on overlapping sliding windows for feature extraction, resulting in substantial redundant computation of shared stimulus context. By exploiting the backbone’s innate causal masking, we encode each training window in a single forward pass and obtain representations for all timesteps simultaneously (Antonello et al., 2023). This reduces feature-extraction cost by approximately 350 × relative to the sliding-window protocol used by the prior SoTA at little performance cost (Gokce et al., 2026a; §4). With adaptation made practical, we show that low-rank adapter (LoRA; Hu et al., 2021) consistently improves brain encoding on all nine multimodal backbones we test, spanning 4.7B– 104 B parameters, the largest models ever adapted to brain encoding to our knowledge. On the same backbone Qwen3-Omni as the prior SoTA (Gokce et al., 2026a), our method BrainLoRA pairs LoRA with a lightweight readout. With only about 5% 

as many trainable parameters, BrainLoRA sets a new SoTA among published methods (§5.1 and §5.2). Moreover, we show the adapted multimodal model remains interpretable. Prior work often identifies a region’s preferred modality by determining which unimodal encoder its readout relies on. With multimodal backbones, we instead use feature attribution to identify modality contribution, which recovers the canonical topography: 2early visual cortex is driven by video, and auditory and lateral temporal cortex by audio. Comparison with the frozen backbone shows that adaptation reweights the contributions of the three modalities, particularly increasing the contribution of the audio inputs, and increases cross-modal mixing within the backbone (§5.4). Finally, to scale the number of adapted models, we develop a batched multi-LoRA kernel that trains many LoRAs jointly on one shared backbone, significantly reducing training time. This enables 200+ independently adapted models, whose ensemble ranks first on the public Algonauts post-challenge leaderboard, surpassing unpublished entries as well. We additionally show that the ensemble performance is jointly governed by ensemble size, individual model quality, and model diversity (§5.5). Together, our results suggest three complementary scaling directions for future brain encoding models under the PEFT paradigm: Scale up the pretrained prior by leveraging increasingly capable backbones; Scale down the adaptation with neural data by updating only a small number of parameters; and Scale out by training a large number of lightweight adapters to a shared backbone with varying specializations (Fig. 1b; §3). Together, PEFT turns a single powerful pretrained model into a family of specialized encoding models without paying the cost of training each model from scratch. Our contributions are as follows: • Efficiency. We make PEFT on large multimodal backbones computationally practical for brain encoding (§4), reducing feature-extraction cost by 350 × via single-pass extraction compared to prior SoTA (Gokce et al., 2026a) and accelerating multi-LoRA training significantly with a batched training kernel. • Effectiveness. LoRA consistently improves performance over frozen backbones on all nine multimodal backbones we test (§5.1). LoRA with a lightweight readout beats the previous published SoTA on both ID and OOD test sets using ≈5% as many trainable parameters (§5.2). • Interpretability. We show that large multimodal backbones for brain encoding remain interpretable: modality attribution recovers the canonical sensory topography that prior work obtains by construction from separate unimodal encoders. Comparing with the frozen backbone reveals that adaptation increases the contribution of audio inputs and the degree of cross-modal mixing in the backbone (§5.4). • Scalability. We scale the backbone up to 104B multimodal models, the largest backbones adapted for brain encoding to our knowledge, and scale out to 200+ independently adapted models (§5.5). The ensemble ranks first on the public Algonauts post-challenge leaderboard at the time of writing. 

# 2 Related work 

We organize related works by where the neural signal enters the encoding model pipeline (see the extended related work in App. A). 

Frozen backbone, trained readout. Most brain encoding methods use pretrained task-optimized back-bones as frozen feature extractors and train readouts to predict neural responses (Naselaris et al., 2011; Huth et al., 2016; Yamins and DiCarlo, 2016; Schrimpf et al., 2021). All top-ranked entries in the Algonauts 2025 challenge (Gifford et al., 2025) followed this paradigm (Gokce et al., 2026a; d’Ascoli et al., 2026; Carl-ström Schad et al., 2025). Compared to more classic voxel-wise ridge regression methods (Naselaris et al., 2011), recent top-performing models have been using more expressive and flexible readout (d’Ascoli et al., 2026; Adeli et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025), yet the training is still confined to only the readout module. 

Backbones fine-tuned by brain data. Much fewer studies have instead directly adapted pretrained models using neural data. Brain-tuning of language and speech models improves not only alignment but downstream semantic tasks (Schwartz et al., 2019; Moussa et al., 2025; Moussa and Toneva, 2025), and the vision analogue gains robustness and behavioral alignment (Li et al., 2019; Federer et al., 2020). PEFT is already used within this family: MEM uses LoRA on vision backbones and won Algonauts 2023 (Yang et al., 2023); BrainWavLM matches a full fine-tune with LoRA adaptation (Vattikonda et al., 2025); Scholz et al. (2025) uses LoRA on a 3B Llama model on Algonauts 2025 and ranked 10th. Our work follows this direction but extends it to substantially larger model scale: to our knowledge, prior models above 15B parameters have been used only as frozen feature extractors, whereas we directly adapt backbones up to 104B. 

Other ways to incorporate neural data. Other approaches incorporate neural signals through auxiliary alignment objectives (Dapello et al., 2023; Gokce et al., 2026b), yet shuffled-target controls sometimes re-3produce much of the reported benefit (Guo et al., 2025); through self-supervised pretraining on neural data alone (Ortega Caro et al., 2024; Lane et al., 2026), which captures intrinsic neural dynamics rather than stimulus-referred computation (Zahorodnii et al., 2025); through unified stimulus–brain models (Wu et al., 2026), or train the model on neural data from scratch (Lurz et al., 2021; Klindt et al., 2017; St-Yves et al., 2023; Khosla and Wehbe, 2026), none of which, however, are yet outperforming brain encoders built on large task-optimized feature backbones on public benchmarks (Schrimpf et al., 2018; Gifford et al., 2025).

# 4 Method 

Task and data. We evaluate on CNeuroMod/Algonauts 2025 (Boyle et al., 2023; Gifford et al., 2025) challenge, which contains whole-brain fMRI recordings, parcellated into 1,000 regions (Schaefer et al., 2018), from subjects viewing seven seasons of Friends , with synchronized video, audio, and transcript. We train on four subjects, validate on Season 6, and evaluate on held-out Season 7 (ID) and movies (OOD). Our main experiments use Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025), the same as the best published method (Gokce et al., 2026a), for a controlled comparison. We also conduct experiments on a diverse set of nine backbones (Tab. 10, 12). 

Single-pass extraction. The prior SoTA (Gokce et al., 2026a) predicts each fMRI timestep from an overlapping sliding window, with 2,800 forward passes per clip (Fig. 2a). However, since the backbone is already causally masked, much of the repeated computation of a sliding window is unnecessary. We therefore encode each 149 s window (100 TRs) with a single forward pass and extract representations for all timesteps simultaneously (Fig. 2b), an approach more common in language encoding models (Antonello et al., 2023). This single-pass design reduces feature-extraction cost by ≈350 × relative to the sliding-window protocol of Gokce et al. (2026a) (from about 700 to 2 GPU-hours on A800), making fine-tuning through the backbone computationally affordable. Under a frozen readout, single-pass features incur only a small performance drop, which we attribute mainly to missing stimulus context at the start of each window (Tab. 3). We thus incorporate a 60s context lead that largely recovers it (Fig. 6). With LoRA fine-tuning, our configuration surpasses the original sliding-window baseline (App. B). 4Sliding window

The modality encoders preceding the decoder are locally bidi-rectional, over 8s of audio blocks and 1s of visual patches. To assess the effect of this limited non-causality, we re-encode strict prefixes of each input and compare representations at shared timesteps (App. F). We observe small differences, which are mainly attributable to length-dependent bf16 MoE numerics, instead of leakage of future stimuli (He and Think-ing Machines Lab, 2025). 

PEFT for Brain Encoding. For our main method on 

Qwen3-Omni , we insert rank-16 LoRA into the attention modules through layer 32 and truncate the decoder at this layer, introducing 8.91M trainable parameters. The adapters and readout are jointly optimized for one epoch. For hy-perparameter tuning, see App. E. We also show that other PEFT methods like IA 3 (Liu et al., 2022) and adapter layers (Houlsby et al., 2019) show similar effects (App. H). 

Readout. We use linear or cross-attention (Adeli et al., 2025) readouts. Backbone features are pooled into one vec-tor per modality at each 2 Hz timestep and aggregated into eight log-spaced temporal-lag blocks aligned with the fMRI sampling rate. A final linear projection maps to 1,000 brain parcels, with 52.3M trainable parameters. We pool the orig-inal ∼19 .3k tokens to 894 vectors per input window. As an alternative, we replace the fixed temporal-lag aggregation with per-TR cross-attention over an expanded set of ∼3.2k tokens, adding only 0.2M parameters. Ablations in §5.3 attribute the improvement of cross-attention over linear readout primarily to the access to the richer token set. 

Closed-form re-fit. After joint optimization with SGD, we re-fit the linear readout W in closed form. Forward passes accumulate subject-specific Gram matrices, after which W is solved using parcel-wise ridge regression following Nunez-Elizalde et al. (2019). As shown in §5.3, the improvement primarily arises from the closed-form re-estimation rather than the parcel-specific regularization.

5.2 A small adapter beats a large readout 

As shown in Tab. 1, the previous single-model SoTA, MIRAGE (Gokce et al., 2026a), uses a frozen backbone plus a 1.1B-parameter transformer-based readout. In contrast, our LoRA+linear model trains only 61.2M parameters ( ≈5% of MIRAGE) and already outperforms it on both ID and OOD test sets (Fig. 7). Replac-ing the linear readout with cross-attention, our best single model method, BrainLoRA , further improves performance to 0.327 on ID and 0.228 on OOD, exceeding the published 15-model MIRAGE ensemble on ID and matching it on OOD, setting a new SoTA on the Algonauts challenge. Ensembling a population of 200+ models further reaches 0.343 on ID and 0.248 on OOD, achieving the highest score on the public leaderboard at the time of writing (§5.5). Selecting a subset of 15 models still maintains strong performance. Full fine-tuning of all 20 .25 B parameters below the feature extraction layer reaches 0.323 (linear) and 0.330 

(cross-attention) on validation, on par with or slightly below the corresponding LoRA configurations, with 

330 × more trainable parameters. The held-out test sets show the same patterns, with a larger decrease on OOD (Tab. 1). Full fine-tuning thus does not outperform LoRA at this data scale, consistent with prior works (Yang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Moussa and Toneva, 2025; Vattikonda et al., 2025). 

The adaptation transfers to unseen subjects and a new dataset. The learned adaptation generalizes beyond both the subjects and the dataset used for training, with gains essentially matching those observed in-distribution. When trained on three subjects and evaluated on a held-out fourth subject with the LoRAs frozen and only the readout re-fitted, it retains 97% of the improvement observed for subjects included in LoRA training (App. M). The same adapter and readout also transfer zero-shot to the Naturalistic Neu-roimaging Database (NNDb; Aliko et al., 2020), collected at a different site with a different scanner and repetition time, and with subjects viewing films. The adapter is applied without any further training on NNDb, including the readout. Even so, it yields a gain of +11 .1 percentage points of the noise ceiling, on par with the +10 .7-point fine-tuning gain on our validation set (App. N). With only minutes of a new sub-ject’s data, solving its readout as a correction to the population readout improves the transfer performance 6(App. O). The adapter thus captures stimulus representations that generalize to other subjects or datasets.

5.4 Interpreting the multimodal backbone 

Encoding models can be used to ask which stimulus information is processed, and therefore drives the prediction of neural activity, in a given brain region. Prior work often answers this by construction, using separate unimodal backbones so that a region’s preferred modality or semantic selectivity can be read off directly from the readout by the weights or the attention patterns (Adeli et al., 2025; d’Ascoli et al., 2026). A single multimodal backbone, however, lacks this explicit separation, with information from different modalities merging early in the backbone. We show that feature attribution methods can estimate modality contribution and examine whether the adapted model recovers the expected cortical topography. 

Design. For each window, we compute exact Shapley values over the three input modalities, {video , audio ,

text }. A modality is ablated by replacing its input embeddings with a content-free mean embedding while preserving token count, position, and temporal alignment (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). Therefore, only stimulus 

information is removed. Evaluating all eight ( 23) modality subsets yields an exact additive decomposition of each modality’s contribution to encoding performance. The same decomposition can also tell us through which token positions each modality’s information reaches the readout. At every timestep, the readout takes three hidden states, one for each modality, at the truncated layer. After many layers of attention, however, each of these states can carry information from all three input modalities, so audio information, for example, may reach the readout through a video token. The attribution recovers the canonical topography. The resulting attribution maps recover the canon-ical cortical organization (Fig. 4a,b, App. Q): Early visual cortex is predominantly driven by video ( 72 .5% ), auditory cortex by audio ( 72 .9% ), and lateral temporal cortex by audio ( 60 .3% ) rather than text ( 12 .5% ). Text contributes most strongly to predicting the activity in the default-mode ( 21 .6% ) and control regions. These patterns are highly robust: 1) all regional contrasts have bootstrap intervals excluding zero, 2) the attribution maps are consistent under a donor-window baseline ( r=0 .994 ) , and 3) two independently trained seeds yield highly consistent contribution maps ( r=0 .996 –1.000 ). 

Adaptation shifts modality attribution toward audio. To see how adaptation changes modality attri-bution, we compare the frozen baseline with the adapted one. The spatial maps are largely unchanged: each modality’s contribution map correlates at 0.963 –0.990 between the frozen and adapted models. What changes is the relative weighting of the three modalities (Fig. 4c). Averaged over parcels, the audio contribution rises by 39% and video by 5% , while text falls by 17% . The dominant modality changes in 249 parcels, all toward audio, with 246 of them switching from video- to audio-dominated and none the other way. Consistent with this, removing audio hurts the performance more after adaptation (App. Q). This shift is robust across seeds, which agree on the dominant modality for 97 .2% of parcels.

5.5 Training a population of independently adapted models 

Efficient multi-adapter training. To efficiently train many adapters, we develop a specialized multi-LoRA training kernel. Since LoRA modules are small and structurally identical, the routed updates for N adapters can be fused into a single batched gather–matrix multiplication, enabling parallel training of N adapters together (Fig. 5a). The speedup arises from improved hardware utilization rather than reduced FLOPs: at 

N =32 , the fused step costs only 1.08 x a single-adapter step, yielding a 29 .8x speedup over sequential training. End-to-end speedups are smaller because of longer sequences and larger batches. See App. C for details. 

Large-scale ensemble performance. We train 261 independently adapted models spanning different hyperparameters, readout architectures, and backbones, and aggregate with a parcel-wise minimum-variance ensemble combiner fitted on out-of-fold predictions (App. G). Ensemble accuracy reaches 0.3483 on validation, 0.3428 on ID, and 0.2483 on OOD, achieving the top score on the public post-challenge leaderboard at the time of writing, outperforming unpublished submissions as well (Tab. 1). All reported models were selected using validation performance only. A carefully selected 15-model subset reaches 0.3465 on validation, within 0.0018 of the full ensemble, at one seventeenth of the inference cost. Both the subset and its weights are chosen on the validation set (App. D). To characterize the effect of ensemble size, we score random subsets of the 261-model pool at every size n

and fit their mean accuracy with m(n) = m∞ − An −b, which results in b ≈ 0.94 (R2=0 .998 ; Fig. 5b). An exponent near 1 is consistent with ensemble theory: if members’ residuals have mean pairwise correlation 

ρ, the ensemble’s residual variance is σ2[ρ + (1 − ρ)/n ] (Ueda and Nakano, 1996; Wood et al., 2023), whose second term shrinks as 1/n . The first term does not shrink with size, which sets the ceiling performance 

m∞. The ensemble ceiling thus depends on how correlated the members’ errors are ( σ2ρ), and hence on the ensemble’s composition rather than its size alone. We next examine, at a fixed pool size, what ensemble properties influence the performance. For random 20-model subsets, mean residual correlation and mean single-model performance explain 5% and 23% of the variance in ensemble score individually, but explain 97% of the variance jointly (Fig. 5c; Krogh and Vedelsby, 1994; Kuncheva and Whitaker, 2003; Douven et al., 2026). These factors are highly correlated in our pool (r=0 .74 ), as models with more diversity tend to be weaker individually. Therefore, separate coefficients of the fit should not be read as the effect of changing one property while holding the other fixed. 9Useful diversity arises primarily from changes in model architecture rather than minor training variations. Models with different readout architectures or backbones show substantially lower residual correlation with the rest of the pool. In contrast, changes in seed, LoRA rank, layer choice, or training data subset provide little diversity (Fig. 9, App. J). 

# 6 Discussion 

Three axes for scaling PEFT for brain encoding. Together, our results suggest three complementary scaling directions for future brain encoding models: 1) Scale up the pretrained prior by leveraging increas-ingly capable backbones; 2) Scale down the adaptation by updating only a small number of parameters; and 

3) Scale out by training a large number of lightweight adapters to shared backbones with various inductive biases (Mind Lab et al., 2026). Together, PEFT turns a single powerful pretrained model into a family of specialized encoding models without training each backbone from scratch. 

Toward a brain foundation model. Our experiments do not constitute a brain foundation model, but scaling along the three directions discussed above could lead to one. A large pretrained backbone could use a single shared adapter trained on neural data spanning tasks, datasets, subjects, and even species. In this work, we only scale out the number of adapters for ensemble. However, future work could train a separate adapter for each task, dataset, subject, or even ROI with a hierarchical structure or with meta-learning. Together, this hierarchical approach and the three scaling directions we discuss above can create a brain foundation model with efficient specializations for different downstream tasks. 

Limitations. Our models are trained on one benchmark with four subjects, all watching audiovisual narra-tive. The adapters transfer to a held-out subject (App. M) and to a second dataset (App. N), but neither test leaves the domain of narrative film. We leave the joint training on multiple datasets and modalities to future work.

# A Extended related work 

The full version of §2, by family: where does the neural signal enter the encoding model pipeline? 

Frozen backbone, trained readout. The dominant paradigm freezes a model pretrained on a behavioral or self-supervised task and fits a readout from its hidden states to neural responses: the lineage running from voxelwise ridge regression (Naselaris et al., 2011; Huth et al., 2016; Nunez-Elizalde et al., 2019) through task-optimized network comparisons (Yamins and DiCarlo, 2016; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Caucheteux and King, 2022) to today’s multimodal encoders. Every leading entry in the Algonauts 2025 challenge leaderboard (Gifford et al., 2025) followed this paradigm, including MIRAGE (Gokce et al., 2026a), which we build on directly, TRIBE (d’Ascoli et al., 2026) and VIBE (Carlström Schad et al., 2025). A retrospective report argued that architectural choices mattered less than ensembling (Scotti and Tripathy, 2025). Readout design has been studied on its own terms (Adeli et al., 2025; Lurz et al., 2021), and a larger readout does not necessarily lead to higher accuracy: an ROI-query transformer readout is ∼37M against classical ridge’s 

∼1,200M, yet it performs better (Adeli et al., 2025). Concurrent work (Zhang et al., 2026) reports a strong OOD score on the Algonauts 2025 leaderboard using a pool of frozen-backbone models with linear readouts, whose per-participant, per-film parameters were selected using public leaderboard scores. By contrast, we selected all models and ensemble weights on validation data alone (App. D). 

Backbones fine-tuned by brain data. A second family updates the stimulus model’s own weights from neural recordings. Inducing brain-relevant bias in language models (Schwartz et al., 2019; Toneva and Wehbe, 2019) leads to brain-tuning of speech models, which improves not only alignment but also downstream semantic tasks (Moussa et al., 2025; Moussa and Toneva, 2025). The vision analogue trains with neural regularization and gains robustness and behavioral alignment (Li et al., 2019; Federer et al., 2020; Safarani et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2026). Our methods fall under this family, and parameter-efficient adaptation is already used within it: BrainWavLM brain-tunes with LoRA and matches a full fine-tune (Vattikonda et al., 2025), and rank-8 LoRA at 0.625% of parameters suffices for cross-participant brain-tuning (Moussa and Toneva, 2025); MEM won the Algonauts 2023 challenge by adapting a vision backbone with LoRA (Yang et al., 2023); recent work RABBiT achieves SoTA on audio cortex prediction by adapting an audio backbone (Moussa and Toneva, 2026). Although several works have explored PEFT for brain encoding, this remains a relatively underexplored paradigm in the literature. What is new about our work is mainly the scale : the largest backbones whose weights have been updated by brain data are around 10B (Scholz et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026), and models above 15B appear in this literature only as frozen feature extractors. Concurrent work (Zhang et al., 2026) also trains LoRA experts inside omni-modal models of up to 104B total parameters, but supervises them with labels derived from fMRI networks rather than with the brain-encoding objective of predicting neural responses. We adapt backbones up to 104B for brain encoding, and we test them on a whole-brain encoding benchmark rather than on a specific brain area or for downstream task transfer. 

Joint task and brain-alignment objectives. A third family trains a primary objective together with an auxiliary alignment term against neural data (Dapello et al., 2023; Pirlot et al., 2022). The binding constraint is that the stimuli with recordings are a tiny fraction of the task corpus, which is precisely the asymmetry parameter-efficient adaptation is built for. This family also involves some methodological warning signs: shuffled- or permuted-target controls sometimes reproduce much of the reported benefit (Federer et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2025), so a gain from neural supervision has to be shown against matched compute. We hold ourselves to the same standard throughout (§5.3), and run the shifted-target control (App. L). 

Brain-only pretraining, aligned afterwards. A fourth family pretrains self-supervised on neural data alone across subjects, sessions and datasets: masked autoencoding for fMRI (Ortega Caro et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024; Lane et al., 2026), EEG (Jiang et al., 2024, 2025), intracranial recordings (Wang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024) and other modalities, and attaches the stimulus afterwards. Unified models over mixed tokens. A fifth family puts stimulus and brain tokens into one model so that encoding and decoding share parameters (Wu et al., 2026; Mai et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2024; Antoniades et al., 2024). It is worth noting that the most recent of these methods adapts a large pretrained backbone with LoRA rather than training from scratch (Wu et al., 2026), the same direction we are going. No model in this family has been shown to beat a specialized encoder on voxelwise correlation; they aim for decoding or semantic-level proxies instead. 

Batched multi-adapter kernels. Routing one batch of tokens to many adapters is a solved problem for inference: Punica’s segmented gather matmul (Chen et al., 2024) and S-LoRA’s paged adapter pool (Sheng et al., 2024) both handle it. Because serving needs no gradients, neither system provides a backward pass. Training systems are more recent and batch per adapter, with each adapter receiving its own inputs, so every group is a full sequence (Yan et al., 2026; Zuo et al., 2026). The per-token form of the primitive instead comes from mixture-of-experts training, where MegaBlocks pads groups to block multiples (Gale et al., 2023) and ScatterMoE does not (Tan et al., 2024); stock PyTorch now also ships a differentiable grouped matmul that takes a distinct matrix per group. Neither the operator nor our two design choices (gathering rather than padding, and carrying the adapter index on a grid dimension) is new. What is specific to our method is the regime. Grouped matmuls are designed for many tokens per group, whereas a pool with one adapter per model, subject or parcel sits at the opposite extreme, where each group holds only a handful of rows. In that regime, the fused kernel’s cost is nearly independent of pool size, while the alternatives’ costs grow with it. At the pool sizes used in this paper, this is merely a speedup; if we were to train one adapter per parcel, our kernel would make training feasible at all (§6).

Memory is the limit: at rank 16 the pool and its optimizer state fill an 80GB card between 32 and 64 adapters.

# F The effect of local non-causality in the backbone 

Single-pass extraction assumes that a state at time t depends only on the stimulus up to t. We encode a span once, then re-encode strict prefixes of the same span from the same start, so the shared tokens and their position ids are identical. If causal truncation held exactly, feature extraction layer states at the shared timesteps would agree. As a control, the same span encoded twice agrees at cosine 1.0000 (minimum 0.99999) on all three streams, so the pipeline is deterministic. Cuts are placed on chunk boundaries, and the final chunk before each cut is excluded, because a truncated span ends with its own closing tokens where the longer span continues with content. The states differ by more than the control allows (Tab. 9). The location of the deviation shows that it is not future stimulus leaking backwards. # H Alternative PEFT methods 

All other adaptation results in this paper use LoRA. To test whether the gain depends on the method, we ran two further PEFT families under the same protocol: same backbone, feature extraction layer, context lead, readout, readout learning rate, step budget and seed. # J Why seeds are not a source of diversity 

§5.5 reports that changing the seed gives almost no ensemble gain, whereas changing the readout or the backbone does. Using the 15 seeds of the main configuration, we show that adapters trained from different seeds compute nearly the same function, so their predictions have little independent content to average. 

Weights. Updates from two seeds are nearly orthogonal in weight space: cos(∆ Wi, ∆Wj ) = 0 .026 , at a relative distance of 1.396 ≈ √2. This follows from the parameterization. ∆W = BA lies in the row space of 

A, which is drawn at random and changes little during training, so any two seeds are orthogonal regardless of what they compute. The update is not small: its largest singular value is 0.6–0.8 of the frozen matrix’s. Adapters should therefore be compared on activations rather than weights. 26 Activations. Fig. 9 compares each seed’s change relative to the frozen backbone at increasing depth. On real inputs, a single module’s output change agrees at 0.151. The composed feature change agrees at 0.355 after one layer, 0.595 after sixteen, and 0.860 at the feature extraction layer, where it is still 45% of the feature norm. Agreement falls back to 0.74 –0.82 by the final layer (Fig. 9b). The rise is not explained by independent per-layer noise averaging out: a model of independent per-layer noise, fitted at layer 1, predicts 

0.82 –0.87 by layer 8, against an observed 0.43 –0.54 .

Readout and prediction. Pairing an adapter with another seed’s jointly trained readout costs 0.0012 ,and with another seed’s re-fit readout 0.0024 , both inside the seed spread. Under one shared readout, the prediction change is 0.418 of the prediction norm and r = 0 .934 correlated across seeds, with a shared fraction of 0.939 . Each seed’s deviation from the consensus is therefore 0.418 √1 − 0.939 = 0 .104 of a prediction. The ensemble analysis measures this member-specific component independently, from the members’ own predictions, and finds that it correlates with the fMRI target at −0.003 .The seed-specific part of a prediction is thus small and carries no signal, and it is what averaging over seeds removes. This is why seed-only pools saturate by ≈12 members while a member with a different readout or backbone still adds accuracy (App. G). 

# K Additional null results 

We summarize the design choices we explored that did not improve the performance. 

Gated-MLP readout. After the re-fit it scores +0 .0007 over the linear readout, inside the 0.00077 seed spread. 

Cross-attention over pooled features. Cross-attention over the same 894 frame-grid vectors that the linear readout uses gives +0 .00030 [+0 .00017 , +0 .00045 ], inside the seed spread. The gain of the cross-attention readout comes from the larger token set, not from attention itself. 

Raw token sequence. Widening the token set from ∼3.2k to the full 19,099-token sequence, including every vision patch token, gives no gain ( +0 .00000 , −0.00075 and +0 .00009 over three seed-matched pairs). 

Training-data subsets. Dropping whole stimuli so that two members share 44 .1% of their training stimuli instead of 99 .3% changes their residual correlation by only +0 .00006 . Training on random fractions of the data lowers accuracy monotonically (0.8/0.6/0.4 of the data → 0.3236/0.3161/0.3109). 

Per-subject adapters. Restricting each adapter to one subject changes the score by −0.0019 [−0.0022 ,

−0.0017 ] relative to the shared adapter, replicated at a second seed. A compute-matched control, with the same extra training but no subject restriction, loses by a similar amount, so the loss comes from continued training rather than from per-subject specialization. 

# L Shift-target control 

The frozen-backbone control of Tab. 2 shows that the adapter’s +0 .0356 does not come from readout training. It does not show that the gain depends on the stimulus. An adapter could in principle profit from any fMRI-shaped supervision, for example the parcels’ spatial covariance, their autocorrelation, or the per-run scale. We test this with a control experiment: we train the same configuration on targets whose stimulus–response pairing is broken, then re-fit the readout in closed form on correctly aligned training data and score it on the usual validation windows. 

Setup. For each subject, the training runs are concatenated in a seeded random order, rolled by a random offset (between 7.3 and 54.5 hours of scan time), and cut back into runs of their original lengths. Every target is still a real response of that subject. Window geometry, per-run z-scoring and within-run autocorrelation 27 are preserved (mean lag-1 autocorrelation changes by less than 0.01), which makes this a stricter control than shuffling timepoints. Only the pairing with the stimulus is broken. Because each movie10 film is scanned twice, we check that no shifted target comes from a stimulus span that overlaps the window the model encodes. The re-fit and all evaluations use aligned data only. The shifted adapter configuration also trains its per-modality projections on misaligned targets. Its matched control is therefore a second run at the same shift with the adapter’s learning rate set to zero, so the two configurations differ only in the adapter.

# N Does the adapter transfer to a new dataset? 

Leaving a subject out (App. M) changes the person but keeps the site, scanner, preprocessing, repetition time and stimulus fixed. The Naturalistic Neuroimaging Database (NNDb; Aliko et al., 2020) changes all of them. Each participant watched one full-length feature film at a repetition time of 1 s. The data are distributed in volumetric MNI space, so they can be parcellated into the same 1,000 Schaefer parcels our readout predicts. We use the 20 participants who watched the same film (5,470 volumes each) and score the frozen and LoRA models of Tab. 1 without retraining the backbone or adapter. 

Stimulus alignment. NNDb does not distribute the films, and our copy differs from the scanned one in rate and offset. The rate difference is a PAL speedup (a 23.976 fps master played at 25 fps), so local timestamps map to scanner time by the ratio 960 /1001 . We estimate the offset from NNDb’s word-level annotations of the film. We verify the alignment in four ways. Re-fitting the clock on the retimed stimulus gives a rate of 1.00002 and a residual offset of −0.075 s. The speech envelope of our media peaks in auditory cortex within 0.09 s of the lag at which NNDb’s word annotations peak. Frame-to-frame visual change peaks in V1 and MT. Finally, a lag scan of both models’ predictions against the brain peaks at zero. 

Readouts. The transferred readout applies the Algonauts-trained weights unchanged, averaging the four subject-specific rows since NNDb participants have none. This matches the validation setting but tests two things at once: whether the features transfer and whether a readout fitted to other people does. The re-fitted 

readout separates the two. It keeps the backbone and projections from Algonauts and solves a fresh ridge readout per NNDb participant, with five-fold cross-validation over contiguous blocks of film time and the penalty chosen on a holdout inside each training fold. All NNDb scores are computed on the model’s 1.49 s grid. 

Noise ceiling. Correlations measured on noisier data shrink for both models by the same factor. A raw gain in r is therefore not comparable across datasets, and a relative gain can be inflated by noise. We report each model as a percentage of each dataset’s own noise ceiling. If a shared signal explains a fraction f of the variance, it converges to f / pf + (1 − f )/k with k other subjects, whereas an ideal stimulus model reaches √f . We invert the first expression for f per parcel and report against √f . # O Transfer to a new subject with little data 

Apps. M and N solve a new subject’s readout on all of that subject’s data, or apply the population readout zero-shot. In practice, a new participant may bring minutes to a few hours of recordings. We therefore ask how the readout should be fitted from little data, and whether the adapter’s gain persists in that regime. 

Setup. We keep everything fixed except the new subject’s readout row: the backbone, the adapter, the per-modality projections, and the training subjects’ data are all kept constant. On Algonauts we use the four 3-subject adapters of App. M, so the held-out subject’s fMRI never reached the adapter. From that subject’s Friends training windows we draw K whole windows (five nested random draws per K, the same windows for every estimator), fit its readout row in closed form, and score it on season 6 as in Tab. 14. We compare the following three ways of training the readout (Tab. 16): • Ridge from scratch : the closed-form re-fit of §4, on the K windows alone. • Anchored ridge : ridge on the residual Y − X ¯W , where ¯W is the mean of the training subjects’ rows, so the estimate shrinks toward the population readout instead of toward zero. With no data, it is equivalent to the population readout. • Low-rank deviation : ¯W + AB ⊤, with B fixed to the top 16 principal components of the training subjects’ residuals under ¯W , and only A fitted. This is a closed-form analogue of the shared and subject-specific decomposition of RABBiT (Moussa and Toneva, 2026). Each estimator uses one penalty for all parcels, chosen by cross-validation over whole calibration windows and never on season 6. For the last two methods, the penalty tuning grid includes α = ∞, so they can fall back to the population readout. The frozen-backbone comparison repeats every fit on the 3-subject frozen-backbone models of Tab. 14.

# R What the pool still misses 

Validation correlation does not separate model error from missing signal and measurement noise. A residual mixes model error, missing signal and measurement noise, and the validation season has no test-retest to separate them. Two properties of our setup allow the separation. First, the member-specific part of each member’s prediction is noise (it correlates with the target at −0.003 , App. J), so the uniform mean of the 261 members is the pool’s shared function with that noise averaged out. Second, all four subjects watched the same 250 validation windows. Scanner and idiosyncratic noise cannot correlate across people, so a residual that is correlated across subjects is stimulus-locked signal that the pool does not produce. Characterizing the residual. Nine low-level stimulus statistics (luminance, contrast, motion energy and cuts from the frames; RMS, envelope modulation and spectral centroid from the audio; words per TR and speech on/off from the transcript), lagged 0–9 s, explain the shared residual at only r = 0 .027 under episode-level cross-validation. Lagged copies of the pool’s own prediction ( −4 to +4 TRs) explain 0.048 , so the residual is not a latency error. It is also not low-dimensional: the top ten components hold 36% of its variance, against 

91% for the prediction. Its spectrum follows that of the ISC ( 66% of the missed covariance lies below 0.05 Hz, against 70% of ISC). The residual is thus slow, distributed, high-dimensional and stimulus-locked, which fits narrative or event-level structure better than a missing low-level feature. This agrees with §5.5: even the changes that most improved the pool left about a third of the shared variance unexplained. 

# S Reproducibility 

Hardware and precision. One NVIDIA A800-80GB per run, for training and extraction. The back-bone runs in bfloat16 with FlashAttention-2 and gradient checkpointing. The vision patch embedding’s 3-D convolution runs in float32, because cuDNN 9 executes it in bfloat16 roughly four orders of magnitude slower. 

Optimisation. AdamW with default β, weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping at global norm 1.0. Batch size 1 window, no gradient accumulation; one window is 100 TRs for all four subjects, so a step carries 400 target rows. 1,429 steps (one epoch over the 1,429 training windows), 30 warm-up steps, cosine decay to zero. Learning rates: LoRA 6×10 −4, per-modality projection 6×10 −4, readout 3×10 −4. No run is resumed from a checkpoint. 

LoRA. Rank 16, α = 32 , dropout 0, on q_proj , k_proj , v_proj and o_proj of every decoder layer (model.layers.\d+.self_attn.(q|k|v|o)_proj ). PEFT default initialization ( A Kaiming-uniform, B zero), so training starts from the frozen model. 8.91M parameters. The decoder is truncated above the feature extraction layer, so layers past 32 are neither run nor adapted.
