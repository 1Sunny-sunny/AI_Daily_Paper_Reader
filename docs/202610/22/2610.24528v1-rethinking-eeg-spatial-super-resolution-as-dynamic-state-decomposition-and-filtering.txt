Abstract 

EEG spatial super-resolution aims to recover high-density neural signals from sparse electrode observations. Existing methods have achieved substantial progress through spatial modeling, gen-erative reconstruction, and temporal representation learning. However, the current EEG state con-tains two different sources of information: predictable evolution inherited from preceding states and newly emerging changes revealed by the current observation. Most approaches typically do not model these two components explicitly. In this work, we formulate streaming EEG spatial super-resolution as a sequential state filtering problem and propose Dynamic State Filtering (DSF). Rather than relying on a single temporal reconstruction pathway, DSF combines a history-aware dynami-cal branch with an innovation-filtering branch that explicitly decomposes waveform-state estimation into prediction and observation-driven correction. To infer how spatially incomplete innovations should affect missing locations, DSF routes the observed innovations across electrodes through a mask-constrained, topology-aware transition operator and incorporates them through uncertainty-aware state correction. The two complementary estimates are finally fused to reconstruct the dense EEG signal. Experiments on SEED, SEED-IV and BCI2000 demonstrate that DSF improves recon-struction fidelity and downstream utility while maintaining robustness under dynamically changing and unreliable sparse observations. 

# 1 Introduction 

High-density electroencephalography (EEG) provides fine-grained spatial measurements of neural ac-tivity, but dense electrode recordings remain costly and inconvenient to acquire because of setup com-plexity, wearing comfort, and sensing reliability (Tang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025). EEG spatial super-resolution (EEGSR) therefore aims to recover high-density signals from sparse electrode observations. Beyond spatial missingness, EEGSR also involves a temporal estimation problem: consecutive EEG seg-ments are not independent, and part of the current neural activity is predictable from preceding states, while newly observed measurements provide additional evidence that cannot be inferred from history alone (McKenna et al., 1994). Existing EEGSR approaches exploit interpolation, spatial dependencies, generative priors, and tem-poral modeling to infer dense EEG from sparse measurements (El Ouahidi et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025, 2026). However, temporal context is typically incorporated as an additional rep-resentation for direct reconstruction. The reconstruction process does not explicitly distinguish a state predicted from preceding EEG from an observation-induced correction that is available only at cur-rently visible electrodes. This distinction becomes particularly important when the current measurement is sparse, corrupted, or temporarily unavailable: historical information can provide a useful prior, but blindly incorporating unreliable current measurements may also distort the estimated state. This motivates us to formulate streaming EEG spatial super-resolution as sequential state filtering. Rather than asking a model to regenerate the complete dense signal at every step solely from the current sparse input, we maintain an evolving state across consecutive EEG windows. Each update therefore contains two distinct sources of information: a predictive prior propagated from the preceding state and an innovation revealed by the current observation. Conventional segment-wise EEG spatial super-resolution estimates each target dense EEG segment primarily from the corresponding sparse observation or temporally aggregated features. The key problem is therefore not merely how to exploit temporal dependencies, but how innovations observed at a subset of electrodes should correct the predicted states of both observed and unobserved electrodes. To realize this formulation, we propose Dynamic State Filtering (DSF) , which combines two com-plementary causal pathways. A history-aware dynamical branch propagates latent states across consec-utive EEG windows and updates them with the current sparse observation, while an innovation-filtering branch explicitly decomposes waveform-state estimation into prediction and observation-driven correc-tion. Rather than propagating raw observations or generic hidden features, the filtering branch treats the observed deviations as innovation sources and routes them toward both observed and unobserved states through a mask-constrained, topology-aware transition operator. The two branches are finally integrated to produce the dense EEG reconstruction. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We formulate streaming EEG spatial super-resolution as causal state filtering across consecutive EEG windows, explicitly separating a history-derived predictive state from corrections introduced by the current sparse observation. • We develop a dual-branch DSF framework that combines causal dynamical modeling with mask-constrained, topology-aware innovation routing, allowing current innovations from visible elec-trodes to correct unobserved states. • We evaluate DSF on three EEG datasets across multiple sparsity levels and observation perturba-tions, together with downstream task evaluation and component analyses, demonstrating consis-tent advantages over static reconstruction and temporal modeling baselines.

Classical Kalman filtering performs sequential estimation through alternating prediction and innovation-based measurement updates Kalman (1960). DSF adopts the general prediction–correction principle. We extend it to partially observed spatial state estimation, where the current state is observed only at a subset of locations and the resulting innovations must be propagated from observed to unobserved locations before updating the full state.

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets and protocol. We evaluate DSF on SEED, SEED-IV, and BCI2000, containing 62, 62, and 64 EEG channels, respectively. EEG signals are manually segmented into consecutive 4-second win-dows whose temporal order is preserved within each recording to construct causal EEG streams. We consider three nominal reconstruction scales, 2×, 4×, and 8×, corresponding to 32, 16, and 8 visible electrodes, respectively. Each scale uses a predefined fixed sparse-electrode layout, which is shared by all compared methods and remains unchanged across consecutive windows under the clean-layout protocol. We adopt a subject-independent evaluation protocol with disjoint training, validation, and test data. Validation is used for model selection, while the test set is reserved for final evaluation. We report NMSE, PCC, and reconstruction SNR on unobserved electrodes as the primary reconstruction metrics. Detailed electrode layouts and evaluation settings are provided in Appendix D. 

Baselines. We compare DSF with both EEG-specific reconstruction methods and general temporal/state-estimation baselines. The EEG-specific group includes spherical-spline interpolation, ESTFormer Li et al. (2025), STAD Wang et al. (2025), MASER Zhang et al. (2024), and SRGDiff Liu et al. (2025). We further compare with Mamba-based temporal aggregation Gu & Dao (2024), a learned latent-space Kalman filtering baseline Kalman (1960), and Latent ODE Rubanova et al. (2019) under the same causal stream protocol. 4.2 Main Reconstruction Results 

We first evaluate the overall reconstruction performance of DSF under clean sparse observations. Ta-ble 1 compares DSF with EEG-specific reconstruction methods and temporal/state-estimation baselines on SEED, SEED-IV, and BCI2000 across three reconstruction scales. DSF achieves the strongest overall reconstruction performance among the evaluated methods. Its advantage is retained as the reconstruc-tion scale increases, although absolute reconstruction quality decreases for all approaches when fewer electrodes are observed. The consistent improvement across datasets and sparsity levels suggests that history-driven prediction and innovation-based correction provide complementary information for re-constructing unobserved electrodes. DSF also maintains practical computational efficiency, achieving sub-millisecond inference latency while remaining faster than the evaluated EEG-specific neural recon-struction methods and Mamba aggregation (Table 12). 

4.3 Robustness under Unreliable Observations 

We further evaluate DSF when current sparse observations are degraded by measurement noise, ad-ditional channel dropout, or their combination. These perturbations simulate practical cases in which visible electrodes become noisy or temporarily unavailable. As shown in Table 2, DSF retains the best reconstruction performance under all three perturbation settings. Together with its clean-setting perfor-mance in Table 1, these results show that the advantage of DSF is preserved when the current obser-vations become noisy or partially unavailable. The historical-context analysis in Appendix E.1 further shows that sequential state propagation remains stable as additional preceding windows are incorporated, without evident error accumulation over the tested sequence lengths.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we rethink EEG spatial super-resolution from static signal reconstruction to dynamic neu-ral state filtering. Instead of directly mapping sparse observations to dense EEG signals, we formulate reconstruction as sequential state estimation, where neural dynamics are decomposed into predictable evolution and observation-induced innovation. Based on this formulation, we propose Dynamic State Filtering (DSF), which propagates historical neural states to obtain predictive priors, selectively propa-gates observation-driven corrections through a state-conditioned transition process constrained by spatial topology, and performs uncertainty-aware state correction before waveform reconstruction. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DSF improves reconstruction fidelity, preserves task-relevant neural rep-resentations, and maintains robustness under dynamically changing and unreliable observations. These results suggest that explicitly modeling neural state evolution provides a promising direction for EEG spatial super-resolution and other incomplete sensing problems involving continuously evolving physi-ological signals.

We adopt a subject-independent evaluation protocol with disjoint training and test subjects. Sparse observations are generated by applying channel-visibility masks to the full-density EEG recordings. For each reconstruction scale, all compared methods are evaluated using the same visible-electrode layouts. For observation-corruption experiments, Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.1

We use NMSE, PCC, and reconstruction SNR as the primary signal-level metrics, computed exclu-sively over electrodes that are absent from the sparse input. # E Analysis of Temporal Dynamics and Filtering Stability 

The core contribution of DSF lies in reformulating static EEG reconstruction as dynamic state filter-ing. To verify the robustness of this temporal modeling mechanism, we conduct analyses from two perspectives: the stability of state accumulation over long sequences and the sensitivity to the amount of available historical context. 

E.1 Stability of Sequential State Propagation 

In dynamic state filtering, the amount of historical information available for reconstructing a target EEG segment depends on the length of the observed sequence. This analysis evaluates whether sequential state accumulation improves reconstruction quality without introducing error drift. Although DSF is trained using streams of length L = 6 , its recurrent state-transition operators are shared across windows and do not depend on a fixed sequence length. For this analysis, the trained model is therefore rolled out autoregressively over longer test streams without retraining or parameter modification. We evaluate the reconstruction NMSE under different historical context lengths on the SEED dataset, where each trial contains consecutive 4-second windows. The performance dynamics are compared against a sequential state estimation baseline, Kalman Filter, which also maintains temporal states but does not explicitly decompose state transitions into predictable evolution and observation-driven inno-vation. As shown in Fig. 4, we observe two key properties of DSF that confirm the robustness of the pro-posed filtering mechanism: • Rapid Context Utilization (Cold-start Robustness): When only the target window is available (h = 1 ), DSF performs reconstruction without preceding temporal observations. As the historical context increases, the reconstruction error decreases rapidly and reaches a stable regime within a short sequence length. This demonstrates that the observation-guided innovation routing can effectively extract useful temporal priors from limited historical context. • Long-term Stability (No Error Accumulation): For longer historical contexts, the reconstruc-tion performance remains stable rather than degrading. Although longer sequences introduce more accumulated states, DSF does not exhibit error drift. This behavior is consistent with stable reliability-aware state updating, which regulates the contribution of observation-driven corrections during sequential propagation.
