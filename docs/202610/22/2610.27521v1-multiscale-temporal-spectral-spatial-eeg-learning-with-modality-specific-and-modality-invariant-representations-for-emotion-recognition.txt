# Multiscale Temporal–Spectral–Spatial EEG Learning With Modality-Specific and Modality-Invariant Representations for Emotion Recognition 

Jian Zhang, Manrong Liu, Yilei Huang, Wenhua Liu, Xiaohui Yang and Lijun Yang* 

Abstract —Multimodal physiological signals provide comple-mentary cues for emotion recognition. They also raise two related challenges. Emotion-related EEG patterns vary across time, frequency bands, electrodes, and brain regions. Different physiological modalities also contain both modality-specific and shared affective information. This paper presents GMSI-Net, an EEG-centered multimodal emotion recognition framework. It has three key components. The temporal-spatial-spectral-region (TSSR) encoder models multiscale temporal-channel patterns, differential entropy features, and local-to-global brain-region relations. The modality-specific representation (MSR) branch uses bidirectional cross-modal attention and learnable gates. It controls information transfer while retaining modality-specific cues. The modality-invariant representation (MIR) branch learns a shared affective representation. An adaptively weighted contrastive loss aligns this representation across modalities. Independent multiscale Conv-Transformer encoders process the peripheral physiological signals. GMSI-Net is evaluated on DEAP and MAHNOB-HCI using within-subject random ten-fold cross-validation. The model reaches 96 .21 ± 1.47% and 96 .41 ± 1.60% 

for valence and arousal on DEAP. It reaches 94 .10 ± 2.86% 

and 94 .48 ± 3.77% on MAHNOB-HCI. These results indicate that combining EEG features with complementary physiological information can improve emotion recognition. 

Index Terms —multimodal emotion recognition,graph neural network, modality-specific representation, modality-invariant representation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Affective computing concerns the recognition and interpre-tation of human affective states by computational systems [1], [2]. Emotion recognition is used in adaptive human–computer interaction, assistive technology, and affect-aware interfaces. Facial expression and speech are informative, although their measurement can be affected by lighting, background noise, and the extent to which emotion is outwardly expressed. Physiological signals provide another source of evidence. Electroencephalography (EEG) records neural activity at high temporal resolution. Electrooculography (EOG), electromyo-graphy (EMG), electrocardiography (ECG), and galvanic skin response (GSR) reflect ocular, muscular, cardiac, and electrodermal responses, respectively. HCI are widely used datasets for evaluating physiological emotion recognition [3], [4]. Two forms of variation matter when working with physio-logical signals. An emotional response changes over time, and its EEG correlates may appear in different frequency bands or scalp regions. Peripheral responses can evolve at different rates. Feature extraction thus needs to account for differences between signals. Their integration also needs to retain information specific to individual sensors. EEG is difficult to represent with a single type of feature. Even a short segment contains temporal fluctuations, channel-dependent activity, and frequency-band patterns. A filter with one receptive field may miss patterns at other temporal scales. Frequency descriptors summarize oscillatory activity but contain limited temporal-order information. Independent channel encoding also provides little information about spatial relations. Differential entropy (DE) is commonly used to characterize frequency-band statistics [5]. Spatial dependencies can be modeled with graph neural networks. DGCNN learns dynamic relations between electrodes [6], while LGGNet and PGCN use local-to-global and hierarchical graph structures [7], [8]. Attention-based methods model longer-range dependencies [9]. In many designs, however, temporal, frequency, and brain-region features are handled in separate paths. Bringing them into a single EEG representation remains a practical difficulty. Temporal features retain the sequence of EEG changes; spectral features describe band-specific activity, and region-level features reflect spatial organization. When each is com-pressed independently, subsequent graph operations may no longer preserve where the temporal and spectral information originated. This motivates interactions between these features before brain-region aggregation. Combining physiological modalities raises a different issue. EEG and peripheral recordings have different channel counts, distributions, and noise levels. Concatenation keeps the encoded features but cannot distinguish useful transfer from irrelevant content. Cross-modal attention introduces selective exchange, although the attended features may still contain noise. Learning a common space captures affective information present in several sensors, but a fully invariant representation can discard cues that occur in only one modality. Cross-modal attention, representation learning, and feature reweighting have been explored to manage this trade-off [10]–[12]. GMSI-Net uses different encoders for EEG and peripheral signals. Temporal, spectral, and brain-region operations are combined in the EEG encoder; peripheral signals are processed arXiv:2610.27521v1 [cs.CV] 20 Oct 2026 2

by separate convolutional and sequential encoders. Two paths then work on the encoded features. One exchanges information between modalities while retaining their individual features. The other learns affective information shared by synchronized signals. Treating these operations separately allows complemen-tary and common information to be modeled without assigning them the same role. GMSI-Net is an EEG-centered multimodal emotion recogni-tion model. Its temporal-spatial-spectral-region (TSSR) encoder handles the EEG representation. A modality-specific repre-sentation (MSR) branch performs gated bidirectional cross-modal attention, and a modality-invariant representation (MIR) branch learns shared affective features with adaptive contrastive alignment. The contributions are as follows:  

> •

TSSR combines multiscale temporal-channel attention, five-band DE features, and local-to-global brain-region graph learning. Temporal, spectral, and spatial information enters a unified EEG representation.  

> •

The MSR branch uses pairwise bidirectional attention with learnable gates. Features can be exchanged between modalities without removing the direct path from each modality’s original representation.  

> •

The MIR branch introduces sample-dependent contrastive weights for shared representation learning. Its output is combined with MSR for classification. The experimental analysis covers DEAP and MAHNOB-HCI, including component ablations, modality combinations, and brain-region comparisons. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. EEG-Based Emotion Recognition 

EEG emotion recognition has been studied using temporal, frequency-band, and spatial information. Spectral descriptors characterize changes in rhythmic activity; DE is one widely used statistic for individual frequency bands [5]. Convolutional networks extract short-range temporal patterns, whereas recur-rent and attention-based models can consider longer sequences. SSA-MTSNet combines spectral-spatial attention, multiscale convolution, and recurrent aggregation [13]. These methods indicate that temporal context and spectral content contribute different information to emotion recognition. Graph-based methods focus on spatial relations between EEG recordings that are not readily represented by a regular image grid. DGCNN learns relations among electrodes dynamically [6]. LGGNet builds local and global graph representations, and PGCN uses a hierarchical spatial structure [7], [8]. Aggregating channels by region reduces the number of graph nodes and introduces a structural prior. This also makes the quality of electrode-level features important: regional aggregation should not remove the local responses on which it depends. An electrode graph captures relations among recording sites. A region graph instead operates on features aggregated from anatomical groups. With fewer nodes, region-level learning is more compact, but it depends on the information retained during aggregation. Local-to-global approaches connect these two levels [7], [8]. Temporal and spatial attention, graph Transformers, and multi-view EEG learning have also been investigated [9], [14], [15]. Attention weights can change with the input EEG segment, while graph operations account for broader spatial relations. Adding both to a network is not sufficient by itself. Their interaction determines whether temporal, spectral, and spatial features contribute to the same representation. TSSR links these views before producing the EEG embedding for multimodal fusion. 

B. Multimodal Physiological Emotion Recognition 

Central and peripheral signals respond to related affec-tive processes, but their measurements are not equivalent. EmotionMeter combines physiological representations for multimodal affect recognition [10]. Other work has used cross-modal attention, complementary pattern learning, and feature reweighting [11], [12], [16]. Cross-modal attention lets information from one sensor influence another. Whether that transfer helps depends on the source features and how closely the modalities correspond in a given observation. Attention need not be symmetric: the information useful from modality A to modality B can differ from that useful in the reverse direction. A learnable gate can limit a transferred update when it is unhelpful, leaving more of the target representation intact. This differs from applying one global weight to an entire modality. Physiological information can be fused at the feature, deci-sion, or representation level. Feature-level methods concatenate encoded signals, while decision fusion combines predictions from separate classifiers. Representation-level fusion learns interactions before classification. Attention and gating provide ways to vary these interactions across samples. A feature transferred through cross-modal attention, however, is not necessarily shared in its statistical form. Muscular and neural responses can each predict emotion without being interchange-able. Shared-space learning addresses information that is consis-tent across sensors. Contrastive objectives bring synchronized observations closer and separate unmatched samples. Such a space reflects affective factors supported by several modalities. Other details may belong to just one sensor and should remain available for prediction. Both forms of information are retained in the proposed model. MSR models gated complementary exchanges, while MIR uses adaptive contrastive alignment for shared affective feature learning. In contrastive learning, synchronized physiological record-ings provide positive pairs; unmatched observations provide negatives. Yet the relevance of a modality can vary from sample to sample. Adaptive weights allow the contribution of each modality to vary across samples. III. M ETHODOLOGY

A. Datasets and Preprocessing 

DEAP contains recordings from 32 participants, each of whom watched 40 one-minute music videos [3]. The inputs comprise 32 EEG channels, two EOG channels, two EMG channels, and one GSR channel. Preprocessed signals are sampled at 128 Hz. The 3-s resting segment is used for baseline correction. Each trial is then divided into non-overlapping 1-s windows. Valence and arousal labels are binarized at 5. MAHNOB-HCI contains synchronized physiological and audiovisual recordings [4]. The experiments use 27 valid subjects and physiological inputs comprising 32-channel EEG, three ECG channels, and one GSR channel. After baseline correction and downsampling, signals are divided into non-overlapping 1-s windows. The threshold for both valence and arousal is 5, as in DEAP. Both datasets are evaluated using within-subject random ten-fold cross-validation. Accuracy is reported as the mean and standard deviation across subjects. The data split strategy is kept unchanged in comparisons between model variants.

D. Ablation Study 

Table IV reports ablations on DEAP and MAHNOB-HCI. EEG subcomponents are assessed in the CMMF-Net configura-tion; TSSR, MSR, MIR, and modality weighting are assessed in GMSI-Net. For w/o TSSR, the EEG encoder is replaced by a vanilla Transformer. Among the evaluated final-model variants, replacing TSSR produces the largest accuracy decrease. On DEAP, valence accuracy falls from 96 .21% to 93 .08% , and arousal accuracy from 96 .41% to 93 .36% when the complete EEG encoder is replaced by a vanilla Transformer. The change is larger on MAHNOB-HCI: 94 .10% and 94 .48% fall to 72 .11% and 

75 .55% . The structured EEG encoder is thus particularly important in the MAHNOB-HCI experiments. The fusion branches provide further gains beyond EEG encoding, though their effects are not the same on the two datasets. Without MIR, DEAP valence and arousal accuracy drop by 0.72 and 0.51 percentage points. Removing MSR produces smaller decreases on DEAP and larger ones on MAHNOB-HCI. Learned modality weighting also performs better than uniform weighting. Cross-modal interaction and shared-space alignment therefore contribute at different levels across the datasets. Accuracy also declines when DE features, brain-region graphs, or peripheral encoders are removed. Both structured EEG features and multimodal interactions contribute to the predictions. Without DE features, MAHNOB-HCI valence and arousal accuracy fall to 73 .24% and 76 .12% . Spectral features have a substantial effect in this setting. The representation-level ablations on DEAP remain relatively close to the full-model accuracy. Removing MSR changes valence by 0.24 and arousal by 0.04 points; removing MIR changes them by 0.72 and 0.51 points. On MAHNOB-HCI, the two branches make more similar contributions. DE features also affect the datasets differently. Their removal produces a modest decline on DEAP but a much larger one on MAHNOB-HCI, indicating a stronger dependence on spectral information in the latter experiments. Taken together, the ablations identify structured EEG feature extraction and cross-modal representation learning as useful parts of the model. TSSR accounts for a large part of the EEG representation gain. MSR, MIR, and adaptive modality weighting bring additional improvements to multimodal fusion. 

E. Effect of Modality Combinations 

Tables V and VI compare the input combinations. The complete four-modal configuration gives the highest accuracy among the tested combinations on DEAP. On MAHNOB-HCI, the highest accuracy is obtained with EEG+ECG+GSR. Not every smaller subset benefits from an added sensor; a new modality may introduce redundancy or noise along with useful information. All tested combinations include EEG, consistent with the EEG-centered design of GMSI-Net. These experiments test what peripheral signals add to EEG rather than whether they can replace it. Accuracy does not increase consistently as modalities are added. The usefulness of peripheral inputs depends partly on signal quality and redundancy between sensors. For DEAP, the evaluated configurations combine EEG with different subsets of EOG, EMG, and GSR. The MAHNOB-HCI experiments instead combine EEG with ECG and GSR. VI. C ONCLUSION 

GMSI-Net was developed for EEG-centered multimodal emotion recognition. It combines multiscale EEG feature learning with modality-specific and shared representation learning. Temporal, spectral, and spatial information is extracted from EEG, while peripheral responses provide complementary cues. The two fusion branches retain features specific to each modality and learn shared affective information. The achieved accuracies are 96 .21 ± 1.47% for valence and 

96 .41 ± 1.60% for arousal on DEAP, and 94 .10 ± 2.86% and 

94 .48 ± 3.77% on MAHNOB-HCI. The experiments also show differences between components. Replacing TSSR reduces performance markedly, especially on MAHNOB-HCI. DE features have a larger effect on MAHNOB-HCI than on DEAP, and the gains from MSR and MIR differ across datasets. In the modality-combination tests, adding one peripheral sensor does not always help. Within the tested combinations, using complementary signals together yields higher accuracies. Brain-region comparisons favor structured EEG features, while the visualizations show clearer class separation after multimodal fusion. These observations point to the value of learning EEG fea-tures across several dimensions without discarding information specific to peripheral modalities. Future work will examine cross-subject generalization, missing-modality robustness, and the efficiency of cross-modal interaction. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
