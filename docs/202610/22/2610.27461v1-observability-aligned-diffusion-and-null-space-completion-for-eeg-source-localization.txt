# Observable and Null-Space Electrophysiological Source Imaging with Diffusion Priors 

Zirui Wen ∗, Shihao Yang ∗, Feng Liu 

Abstract —Electrophysiological source imaging is severely un-derdetermined due to the lead-field matrix has limited rank relative to the source dimension and strongly correlated columns. Scalp EEG measurements constrain only the observable row-space component, leaving a high-dimensional null space unob-servable. We propose a two-stage framework coupling poste-rior diffusion refinement with deterministic null-space comple-tion. A minimum-norm-initialized, singular-coordinate Posterior-DDIM sampler combines a spatiotemporal diffusion prior with direction-dependent measurement reliability to refine the observ-able component. A frozen ridge predictor and nonlinear temporal residual network then estimate the null component under explicit projection constraints, preserving the refined row estimate and its predicted EEG exactly. All learned components are trained exclusively on simulated cortical activity and its row–null de-composition, without paired EEG–source training data. Across simulations with varying noise levels, the method achieved higher frame-level area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and average precision, together with lower localization error, than conventional and learning-based baselines. Exploratory evalua-tions on evoked EEG and simultaneous scalp EEG–stereo-EEG recordings showed condition- and event-dependent concordance with cross-modal and intracranial references. 

Index Terms —Diffusion models, electroencephalography source imaging, inverse problems, source localization. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# ELECTROENCEPHALOGRAPHIC source imaging (ESI) aims to reconstruct cortical activity from scalp electroen-cephalography (EEG), combining the millisecond temporal resolution of EEG with spatially resolved representations of brain dynamics [1]–[3]. It has been widely used for epilep-togenic source characterization, network analysis, and non-invasive functional mapping, although its accuracy remains strongly influenced by the forward model and the assumptions imposed on the source distribution [4], [5]. The fundamental difficulty of ESI lies in its severe underdetermination. The number of candidate cortical sources substantially exceeds the number of EEG sensors, such that many distinct source configurations can produce identical or nearly identical mea-surements [6]. Classical ESI methods address this ambiguity through different structural assumptions. Minimum-norm esti-mators and their standardized variants favor distributed, low-energy solutions [7]–[9], while beamforming methods localize

Sparse and mixed-norm formulations promote more focal source config-urations [11]–[13], whereas extent-oriented approaches intro-duce spatial structure to better recover distributed activation [14], [15]. While these assumptions make the inverse problem tractable, they necessarily introduce information that is not directly determined by the measurements [16]. Consequently, the central goal of ESI is not to identify a unique cortical waveform, but to recover physiologically meaningful source activity as reliably as possible under this intrinsic ambiguity. Data-driven approaches have emerged as a flexible alter-native for capturing source structures that are difficult to en-code through hand-crafted regularization. Early deep learning approaches explored spatiotemporal recurrent modeling and convolutional source estimation [17], [18], while subsequent methods incorporated biophysical dynamics and physics-informed reconstruction [19], [20]. More recent studies have increasingly embedded the structure of the inverse problem into learnable optimization frameworks [21]–[24]. Most of these approaches, however, rely on a single learned mapping to perform measurement inversion and source regularization simultaneously. As a result, the reconstructed activity does not explicitly distinguish information supported by the EEG from structure introduced by the learned prior. Diffusion models provide a natural alternative by separating prior learning from measurement conditioning. An unconditional diffusion model can learn spatiotemporal source distributions independently of EEG observations [25], [26], while the known forward operator can be incorporated only at inference time with-out retraining the generative prior [27]–[30]. The remaining challenge is how to exploit such a prior when the lead field constrains only a low-dimensional, strongly anisotropic observable subspace, while a large portion of cortical activity lies in its null space. This distinction is central to how a diffusion prior should be used for ESI. Since the EEG likelihood depends only on the projection of cortical activity onto the row space of the lead field, measurement guidance can directly constrain only the observable component, whereas any null-space perturbation leaves the predicted EEG unchanged. Most diffusion-based in-verse solvers do not explicitly separate these two roles. In dif-fusion posterior sampling (DPS) [27], for example, likelihood guidance can affect the null component only indirectly through the Jacobian of the learned denoiser, leaving measurement-equivalent null-space completions primarily determined by the learned prior. Measurement-consistent methods such as denoising diffusion restoration models (DDRM) and Posterior-DDIM [28], [29] provide more explicit conditioning along observable singular directions, but data consistency alone arXiv:2610.27461v1 [cs.CE] 20 Oct 2026 IEEE JOURNALS MANUSCRIPT 2

does not ensure correct cortical localization. Distinct source configurations can remain equally consistent with the EEG while differing substantially in spatial support. Moreover, the observable directions themselves are not equally informative. The singular values of a realistic lead field span more than two orders of magnitude (Section III-A), making weakly observed directions considerably more sensitive to measurement noise. These limitations motivate treating the row and null compo-nents as distinct estimation problems rather than resolving both through a single diffusion trajectory. Range–null formulations make this separation explicit. The denoising diffusion null-space model (DDNM) assigns the measurement-determined range component through the pseudoinverse and uses the generative prior to recover the null component [30]. For an ill-conditioned lead field, however, direct pseudoinverse recovery can strongly amplify noise along weak singular directions. A posterior factorization into a row-space term and a conditional null-space term [31] further clarifies how the unobservable component should be estimated for localization. Once the row component is given, the EEG provides no additional information about the null component, and the minimum mean-square-error point estimate is therefore its conditional mean. In contrast, an individual posterior sample retains conditional variability that can introduce unsupported spatial activity into a localization map (Section III-A). These observations suggest assigning the two subspaces to different estimators, with reliability-aware inference in the observable row space and deterministic conditional completion in the null space. Accordingly, we formulate learned-prior ESI as two observability-aligned estimation tasks (Fig. 1). Stage I recon-structs the row-space component using a singular-coordinate Posterior-DDIM sampler initialized from the minimum-norm estimate (MNE), balancing measurement reliability along each observable direction against the learned source prior. Stage II then performs deterministic null-space completion following the conditional-mean principle above. A frozen ridge map captures the linearly predictable component, while a temporal convolutional network (TCN), trained on clean row–null pairs, predicts the remaining nonlinear dependence. Both completion terms are explicitly projected onto the lead-field null space and therefore cannot modify either the refined row estimate or its predicted EEG. The resulting reconstruction thus pre-serves measurement-supported information in the observable subspace while using the learned prior only to complete the unobservable component, yielding a representative source estimate tailored to localization. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 1) Source-only prior learning. All learned components, including the diffusion prior, ridge map, residual basis, and TCN, are trained exclusively on simulated cortical activity and its lead-field-defined row–null decomposi-tion, without using EEG measurements or paired EEG– source training samples. This design separates source-prior learning from measurement-conditioned inversion. 2) Observability-aligned diffusion reconstruction. An MNE-initialized, singular-coordinate Posterior-DDIM sampler combines the learned source prior with direction-dependent measurement reliability to refine the observable row-space component. The reverse process operates in the full source space, while only the refined row component is retained for subsequent reconstruction. 3) Deterministic, measurement-preserving null-space com-pletion. Motivated by conditional-mean estimation, a frozen ridge predictor and a TCN-based nonlinear resid-ual complete the unobservable component from the refined row estimate. Both terms are confined to the lead-field null space, ensuring that neither the refined row component nor its predicted EEG is altered. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Conventional Priors for EEG Source Imaging 

Under the linear forward model, scalp EEG is a lead-field projection of cortical activity corrupted by noise. Since many cortical configurations can explain the same measurements, conventional ESI methods resolve this ambiguity through explicit structural assumptions, many of which admit a unified Bayesian interpretation through different source covariance models and inference rules [32]. Minimum-norm estimation (MNE) favors distributed low-energy solutions [7], while sLORETA and dSPM standardize or noise-normalize the esti-mate [8], [9], and LCMV performs localization through adap-tive spatial filtering [10]. Sparse and mixed-norm formulations promote focal or temporally structured activity [11], [12], whereas extent-oriented methods impose structure on spatial variations, source boundaries, or cortical geometry to better recover distributed generators [13]–[15]. These approaches are interpretable and require no learned training distribution, but their priors are analytically specified and offer limited flexi-bility for modeling complex spatiotemporal source dynamics. In our framework, MNE serves as an observation-informed initialization for row-space refinement and as the basis of the residual noise-level heuristic in Section IV-A. 

B. Deep Learning for EEG Source Imaging 

Deep learning methods replace hand-crafted priors with source structure learned from simulated data. Representa-tive approaches include convolutional source estimation [18], spatiotemporal recurrent modeling [17], neural-mass-informed networks [19], and physics-informed volumetric reconstruc-tion [20]. A complementary line of work embeds iterative solvers into trainable architectures. XDL-ESI learns spatiotem-poral regularization through unfolded optimization [21], while ADMM-ESINet, VSSI2p-Net, and D-ADMM-Net incorporate learned sparsity, variation, or diffusion-based denoising into unfolded ADMM updates [22]–[24]. Despite their architectural differences, these methods do not explicitly separate source reconstruction according to lead-field observability. Measurement-supported structure and information introduced by the learned model therefore remain coupled. Our framework instead separates these roles at the subspace level. C. Diffusion Priors for Linear Inverse Problems 

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) learn a generative prior by reversing progressive noising [25], while denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIMs) enable acceler-ated deterministic or partially stochastic sampling [26]. For linear inverse problems, DPS incorporates likelihood gradients into the reverse process [27], DDRM exploits the singular structure of the forward operator [28], and DDNM sepa-rates range-space consistency from null-space generation [30]. Posterior-DDIM further derives singular-coordinate posterior transitions that balance measurement and prior information according to direction-wise reliability [29]. These methods provide principled measurement conditioning, but they do not by themselves resolve ambiguity among measurement-equivalent cortical supports. We therefore use Posterior-DDIM only to refine the observable row-space component, with MNE initialization and direction-dependent reliability, while assign-ing the null component to a separate deterministic completion stage (Section III). 

D. Observable–Null Decomposition 

The decomposition of inverse solutions into observable and null-space components is well established in inverse-problem theory. In classical M/EEG analysis, the minimum-norm pseudoinverse recovers the component determined by ideal noiseless measurements, whereas the null-space component requires additional assumptions [16]. Learned inverse methods have incorporated the same geometry through null-space networks that preserve predicted measurements [33], explicit range–null decomposition [34], and diffusion restoration with range-consistent and prior-driven null components [30]. More recently, Guo et al. factorized the inverse posterior into an identifiable component and a conditional null-space distribution to characterize intrinsic ambiguity [31]. Building on this decomposition, our framework assigns the two subspaces different estimators according to their observ-ability. The noisy and anisotropically observed row compo-nent is refined through singular-coordinate posterior diffusion, whereas the unobservable null component is completed de-terministically using a conditional-mean-motivated estimator. This separation exploits measurement reliability where EEG is informative while avoiding unnecessary stochastic variability in the null-space contribution to localization. III. M ETHOD

A. Data, Metrics, and Implementation 

We simulated 128-frame source sequences with The Virtual Brain (TVB) [38] on Ns = 2 ,052 cortical nodes, each con-taining one to six stimulated centers followed by propagated dynamics. EEG was generated with a fixed 64 × 2,052 lead field and white Gaussian noise at SNRs of 0, 10, 20, and 30 dB, defined on Frobenius norms. We call each source sequence an identity and each EEG realization at one SNR an observation. The 12,000 identities were split into 8,000 training, 2,000 validation, and 2,000 test identities. The source prior, ridge map, residual basis, and TCN were trained on clean training identities, and the learned baselines were retrained on simulated EEG at all four SNRs. Unless otherwise stated, all methods were evaluated on the 2,000 test identities (8,000 observations). In simulation, Stage I used the generating noise level. C. Ablations 

Table II crosses two row inputs with two completions, keeping the completion weights and scoring rules fixed. With ridge + TCN completion, Posterior-DDIM rows improved all four metrics relative to MNE rows at every SNR and reduced LE by 21.82 mm [21.35, 22.32], most strongly at 0 dB (39.08 mm). The refined row was thus the better input for completion. Null-space completion supplied most of the ranking gain, raising frame AUC from 0.8213 for the refined row alone to 0.8977 with ridge completion. The learned residual then mainly improved support overlap and spatial agreement: it increased AP by 0.0644 [0.0593, 0.0695] and reduced LE by 14.67 mm [14.05, 15.30], whereas its AUC gain was not resolved. With exact clean rows, the same residual reduced LE to 21.00 mm; the gap to the deployment value of 30.90 mm reflects row estimation error, which the completion cannot correct. In a separate control with a 64-dimensional basis, a framewise multilayer perceptron slightly exceeded the TCN in AP (0.5211 versus 0.5099), indicating that the main benefit stems from nonlinear completion; temporal context did not provide an additional advantage in this setting. 

D. Sampled Versus Deterministic Completion 

On a fixed subset of 60 test identities (240 observations), we compared deterministic completion with the sampler’s own null realization and with DDNM+ [30], which used the same source prior, MNE initialization, and 100 denoiser evaluations (Table III). Deterministic completion increased AP by 0.222 [0.168, 0.279] over the sampled null component and by 0.272 [0.214, 0.333] over the full DDNM+ pipeline, whereas the LE differences were not resolved. Consistent with (4), sampling the null component mainly degraded the ranking of true support nodes. 

E. Comparison with Baselines 

We compared four learned baselines, ConvDip [18], XDL-ESI [21], ADMM-ESINet [22], and VSSI2p-Net [23], reim-plemented and retrained following the original paper, and four conventional baselines, MNE [7], sLORETA [8], LCMV [10], and a framewise ADMM solver with source and graph-variation sparsity (Structured ADMM) [13], [14]. As shown in Table IV, the proposed method attained the highest AUC and AP and the smallest LE at all four SNRs, and the highest F1 at 0–20 dB. Its overall AP exceeded that of the strongest baseline in AP, ConvDip, by 0.0463 [0.0397, 0.0530], although the margin at 10 dB was not resolved. At 30 dB, Structured ADMM attained a higher F1, whereas the proposed method retained a 5.12 mm smaller LE. These gains come at a computational cost: on a single RTX 5070 GPU, the proposed pipeline required 1.88 s per sequence, compared with 0.8– 96 ms for the learned baselines. 

F. Robustness to Noise and Forward-Model Mismatch 

To test sensitivity to the idealized simulation assumptions, we reran the frozen pipeline without retraining on validation identities at four SNRs under three departures (Table V): the residual-based noise estimate used for recorded EEG in place of the generating noise level, a fixed 5% RMS channel-gain error with inference based on the nominal lead field, and correlated sensor noise with a 50% common-mode component and unchanged marginal variance. Relative to the nominal condition, the estimated noise level reduced AP by 0.0324 [0.0175, 0.0467] and increased LE by 1.73 mm [0.16, 3.15], and the gain error increased LE by 2.58 mm [0.80, 4.26], whereas correlated noise produced no detectable change. Performance thus degraded only moderately when these assumptions were relaxed.

V. D ISCUSSION 

A central feature of the proposed framework is the explicit separation of measurement-constrained and prior-driven in-formation. The row-space component is refined using both the EEG and the learned diffusion prior, whereas Stage II introduces only structure that is invisible to the measurement operator. The matched ablations showed that posterior row refinement and nonlinear null-space completion contributed distinct improvements to localization when the other compo-nent was held fixed (Section IV-C). Because the completion is restricted to the lead-field null space, it cannot alter the EEG predicted by the refined row estimate. Improvements introduced by Stage II therefore arise from learned source structure rather than additional fitting of the measurements. At the same time, this separation makes the limitation of prior-driven completion explicit: the EEG provides no direct means to verify structure added in the null space, and inaccurate priors may introduce measurement-invisible activity [42]. The comparison between sampled and deterministic com-pletion further supports treating the null component differently from the observable component. Once the row component is specified, the EEG provides no additional information about the remaining null-space variability. For a representative point estimate, sampling this conditional variability can therefore introduce source activity that is plausible under the prior but unnecessary for localization. In our experiments, deterministic completion improved the identification of true support relative to retaining the sampler’s null realization (Section IV-D), con-sistent with the conditional-mean motivation in Section III-A. This result should not be interpreted as a general preference for deterministic inference. The comparison was performed on a matched subset of the test data and does not isolate sampling from the use of a learned conditional predictor. Moreover, deterministic completion deliberately suppresses conditional variability and therefore does not characterize posterior uncertainty. Applications concerned with ambiguity or uncertainty quantification would instead require modeling or sampling the conditional null distribution. The exact-row diagnostic shows that substantially bet-ter completion is possible when the true observable component is available, indicating that row-estimation error propagates directly into Stage II. A related source of mismatch is that the residual model is trained on clean row components but evaluated on estimated ones. Training the completion model on Stage I outputs may reduce this discrepancy and improve robustness to row-estimation errors. The two stages also have different dependencies on the forward model. The row–null decomposition, ridge map, and residual basis are defined by the lead field, making the null-space completion specific to a given forward model. The diffusion prior is independent of EEG measurements but remains tied to the cortical source space on which it is trained. Consequently, deployment on a new participant requires an appropriate individual forward model and refitting of the completion modules, while a different cortical source space additionally requires retraining the source prior. The individual-geometry coreg-spikes experiment (Section IV-I) demonstrates this procedure but does not establish cross-subject generalization. The present evidence therefore has a defined scope. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced an observability-aligned formulation of EEG source localization that treats the observable and unobservable components of the inverse problem as distinct estimation tasks. Singular-coordinate Posterior-DDIM refines the noisy row-space component by balancing a learned diffusion prior against direction-dependent measurement reliability, while determin-istic ridge and nonlinear residual models complete the null component from the refined row representation. Restricting the completion to the lead-field null space preserves the refined row estimate and its predicted EEG exactly, allow-ing measurement-constrained reconstruction and prior-driven completion to remain explicitly separated. Across simulations, the proposed framework achieved higher frame-level AUC and average precision and lower localization error than eight baselines at every tested noise level. Matched ablations further attributed complementary gains to posterior row refinement and learned null-space completion, while deterministic com-pletion improved support identification relative to retaining sampled null-space realizations. Exploratory applications to evoked EEG and simultaneous scalp EEG–SEEG data showed condition- and event-dependent agreement with cross-modal references. These results support observability-guided dif-fusion reconstruction as a principled approach to severely underdetermined EEG source localization, while highlighting improved row estimation, robustness to model mismatch, and validation with known source locations as important directions for future work.
