Abstract. The Wilson–Cowan model is a classic example of a “neural mass model”—a simplified yet powerful way to describe the activity of large networks of neurons. It is particularly interesting because, even in its relatively simple form, it can produce a surprising variety of behaviours, from steady rhythms to complex oscillations. A key factor influencing these dynamics is the presence of time delays, reflecting the time it takes for signals to travel between neurons. This article offers an accessible introduction to the Wilson–Cowan model, focusing on how these time delays affect its behaviour. We explore the core concepts behind the model, survey the existing literature, and introduce some numerical techniques specifically designed for analysing such delay differential equa-tions. We also provide a practical, hands-on tutorial on using the DDE-BIFTOOL software package, demonstrating its application to the Wilson-Cowan model. Downloadable code allows the reader to reproduce our results and to explore the model’s dynamics further, including MATLAB code for all computations, using DDE-BIFTOOL for the continuation, and Python versions of simulation codes.

1. Introduction. Computational neuroscience plays a crucial role in bridging the gap between experimental observations of brain activity and our theoretical under-standing of how the brain functions [12]. It employs mathematical and computational tools to model and simulate neural systems, ranging from individual neurons [7, 24] to large-scale brain networks [9, 30, 35]. This field has a rich history, with early suc-cesses like the Hodgkin-Huxley model [22], which, through its detailed description of the ionic currents underlying the action potential, earned its creators the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine [36]. However, even with such detailed models, capturing the full complexity of neural systems remains a challenge. While simplified models can offer valuable insights, incorporating more physiological detail is often crucial for accurately reproducing observed brain dynamics. This is particularly true when con-sidering the influence of factors like the finite speed of signal propagation along axons and the time required for synaptic transmission. These inherent delays in neural com-munication, often neglected in simpler models, can significantly impact the behaviour of neural circuits [1, 34]. Neural mass models offer a simplified yet powerful approach to understanding the collective dynamics of large populations of neurons [9]. These models operate at a mesoscopic scale, averaging the activity of many neurons within a specific brain region to describe the overall behaviour of that population. Rather than simulating the intricate spiking activity of individual neurons, neural mass models focus on capturing the average firing rate and synaptic activity, thus significantly reducing computational complexity. This simplification allows researchers to explore large-scale brain networks and investigate how different brain regions interact. These models are particularly useful for understanding phenomena like oscillations, synchrony, and the impact of various perturbations on brain activity, providing a crucial bridge between microscopic neuronal details and macroscopic brain function measurable with techniques like EEG

This review focuses on one such crucial physiological detail: time delays and their effect on the dynamics of neural mass models, specifically within the context of the Wilson–Cowan model [43]. We will explore how incorporating these delays, which arise from the finite speed of action potential propagation and synaptic transmission, can dramatically alter the predicted behaviour of neural populations, leading to phenomena such as oscillations, synchrony, and even instability and chaos. Understanding the role of time delays is essential for developing more realistic and predictive models of brain function [8]. Within the family of neural mass models, the Wilson–Cowan model stands as a foundational example [43]. This model, developed in the 1970s, describes the in-teraction between excitatory and inhibitory populations of neurons. It captures the essential dynamics of neuronal feedback loops, demonstrating how the interplay be-tween excitation and inhibition can give rise to a rich repertoire of behaviours, includ-ing stable states, oscillations, and bistability [23]. The Wilson–Cowan model, while relatively simple, provides a valuable framework for understanding fundamental prin-ciples of neural population dynamics and serves as a stepping stone for more complex models. Its relative tractability makes it an ideal system for exploring mathemat-ical techniques applicable to a wider range of neural mass models, including those incorporating time delays, which we will focus on in this review. We begin this tutorial with a general introduction to delay differential equations (DDEs) and their properties. We then introduce the Wilson–Cowan model as a key example of a neural mass model, highlighting its structure and biological interpreta-tion. Following this, we discuss numerical methods suitable for solving DDEs, laying the groundwork for our analysis. We then delve into basic bifurcation analysis tech-niques, primarily using the DDE-BIFTOOL software package [15, 16], to explore how time delays influence the stability and qualitative behaviour of solutions to the Wilson–Cowan system. We further examine the emergence of complex dynamics, in-cluding chaos, in this model due to the presence of delays. We provide pointers to further reading for those interested in exploring these topics in greater depth. Fi-nally, we summarise our findings and offer concluding remarks on the importance of considering time delays in neural mass models. 2. Delay Differential Equations. Delay differential equation (DDE) models are playing an increasingly prominent role across a range of life science applications [39]. They are particularly crucial in neuroscience [8] since neural communication delays necessitate DDEs to model neural dynamics accurately, impacting oscillations, synchrony, and network stability, which is essential for understanding both healthy and diseased brain function. The focus of this review will be the following class of delay differential equations: (2.1) dx

dt = F(x(t), x(t − τ )) , x(t) ∈ Rn, t ≥ 0,

where τ is the delay, and x(t) ∈ Rn is a state in configuration space describing the average activity of a neuronal population. In relevant neural dynamics models, the function F : Rn(d+1) → Rn (where d is the number of delays in the system, here equal to one) typically combines a decay term with a sigmoidal input function, providing a simplified yet effective way to model the essential dynamics of neural populations. 2.2. Numerical Methods for DDEs. Solving delay differential equations in MATLAB is simplified by the built-in dde23 function. This versatile solver can handle a variety of DDEs, including those represented by (2.1), and is based on a modified Runge–Kutta method, analogous to the ode23 solver for ordinary differential equa-tions. (See [37] for more details.) Alongside the MATLAB dde23 solver, we also solve (2.1) using the Euler map approximation, in which time-discretisation allows us to replace the DDE system with a difference equation: 

xk+1 = xk + hF(xk, xk−m), k ≥ 0,

xk = ϕ(kh ), −m ≤ k ≤ 0,

(2.9) in which h = τ /m (m ∈ N) is the step size and xk denotes the numerical approxima-tion of x at time t = kh . We note that, although xk+1 depends only on xk and xk−m,continuing the iteration requires all of the intermediate iterates xk−m, . . . , xk−1. For simplicity, we restrict our analysis to step sizes that are integer submultiples of the time delay τ , thus avoiding the need for interpolation. Note that the Euler map of-fers a computationally efficient approach to the calculation of dynamic observables, such as the Lyapunov exponent (as discussion in Section 6). For such observables, the emphasis is typically on long-term dynamics, where qualitative agreement with the true solution is often prioritised over high levels of numerical accuracy. Addition-ally, this simplified framework facilitates understanding the fundamental principles of numerically solving DDEs, making it a valuable pedagogical tool. In Section 4 we apply the Euler map to the Wilson–Cowan model and compare it with MATLAB’s dde23 .

The first (for η = ηs) governs in-phase (synchronous) perturbations, in which the activities of the two populations move together, and the second (for η = ηa) anti-phase perturbations, in which one rises as the other falls. Our task is now to understand how changes in τ result in changes in the stability of the equilibrium. That is, does changing τ result in the solutions of (3.2) having a change in the sign of their real part? As (3.2) is a continuous function, the equilibrium can only change stability when a root crosses the imaginary axis. For a real root λ to change sign, it would need to cross the point λ = 0, which would require η = 1 in (3.2). However, given that this statement is independent of τ , this would not constitute a delay-induced instability. Thus, delay-induced instabilities must arise as a complex conjugate pair of roots together cross the imaginary axis. That is, the instability must occur via a Hopf bifurcation. At the Hopf bifurcation, we can write λ = ±iω , and (3.2) becomes (3.3) 1 ± iω = η e ∓iωτ , η ∈ { ηs, η a}.

Computing the modulus of both sides of (3.3) provides the restriction 1 + ω2 = η2,or equivalently ω = pη2 − 1, which has real solutions only if |η| > 1. Comparing the arguments of each side of (3.3), and noting that arg (1 + iω ) = arctan ω, we can compute the τ -values at which the Hopf bifurcations occur as (3.4) τj = arg η − arctan ω + 2 πj ω , j = 0 , 1, 2, . . . , 

where arg η is either zero (if η > 1) or π (if η < −1), and the 2 πj term accounts for the fact that complex arguments are only uniquely defined modulo 2 π. Equation (3.4) reveals an infinite sequence of equally spaced Hopf bifurcations, a distance 2 π/ω 

apart, which is also the period of the oscillation born at each one. As the delay 

τ increases, we obtain an increasingly diverse spectrum of oscillatory solutions that would not otherwise be present in a corresponding ODE model. Finally, it remains to confirm whether the equilibrium is losing or gaining stability at each Hopf bifurcation. To do so, we differentiate (3.2) with respect to τ , which after some rearrangement yields the equation (3.5) dλ

which tells us whether λ is an increasing or decreasing function of τ . At the Hopf bifurcation, we have λ = ±iω , and the real-part of (3.5) becomes ω2/|1 + τ + iωτ |2,which is always positive. Thus, every λ crosses the imaginary axis in the direction left to right, corresponding to a loss of stability for the equilibrium configuration. A unqiue equilibrium in the undelayed model displays an infinite sequence of Hopf bifurcations as we increase the delay. (We have explored the first of these in Figure 2(a).) The resulting orbits can then be destabilised by torus bifurcations. Additionally, we have seen isolas of periodic orbits that display period doubling bifurcations and folds of orbits. In essence, the theme here is that as the delay is increased, the dynamics get progressively more intricate. Indeed, traversing an intricate sequence of torus or period doubling bifurcations is a hallmark route to chaotic dynamics, which we explore further below. 

6. Chaos in the Wilson–Cowan Model. We examine chaotic solutions in the Wilson–Cowan model by computing Lyapunov exponents as the delay is increased. Lyapunov exponents are essentially a measure of the extent to which trajectories starting at neighbouring points in phase-space diverge from one another, with positive Lyapunov exponents indicating chaotic behaviour. Listing 6 estimates the largest Lyapunov exponent (LLE, denoted λmax ) by following two nearby solutions with the Euler map, written as in Listing 2 but this time for the “rich” parameter set. Since the state of a DDE at time t is the whole function on [ t − τ, t ], the distance between the two solutions is measured over all m + 1 stored values, not just the current one. The last m + 1 states are kept in a circular buffer, so the delayed state is always the oldest column and is overwritten as soon as it has been used. (For further information on Lyapunov exponents and their computation in the DDE case, we direct the reader to [19] and [44].) 7. Advanced Topics and Future Directions. The model in this review is deliberately minimal: two populations, one fixed delay and no randomness. Each of these simplifications can be relaxed. Below we describe four potential extensions, briefly outlining any necessary modifications to the approach and anticipated results.

7.3. Noise. Real neural populations are noisy, because they contain a finite number of neurons and receive inputs that the model does not describe. The simplest approach to incorporating noise adds a small random kick at every step of the Euler map (2.9): 

xn+1 = xn + h F(xn, xn−m) + σ√h ξn,EXPLORING NEURAL DYNAMICS WITH TIME DELAYS 19 where each ξn is a vector of independent random numbers from the standard normal distribution and σ sets the strength of the noise. This is the Euler–Maruyama method [6]. In Listing 2 it amounts to adding + sigma*sqrt(h)*randn(2,1) to the update. In the limit of small noise, we would anticipate the corresponding bifurcation analysis to approximately recover the results above; however, due to the additional instability that the noise represents, irregular oscillations and complex solutions generally appear in simulations slightly before the predicted bifurcation values in the above analysis. We note also that the addition of noise renders quasi-periodic, mode-locked and weakly chaotic motion harder to tell apart. The bifurcation diagrams of the model without noise remain the best guide to what the noisy model will do [5]. 

7.4. Applications in neuroscience. Delayed neural mass models are widely used to study brain rhythms and their disorders. For example, in Parkinson’s disease, patients show excessive oscillations at 13–30 oscillations per second (the beta band) in a loop between two interconnected regions deep in the brain, the subthalamic nucleus and the globus pallidus. Models of this loop of Wilson–Cowan type, with a delay on each connection, identify the delays and connection strengths for which the beta oscillations appear [31]; the analysis is a version of our calculation of the Hopf delays above. Similarly, in epilepsy, neural mass models reproduce features of the brain activity recorded at the start of seizures [42], and the way seizures start and stop has been classified by the bifurcations through which a model enters and leaves its seizure state [26]. The same tools apply to the rhythms recorded by EEG and MEG [11, 25]. In each case, the questions are the ones we have asked here: at what delay does the resting state become unstable, what replaces it, and how does that change as the parameters vary? 

8. Discussion and Further Reading. Using the Wilson–Cowan model with a single delay, we have introduced the main analytical and numerical tools for studying delay differential equations. The model is simple enough for its first bifurcation to be found by hand, yet rich enough to display torus bifurcations, period doubling, isolas of periodic orbits and chaos as the delay alone is varied. We have seen that the delay does not change the equilibria of the model, only their stability (Subsection 3.1). For the symmetric model, the characteristic equation factorises into in-phase and anti-phase parts, and the delays at which the equilibrium loses stability can be written down exactly: they form an infinite, equally spaced sequence of Hopf bifurcations, and at every one of them the equilibrium loses sta-bility. Direct simulation with the Euler map and with dde23 confirmed this picture (Section 4). Numerical continuation with DDE-BIFTOOL then allowed us to follow equilibria and periodic orbits as the delay varies, to compute their stability through eigenvalues and Floquet multipliers, and to locate torus bifurcations, period dou-blings and folds of periodic orbits (Section 5). We also observed that not every orbit of interest is born at a Hopf bifurcation: the asymmetric orbits of Figure 2(b) lie on an isola, which can only be reached from a good initial guess, here a simulation. Two-parameter continuation (Figure 5) placed these one-parameter results in a wider context. Finally, simulations and Lyapunov exponents showed how the torus bifurca-tion of the anti-phase orbit leads, through quasi-periodic motion and mode locking, to chaos (Section 6). Our aim throughout this tutorial has been to provide an accessible introduction to the topic of delay differential equations and their simulation and analysis. For the reader interested in finding out more, we recommend the accessible introductions of [18] and [39], with more advanced treatments in [14] and [21]. Numerical methods 20 M. R. NELSON AND J. J. CROFTS 

for solving DDEs are covered in [2] and [37], and the numerical computation of the stability of equilibria of DDEs is covered in more detail in [4]. For bifurcation theory in general, including the torus, period-doubling and fold bifurcations met here, see [27, 40]. In order to continue development of more advanced understanding of DDE-BIFTOOL code beyond that shown in the listings in this paper and the supporting codes online, we refer the reader to the DDE-BIFTOOL manual [38] and the excel-lent demonstrations distributed with the package itself. For those preferring alterna-tives to Matlab, the Julia library DDEBifurcationKit.jl offers similar capabilities to DDE-BIFTOOL [41]. Similarly, MatCont, a widely used MATLAB package for the continuation of ODEs with a graphical interface, has also recently been extended to delay equations and to the computation of Lyapunov exponents [28], providing an alternative to DDE-BIFTOOL within MATLAB. The role of delays in neural systems is reviewed in [8], and the delayed Wilson–Cowan model studied here was analysed in detail in [10]. Wider introductions to mathematical neuroscience are given by [12] and [17], and large-scale brain network models are reviewed in [3] and [13]. In conclusion, time delays are an unavoidable feature of neural communication, and even a small delay can significantly change the qualitative behaviour of a neural population model. Because the effects of a delay cannot, in general, be guessed from the undelayed model, they must be computed. We hope that the combination of exact calculation, simulation and numerical continuation presented here, together with the accompanying code, will help readers to explore delayed models of their own.
