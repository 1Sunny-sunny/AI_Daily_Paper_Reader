Tony Chae 1, Kleanthis Avramidis 2, Dimitra Emmanouilidou 3, and Ivan J. Tashev 3

ABSTRACT Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers a promising approach for monitoring the hemodynamic correlates of neural activity in naturalistic settings. However, existing modeling methods for fNIRS are largely confined to small-scale, single-session data with low sampling rates, driven by high heterogeneity in acquisition devices and optode montages. In addition, the complex spatiotemporal structure of fNIRS signals further restricts model scalability and transferability across settings. As a result, it remains unclear whether a single fNIRS encoder can learn representations that are usable across subjects, tasks, devices, and montages. Here, we show that a montage-aware masked autoencoder, pretrained on 12 public fNIRS datasets spanning 268 subjects and 567 recording hours, learns generalizable neurovascular representations that transfer across three external datasets. Our fNIRS foundation model, MtReNIRS, jointly encodes the slow hemodynamic nature of fNIRS, chromophore identity and cortical location by mapping heterogeneous montages onto a shared anatomical reference. MtReNIRS achieves cross-subject performance on par or better than fully supervised baselines and excels in few-shot within-subject calibration, where it outperforms prior state-of-the-art fNIRS models, while remaining robust to sparse montages. Our results indicate that montage-aware self-supervision yields physiologically grounded, transferable fNIRS representations, providing a scalable foundation for label-efficient brain-computer interfaces. 

INDEX TERMS Functional near-infrared spectroscopy, brain-computer interfaces, brain foundation models, montage harmonization, self-supervised learning. 

I. Introduction 

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a neu-roimaging technology that measures cortical changes in oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR). Its non-invasive and portable sensors offer a promising way to expand brain-computer interfaces outside the lab and in everyday scenarios. Compared to electroencephalography (EEG), the fNIRS optical sensors can provide a complemen-tary view of brain function with higher spatial resolution, while being more robust to myoelectric artifacts [1]–[3]. Collecting and annotating fNIRS data is costly and time-consuming, and usually requires domain expertise. Further, the scalability of computational modeling methods is often hindered by the necessary subject-specific calibration, which makes self-supervised foundation models particularly attrac-tive. Prior work using large-scale pretraining on neural data has shown improved performance, generalization, and label efficiency, though existing pretrained models have predomi-nantly targeted EEG [4]–[6] and fMRI [7], [8] modalities. Comparable evidence for modeling fNIRS signals remains very limited. Fully supervised Transformer-based classifiers capture temporal dependencies within individual datasets [9], [10], while early fNIRS foundation-model studies have fo-cused mainly on within-subject few-shot classification [3], [11]. It therefore remains unclear whether a single pretrained fNIRS encoder can learn representations that transfer across subjects, tasks, acquisition devices, and sensor montages. Acquisition devices, sampling rates, signal representations, source–detector (S–D) geometries, and cor-tical coverage vary substantially, while individual datasets remain relatively small. A model trained on a single study may therefore learn study-specific acquisition characteristics rather than reusable neurovascular structure. Scaling fNIRS requires more than pooling unlabeled recordings. Spatial channels across different montages must be aligned to a common anatomical reference so that each fNIRS signal can be consistently related to the underlying cortical activity. The pretraining objective should also reflect fNIRS physiology. HbO/HbR signals are slow, delayed, and highly autocorrelated, which differ fundamentally from the fast electrophysiology that many brain-signal encoders were designed for [14]. To our knowledge, prior fNIRS pretraining models have not jointly combined montage-aware scale, physiology-matched mask reconstruction, and cross-subject evaluation. This motivates our central question: Can we learn transferable neurovascular representations at scale? 

We introduce MtReNIRS (Montage-aligned transferable 

Re presentations for f NIRS ), a foundation model that learns neurovascular representations with a montage-aware masked autoencoder. To promote generalizable representations, we train on 12 public datasets selected to maximize task, device, and subject diversity, spanning 268 subjects, 567.1 recording hours, and broad cortical coverage (Fig. 1). We map hetero-geneous S-D midpoint geometries to a shared standard 10– 05 reference [15] and combine each 10-s HbO/HbR segment with its canonical coordinate and chromophore identity. The model then reconstructs randomly masked windows without knowledge of task labels. Transferability is evaluated on three unseen datasets of cognitive, auditory, and motor tasks. Our main contributions are: (1) We develop a montage harmonization framework that maps heterogeneous fNIRS recordings to a shared anatomical representation, enabling joint pretraining across datasets with different sensor layouts. The frame-work supports 3-D coordinate alignment, anatomically anchored 2-D placement, and direct label assignment depending on the available montage information. (2) We introduce a compact encoder of 1.23 M parameters that jointly represents fNIRS signal dynamics, anatom-ical location, and chromophore identity to learn physi-ologically grounded and transferable representations. (3) We systematically evaluate knowledge transfer on three unseen datasets through cross-subject leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) classification and within-subject calibration using only 2–8 labels per class. MtReNIRS outperformed most existing fNIRS pretraining meth-ods [3], [11], [16], [17] and fully supervised ap-proaches [9]. Finally, we additionally examine the effects of token du-ration, spatial correspondence, pretraining duration, and ro-bustness to reduced-channel settings. 

II. Related Work 

Supervised fNIRS models range from classifiers built on summary statistics to convolutional, recurrent, and attention-based networks [1], [10]. Wang et al. [9] introduced fNIRS-T for supervised multichannel hemodynamic decoding, while Lee et al. [18] extended this framework with optode-layout-aware spatial embeddings. However, both operate within fixed, dataset-specific input spaces, limiting transfer across devices and montages. Our approach preserves S-D pair identity while accommodating variable sensor counts and arrangements. BERT-style masked prediction in language and ViT-based masked autoencoding in computer vision established the objective of reconstructing hidden inputs from visible con-text [19], [20]. This principle has since yielded useful repre-sentations for sleep, multimodal physiology, and fNIRS [3], [11], [16], [17]. Related EEG-BCI studies have also explored cross-subject decoding and generalization across datasets

> III. Datasets and Methods
> A. Pretraining Datasets

We assembled 12 public fNIRS datasets that varied in device, task, cortical coverage, sampling rate, measurement density, and signal representation [23]–[34], as described in Fig. 1. The datasets include tasks with cognitive, motor, auditory, affective, clinical, and naturalistic recordings from prefrontal, frontal, temporal, central, sensorimotor, parietal, occipital, and whole-head coverage. The overall pretraining dataset contained 268 subject entries, 1,389 runs, and over 567 recording hours total. After quality control, two runs were excluded for poor signal quality. We segmented these runs into 203,420 non-overlapping 10-s windows. We treated each channel as independent inputs regardless of subject identity. A subject-disjoint split within each dataset assigned approx-imately 80% of entries to training and 20% to validation, which yielded 213 training and 53 validation entries in total. Separately, we reserved three additional datasets exclu-sively for downstream evaluation. None of the downstream subjects or recordings were included in either training or validation sets used for pretraining. These datasets were chosen to cover different subject counts and task types, while also considering datasets used in prior literature benchmarks [35], so we could test how well the learned representa-tions can transfer across different settings. Gunther2014 :8–subject corpus containing recordings of mental arithmetic tasks and resting states [35]; Shader2021 : 8–subject corpus containing auditory, visual, and control speech tasks [36]; and SujinBak2019 : 30–subject corpus with left-hand, right-hand, and foot motor trials [37]. Exact event extraction yielded 348, 368, and 2,250 10-s samples, respectively. We used 10-s segments throughout the study to capture the multi-second rise and peak of the local hemodynamic response while preserving finer temporal structure. We lim-ited individual windows to 10-s because longer windows could extend beyond the labeled event boundaries in the downstream datasets and potentially mix adjacent conditions. 

> B. Signal Pre-processing and Montage Harmonization

As shown in Fig. 2A, we applied a dataset-adapted pre-processing pipeline to recordings, provided either as raw intensity, optical density, or HbO/HbR concentrations. We retained only valid S-D pairs that were separated by 8– 35 mm, when this information was available in the raw files. We then converted raw continuous-wave intensity to optical density, marked wavelength channels with a scalp-coupling index below 0.5, applied temporal derivative distribution repair, and used the modified Beer–Lambert law with a partial pathlength factor of 6.0. Runs were rejected only when no channel reached scalp-coupling index of 0.5. For recordings provided as raw intensity, we applied the full preprocessing pipeline; for those only provided with processed HbO/HbR concentrations, we skipped the preceding conversion steps. We then band-pass filtered each run from 0.01 to 0.5 Hz, resampled to 10 Hz, and removed flat or abnormally low-variance chromophore channels. This branching preserved each dataset’s available signal informa-tion while producing a common HbO/HbR representation. We z-scored each retained chromophore channel over its complete run using the population mean and standard devi-ation, then clipped model inputs to [−6, 6] . Pretraining win-dows did not overlap, and downstream examples used exactly 

[onset , onset + 10 s) without a hemodynamic-response shift. Montage harmonization mapped each physical S-D pair to a shared canonical 10–05 slot (Fig. 2B; Fig. 3). D. Downstream Evaluation Protocols 

Our evaluation comprised four adaptation strategies for pre-trained MtReNIRS and four fully supervised baselines (no pretraining). MtReNIRS-LP was used as the default setting when comparing with prior work, unless otherwise stated. The adaptation strategies were: (a) MtReNIRS-LP : the pretrained encoder was kept frozen, and only a linear classification head was trained; (b) MtReNIRS-MLP : the pretrained encoder was kept frozen, and an MLP classi-fication head with one 64-unit hidden layer and GELU activation was trained; (c) MtReNIRS-LoRA : low-rank adaptation of the pretrained encoder using rank-8 updates with α = 16 in the attention and feed-forward linear layers; (d) MtReNIRS-Full-FT : the pretrained encoder and clas-sification head were jointly fine-tuned. The non-pretrained baselines were: (e) Scratch-FT : the same MtReNIRS archi-tecture was trained from random initialization without pre-training; (f) CNN : 1-D, two-layers, with 64 filters ( K = 5 ), global average pooling, and a linear head, trained on prepro-cessed HbO/HbR signals ; (g) LSTM : bidirectional one-layer with 64 hidden units per direction, temporal mean pooling, and a linear head, trained on preprocessed HbO/HbR signals; (h) Stat-SVM : an RBF SVM ( C = 1 ) using eight temporal statistics per chromophore channel as input features. E. Metrics and Statistical Analysis 

We used macro-F1 as the primary metric and treated each subject as the independent unit for statistical analysis. To help readers compare our findings with prior work, Table 3 additionally reports classification accuracy. For LOSO, we calculated each held-out subject’s score and reported the mean and population standard deviation across subjects. For few-shot evaluation, we first averaged the 50 seed-level scores within each subject and then summarized those subject means. Thus, repeated few-shot draws from the same subject were not treated as independent samples. For paired model comparisons, we calculated the macro-F1 difference between the two models separately for each subject. We formed 95% percentile confidence intervals for the mean difference by resampling subjects with replacement 20,000 times, and applied two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to the subject-level paired differences. Reported p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Since a generalizable model should capture anatomical cor-respondence across different pretraining tasks, we tested whether the model learned this correspondence from the po-sitional encodings seen during pretraining. > D. Robustness to Reduced Sensor Coverage

Sparse-sensor robustness is desirable for practical wearable BCIs, where fewer sensors improve portability and comfort while simplifying setup [41]. However, reducing sensor count can make decoding more sensitive to noise and poor channel quality. We therefore evaluated MtReNIRS-LP under reduced sensor coverage on SujinBak. Its symmetric sensori-motor montage minimizes regional confounds when sensors are removed. The reference montage contained 18 S-D pairs arranged into nine bilateral region groups. For each sparse setting tested (2, 4, 8, 12, or 16 S-D pairs), we generated nine balanced subsets in which each group appeared equally often. Performance was averaged over subjects. As shown in Fig. 6, MtReNIRS-LP had the highest median macro-F1 at every retained S-D pair count. Its median increased from 0.413 with two pairs to 0.683 with 16 pairs, compared with 0.689 for the symmetric 18-pair reference and 0.695 for the full 20-pair montage. At 16 pairs, Scratch-FT and Stat-SVM reached 0.664 and 0.658, respectively. These results suggest that the pretrained representation remains robust under substantial reductions in sensor coverage, ap-proaching full-montage performance with 16 retained S-D pairs. However, this analysis was performed on a single motor dataset and does not establish that the same robustness profile generalizes to other cortical regions or tasks. 

> VI. Discussion

Pretraining Benefits Personalized Adaptation More Than Zero-Calibration Transfer : Across datasets, the largest and most consistent gains emerged in the within-subject few-shot setting rather than pure cross-subject transfer. This suggests that the pretrained encoder learns neurovascular features that generalize across tasks and acquisition settings, while subject-specific physiological variability remains a dominant source of error. Consequently, a small number of subject-specific labels appears sufficient to adapt a generic representation to a new user. 

Anatomical Alignment Is Important For Learning Transferable Representations : The consistent degradation observed after coordinate shuffling suggests that transferabil-ity is derived not only from waveform structure but also from preserving anatomical correspondence across heterogeneous montages during pretraining. 

Limitations and Future Work : Several limitations should be considered in this work. Temporal windowing limita-tion : fNIRS measures a delayed and temporally extended hemodynamic response rather than neural activity directly, making representation quality dependent on the duration and timing of the analysis window. Although the 10-s configuration performed best in our experiments, different tasks and subjects may exhibit different response latencies, and future work should disentangle the effects of win-dow duration from representation learning itself. Limited downstream datasets : while pretraining was performed at relatively large scale for fNIRS, downstream evaluation was limited to the three held-out datasets used in prior work and may not fully characterize generalization across the broader range of tasks, acquisition settings, and experimental paradigms encountered in practice. Cross-device transfer not isolated : Although pretraining successfully integrated recordings across multiple devices and montages, down-stream evaluation could not isolate device effects because device, task, and montage differences co-occurred across datasets. Thus, the device invariance of the learned repre-sentations remains unclear. Cross-subject variability : cross-subject transfer remains intrinsically challenging because anatomical variability, vascular dynamics, and sensor place-ment differ across individuals. Future work should evaluate larger and more diverse benchmarks, including controlled cross-device studies that vary acquisition hardware while holding other factors fixed. Multimodal pretraining : Recent EEG–fNIRS work suggests that cross-modal pretraining can improve fNIRS-only downstream performance [3]. Future work could therefore test fusion strategies of complementary neurophysiological signals and explore the transferability and robustness of such multimodal frameworks. 

> VII. Conclusion

We introduced MtReNIRS, a montage-aware foundation model for fNIRS pretrained across heterogeneous datasets spanning subjects, devices, tasks, and sensor layouts. The learned representations transferred to unseen datasets, with the largest benefits in few-shot subject adaptation. Together, the transfer and ablation results suggest that anatomically aligned self-supervision can serve as a scalable mechanism for learning reusable neurovascular representations, enabling future label-efficient fNIRS and multimodal BCI systems.
