Abstract Predictive Coding (PC) is an influential account of how brain representations emerge through hierarchical, local, self-supervised prediction-based learning, without requiring globally propagated error signals. Despite extensive evidence supporting PC, a striking paradox persists: computational models built on these biologically grounded principles have consistently failed to match state-of-the-art Deep Neural Networks in performance and brain-alignment. Here, we show that this paradox can be overcome. We introduce Predictive Brain Networks (PBNs), a hierarchical recurrent framework that implements local PC-based learning and achieves representational alignment with the human ventral visual stream matching or exceeding established vision models. Importantly, PBN top-down predictions encode unique, brain-aligned information not captured during bottom-up feature extraction, with this contribution increasing in locally trained variants. Further, PBNs substantially outperform existing PC-inspired networks on downstream classification tasks, reproduce key signatures of PC (with higher-level predictions shaping lower-level visual responses as observed in the primate cortex), and support uncertainty estimation. PBNs therefore close a longstanding gap in computational neuroscience by combining two properties that have been largely separated: the brain-inspired learning mechanisms of predictive coding and the representational power of modern deep networks. They provide a scalable framework for studying biologically grounded learning without sacrificing the capacity to produce rich neural representations. 

# Introduction 

The brain learns remarkably rich representations from sensory experience. Explaining how such powerful learning can emerge from largely unsupervised, biologically plausible local computations is a central challenge in neuroscience. The Bayesian Brain family of theories offers one influential account, with Predictive Coding (PC) as a special case proposing that learning is driven by hierar-chical, locally computed prediction errors. This account has been highly successful in explaining a wide range of perceptual and cognitive phenomena ( Rao and Ballard, 1999 ; Summerfield et al., 2008 ; Hohwy et al., 2008 ; Garrido et al., 2009 ; Feldman and Friston, 2010 ; Auksztulewicz and Fris-ton, 2016 ; Walsh et al., 2020 ; Feuerriegel et al., 2021 ; Aizenbud et al., 2025 ). While a complete computational formalization of PC remains a matter of debate ( Shipp, 2016 ;

Millidge et al., 2024 ), a general consensus can be identified regarding requirements a given sys-tem must fulfil to qualify as implementing of PC (from here on “PC-compliant”). Requirement 1 :

1 of 29 arXiv:2610.37327v1 [q-bio.NC] 27 Oct 2026 the system is organized hierarchically, with each level repeating the same computational task at a different level of abstraction ( Sprevak, 2024 ; Walsh et al., 2020 ; Millidge et al., 2021 ). Require-ment 2 : part of the system performs the role of a generative model, most commonly implemented as a top-down process that predicts incoming bottom-up information ( Spratling, 2017 ; Sprevak, 2024 ; Walsh et al., 2020 ; Millidge et al., 2021 ). Requirement 3 : learning is driven based on the mismatch (prediction error) between this top-down prediction and the bottom-up input ( Spratling, 2017 ; Sprevak, 2024 ; Walsh et al., 2020 ; Millidge et al., 2021 ). Requirement 4 :, the predictions and errors are represented in functionally, anatomically, or structurally distinct neuronal populations (Spratling, 2017 ; Sprevak, 2024 ; Walsh et al., 2020 ; Millidge et al., 2021 ). Requirement 5 : learn-ing is implemented through spatially local mechanisms that update synaptic connection weights (Spratling, 2017 ; Sprevak, 2024 ; Millidge et al., 2021 ). Requirement 6 : the predictions created are of probabilistic, or bayesian nature ( Spratling, 2017 ; Sprevak, 2024 ; Millidge et al., 2021 ). Beyond these mechanistic requirements, successful learning is generally expected to transform high-dimensional sensory inputs into compact, abstract representations that capture underlying structure ( Edelman and Intrator, 1997 ; Gibson and Gibson, 1955 ; Wolff, 2019 ). Accordingly, a suc-cessful PC-compliant system should implement the above computational requirements to learn such abstract representations of sensory information, as is also observed in the brain ( Edelman and Intrator, 1997 ; Gibson and Gibson, 1955 ; Wolff, 2019 ). In recent years, an alternative approach for computational neuroscience has gained traction: Deep Neural Network (DNN) models are used to formalize neuroscientific hypotheses through their architectures, objectives, training data, and learning rules, and the resulting activations and behavior are compared to those observed in the brain (an approach referred to as neuroconnec-tionism ) ( Doerig et al., 2023 ; Golan et al., 2023 ; Cichy and Kaiser, 2019 ). Through this approach, established DNNs originally created for engineering goals and only loosely inspired by biology ( He et al., 2016 ; Liu et al., 2022 ; Dosovitskiy, 2020 ) have proven to learn rich and generally applicable representations which provide some of the strongest predictors of brain activity in vision ( Cichy et al., 2016 ; Kietzmann et al., 2019 ; Güçlü and Van Gerven, 2015 ; Khaligh-Razavi and Kriegeskorte, 2014 ; Doerig et al., 2025 ), audition ( Kell et al., 2018 ; Saddler et al., 2021 ), language ( Caucheteux and King, 2022 ; Schrimpf et al., 2021 ), and more ( Schrimpf et al., 2018 ; Conwell et al., 2022 ). The computationally well-defined and empirically supported PC account of perception and learning in neural systems should be a natural fit for neuroconnectionism. If PC captures im-portant brain computations, then integrating the relevant computational characteristics into PC-compliant DNNs should lead to powerful brain models. However, a striking paradox has persisted: PC-compliant computational models have so far drastically failed to produce rich brain-like rep-resentations on par with those of established DNN ( Pinchetti et al., 2025 ; Biscione and Bowers, 2023 ). This disadvantage of PC-compliant DNNs is not for lack of effort. Numerous projects have set out to instantiate the PC theoretical framework in computational models ( Lotter et al., 2016 ; Jiang and Rao, 2024 ; Whittington and Bogacz, 2017 ; Wen et al., 2018 ; Millidge et al., 2024 ; Innocenti et al., 2026 ; Kerjan et al., 2026 ). So far, none of these approaches have succeeded in strictly ad-hering to the principles of PC while producing representations that compete with those of other established DNN architectures in terms of brain alignment and ability to perform downstream tasks ( Pinchetti et al., 2025 ; Biscione and Bowers, 2023 ). A prominent example is PredNet ( Lotter et al., 2016 ), a highly influential system that sparked interest in PC-inspired DNNs. It successfully implemented several of our stated requirements (1, 2, 3, and 4) in an end-to-end trainable neural network and performed only slightly below other unsupervised DNNs of the time on next frame prediction (38.9% peak-signal-to-toise-ratio on ( Ionescu et al., 2013 ) compared to 42.1% for ( Finn et al., 2016 ); see ( Lotter et al., 2016 )). However, it has substantially lower brain-alignment than other DNN architectures ( Zhuang et al., 2021 ), lacks local error minimization (requirement 5) ( Rane et al., 2020 ), in its original implementation does not produce bayesian or probabilistic predictions (requirement 6) ( Lotter et al., 2016 ), and fails to replicate experimental hallmarks of PC ( Biscione

First it restricts the ap-plicability of PC-inspired computational models to simple settings rather than the more complex naturalistic-input settings where modern DNNs thrive ( Deco and Schürmann, 2001 ; Spratling, 2010 ;

Auksztulewicz and Friston, 2015 ; Pinotsis et al., 2017 ; Pouget et al., 2013 ; Rane et al., 2020 ), Second, it leaves PC without a compelling computational demonstration that its proposed mechanism is sufficient for learning rich, brain-aligned representations. This leads to an apparent paradox: how can it be that a theory of brain computations as developed, influential, and empirically substanti-ated as PC does not lead to competitive models to learn rich and brain-aligned computations? Here, we show that this paradox can be overcome. We introduce Predictive Brain Networks (PBNs), a general framework for constructing fully PC-compliant models that combine principles of PC with the representational power of modern DNNs. First, we show that PBNs far surpass previous state-of-the-art PC-inspired DNNs by achieving representational alignment with the hu-man visual hierarchy drastically better than PredNet ( Lotter et al., 2016 ) and on par or better than ResNet-50 ( He et al., 2016 ) and ConvNeXt ( Liu et al., 2022 ), closing the decade-long gap in brain alignment left open by PC-inspired models. Second, the brain alignment of PBNs reflects specif-ically predictive computations: top-down predictive decoder representations explain neural vari-ance beyond bottom-up encoders, with this contribution increasing as learning becomes more local. Third, PBNs replicate key empirical and theoretical signatures of PC: top-down predictions observed in macaque face processing ( Schwiedrzik and Freiwald, 2017 ). Additionally, we show that the representations learnt by PBNs are sufficiently rich to successfully perform downstream tasks and that PBNs are capable of modeling uncertainty using ( Gal and Ghahramani, 2016 ).

# Results 

General architecture 

To construct PC-compliant DNNs that satisfy requirements 1-6 listed earlier, we developed a flex-ible framework that we refer to as predictive brain networks (PBNs). PBNs can be described as a hierarchy of recurrent autoencoders. At each hierarchical level, an encoder maps inputs into latent representations and a decoder uses these representations to predict future inputs (for an overview, see Figure 1). These autoencoders are stacked such that each higher-level unit encodes the latent representation of the level below, while skip connections allow information exchange across the hierarchy. This hierarchical organization enables each level to be trained using a local loss that compares its prediction with its input, while restricting gradient flow between levels can be used to enforce local learning while still allowing the formation of high-level abstract represen-tations (for details, see Methods, and Figure 1). This architecture addresses the key computational requirements of PC. Its hierarchical organiza-tion consists of repeating computational blocks (requirement 1) each implementing a generative model (requirement 2). Learning is driven by prediction errors between bottom-up inputs and top-down predictions at each hierarchical level (given a PC-compliant loss; requirement 3), with these signals represented in distinct populations (requirement 4). Gradients used for learning can be restricted to a given hierarchical level to enforce strictly local learning (requirement 5). The probabilistic nature of predictions (requirement 6) can be addressed by combining the loss with dropout and weight decay to implement Monte Carlo dropout as an approximate Bayesian infer-ence method ( Gal and Ghahramani, 2016 ) (see Methods). Together, these components enable the model to learn rich, brain-aligned representations across hierarchical levels. The PBN framework is designed to maximize flexibility: it can be used for different data do-mains and network architectures, and both loss functions and gradient flow can be configured flexibly. This enables a broad range of models to be implemented within a common framework, provided they retain the core structure of a hierarchical, autoregressive autoencoder. Our aim is to facilitate the development and comparison of alternative PBN variants, allowing researchers to systematically implement and test competing neuroscientific hypotheses about PC, learning, and perception. 

Model variants 

We trained PBNs on naturalistic video sequences ( Monfort et al., 2019 ; Gifford et al., 2024 ; Dave et al., 2020 ) and compared them with established vision models used in neuroconnectionist re-search. We focused on vision because it represents the largest and most established modality, with widely used models and datasets. To investigate how PC-specific learning constraints shape learned representations, we constructed six PBN variants that share the same underlying architec-ture while varying PC-related components (see Table 1, Figure 6). Our principal variant, PBN local gradients , satisfies all six PC requirements outlined above, while producing rich, brain-aligned representations. It uses a Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) Oord et al. (2018 ) loss at each hier-archical level, and purely local learning (by using blocked gradient flow between hierarchy levels). As our first group of control conditions, we constructed three model variants that contrast our PBN local gradients variant with less PC-compliant variants. PBN untrained shares the same ar-chitecture but receives no training, providing an architectural baseline for representations in the absence of learning. PBN backprop uses standard backpropagation ( Rumelhart et al., 1986 ), with a CPC loss at the highest level and a reconstruction loss at the lowest level (equivalent to a U-Net autoencoder ( Ronneberger et al., 2015 ), with a contrastive loss at the bottleneck and a reconstruc-tive objective), providing a baseline for conventional end-to-end learning with a single high-level contrastive objective. PBN backprop + local loss applies local CPC losses at each hierarchy level but allows gradients to flow between levels. To compare these PBN variants with established DNN models, we included ResNet-50 (He et al., 2016 ) and ConvNeXt-tiny (Liu et al., 2022 ), two standard vision models trained under supervised conditions on ImageNet and selected among the top-performing models on Brain-Score ( Schrimpf et al., 2018 ), a standard benchmark for computer models of human visual neurophysiological data. We additionally included PredNet (Lotter et al., 2016 ), the currently most cited PC network (see In-troduction). Representational similarity to the brain 

We first evaluated how closely PBN representations matched human visual representations and compared them with established vision DNNs. We compared model representations with human responses to the 515 images shared across participants in the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) ( Allen et al., 2022 ), a large-scale fMRI dataset of human vision, using reweighted ( Storrs et al., 2021 ) and noise-ceiling-corrected representational similarity analysis (RSA) ( Kriegeskorte et al., 2008a ) across seven visual regions (see Figure 2, for pairwise comparisons see Figure 7). Our principal model, PBN local gradients, significantly outperformed established DNNs in 7 of 14 region-by-model com-parisons (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 𝑝 FDR < 0.01 ). The two less PC-compliant controls, PBN back-prop and PBN backprop + local loss, also showed strong representational similarity to human vi-sual responses. Across their 28 comparisons with established DNNs, these variants significantly outperformed the DNNs in 16 (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 𝑝 FDR < 0.05 ) comparisons and were significantly outperformed in two (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 𝑝 FDR < 0.05 ). In contrast, the two

Across their 28 com-parisons, established DNNs significantly outperformed these variants in 21 (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 𝑝 FDR < 0.05 ) comparisons, whereas the subtractive PBN variants significantly outperformed the established DNNs in 2 (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 𝑝 FDR < 0.01 ), with subtractive PBN vari-ants performing well in early visual cortex, but drastically dropping in higher areas. All five trained PBN variants significantly outperformed PredNet across all seven visual regions, comprising all 35 model-by-region comparisons (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 𝑝 FDR < 0.05 ). This result holds for the principal PC-compliant condition and the two less PC-compliant controls. The two prior-subtraction variants, while still substantially outperforming PredNet across all seven areas, show lower representational similarity than established DNNs, which suggests that explicit prior subtraction may limit brain-representational similarity. As a confirmatory analysis, we additionally implemented an alternative approach that instead of reweighted RSA uses the model layer repre-sentation with the highest correlation to the corresponding brain area. This analysis is reported in Figure 8 and yielded qualitatively similar results.

PBN decoders capture unique information

This distinction is important because the decoder captures additional brain-response variance through the predictive, recurrent and top-down processing central to PC, which conventional feedforward models cannot capture. PBN backprop did not exceed PBN local gradients in any region. This pattern is consistent with an increase in unique decoder information as PC constraints are strengthened, a trend also observed across the other CPC-based PBN variants (see Figure 9). The non-CPC-based PBN classic PC variant was the ex-ception: although it did not reach the unique decoder variance of the CPC-based models, it still explained significantly more unique variance than the PBN untrained baseline in the early, mid-ventral, midlateral, midparietal and ventral regions (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 𝑝 FDR < 0.022 ), but not in the lateral or parietal regions. Together, these results indicate that CPC-based PBN variants learn top-down decoder representations that explain brain variance beyond their bottom-up en-coder representations, consistent with Bayesian brain theories. The contribution is strongest in the more PC-compliant variants and weaker in the PBN classic PC model. Importantly, PBN local gradi-ents not only matches established DNNs in brain alignment but also captures unique brain-related variance through its decoder, highlighting the value of its PC-compliant architecture.

Higher-level predictions reshape expectations in lower layers in brains and PBNs 

Having established that the PBN local gradients model learns rich, brain-aligned representations while remaining fully PC-compliant, we now tested whether this PBN variant can model neuro-scientific hallmarks of PC that are not captured by feedforward DNNs (Figure 4). PC entails that predictions from higher-level representations can shape the information in lower-level areas. In face processing, lower-level areas are more sensitive to face view, whereas higher-level areas rep-resent identity across different views. Schwiedrzik and Freiwald (2017 ) tested in macaque mon-keys whether learned predictions allow these higher-level representations to shape lower-level prediction errors. After macaques learned sequences of face pairs, the researchers changed the preceding face to violate the learned association in identity (a different person), view (the same person from a different angle), or both, while keeping the subsequent face unchanged. They com-pared early neural responses with late responses to test whether prediction errors changed over time as recurrent feedback could influence processing. In the lower view-specific area, view viola-tions elicited prediction errors early on, but these errors diminished in the late response window, whereas identity-violation errors persisted. This pattern indicates that lower-level prediction errors increasingly reflect the identity-specific, view-invariant properties of higher-level representations, consistent with top-down predictions shaping lower-level processing. To test whether PBNs reproduce this temporal signature, we fine-tuned the PBN local gradients variant to predict face sequences from ( Thomaz and Giraldi, 2010 ), reproducing the experimental

Another hallmark of Bayesian brain and PC theories, is to provide a way to represent uncertainty about its predictions. One common approach in probabilistic PC models is variational inference, in which uncertainty is represented by an approximate posterior distribution over latent variables. For example, variational autoencoders parameterize the posterior over each latent variable using a predefined distribution, typically a unimodal Gaussian ( Kingma and Welling, 2013 ). This makes uncertainty explicit, but also constrains the form that the latent posterior can take. An alternative is to approximate Bayesian inference through uncertainty over the network’s weights rather than by explicitly specifying a parametric distribution over latent representations. Gal and Ghahramani 

(2016 ) mathematically proved that any network trained with dropout and weight decay under a standard loss can be interpreted as performing approximate variational inference over network weights. In line with this approach, we estimate prediction uncertainty in PBNs using Monte Carlo dropout ( Gal and Ghahramani, 2016 ). For a given input, we perform multiple forward passes with dropout enabled, producing a set of stochastic predictions. The distribution of these predictions provides an estimate of the model’s predictive uncertainty ( Gal and Ghahramani, 2016 ). This pro-cedure has a natural analogue in sampling-based theories of neural computation, which propose that stochastic neural activity represents samples from an underlying posterior distribution over possible interpretations of the sensory input ( Barber et al., 2003 ; Ma et al., 2006 ; Orbán et al., 2016 ), and can therefore be seen as a more natural analogy than strictly parametrizing variational methods. To demonstrate PBN capability of uncertainty quantification, we devised a visual Markov-chain experiment in which four possible states were represented by white corners in an otherwise black input frame, with transitions between states governed by a predefined Markov transition matrix (see Figure 5, top). For example, when the top-left corner (A) was active in the preceding frame, the probabilities of the top-left (A), top-right (B), bottom-left (C), and bottom-right (D) corners being active in the next frame were 0%, 50%, 50%, and 0%, respectively (the complete transition struc-ture is shown in Figure 5, bottom left). We fine-tuned the PBN local gradients model on these sequences for 100 epochs (for details, see Methods) and evaluated it on a separate test set. After positively confirming that the model is capable of doing next-frame prediction (reported in Figure 10), we investigated whether the model is capable of learning the inherent rules of a markov chain and of correctly representing its inherent uncertainty. To do so, we first evaluated how accurately the model recovered the transition structure by measuring the predicted activity for each of the four possible states. During training, the predicted transition structure converged from an initially near-random configuration towards the transition matrix used to generate the stimuli, resulting in a significant difference between the first and final epochs (Wilcoxon 𝑊 = 135 , 𝑝 < 10 −4 ; see Figure 5, center). We next tested whether the stochasticity introduced by dropout captured the uncer-tainty inherent in the learned transition structure. While the model under deterministic conditions logically produced no variance over different entropy levels, the average variance across Monte Carlo dropout predictions increased with the entropy of the true transition distribution (see Figure 5, bottom right). This demonstrates that Dropout Monte Carlo after ( Gal and Ghahramani, 2016 )can be used to quantify uncertainty in PBN networks.

Implications for PC models 

Our results establish PBNs as a PC-compliant framework capable of learning brain aligned ab-stract representations that generalize to downstream tasks and have representational properties that conventional feedforward networks do not offer, including distinct brain-aligned top-down de-coder representations, reproducing hallmarks of PC in biological brains ( Schwiedrzik and Freiwald, 2017 ), and being capable of quantifying uncertainty. Together, these findings provide evidence that systems exclusively built around PC can support brain-aligned representations and computations extending to high levels of abstraction. More broadly, PBNs provide a flexible unified framework in which PC models can be constructed, systematically constrained, and compared under controlled conditions. By training parameter-matched model variants that progressively impose different PC principles, the framework enables individual components of PC theory to be isolated and evaluated against one another. This pro-vides neuroconnectionism with a framework for testing PC hypotheses at a level of representa-tional complexity previously difficult to access. 

Prior subtraction and other operations 

Previous work has suggested that the explicit subtraction operation between bottom up informa-tion and top-down predictions assumed by classic PC may be fundamentally problematic for hi-erarchical representation learning ( Rao et al., 2023 ; Lowet and Uchida, 2024 ). Strict subtraction removes information that is explained away by top-down predictions at lower levels or earlier time points, implying that information about predictable stimuli should progressively disappear from deeper or later representations. This assumption is difficult to reconcile with evidence that highly predictable stimuli can nevertheless elicit robust, increasingly abstract representations throughout the ventral visual stream ( Grill-Spector and Weiner, 2014 ; Hong et al., 2016 ; Kriegeskorte et al., 2008b ; Isik et al., 2014 ). Our model comparisons support this concern: PBN variants using sub-traction generally performed worse than subtraction-free variants in all our comparisons. These results provide evidence that the subtraction operation of classic PC can impair the propagation of information required for hierarchical abstraction. These findings are consistent with propos-als from PC literature that the interaction between top-down predictions and bottom-up signals may instead rely on mechanisms such as precision weighting, gating, or scaling, ( Feldman and Friston, 2010 ; Bastos et al., 2012 ; Gilbert and Li, 2013 ; De Meyer and Spratling, 2011 ; Kanai et al., 2015 ). The range of proposed mechanisms reflects an ongoing debate about how top-down pre-dictions interact with bottom-up signals in cortical computation. Determining which mechanisms best capture these interactions is an important direction for future work. PBNs provide a flexible framework in which these alternatives can be easily implemented and compared under controlled and naturalistic conditions. 

Contrasting versus Predicting 

A specific feature of most PBN variants considered here is the use of contrastive predictive coding (CPC) ( Oord et al., 2018 ), which requires negative samples and thereby introduces dependencies between stimulus representations across time. This makes learning temporally non-local. Oord et al. (2018 ) describe several variants of the CPC objective that contrast the true future sample with negative samples drawn from different temporal distances, thereby increasing or decreasing the temporal non-locality. One possible way to reconcile this dependency with biological constraints is to assume that temporally distant representations can be retained in memory and accessed locally during learning. An alternative approach that omits temporal non-locality altogether is to learn representations using purely predictive or invariance-based objectives rather than contrasting against other sam-ples, as used here in the PBN classic PC variant. Such objectives are theoretically capable of learn-ing useful representations without explicit negative samples ( Chen and He, 2021 ; Assran et al., 

> 13 of 29

2025 ) and may produce more brain-like representations and phenomenological correspondences (Gütlin and Auksztulewicz, 2025 ; Gütlin et al., 2026 ). However, our current results do not support the scalability of this approach in the PBN framework. Methods such as SimSiam ( Chen and He, 2021 ), BYOL ( Grill et al., 2020 ), VICReg ( Bardes et al., 2021 ), or V-JEPA ( Bardes et al., 2024 ; Assran et al., 2025 ) provide possible approaches to solve this issue, but their suitability in hierarchical lo-cal learning contexts remains unresolved. Investigating PBN networks with different losses and comparing their performance to contrastive PC losses therefore represents a promising direction for future work. 

Insights for Deep Learning 

While the feasibility of local learning in DNNs has been investigated previously ( Siddiqui et al., 2023 ), we show that it extends to brain-like, rich hierarchical representations. This finding is striking be-cause backpropagation provides the linearly optimal weight update in gradient descent, so any departure from backpropagation is sub-optimal in this sense. Optimization becomes harder in lo-cal learning where gradients do not flow across hierarchical levels, because there is no mechanism ensuring that weight updates at one hierarchical level will also benefit other levels. The fact that despite this additional constraint, locally restricted PC-inspired models are capable of learning rich representations, opens up new possibilities for investigating new types of learning systems. For example, local PC-based systems might have potential advantages, such as more resilience against catastrophic forgetting ( Lee et al., 2022 ), faster convergence (consistent with our observations dur-ing training), or potentially more biologically aligned learning trajectories, although this remains to be investigated systematically. This can be addressed in future work, facilitated by the framework introduced here. 

# Conclusion 

In this work, we introduced Predictive Brain Networks (PBNs), a framework that combines the bi-ological principles of predictive coding with the representational power of deep neural networks. We showed that PBNs learn rich, brain-aligned representations while capturing key signatures of predictive coding. Beyond providing a computational demonstration of the power of predictive coding, PBNs offer a flexible framework for systematically testing hypotheses about learning and information processing in the brain. 

# Methods 

Model architecture 

The PBN framework is a hierarchical recurrent architecture composed of stacked autoencoders, with the overall architecture illustrated in Figure 1. Each hierarchy level is implemented as an independent autoencoder whose input is the compressed latent representation of the level be-low. Stacking these autoencoders forms a connected hierarchy of encoders and decoders that is trained as a single system, enabling progressively higher levels to learn increasingly abstract rep-resentations while retaining the local learning structure of each level. PBNs combines elements from autoencoders ( Kingma and Welling, 2013 ; Ronneberger et al., 2015 ; Sønderby et al., 2016 ;

Chung et al., 2015 ), contrastive learning ( Oord et al., 2018 ; Hinton, 2022 ; Chen et al., 2020 ), PC networks ( Lotter et al., 2016 ; Whittington and Bogacz, 2017 ; Millidge et al., 2024 ), teacher-student networks ( Grill et al., 2020 ; Chen and He, 2021 ), localized learning ( Siddiqui et al., 2023 ), and self-supervised sequence learning models ( Bardes et al., 2024 ; Assran et al., 2025 ; Tong et al., 2022 ;

Zbontar et al., 2021 ). Each hierarchy level contains two skip connections. The posterior connec-tion is a forward encoder-to-decoder connection analogous to the skip connection in a U-Net and provides the information required to form the posterior. The prior connection transmits the pre-vious top-down decoder state to the encoder, where it is combined with the new bottom-up input 

> 14 of 29

to incorporate prior information. The prior connection makes the architecture inherently recur-rent: the decoder state at each hierarchy level is retained and used as the prior for the subsequent encoder input. Because the decoder predicts the subsequent element in the sequence, this recur-rence also makes PBNs inherently autoregressive. The basic recurrent state is necessarily tied to the decoder output. PBNs therefore optionally include a memory module in the posterior pathway to provide additional recurrent capacity and represent ongoing states that are not constrained to the form of the decoder output. The memory module is not required for the basic architecture. The architecture is modular, with individual components and operations replaceable according to the PC formulation being implemented. In this sense, the framework can represent a broad range of PC and Bayesian brain models while retaining the same hierarchical encoder-decoder structure. 

Loss 

Losses can be placed at any location in the network via loss layers, using the variables available at that location. In a PC setting, the most meaningful location is the prior fill-in layer, where the top-down decoder output from the previous time step can be compared with the bottom-up encoder input at the new time step. This comparison can be formulated either as an approximation ob-jective (using MAE, MSE, or other invariance-based distance metrics) as in typical prediction-based PC approaches, or as a contrastive objective that promotes similarity between matching repre-sentations and separation between mismatched representations, as in CPC ( Oord et al., 2018 ) or Forward-Forward ( Hinton, 2022 ). The posterior fill-in can likewise be used to define approxima-tion or contrastive losses, although such losses are less directly tied to the PC formulation. Other network locations can provide meaningful sites for additional regularization; for example, losses applied at the end of the encoder can impose activity or covariance regularization to enforce PC-like sparsity assumptions or variational-autoencoder-like latent-prior assumptions. This flexibility allows the framework to implement a broad range of PC methods while retaining a common un-derlying architecture. 

Stop gradients 

The general understanding of the brain is not that processes approximating gradient descent and backpropagation ( Rumelhart et al., 1986 ) are generally impossible, but rather that they become progressively less likely the longer the chain of nonlinear operations involved ( Lillicrap et al., 2020 ;

Lillicrap and Santoro, 2019 ; Whittington and Bogacz, 2019 ; Bartunov et al., 2018 ). We do not deny gradient descent or shallow backpropagation by default; rather, we aim to prevent biologically im-plausible gradient transmission through gigantic networks and the reliance on exactly calculated partial derivatives over thousands of nonlinear operations, which we consider beyond the capa-bility of the brain. Accordingly, we use stop gradients to restrict gradient flow to single hierarchy levels. Stop gradients can be placed at any point in the network via stop-gradient layers, though the most meaningful variant is typically to place them between hierarchy levels. Different place-ments nonetheless allow for much more fine-grained control, for instance determining whether gradients are permitted to flow backwards through time within a single hierarchy level. Placement can likewise determine whether a hierarchy’s encoder receives gradients from the immediately higher loss, that is, whether only the top-down prediction is tuned to approximate the bottom-up signal, or whether the bottom-up signal is tuned as well by the immediately adjacent top-down prediction; the latter works well for contrastive losses but can produce trivial solutions in pure in-variance settings. Controlling the placement of stop gradients is thus one of the most important factors in setting the learning conditions of the model. 

Vision PBN implementation 

For the implementation evaluated in this study, we implemented a PBN optimized for visual fea-ture extraction from video time series. We adapted the PBN encoder to approximately match 

> 15 of 29

the stage-wise structure of ConvNeXt, using projection and downsampling layers to obtain corre-sponding spatial resolutions and channel dimensions and matching the number of residual con-volutional blocks at each stage. The encoder comprises four convolutional stages with 96, 192, 384, and 768 channels, with progressively reduced spatial resolution; the first three stages each contained three residual convolutional blocks, while the fourth contained nine blocks arranged in three groups of three (evidence that learning occurred in all of those blocks for PBN local gradients is given in Figure 11). The final convolutional stage is followed by global average pooling and two dense layers. Unlike the standard ConvNeXt implementation, which uses Layer Normalization, we used Batch Normalization, as this provided better performance with the CPC objective. To enable Monte Carlo dropout, we used standard dropout with a rate of 0.1 throughout the residual and dense layers rather than the ConvNeXt-specific branching/drop-path formulation. We designed the decoder to broadly mirror the encoder hierarchy, transforming the latent representation back to the input space using a minimal sequence of dense, upsampling, transposed-convolution, and skip-convolution layers. For all PBN models except the classic PC model, each hierarchy level incor-porates a memory cell (Continuous Gated Cell) that projects the current input into a 32-dimensional memory state and uses a learned sigmoid gate to update this state from the previous memory and current input. The updated memory is projected back into the input space and added residually to the current activation. 

Model variants 

With this general architecture, we built a range of models and varied aspects of the architecture, specifically the loss, and the gradient flow (see Figure 6). For the first four variants, we used the PC variant CPC (CPC, ( Oord et al., 2018 )), a contrastive objective that aligns with the principles of PC insofar as it can be framed as reducing the activational distance between the model’s predic-tion and the true, matching future input, while maximizing the activational distance to unexpected, mismatching inputs. At the lowest level of the architecture, all implemented models use a simple mean squared error (MSE) loss for pixel-level reconstruction, as there is no need to compress infor-mation at the pixel level (CPC would still work at this level but yield qualitatively less interpretable reconstructions). This reconstruction objective enables us to visualize model predictions and es-timate pixel-wise uncertainty using Monte Carlo dropout. Although pixel-perfect reconstruction is not the primary modeling objective and reconstructions are generally coarse, they provide an additional view of network behavior (see Figure 12). We further introduced low, naturalistically plausible augmentations to simulate natural noise or unsharpness (Gaussian smoothing), lighting changes (hue), and saccades (random crops, which we constrained to a lower and constant level to approximate saccadic movement ( Wang et al., 2021 ; Thorat et al., 2025 )), omitting implausible augmentations like unconstrained random cropping with zoom or image flips. We found this set of light augmentations to be sufficiently naturalistic; However, while developing this study we found that similar effects can be achieved using mere masking (representing noisy retinal input). Using these components, we defined six model variants (for a visual explanation, see Figure 6). First, PBN untrained is an untrained version of the same architecture as the trained PBN variants, providing an additional control that quantifies the variance accounted for by the architecture alone. Second, PBN backprop is a variant using a single CPC loss at the highest level together with the re-construction loss, with gradients allowed to flow through the entire network. This model therefore behaves very similarly to a typical self-supervised model. Third, PBN backprop + local loss uses a CPC loss at each level of the hierarchy and thus formally complies with the definitions of PC above, but still allows gradients to flow through the entire network. Fourth, PBN local gradients uses a CPC loss at each level of the hierarchy and additionally blocks gradient flow between hierarchy lev-els, such that the gradients of each loss are restricted to a single level. Fifth, PBN local gradients + subtract uses a CPC loss at each level of the hierarchy, blocks gradient flow between hierarchy lev-els, and additionally introduces the classic PC operation of explicit subtraction between the prior and the incoming input, such that only the unexplained residual is propagated forward. This sub-

> 16 of 29

traction operation is not required to qualify as a PC model but constitutes a distinguishing feature of classic PC ( Rao and Ballard, 1999 ). Finally, PBN classic PC is a model variant designed to ap-proximate classic PC as closely as possible: no contrastive loss, only a local MSE reconstruction loss at every level of the hierarchy; strictly local gradients, restricted within each hierarchy level; and no augmentation, as reference models trained with augmentation or masking did not outper-form this unaugmented variant. Importantly, this variant also includes the subtraction operation characteristic of classic PC. 

Control models 

ResNet-50 imagenet 

We used the standard supervised ImageNet-trained ResNet-50 model ( He et al., 2016 ) from keras.application (Chollet et al., 2015 ), comprising 25,583,592 trainable parameters. ResNet-50 is widely used as a model in NeuroAI and neuroconnectionist research. For inference, each video frame was resized to the input dimensions required by the model (224 × 224 × 3) and scaled to the required input range (0–255). We extracted activations over time by presenting each video frame independently as an image and compiling the resulting activations across all network blocks and time steps. 

ConvNeXt-tiny imagenet 

ConvNeXt-tiny imagenet ( Liu et al., 2022 ) was chosen as a reference model for producing highest brain alignment in the Brain-score vision/neural benchmark ( Schrimpf et al., 2018 ) at the time of the conception of this project (retrieved 2025-01-15) and for being easily accessible via keras.applications.Con (Chollet et al., 2015 ), comprising 28,589,128 trainable parameters. For inference, each video frame was resized to the input dimensions required by the model (224 × 224 × 3) and scaled to the re-quired input range (0–255). We extracted activations over time by presenting each video frame independently as an image and compiling the resulting activations across all network blocks and time steps. 

PredNet 

We used the original PredNet implementation from ( Lotter et al., 2016 ), written in Keras, adapting it to our Keras version while keeping the operations equivalent. We used a similar filter ratio to that of the original paper, with the latent-state filter count scaling by a factor of two across layers (32, 64, 128, 256, and 512), while adapting the number of layers such that the PredNet model contains a similar number of parameters to the PBN models (33,726,563 versus 34,610,952). This resulted in a five-layer PredNet, matching the number of hierarchy layers in the PBN models. PredNet was trained on the same dataset as the PBN models. We used the same normalization as in the original paper, scaling inputs to [0, 1], and used an input size of 128 × 128 × 3, which is the closest match to the 128 × 160 × 3 input used in the original paper. The original paper primarily reports two loss settings: 𝐿 0, in which only the lowest-layer loss is weighted (weight 1) and all other layer losses are set to zero, yielding better reconstruction but worse classification performance, and 𝐿 all ,in which the lowest-layer loss is weighted by 1 and all other layer losses by 0.1, yielding slightly worse reconstruction but better classification performance. We used the 𝐿 all setting because we are interested in the abstract representations formed by the model. 

Training 

We trained all relevant models (PredNet and all PBN models except for the untrained variant) on short naturalistic video clips from ( Monfort et al., 2019 ; Gifford et al., 2024 ; Dave et al., 2020 ). We used the test split of the MIT dataset as the held-out test set. Videos ranged from 1 s to over 2 h in duration, with most videos being approximately 3 s long; videos longer than 3 s were segmented into clips of at most 3 s. Although the models can process sequences of arbitrary temporal length, training was performed on sequences of 6 frames sampled at 3.2 Hz. Models were implemented 

> 17 of 29

using Keras 3 ( Chollet et al., 2015 ) (making all models work with Pytorch, TensorFlow, or JAX back-ends) and trained on a TensorFlow backend ( Abadi et al., 2015 ). Training used stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum and cosine learning-rate decay, with an initial learning rate of 0.075 decayed to zero over approximately 2.6 million training steps, momentum of 0.9, weight decay of 1e-6, and global gradient clipping at a norm of 1. During training, the training data were shuffled after each batch. Models were trained using an early-stopping/model-selection procedure, retain-ing the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Training continued for at least 350 epochs and was terminated when validation performance failed to improve for 50 epochs; all trained models converged before reaching the minimum training duration. Post-training test examples, includ-ing inputs, model predictions, and uncertainty estimates, for the PBN local-gradients variant, are shown in Figure 12. 

Representational similarity analysis 

We evaluated whether PBN models match commonly used models in terms of brain similarity using representational similarity analysis (RSA; ( Kriegeskorte et al., 2008a )). For this, we used stimulus images and average fMRI responses from the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD; ( Allen et al., 2022 )), restricted to the 515 samples shared across all subjects. Images were reshaped to (128, 128, 3) and repeated across three time steps as a still-image video to allow the recurrent models a warmup period. Human RDMs were constructed by taking the average fMRI response to each image, filtered by region (early, midventral, midlateral, midparietal, ventral, lateral, parietal; following a region definition from ( Thorat et al., 2025 ; Allen et al., 2022 )), and calculating the pairwise correlational distance (1 - rho; scipy.spatial.distance.pdist) between images, yielding a set of RDMs for each region and subject. Model RDMs were extracted by feeding the same images into each model and extracting activations after each processing stage (each hierarchy level for PBN, each block and average pooling stage for ResNet-50 and ConvNeXt, and each layer for PredNet), yielding a set of RDMs for each time step and layer of each model. We normalized each correlation by the estimated lower-bound RSA noise ceiling following ( Nili et al., 2014 ), calculated for each region as the mean leave-one-subject-out correlation with the average RDM of the remaining subjects. To ensure that these results did not simply reflect selection of maximally favorable time steps or layers, we used a reweighted RSA approach. For each subject and region, we fitted each layer and time step of each model on external, non-515 NSD images, drawing 32 samples and fitting the model to predict the brain region over 200 separate draws, following the approach outlined in ( Storrs et al., 2021 ). The resulting betas for each subject, region, layer, and time step were then applied to the held-out set of 515 test images to generate a reweighted RDM for each model, which was Spearman correlated to each region of each subject. Pairwise comparisons between all model combinations (reported in Figure 7) were performed using Wilcoxon paired-samples tests (( Wilcoxon, 1945 )) with false discovery rate correction following the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (( Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995 )). To confirm our results, we performed an alternative approach, where we simply calculated the maximum Spearman correlation across layers and time steps, yielding one maximum model-region correlation per subject. This additional analysis yielded qualitatively similar results. Its subject-average Spearman correlation and spread, together with pairwise comparisons between model variants, are shown in Figure 8. 

Unique decoder variance 

To determine whether the decoder explains variance in human brain responses that is not already explained by the encoder, we quantified the variance uniquely attributable to decoder represen-tations. For each PBN model, subject, and visual region, we extracted all encoder and decoder activations for the NSD ( Allen et al., 2022 ) 515 shared images as described above and fitted linear regression models using all layers and time steps. We first fitted a model containing the encoder and decoder representations jointly and then a nested model containing encoder representations

alone. The unique variance explained by the decoder was defined as the incremental coefficient of determination, 𝑅 2(brain ∣ decoder ) = 𝑅 2(brain ∣ all encoder layers ∪ all decoder layers ) − 𝑅 2(brain ∣

We corrected the resulting values for the natural noise ceiling by dividing the explained variance by the squared noise-ceiling correlation mentioned above ( Nili et al., 2014 ). Latent representations readout performance 

To evaluate general readout performance, we used two action-recognition video datasets: the test partition of Moments in Time ( Monfort et al., 2019 ) and the UCF-101 ( Soomro et al., 2012 ) bench-mark, which is commonly used but easier to solve. Videos were converted into fixed-length clips matching the training clips in frame size, length, and frame rate. For each dataset, we extracted latent representations from each evaluated model using the deepest encoder state available. We evaluated the extracted representations using two lightweight downstream readouts, a 1-nearest-neighbor (kNN-1) classifier and a logistic-regression classifier, each using 5-fold cross-validation. For each dataset and model, we report the mean and standard deviation across cross-validation splits. 

Replicating neural effects of prediction 

To test whether PBNs can reproduce neuroscientific findings that are difficult to model with estab-lished deterministic DNNs, we evaluated whether it reproduces the effects reported in ( Schwiedrzik and Freiwald, 2017 ), in which lower levels of macaque visual cortex inherit prediction signals from higher-level, identity-selective processing stages. We closely followed their violation-of-expectation paradigm, in which macaques learn to predict a specific face image following another face image, establishing expectations over both identity and viewpoint. We used the face stimuli from ( Thomaz and Giraldi, 2010 ), which provide the same relevant stimulus categories as ( Schwiedrzik and Frei-wald, 2017 ) while offering a larger, higher-quality, and more systematically organized stimulus set. Predictor and successor identities were paired one-to-one across two non-overlapping groups of face identities, with face rotations of -90°, 0°, and 90°. Viewpoint transitions were balanced across identity pairs to prevent systematic viewpoint biases. The resulting predictor-successor pairs were used to train the network for 100 epochs. After training, we evaluated four variants: training, view violation, identity violation, and com-bined view + identity violation. As in the original paradigm, the successor image was physically identical across all variants, with only the predictor image manipulated, ensuring that differences in the response to the subsequent stimulus could not be attributed to differences in its low-level image statistics. We passed all variants through the fine-tuned PBN and extracted latent encoder and decoder states from the first two hierarchy levels, theoretically best corresponding to the low-est level used in the original study. Prediction error was quantified as the difference between the encoder latent and the corresponding decoder prediction. For each condition, prediction error was expressed as the percentage change relative to the trained-condition baseline, |𝑃 𝐸 condition −𝑃 𝐸 trained ||𝑃 𝐸 trained | ×100 .We evaluated the first three time steps after the stimulus switch, defining the first as the early win-dow and the second and third as the late window. To test whether identity violations produced a stronger temporal increase in prediction-error change than viewpoint violations, we calculated the early-to-late change for each condition and compared these changes using one-sided paired-samples t-tests ( Student, 1908 ).

Next frame prediction 

We also used the Markov dataset and fine-tuned model to test whether the model was capable of predicting future states rather than merely encoding the current state (reported in Figure 10). Specifically, we tested whether the fine-tuned model output was closer to the future state than the current state itself, i.e., whether MSE( 𝑋 𝑡 +1 , model( 𝑋 𝑡 )) < MSE( 𝑋 𝑡 +1 , 𝑋 𝑡 ). This was evaluated using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. 

Uncertainty quantification 

We used a Markov chain experiment to investigate whether the model could 1. approximate the transition uncertainty of a known Markov process and 2. reflect differences in Markov-state un-certainty in the variance of its predictions under Monte Carlo dropout. We constructed a visual Markov chain in which each input frame was a black image containing one of four possible white-corner states, corresponding to four discrete Markov states: top left (A), top right (B), bottom left (C), and bottom right (D) (see Figure 5, top). The state in the current frame determined the probability distribution over states in the subsequent frame according to a predefined 4x4 Markov transition matrix, which served as the ground-truth transition model (see Figure 5, bottom left). Overall, 1152 training and 1152 validation transitions were generated from this process, in which the temporal structure of the visual input was fully determined by the predefined transition matrix. We fine-tuned the PBN local gradients model on these sequences for 100 epochs. To quantify convergence toward the true Markov dynamics, we computed the mean squared error (MSE) between the model-estimated and ground-truth transition matrices at each epoch and tested whether the final model-inferred matrix had lower error than the initial matrix using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To determine whether predictive variance reflected the uncertainty in-herent in the Markov process, we quantified the uncertainty of each true transition distribution using Shannon entropy ( Shannon, 1948 ), calculated from each row of the ground-truth transition matrix. We then compared the entropy of each state with the variance of the model-inferred pre-dictions with and without Dropout Monte Carlo. We fit an ordinary least-squares linear regressions between transition entropy and aggregated predictive variance separately for the dropout and no-dropout conditions to show that the Dropout Monte Carlo condition correlated strongly with the true transition entropy, while the non-dropout condition did not.
