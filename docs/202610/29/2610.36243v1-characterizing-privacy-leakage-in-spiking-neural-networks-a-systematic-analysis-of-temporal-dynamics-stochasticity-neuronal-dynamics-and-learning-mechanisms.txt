# Characterizing Privacy Leakage in Spiking Neural Networks: A Systematic Analysis of Temporal Dynamics, Stochasticity, Neuronal Dynamics and Learning Mechanisms 

## Ayana Moshruba and Maryam Parsa 

Electrical and Computer Engineering, George Mason University Fairfax, Virginia, USA 

amoshrub@gmu.edu 

Abstract 

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) encode information not only in whether neurons fire but also in when they fire, and this temporal dimension shapes what a trained network reveals about its training data. Biologically inspired firing patterns can reduce membership leakage in SNNs without any change to the network architecture, which positions temporal dynamics as a design variable for privacy. A change in firing behavior, however, alters stochasticity, spike timing, neuronal response, and the learning problem at once, so harnessing this effect requires identifying which of these properties produces it. This study traces that origin by separating temporal representation, stochasticity, intrinsic neuron dynamics, and learning within a common experimental framework, evaluated under a black-box membership inference attack across image and tabular benchmarks. Varying the temporal representation shows that burst-like firing patterns reduce leakage relative to regular spiking, but at a cost in accuracy. Injecting randomness into the input, the spike timing, and the spike trains does not reproduce this reduction in leakage, which shows that the reduction arises from how spikes are arranged in time rather than from noise. The privacy benefit further depends on where these firing patterns originate. When neurons generate these patterns through their own adaptive dynamics, leakage falls while accuracy is preserved, so privacy no longer comes at the expense of performance. The learning algorithm then sets the baseline level of leakage. Under evolutionary training, the network already exhibits MIA performance close to 0.5, leaving temporal dynamics little room to improve privacy further. Privacy in SNNs is therefore not an inherent property of spikes but a product of how temporal activity is generated, processed, and learned, which offers a principled basis for designing neuromorphic systems that protect sensitive data. 

Keywords: spiking neural networks, membership inference attacks, temporal dynamics, neuro-morphic privacy, stochasticity, adaptive exponential integrate-and-fire neurons 

# 1 Introduction 

The growing use of machine learning in resource-constrained and privacy-sensitive environments has created a need for models that can balance computational efficiency with reliable protection of training data. Neuromorphic computing has received increasing attention in this context because it provides an event-driven alternative to conventional neural computation [1]. Spiking neural networks (SNNs), which form a central computational model within neuromorphic systems, process information through discrete spikes distributed over time. Their temporal mode of computation supports energy-efficient processing and allows information to be represented through both spike 1arXiv:2610.36243v1 [cs.NE] 26 Oct 2026 occurrence and spike timing. These properties have motivated the use of SNNs in edge intelligence [2], autonomous systems [3, 4], and healthcare [5, 6], where computational efficiency and the local processing of potentially sensitive data can be important. The privacy characteristics of SNNs have received increasing attention as these models have been considered for applications in which training data may contain sensitive or confidential infor-mation. Membership inference attacks (MIAs), which determine whether a particular sample was used during training, have become one of the principal tools for studying such leakage. Initial com-parative studies suggested that SNNs can provide stronger resistance to membership inference than conventional Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) across a range of datasets and learning settings [7]. This observation suggested that the event-driven and temporal nature of spike-based computation may influence how information about individual training samples is retained and exposed by the model. Subsequent work, however, has shown that this privacy behavior cannot be characterized as a uniform property of SNNs. Membership leakage varies across static and neuromorphic datasets, and in some settings SNNs can be as vulnerable as, or even more vulnerable than, their ANN counterparts [8]. The apparent privacy advantage also changes with simulation latency, which shows that the temporal extent of spike-based computation can directly affect membership inference vulnerability [9]. At the same time, characteristics internal to the training process have been shown to alter the privacy-performance tradeoff. Surrogate-gradient selection and numerical quantization can significantly change membership leakage even when the broader network architecture remains unchanged [7]. These findings suggest that privacy in SNNs is not determined by the use of spikes alone, but by the particular computational and learning mechanisms through which spike-based representations are formed and optimized. The role of temporal behavior becomes especially important in this context. Recent results have shown that biologically inspired firing patterns such as Poisson Burst and Delayed Burst can produce substantially different membership-inference behavior under the same underlying LIF architecture [10]. These findings suggest that SNN privacy is not a fixed architectural property, but instead depends on how temporal information is represented, processed, and learned. What remains unclear is why these changes in temporal and computational behavior lead to different levels of membership leakage. Temporal firing patterns provide a particularly useful setting for examining this question be-cause they alter how the same input is represented over time while preserving the broader compu-tational framework of the network. Regular spiking, bursting, and delayed responses differ in spike count, temporal organization, onset characteristics, and degree of stochasticity. These differences can change the information presented to the network without requiring a change in the underly-ing architecture. Previous observations showed that such changes in temporal representation are accompanied by measurable differences in the privacy-performance tradeoff [10]. However, the ob-served relationship between firing behavior and privacy does not by itself identify the mechanism responsible for that variation. The privacy characteristics associated with a given firing pattern may arise from several interact-ing sources, including stochasticity in spike generation, the temporal organization of the resulting spike trains, the intrinsic dynamics of the neuron model, and the learning process through which network parameters are optimized. Because these factors are closely coupled in SNN computa-tion, changes in privacy cannot be attributed to temporal structure alone without further analysis. This motivates a controlled analysis that isolates stochasticity, neuron dynamics, and learning to determine how each shapes membership leakage and predictive performance. In this study, we first examine homogeneous and heterogeneous configurations of Rate, Poisson-Burst, and Delayed-Burst dynamics under a common LIF-based network to characterize how tem-2poral organization affects the privacy-performance tradeoff. We then examine the role of stochas-ticity through temporal jitter, input noise, and spike-level noise, with the aim of determining how perturbations introduced at different stages of spike processing affect membership leakage. Next, we study intrinsic neuron dynamics using Adaptive Exponential Integrate-and-Fire neurons and compare privacy across firing behaviors that arise from the neuron model itself. Finally, we ex-amine the influence of learning through gradient-based and evolutionary optimization, along with differentially private training. This progression provides a controlled basis for separating the effects of temporal representation, stochasticity, neuron dynamics, and learning, and for determining how their interaction shapes the privacy-performance behavior of SNNs. The results show that the privacy behavior of SNNs cannot be attributed to temporal struc-ture or stochasticity alone. Homogeneous and heterogeneous temporal dynamics alter the privacy-performance tradeoff, but increasing randomness through temporal jitter, input noise, or spike-level noise does not produce a consistent privacy gain and often degrades utility instead. Intrinsic neuron dynamics introduce a separate effect, as AdEx neurons reduce membership leakage while preserving or improving classification performance across the evaluated datasets. Learning further conditions the observed privacy behavior, since the same temporal dynamics exhibit different leakage char-acteristics under gradient-based, evolutionary, and differentially private training. These findings indicate that membership privacy in SNNs emerges from the interaction among temporal organiza-tion, stochasticity, neuron dynamics, and learning rather than from any one component in isolation. The contribution of this study is a systematic separation of these factors and an explanation of why biologically inspired temporal dynamics exhibit different privacy characteristics. 

# 2 Background & Related Work 

## 2.1 Spiking Neural Networks 

SNNs extend conventional neural computation by representing neuronal activity through discrete events distributed over time [11, 12]. Rather than propagating continuous-valued activations be-tween layers, an SNN communicates through spike trains, where both the occurrence and timing of spikes can convey information [13]. This temporal representation introduces an internal state that evolves throughout the simulation period, which distinguishes SNN computation from the predominantly static activation transformations used in conventional ANNs. A spike train generated by a presynaptic neuron j can be represented as a sequence of discrete firing events [14]. The behavior of an SNN depends strongly on the neuron model used to represent membrane dynamics and spike generation. A neuron model determines how incoming synaptic activity is accumulated, how the internal neuronal state evolves over time, when an output spike is produced, and how the state changes after firing [15, 16]. Different neuron models therefore provide different levels of computational complexity and biological realism, ranging from simple threshold-based formulations to adaptive dynamical systems capable of reproducing diverse firing behaviors [17, 16]. 

Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) Neuron. LIF neuron is one of the most widely used neuron models for representing spiking neuronal dynamics because it provides a simple and computationally efficient approximation of membrane integration and spike generation [15, 18]. The LIF model captures the fundamental processes of integration, leakage, thresholding, and reset with relatively low computational complexity [15, 18]. Its computational simplicity has made integrate-and-fire formulations particularly suitable for large-scale simulations and neuromorphic hardware implementations [17, 16]. This simplicity, however, also limits the range of intrinsic firing behaviors that can be reproduced compared with more expressive neuron models [18, 17]. 

Izhikevich Neuron. The Izhikevich neuron model provides a biologically expressive representa-tion of neuronal activity while retaining relatively low computational complexity [17]. In addition to the membrane potential, the model maintains a recovery variable that represents slower ionic processes and provides negative feedback to the membrane potential [17]. The interaction between these two state variables, together with a small set of model parameters, allows the neuron to repro-duce a broad range of biologically observed firing behaviors, including regular spiking, fast spiking, intrinsic bursting, chattering, and low-threshold spiking [17]. This flexibility makes the Izhikevich model useful for representing temporal firing behaviors that cannot be generated intrinsically by the standard LIF formulation. The use of the Izhikevich model in gradient-based machine learning is less straightforward be-cause its continuous membrane and recovery dynamics are interrupted by an event-triggered reset of both state variables after a spike [17]. This reset introduces a discontinuity into the neuronal trajectory, while the spike-generation mechanism introduces the same non-differentiability that complicates gradient-based training in SNNs more generally [19, 20]. Consequently, direct applica-tion of conventional backpropagation through the exact spiking dynamics is nontrivial. Surrogate-gradient methods provide one practical solution by replacing the derivative of the non-differentiable 4spike function during the backward pass, which allows models with Izhikevich-type dynamics to be incorporated into gradient-based SNN training frameworks [19, 20]. 

Adaptive Exponential Integrate-and-Fire Neuron(AdEx) AdEx neuron provides a biolog-ically expressive alternative to simpler integrate-and-fire models. AdEx combines an exponential spike-initiation mechanism with an adaptation variable that represents the effect of previous neu-ronal activity on subsequent membrane dynamics [18]. The subthreshold membrane and adaptation dynamics are governed by a two-dimensional system of differential equations, while spike generation is followed by an explicit reset of the membrane potential and adaptation state [18, 21]. Appropriate parameter configurations allow the model to reproduce a diverse set of firing behaviors, including tonic spiking, adaptation, initial and regular bursting, delayed spiking, delayed bursting, transient spiking, and irregular spiking [21]. The model can also reproduce characteristics associated with regular-spiking pyramidal neurons and fast-spiking interneurons [21]. The continuous subthreshold dynamics of AdEx are smooth, and the exponential term provides a gradual spike-initiation mechanism compared with the strict voltage threshold of the conven-tional LIF model [18]. The complete spiking model nevertheless remains non-differentiable because spike emission and state reset are discrete events [20]. Gradient-based training therefore requires an approximation of the spike derivative, as it does for other deterministic spiking neuron models. Surrogate-gradient methods provide such an approximation and allow Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) to be applied to SNNs with nonlinear and adaptive neuron dynamics [19, 20]. AdEx is therefore compatible with modern gradient-based SNN training when an appropriate surrogate treatment of spike generation is used, while retaining substantially richer intrinsic neuronal dynam-ics than the standard LIF formulation [18, 21, 20]. 

2.1.2 Learning in SNNs 

Training SNNs requires the optimization of synaptic parameters while accounting for the temporal evolution of neuronal states and the discrete nature of spike generation [19, 13]. The threshold operation that produces a spike is non-differentiable, which prevents the direct application of con-ventional gradient-based optimization through the exact spike function [22, 19]. Several learning strategies have therefore been developed to address the challenges associated with the binary and dynamical nature of SNNs [19, 13]. In this work, the two learning paradigms of primary interest are gradient-based backpropagation and evolutionary optimization. 

Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) Learning. Gradient-based SNN training com-monly relies on surrogate-gradient methods to overcome the non-differentiability of spike genera-tion [19, 13]. During the forward pass, spike generation remains discrete and follows the threshold dynamics of the neuron model. During the backward pass, the derivative of the non-differentiable spike function is replaced by a differentiable surrogate so that gradient information can propa-gate through the network [19]. This approach preserves discrete spike generation during forward computation while providing an approximate gradient for parameter optimization. For temporally simulated SNNs, surrogate-gradient learning can be combined with BPTT, where the recurrent neuronal dynamics are unfolded across the simulation horizon and gradients are propagated through the temporal evolution of the neuronal states [19, 13]. Temporal backprop-agation methods have also been developed specifically for SNNs. For example, SLAYER introduced a temporal credit-assignment mechanism that propagates errors through both layers and time while addressing the non-differentiability of the spike-generation function [22]. These approaches permit 5SNNs to be optimized using gradient-based learning while retaining event-based spike generation during the forward simulation. 

Evolutionary Learning. Evolutionary learning provides an alternative optimization strategy that does not require gradient information or differentiable neuron dynamics. Evolutionary Op-timization for Neuromorphic Systems (EONS) applies evolutionary search directly to SNN design and training and can optimize network parameters and structure without differentiating through the spike-generation process [23]. The optimization begins with a population of candidate SNNs, where each candidate is evaluated according to a task-specific fitness objective. Candidate networks are selected according to their fitness, and evolutionary operators such as mutation and crossover are applied to construct subsequent generations [23, 24]. This process is repeated over successive generations until a predefined termination criterion is reached. The absence of gradient requirements makes evolutionary optimization compatible with discrete spike events, recurrent network structures, and neuron models whose dynamics are difficult to opti-mize through conventional backpropagation [23]. This flexibility also comes with a computational cost because multiple candidate networks must be evaluated repeatedly throughout the evolution-ary process. The scalability of evolutionary SNN optimization can therefore become challenging as the task and network complexity increase [24]. Backpropagation-based and evolutionary learning represent fundamentally different mechanisms for optimizing SNNs. Backpropagation relies on gradient information and surrogate approximations of spike generation, whereas evolutionary learning searches the parameter space through population-based optimization without requiring differentiability [19, 23]. These differences provide distinct learning conditions under which the predictive and privacy characteristics of SNNs can be examined. 

## 2.2 Privacy in SNNs 

Privacy research in SNNs has developed from two complementary questions: how privacy protec-tion can be incorporated into spike-based learning, and whether the computational properties of SNNs themselves influence information leakage. The first question has motivated work on differen-tial privacy, encrypted inference, and privacy-preserving distributed learning. The second has led to direct evaluations of SNNs under membership inference, model inversion, and gradient-based leakage attacks. The resulting literature shows that privacy in SNNs is shaped by several interact-ing design choices, including the temporal representation, neuron model, optimization procedure, numerical precision, simulation duration, and threat model. Early studies primarily focused on introducing explicit privacy protection into SNN pipelines. PrivateSNN addressed data and class leakage during ANN-to-SNN conversion by replacing real conversion data with synthetic samples and subsequently adapting the converted model through temporal spike-based learning [25]. The method reduced the forms of leakage considered in the study while preserving most of the predictive utility of the original model. DPSNN approached the problem through differential privacy and incorporated gradient perturbation directly into SNN training [26]. Because privacy noise degraded predictive performance, the method introduced tem-poral enhanced pooling to recover part of the lost utility. A related approach, Encrypted-SNN, applied differential privacy during ANN-to-SNN conversion and showed that privacy protection could be incorporated into the conversion process without eliminating useful classification perfor-mance [27]. This early body of work demonstrated that established privacy mechanisms could be adapted to spike-based learning, but it did not explain whether the internal computational properties of SNNs influence privacy independently of those mechanisms. 6A separate line of research has examined privacy during inference through homomorphic en-cryption. Nikfam et al. evaluated encrypted SNN inference under the BFV scheme and showed that SNNs could retain competitive accuracy under encryption, although temporal simulation in-troduced additional computational cost [28]. Li et al. subsequently developed a discretized SNN implementation under TFHE, where firing and reset operations were executed directly over en-crypted data [29]. The same direction was extended to convolutional SNNs through homomorphic implementations of LIF dynamics and parallelized bootstrapping [30]. Later frameworks such as SpyKing examined fully homomorphic SNN inference across multiple datasets and encryption con-figurations [31], while PrivSpike extended encrypted inference to deeper SNN architectures through CKKS-based computation and specialized approximations of LIF operations [32]. These approaches demonstrate that strong cryptographic protection is compatible with SNN inference, although their primary objective is to conceal the input during computation rather than to explain why a trained SNN leaks more or less information under a privacy attack. Direct attack-based evaluations introduced a different perspective on SNN privacy. Model in-version studies showed that spike-based computation should not be assumed to prevent information recovery solely because spikes are discrete and non-differentiable. BrainLeaks introduced white-box inversion methods adapted specifically to SNNs and demonstrated that private information could still be reconstructed when the attack accounted for the structure of spike-based computation [33]. Under black-box access, however, Poursiami et al. observed substantially stronger resistance for SNNs than for comparable ANNs [34]. The difference between these findings illustrates an impor-tant characteristic of SNN privacy: the amount of information exposed by the model depends not only on the architecture, but also on the attacker’s access and on how the attack interacts with temporal computation. Membership inference has provided a broader framework for studying this dependence. Moshruba et al. compared SNNs and ANNs across multiple datasets, learning algorithms, software frame-works, and model configurations and reported lower membership leakage for SNNs across the eval-uated settings [7]. The magnitude of this difference was not constant. Evolutionary learning produced different privacy behavior from gradient-based optimization, and differentially private training affected SNN and ANN utility differently under comparable privacy constraints. These results suggested that the observed privacy advantage was linked to the computational and learning configuration of the SNN rather than to the presence of spikes alone. Later studies reinforced this view and showed that SNNs do not exhibit a uniform privacy advantage across all settings. Li et al. evaluated several MIAs across static and neuromorphic datasets and found that the relative vulnerability of SNNs depends strongly on the data modality and training configuration [8]. SNNs could exhibit greater membership leakage than ANNs on neuromorphic datasets, while their relative behavior on static datasets varied across experimental conditions. The same study showed that ANN-to-SNN conversion and data augmentation could substantially change attack effectiveness. Guan et al. examined the temporal dimension more directly and found that membership-inference resilience decreased as simulation latency increased [9]. An input-dropout strategy adapted to SNNs further improved attack effectiveness. These findings show that privacy can change even when the broad network family remains the same, which places greater importance on the internal temporal and learning configuration of the model. This shift toward mechanism-level analysis has led to closer examination of the components that define SNN computation. Surrogate gradients are one such component because they determine how the non-differentiable spike function is approximated during gradient-based learning. Moshruba et al. showed that different surrogate-gradient formulations produce different levels of membership leakage and that numerical quantization further changes the privacy-performance tradeoff [35]. Privacy behavior has also been linked to the underlying neuron model. The Adaptive Differential 7Privacy SNN (ADPSNN) framework introduced an adaptive differential privacy mechanism based on the relationship between output spikes and class labels and reported different utility charac-teristics for LIF and IF neurons under privacy perturbation [36]. The Relevance-based Adaptive Differential Private Spiking Neural Network (RADP-SNN) framework subsequently used neuron-level relevance to determine the allocation of differential privacy noise and similarly found that the resulting privacy-utility behavior depended on the underlying neuron model [37]. Differentially private SNNs have also been studied in application specific settings such as social robotics, where privacy protection was evaluated alongside robustness and resource efficiency [38]. Privacy analysis has further been extended to graph structured spiking models through membership inference and structural differential privacy [39]. These results show that privacy mechanisms interact with the structure and learning dynamics of the SNN rather than acting independently of them. Temporal representation introduces another important source of variation. Biologically inspired firing patterns can change the spike sequence presented to the network without requiring any mod-ification to the underlying architecture or neuron model. Moshruba et al. examined Poisson Burst and Delayed Burst dynamics within LIF-based SNNs and found that the resulting temporal firing patterns produced distinct privacy-performance tradeoffs under membership inference [10]. Pois-son Burst preserved utility close to conventional rate dynamics while reducing membership leakage, whereas Delayed Burst produced stronger privacy in several settings at a larger utility cost. This result is particularly relevant because the observed privacy differences arise under the same LIF architecture. The temporal dynamics itself therefore becomes a source of measurable privacy vari-ation. At the same time, burst-based dynamics modify several properties simultaneously, including spike count, timing, onset, stochasticity, and temporal organization. The observed privacy effect cannot therefore be assigned to any one of these characteristics without further analysis. The interaction between privacy and SNN computation has also been examined in federated settings. Han et al. combined federated SNN learning with differential privacy at both the client and server levels and showed that privacy perturbation could reduce membership and property inference while preserving the energy advantages of spike-based computation [40]. More recent work has examined the information contained in the shared gradients themselves. Aksu et al. adapted gradient inversion attacks to federated SNNs and found substantially weaker reconstructions than those obtained from ANN gradients under the evaluated conditions [41]. Their analysis associated this behavior with the event-driven forward process and surrogate-gradient training, which again links privacy to the mechanism through which the model represents and propagates information. Privacy mechanisms can also alter the temporal signals on which SNN learning depends. Pereira et al. showed that differential privacy operations such as clipping and noise injection systemat-ically change firing rate statistics in federated SNNs, with corresponding effects on aggregation signals and client-selection stability [42]. This result is important because the relationship operates in both directions: temporal behavior can influence privacy, and privacy protection can modify the temporal behavior of the network. Recent federated frameworks have started to account for this interaction explicitly. The Privacy-Preserving and Communication-Efficient Federated Spiking Neural Learning framework, PriSparK, combines event-sparse updates, differential privacy, error feedback, and heterogeneity-aware aggregation to address privacy and communication efficiency within a unified federated SNN framework [43]. A distributed Integrated Sensing and Communi-cation (ISAC) framework similarly combines federated SNN learning, secure aggregation, adaptive differential privacy, and spike-aware temporal processing for privacy-sensitive edge sensing [44]. These studies extend the privacy problem from individual SNN models to distributed learning sys-tems in which temporal activity, privacy protection, communication, and optimization are closely connected. The current literature therefore supports a more nuanced interpretation of SNN privacy than 8the early question of whether SNNs are inherently more private than ANNs. Explicit protec-tion mechanisms such as differential privacy, homomorphic encryption, and secure aggregation can reduce information exposure, but their utility and effectiveness depend on the computational struc-ture of the SNN. Direct attack evaluations further show that SNNs can exhibit privacy behavior that differs substantially from ANNs, but the direction and magnitude of that difference depend on the attack model, dataset, simulation latency, optimization procedure, and model configuration [33, 7, 8, 9, 34, 41]. Studies of surrogate gradients, neuron models, temporal dynamics, and firing-rate encoding provide additional evidence that privacy is closely linked to the internal mechanisms of spike-based computation [35, 10, 36, 37, 42]. An important gap nevertheless remains. Most existing studies isolate one factor at a time, such as latency, surrogate gradients, quantization, neuron models, differential privacy, or temporal dynamics. As a result, the literature identifies many conditions under which SNN privacy changes, but it does not explain how these conditions relate to one another or which mechanisms are respon-sible for the observed differences. This limitation is particularly important for biologically inspired temporal dynamics, where changes in firing behavior can simultaneously alter stochasticity, spike timing, burst structure, neuronal response, and the learning process. A systematic analysis that separates these effects is therefore necessary to determine where the corresponding privacy behavior originates and how each component contributes to the resulting privacy-performance tradeoff. 

## 2.3 Membership Inference Attacks(MIAs) 

MIAs evaluate whether a trained machine learning model reveals information about the individual samples used during training. Given a target sample and access to a trained model, the adversary attempts to determine whether that sample belonged to the model’s training set. The problem can be formulated as a binary inference task. For a target model fθ trained on dataset Dtrain , an attack 

A predicts a membership variable ˆm = A (x, y, f θ(x)) , ˆm ∈ { 0, 1}, (5) where ˆ m = 1 denotes a predicted member and ˆ m = 0 denotes a predicted non-member. De-pending on the threat model, the adversary may observe the predicted class, confidence scores, loss values, intermediate representations, gradients, or model parameters. The objective remains the same: to distinguish samples that influenced model training from samples that did not [45, 46]. The privacy significance of membership inference depends strongly on the application. Knowl-edge that a particular record was used to train a model may itself reveal sensitive information when membership in the training set is associated with a medical condition, financial status, demographic group, or other confidential attribute. The attacker does not need to reconstruct the original record for a privacy violation to occur. The membership decision alone can disclose whether an individ-ual contributed data to a sensitive dataset [45]. MIAs have consequently become a widely used empirical framework for measuring whether information about individual training samples remains detectable through a trained model. [47, 48]. The modern formulation of membership inference was introduced by Shokri et al. [45] under a black-box threat model. Their attack exploits the observation that a model can behave differently on samples that participated in training and samples that were not observed during optimization. To learn this distinction, the adversary trains a collection of shadow models on datasets drawn from a distribution similar to that of the target model. Because the attacker controls the training sets of the shadow models, the membership status of each sample is known. Prediction outputs from member and non-member samples are then used to train an attack classifier, which learns to distinguish the response patterns associated with the two groups. The resulting classifier is 9applied to the outputs of the target model to infer the membership status of previously unseen samples. This formulation established that model outputs alone can reveal information about the composition of the training set, even when the attacker has no direct access to the target model’s parameters or training data. Later work showed that successful membership inference does not necessarily require the com-plete shadow-model setting assumed in the original attack. Yeom et al. demonstrated that the loss of a target model on an individual sample can itself provide a membership signal and established a formal connection between membership leakage and generalization behavior [46]. Their analysis showed that overfitting is sufficient for membership inference vulnerability, although it is not nec-essary. Salem et al. subsequently relaxed several assumptions of the Shokri attack and showed that effective membership inference can be performed with fewer shadow models, limited knowledge of the target architecture, and auxiliary data that do not precisely match the target training distribu-tion [49]. These developments established that membership leakage is not restricted to adversaries with extensive knowledge of the target training process. Research on MIAs also expanded the range of information available to the attacker. While the original Shokri attack relies on black-box prediction outputs, white-box attacks use internal information such as model parameters, intermediate activations, gradients, or parameter updates. Nasr et al. showed that such internal signals can expose additional membership information and developed white-box attacks for both centralized and federated learning settings [50]. At the opposite end of the access spectrum, Choquette-Choo et al. demonstrated that confidence scores are not strictly necessary for membership inference [51]. Their label-only attacks infer membership from the stability of the predicted class under input perturbations. This result showed that restricting an application programming interface to return only class labels does not necessarily eliminate membership leakage. The methodology used to evaluate MIAs has also evolved. Early attacks commonly reported aggregate attack accuracy or related average-case measures, but subsequent studies showed that such metrics can obscure substantial differences in privacy risk across individual samples. Song and Mittal developed a systematic evaluation framework that compared multiple attack signals and emphasized the need to assess privacy leakage beyond a single attack classifier or aggregate accuracy value [47]. Carlini et al. later formulated membership inference as a per-example hypothesis-testing problem and introduced the Likelihood Ratio Attack (LiRA) [48]. LiRA estimates how likely an observed model response is under the hypotheses that a sample was included or excluded from training. Their analysis also emphasized attack performance at very low false-positive rates, where an attacker can identify a smaller number of members with substantially greater confidence. These advances demonstrate that membership inference is not a single fixed attack, but a family of inference procedures that differ in adversarial access, assumptions, attack signals, and evaluation objectives. Despite these methodological differences, the central principle remains consistent: membership inference measures whether the behavior of a trained model contains a detectable statistical dis-tinction between training samples and samples that were not used for training. This property makes MIA particularly useful as an empirical privacy measure when the objective is to compare how changes in model design or training affect information leakage. In this study, we use the original black-box shadow-model attack introduced by Shokri et al. [45]. A shadow model is used to generate member and non-member prediction outputs, and a binary attack classifier learns the corresponding membership signal from these responses. We retain this attack formulation across all experimental conditions so that changes in attack performance reflect changes in the information exposed by the trained SNN rather than differences in the attack methodology. MIA therefore serves as a consistent analytical measure for examining how temporal representation, stochasticity, 10 neuron dynamics, and learning mechanisms affect membership leakage. 

# 3 Experimental Methodology 

## 3.1 Datasets and Model Architecture 

We evaluate the temporal dynamics across five classification benchmarks: MNIST [52], Fashion-MNIST (FMNIST) [53], CIFAR-10 [54], Iris [55], and Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) [56]. The first three datasets represent image-classification tasks, while Iris and Breast Cancer provide lower-dimensional tabular settings. MNIST contains 60 ,000 training and 10 ,000 test images of handwritten digits represented as 28 ×28 grayscale images across 10 classes. Fashion-MNIST follows the same image dimensions and train/test sizes but contains clothing images from 10 categories. CIFAR-10 contains 50 ,000 training and 10 ,000 test 32 × 32 RGB images across 10 object classes. Iris contains 150 samples described by four numerical features across three classes, while the Breast Cancer dataset contains 569 samples represented by 30 numerical features for binary classification. For CIFAR-10, training images are augmented using random 32 × 32 crops with four-pixel padding and random horizontal flipping. Both training and test images are converted to tensors and normalized using a mean and standard deviation of 0 .5 for each RGB channel. MNIST and FMNIST images are resized to 28 × 28, converted to grayscale tensors, and processed consistently across their training and test partitions. For Iris and Breast Cancer, the data are divided into training and test sets using an 80 /20 split with a fixed random state of 42. Numerical features are standardized using a StandardScaler fitted on the corresponding training set and subsequently applied to the test set. For MNIST and FMNIST, a convolutional SNN with two convolutional layers is used. The layers contain 32 and 64 output channels, respectively, with 3 × 3 kernels and max-pooling after each convolutional stage. The resulting representation is flattened and passed through a fully connected layer with 1000 hidden units followed by a LIF layer. A final fully connected layer maps the representation to 10 output classes and is followed by another LIF layer. For CIFAR-10, a larger convolutional SNN is used to accommodate the greater complexity of natural RGB images. The network contains four convolutional layers with 64, 64, 128, and 128 output channels. Batch normalization is applied after each convolutional layer, while max-pooling is applied after the second and fourth convolutional layers. The resulting representation is passed through fully connected layers with 1024 and 512 units, each followed by LIF dynamics, before reaching the 10-unit LIF output layer. For the tabular datasets, a fully connected SNN is used. The Iris model maps four input features to a hidden layer with 1000 units followed by a three-unit output layer. The Breast Cancer model follows the same structure with 30 input features and a two-unit output layer. LIF neurons are used after both fully connected layers. Across all models, the membrane decay parameter is fixed at β = 0 .95. The model architecture and neuron parameters remain fixed across temporal dynamics condi-tions within each dataset. Only the temporal dynamics or the experimental factor under inves-tigation are changed. This controlled setup allows differences in classification performance and membership-inference leakage to be examined without introducing changes in model capacity. 

## 3.2 Biologically Inspired Temporal Dynamics 

To investigate the influence of temporal firing behavior on privacy, we employ three temporal dynamics schemes: Rate , Poisson Burst , and Delayed Burst . The two burst-based schemes are 11 Rate-Based Spike Dynamics Poisson-Burst Spike Dynamics Delayed-Burst Spike Dynamics Figure 1: Illustration of the three temporal dynamics schemes used in this study. Rate dynamics represents input magnitude through spike frequency, such that stronger inputs gen-erate denser spike trains. Poisson-Burst dynamics generates stochastic bursts at discrete temporal intervals, where stronger inputs increase the probability of burst occurrence and the number of spikes within each triggered burst is sampled from a Poisson distribution. Delayed-Burst dynamics represents input magnitude primarily through burst latency, where stronger inputs produce earlier burst onset and weaker inputs produce later responses. Rather than replacing the underlying LIF neurons with the computationally more com-plex Izhikevich formulation, the temporal behavior is introduced during spike encoding while the downstream LIF architecture is kept unchanged. This separation allows us to study the effect of temporal representation while controlling for the underlying neuron model. Figure 1 illustrates how the three dynamics schemes respond to different input intensities. 

Rate Dynamics: Rate dynamics serves as the baseline temporal representation. Each normal-ized input value determines the probability of generating a spike at each simulation step. Higher input intensities therefore produce denser spike trains, while lower intensities produce progressively sparser activity, as illustrated in the first column of Figure 1. All inputs are normalized to the range [0 , 1] before temporal dynamics. 

Poisson Burst Dynamics: Poisson Burst dynamics introduces stochastic burst activity. The simulation window is divided into fixed temporal intervals, and a Bernoulli trial is performed at each interval for every input feature. The probability of initiating a burst is determined by the normalized input intensity, such that stronger inputs are more likely to generate bursts. When a burst occurs, its size is sampled from a Poisson distribution and constrained to a predefined range, while spikes within the burst are separated by a fixed inter-spike interval. In our implementation, the Poisson mean is set to λ = 3 .0, candidate burst events are evaluated every 5 timesteps, the sampled burst size is clipped to the range [1 , 8], and the inter-spike interval is fixed at 1 timestep. The first possible burst can occur at t = 0. As shown in the middle column of Figure 1, stronger inputs consequently produce more frequent burst events, while the number of spikes within each burst remains stochastic. 12 Delayed Burst EDynamics: Delayed Burst dynamics represents input magnitude primarily through burst onset latency. For each input feature, the expected delay is inversely related to its normalized intensity, such that stronger inputs are expected to fire earlier and weaker inputs later in the simulation window. The expected delay is converted into the parameter of a geometric distribution from which the actual onset delay is sampled. To limit excessive temporal variance, the sampled delay is clipped to a bounded interval around its expected value. In our implementation, the clipping range is controlled by a ratio of 0 .2 of the maximum allowable delay. Once the burst begins, 4 spikes are generated with an inter-spike interval of 1 timestep. The gain and offset parameters are set to 1 .0 and 0 .0, respectively, and the earliest possible burst onset is t = 0. This behavior is illustrated in the final column of Figure 1, where stronger inputs produce earlier bursts and weaker inputs produce progressively later responses. 

## 3.3 Learning Algorithms 

3.3.1 Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT): 

Gradient-based optimization was performed using BPTT, which enables temporal credit assignment across the simulation horizon. Since spike generation is inherently non-differentiable, the backward pass employed the Fast Sigmoid surrogate gradient to approximate the derivative of the spiking function, allowing gradients to propagate through the membrane dynamics while preserving discrete spike generation during the forward simulation. This approach enables end-to-end optimization of the network without altering the underlying neuron model. During training, each input sample was first converted into a temporal spike sequence using one of the three dynamics schemes described in Section3.2. The encoded spike trains were then propagated through the network over T = 10 discrete simulation steps, where membrane potentials evolved according to the LIF neuron dynamics. Output spike counts accumulated across the simu-lation window were used to compute the cross-entropy loss, after which gradients were propagated through both the spatial network layers and temporal simulation steps using BPTT. All networks were optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −3 and a batch size of 128 for 50 training epochs. Unless otherwise specified, the membrane decay constant was fixed at β = 0 .95, the membrane threshold at Vth = 0 .2, and identical network architectures and optimization settings were maintained across all temporal dynamics. Consequently, any observed differences in predictive performance or privacy can be attributed to the temporal dynamics strategy rather than variations in the optimization procedure. 

3.3.2 Evolutionary Learning: 

To examine whether the observed privacy characteristics extend beyond gradient-based optimiza-tion, we additionally trained SNNs using an evolutionary learning framework. Unlike BPTT, evolu-tionary optimization directly searches the parameter space without relying on gradient information, making it naturally applicable to spiking networks with non-differentiable neuron dynamics. Evolutionary optimization was implemented using the EONS framework. Each individual in the population represents a candidate SNN whose synaptic parameters constitute its genome. During every generation, all candidate networks were evaluated on the complete training set, and classi-fication accuracy was used as the fitness objective. Based on their fitness values, parent networks were selected according to the EONS selection strategy and used to generate offspring through crossover and mutation. The newly generated offspring formed the next population while elite individuals were preserved to retain the best-performing solutions across successive generations. ## 3.4 Attack and Evaluation Metrics 

Privacy leakage is evaluated using a black-box MIA, which determines whether a given sample belongs to the target model’s training set from the model representation produced for that sample. The same attack architecture and evaluation procedure are maintained across all experiments so that changes in attack performance can be attributed to the temporal dynamics, neuron models, or learning mechanisms being evaluated. The attack follows the shadow-model framework illustrated in Figure 2. To construct a larger 14 and more diverse attack training set, three shadow models with the same architecture as the target model are trained independently using different shadow train/test splits. Samples used to train a shadow model are treated as members ( IN ), while samples from its corresponding held-out split are treated as non-members ( OUT ). Each sample is queried once, following the original attack setting. The final membrane-potential representation at the last simulation timestep is extracted as the attack feature. The member and non-member representations produced by all three shadow models are then pooled into a single attack training set. For attack evaluation, the target model is queried using its training and test samples. Rep-resentations from the target training set are labeled as members, while representations from the target test set are labeled as non-members, which together form the attack test set shown in Fig-ure 2. Attack examples are grouped according to their class labels, and a class-specific radial basis function (RBF) support vector machine is trained using the pooled shadow-model representations. The trained attack classifier is then applied to the corresponding target-model representations to infer membership. The use of multiple shadow models therefore increases the amount and diversity of attack training data without changing the membership decision procedure itself. Privacy leakage is quantified using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). An AUC of 0 .5 indicates performance equivalent to random guessing, while values approach-ing 1 .0 indicate increasingly successful membership inference. Therefore, AUC values closer to 0 .5represent lower observable membership leakage under the evaluated attack. Model utility is measured using classification accuracy on the corresponding test set. Together, test accuracy and MIA AUC characterize the privacy-performance tradeoff across the different temporal dynamics, neuron models, and learning mechanisms evaluated in this study.

## 4.2 Heterogeneous Temporal Dynamics 

Biological neural systems exhibit substantial diversity in their firing behavior. Neurons within the same neural population can show different response patterns, including regular spiking, fast spiking, bursting, and adaptation [57, 58]. Motivated by this observation, we ask whether introducing analogous diversity into the temporal representation of the input affects the privacy-performance characteris-tics of SNNs. We do not attempt to reproduce biological neuronal heterogeneity directly. Instead, we construct a simplified heterogeneous temporal dynamics that combines the Rate, Poisson-Burst, and Delayed-Burst dynamics examined in the homogeneous setting. # 6 Intrinsic Neuron Dynamics and Privacy 

SNNs can be constructed using a range of neuron models that differ in the complexity of their internal dynamics and the firing behaviors they can express. The LIF neuron remains one of the most widely used models because of its computational simplicity and its compatibility with 24 gradient-based SNN training [60, 61]. However, its dynamics are comparatively limited, since the membrane potential primarily integrates incoming current with leakage until a threshold-triggered spike and reset occur. More expressive neuron models can reproduce a substantially broader range of temporal behaviors. One prominent example is the Izhikevich neuron model, which combines computational effi-ciency with the ability to generate diverse firing regimes, including regular spiking, bursting, delayed firing, rebound activity, and frequency adaptation [17]. These firing characteristics motivated the temporal patterns examined earlier in this work, where Poisson-Burst and Delayed-Burst dynamics were introduced at the input level while the downstream network continued to use LIF neurons. That formulation allowed the temporal representation itself to be studied independently, but it does not address whether the intrinsic dynamics of the neuron model can also influence privacy. Because intrinsic neuron dynamics shape the evolution of spike activity within the network, they may provide an additional source of variation in privacy behavior beyond the input-level temporal patterns examined earlier [7, 9]. We therefore investigate whether changing the neuron model itself produces corresponding differences in membership leakage. Directly replacing the LIF encoding with the Izhikevich formulation is not straightforward within the gradient-based training framework used in this study. We therefore consider the AdEx neuron model, which extends integrate-and-fire dynamics with an exponential membrane term and an adaptation variable [18]. AdEx can reproduce multiple electrophysiological firing classes while remaining sufficiently compact for integration into trainable SNN architectures [18]. This research therefore shifts the source of temporal diversity from the input encoding to the neuron itself. Rather than externally constructing Rate, Poisson-Burst, or Delayed-Burst spike trains and processing them with the same LIF dynamics, we investigate whether richer firing regimes can emerge from the internal dynamics of AdEx neurons and whether those dynamics produce different privacy–utility behavior.

In particular, changes in the adaptation and reset parameters can produce tonic spiking, initial bursting, regular bursting, delayed activity, and other firing regimes [21]. These properties make AdEx suitable for examining whether privacy behavior can be influenced by intrinsic neuronal dynamics rather than by input-level temporal structure alone. # 7 Learning and Training Mechanisms in Privacy 

## 7.1 Privacy under Gradient-Based and Evolutionary Learning 

The learning algorithm determines how individual training samples shape the network parameters and, in turn, how much sample-specific information the trained model retains. Gradient-based training updates parameters using loss derivatives computed directly on the training data. Evolu-tionary learning instead explores a population of candidate networks and selects among them using a scalar, task-level fitness, without access to gradient information. Because the two paradigms couple the training data to the model in fundamentally different ways, they may yield different membership-leakage profiles even when the temporal representation is held constant. We therefore evaluate Rate, Poisson-Burst, and Delayed-Burst dynamics under both paradigms to determine whether the privacy behavior observed under backpropagation is specific to gradient-based opti-mization or persists under a fundamentally different learning process. Evolutionary optimization is implemented using EONS within the TENNLab framework [62]. This choice follows our earlier study [7], in which evolutionary SNNs yielded MIA AUC values close to 0.5 across variations in population size, mutation rate, crossover rate, and processor configura-tion, and exhibited lower leakage than surrogate-gradient models on Iris and Breast Cancer. As described in Section 3.3, the evolutionary experiments are restricted to these two datasets because of the computational cost of evaluating entire populations. The three temporal dynamics are in-corporated into the EONS pipeline as follows: Rate dynamics uses the native TENNLab encoder output, whereas Poisson-Burst and Delayed-Burst spike trains are generated from the firing prob-abilities derived from this encoder, following the same temporal definitions used in the preceding 29 experiments. For each temporal configuration, the target and shadow SNNs are evolved independently, with classification accuracy as the fitness objective. Each shadow model is evolved on its corresponding data subset using the same procedure as the target model, after which the standard shadow-model MIA pipeline is applied. The temporal representation and the attack procedure are therefore held fixed across learning paradigms, so that differences in utility and membership leakage can be attributed primarily to the learning algorithm. 

7.1.1 Result and Analysis 

Figure 16 shows that Rate dynamics yield the highest or near-highest accuracy under both learn-ing paradigms, but the accuracy cost of introducing burst-based dynamics depends strongly on the learning paradigm. Under evolutionary learning, accuracy decreases consistently from Rate to Poisson-Burst to Delayed-Burst. On Iris, Delayed-Burst results in a 27.3% relative accuracy degradation from the Rate baseline, compared with 13.8% under backpropagation. On Breast Cancer, backpropagation retains its accuracy under both burst-based dynamics. As discussed in Section 4.1, burst-based dynamics make temporal integration more difficult for the downstream LIF network. Backpropagation can compensate for this difficulty because it uses gradient information across the simulation window to adjust the weights to the temporal pattern of the input spikes. Evolutionary learning has no access to gradient information and relies on task-level fitness alone, so it compensates less effectively. The learning paradigm therefore determines how well the network handles burst-based dynamics. Figure 17 shows that under backpropagation, MIA AUC decreases progressively from Rate to Poisson-Burst to Delayed-Burst on both datasets, consistent with Section 4.1. Under evolutionary learning, MIA AUC remains close to 0.5 across all temporal dynamics. Because the Rate baseline already yields an MIA AUC near 0.5, which corresponds to random guessing, little reduction in leakage can be observed in this setting. The evolutionary results are therefore inconclusive with respect to the effect of burst-based dynamics on membership leakage. Considering accuracy and leakage, Figures 16 and 17 indicate that the trade-off introduced by burst-based dynamics is conditioned by the learning paradigm. Burst-based temporal dynamics improve privacy under backpropagation. Under evolutionary learning, the Rate baseline already exhibits near-ideal privacy, so the effect of burst-based dynamics is confined to utility, where they cause a substantial degradation. The privacy benefit of burst-based dynamics is therefore contingent on the baseline leakage established by the learning paradigm. 

## 7.2 Differentially Private Training 

Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DPSGD) provides a formal mechanism for lim-iting the influence of individual training samples during optimization. DPSGD clips per-sample gradients and adds calibrated noise before the parameter update, which bounds the contribution of any individual record to the learned model [63]. The resulting privacy guarantee is characterized by (ϵ, δ ), where ϵ represents the privacy budget. A smaller ϵ imposes a stronger privacy guarantee and requires greater perturbation during training, which can reduce predictive utility. As ϵ increases, the privacy constraint is relaxed, less perturbation is required, and classification performance can recover. The variation of utility across privacy budgets therefore provides a direct measure of the privacy–utility tradeoff. We use DPSGD to examine how this tradeoff interacts with Rate, Poisson-Burst, and Delayed-Burst temporal dynamics. The objective is to determine whether the temporal dynamics retain 30 (a) Iris (b) Breast Cancer Figure 17: Membership-inference vulnerability of SNNs trained with evolutionary learning and backpropagation under Rate, Poisson-Burst, and Delayed-Burst temporal dynamics on (a) Iris and (b) Breast Cancer. different utility characteristics when the level of privacy protection is explicitly controlled, and whether burst-based dynamics respond differently to the optimization noise introduced by DPSGD. DPSGD is implemented using Opacus [64]. We evaluate privacy budgets from ϵ = 0 .5 to 2 .0, with δ = 10 −5 and a maximum gradient norm of 1.0. For each target ϵ, Opacus calibrates the required noise level while the SNN architecture and temporal-dynamics configuration are otherwise maintained across experiments. 

7.2.1 Result and Analysis 

Figure 18 shows that, at an identical privacy budget, the temporal dynamics retain different amounts of accuracy. At strict budgets, the non-private ordering is preserved, with Delayed-Burst the most affected. As ϵ increases, Delayed-Burst recovers to the accuracy of Rate on both datasets, whereas Poisson-Burst remains approximately 7 percentage points below Rate, a deficit larger than that observed without privacy constraints. Since ϵ imposes the same privacy guarantee on all three dynamics, these differences reflect the utility that each dynamic sacrifices to attain equal protection. The origin of this cost becomes apparent when DPSGD is compared with the perturbations of Section 5. There, noise corrupted the spike representation itself, and burst-based dynamics lost more accuracy than Rate because the task-relevant information they carry resides in the temporal organization of spikes. DPSGD instead perturbs only the parameter updates and leaves the repre-sentation intact. Under this perturbation, Delayed-Burst, whose onset delay is bounded, matches Rate once the noise is reduced, which indicates that its earlier sensitivity was tied to corruption of the representation and not to the dynamic itself. Poisson-Burst, in which both burst occurrence and burst size are random, remains below Rate in both settings, which suggests that its intrinsic stochasticity compounds with the injected noise regardless of where the latter enters. Together with Sections 5 and 7.1, this shows that the effect of noise on a temporal dynamic depends on the stage at which it is introduced, the representation or the learning process, and on the intrinsic stochasticity of the dynamic on which it acts. The privacy–utility behavior of temporal dynamics is therefore an interaction effect and not an intrinsic property of the dynamics, consistent 31 (a) MNIST (b) F-MNIST Figure 18: Classification performance of Rate, Poisson-Burst, and Delayed-Burst temporal dynam-ics under increasing DPSGD privacy budgets on (a) MNIST and (b) F-MNIST. # 8 Discussion 

The temporal dynamics of SNNs determine how information about each input is represented and retained over time, and changes in these dynamics alter membership leakage even when the net-work architecture remains fixed. This shows that privacy in SNNs is not a fixed architectural property. The central issue is where this variation originates. A shift from Rate to Poisson-Burst or Delayed-Burst modifies several properties simultaneously, including spike timing, burst organiza-tion, stochasticity, and the temporal distribution of activity. The resulting privacy differences may arise from any of these factors, or from their interaction with neuronal dynamics and the learning process. The experiments in this study were designed to progressively separate these effects and identify the mechanisms that shape the observed leakage behavior. The initial comparison of homogeneous and heterogeneous temporal dynamics provides the first evidence that temporal organization itself contributes to the observed privacy variation. Under a common LIF architecture and learning procedure, Rate, Poisson-Burst, and Delayed-Burst pro-duce clearly different privacy-utility profiles. Burst-based dynamics generally reduce membership leakage relative to Rate, but the reduction is accompanied by lower classification accuracy. Intro-ducing temporal heterogeneity further shows that privacy does not improve simply by increasing the amount of burst-based activity. Moderate mixtures can reduce leakage while retaining much of the utility of Rate dynamics, whereas stronger heterogeneity can reduce accuracy without pro-ducing a corresponding privacy gain. The privacy effect is therefore linked to how information is 32 distributed across temporal patterns, rather than to the presence of bursting alone. One possible origin of this behavior is the additional stochasticity introduced by the burst-based dynamics. If randomness were the dominant source of privacy, then deliberately increasing stochastic variation should produce a consistent reduction in membership leakage. The perturbation experiments do not support that interpretation. Temporal jitter reduces timing precision and often decreases utility, yet its effect on leakage is non-monotonic. Input noise can reduce leakage in some settings, but the three temporal dynamics continue to exhibit different responses as the perturba-tion increases. Spike-level noise produces somewhat stronger reductions under heavy corruption, but the effect remains dependent on the underlying temporal structure. Across these settings, in-creasing stochasticity changes privacy, but does not reproduce a common or progressively stronger privacy effect. The results therefore separate stochasticity from the broader temporal mechanism: randomness contributes to leakage behavior, but it does not account for the privacy differences among the temporal dynamics by itself. The manner in which perturbation enters the system provides an additional distinction. Input noise alters the signal before temporal activity is formed, temporal jitter changes the timing of existing spikes, and spike noise directly modifies the generated spike sequence. DPSGD introduces variability at a different stage by perturbing the optimization process while leaving the temporal representation intact. These interventions do not produce equivalent responses. Delayed-Burst is highly sensitive under strict DPSGD budgets but recovers strongly as the privacy constraint is relaxed, whereas Poisson-Burst retains a larger utility deficit. The same temporal dynamic can therefore respond differently depending on whether variability acts on the representation itself or on the process through which that representation is learned. Privacy-related effects cannot be attributed to noise magnitude alone. They depend on the computational stage at which the perturbation interacts with temporal information. Temporal structure also does not act independently of the neuron that processes it. The com-parison between LIF and AdEx shows that richer intrinsic neuronal dynamics change both utility and membership leakage across the evaluated firing regimes. AdEx consistently reduces leakage relative to LIF while preserving or improving classification performance. This result is important because the source of temporal behavior is no longer confined to the input representation. Under AdEx, membrane evolution and adaptation shape how activity develops within the network itself. The privacy behavior of a firing regime therefore depends not only on the temporal pattern pre-sented at the input, but also on the internal dynamics through which that pattern is transformed. This places intrinsic neuron dynamics alongside temporal organization as a distinct contributor to the privacy-utility behavior of the network. The dependence becomes even clearer when the learning mechanism is changed. Under back-propagation, Poisson-Burst and Delayed-Burst generally reduce membership leakage relative to Rate. Under evolutionary learning, the Rate condition already produces MIA performance close to random guessing, leaving little observable privacy improvement for the burst-based dynamics while their utility continues to decline. The privacy advantage associated with a temporal dynamic is therefore conditioned by how the model learns from individual samples. Gradient-based optimiza-tion directly propagates sample-dependent error information through the temporal computation, whereas evolutionary optimization selects candidate networks through task-level fitness without the same form of gradient-based sample coupling. These different mechanisms alter the amount and form of training-specific information retained by the resulting model. A temporal dynamic cannot therefore be assigned a fixed privacy characteristic independently of the learning process in which it is used. Viewed together, the results support a more complete account of where membership privacy originates in these SNNs. Temporal dynamics determine how task information is distributed across 33 spike events and time. Stochasticity perturbs that representation, but its effect depends on where it is introduced and on the temporal structure already present. Intrinsic neuron dynamics determine how temporal activity is integrated and transformed as it propagates through the network. The learning mechanism determines how information from individual samples shapes the final model. Membership leakage emerges from the interaction among these processes. The lower leakage ob-served for a particular temporal dynamic is therefore not an intrinsic property of that pattern, but the outcome of how that pattern is generated, processed, and learned within a specific computa-tional configuration. This interpretation also changes how privacy-aware SNNs should be designed. Selecting a temporal dynamic solely because it produces lower MIA AUC in one experimental setting is unlikely to generalize across neuron models or training procedures. The heterogeneous results show that privacy gains can arise without completely replacing Rate dynamics, while the AdEx experiments show that modifying intrinsic neuronal behavior can improve privacy without imposing the same utility loss observed when stronger temporal structure is introduced only at the input. The learning experiments further show that a temporal modification can provide substantial privacy benefit in one optimization regime and little additional benefit in another. Privacy-aware design should therefore treat temporal dynamics, neuronal dynamics, and learning as coupled design dimensions. The objective is not to maximize bursting, stochasticity, or temporal complexity independently, but to identify configurations that reduce sample-specific distinguishability while preserving task-relevant temporal information. The present findings are bounded by the scope of the evaluated models and attacks. Privacy is measured through a consistent black-box shadow-model MIA, which enables controlled comparison but does not characterize all forms of information leakage. The analysis also considers a limited set of temporal dynamics and two neuron models, and evolutionary learning is evaluated only on the smaller tabular datasets because of its computational cost. These constraints leave an important next step: determining how membership information develops internally across time and network depth. Tracking the separation of member and non-member representations through successive temporal and neuronal states could reveal where the leakage signal first emerges and how different mechanisms suppress or amplify it. Extending the same analysis to additional neuron models, learning rules, neuromorphic datasets, and attack strategies would further establish the extent to which the interaction identified here governs privacy across SNN systems. 

# 9 Conclusion 

This work investigated the origins of membership leakage in SNNs by systematically separating the contributions of temporal representation, stochasticity, intrinsic neuron dynamics, and learning. The results show that privacy in SNNs is not a fixed property of spike-based computation or of any single temporal pattern. Biologically inspired temporal dynamics alter membership leakage through the way they distribute task information across spike events and time, but when imposed at the input they reduce leakage at a cost in accuracy. Stochasticity influences leakage without accounting for the differences among temporal dynamics, and its effect depends on the stage at which it enters the computation. Intrinsic neuron dynamics act as a distinct contributor, as adaptive neurons reduce leakage while preserving or improving classification performance. The learning mechanism further determines whether a temporal dynamic yields any observable privacy benefit. Membership leakage therefore emerges from the interaction among how temporal activity is generated, processed, and learned within a given computational configuration. This understanding provides a principled basis for privacy-aware neuromorphic design, in which designers select temporal dynamics, neuronal 34 dynamics, and learning mechanisms jointly rather than in isolation. Future work will examine how membership information develops across time and network depth. It will also extend this analysis to additional neuron models, learning rules, neuromorphic datasets, and attack strategies, with the goal of establishing design principles for spiking systems that remain both efficient and private in sensitive applications.
