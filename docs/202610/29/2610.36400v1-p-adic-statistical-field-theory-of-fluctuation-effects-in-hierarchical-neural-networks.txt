# p-Adic statistical field theory of fluctuation effects in hierarchical neural networks 

W. A. Z´ u˜ niga-Galindo ∗

University of Texas Rio Grande Valley, School of Mathematical & Statistical Sciences, One West University Blvd, Brownsville, TX 78520, United States 

(Dated: Draft of October 28, 2026) The Buice–Cowan field theory [PRE 75 , 051919 (2007)] describes fluctuations and correlations of neural activity through a path integral whose mean-field limit is the Wilson–Cowan equation and whose critical behavior belongs to the directed percolation class. In its continuum limit the neurons are the points of a Euclidean space and activity spreads by ordinary (local) diffusion. This description encodes neither the hierarchical organization of cortical networks nor their small-world property. We formulate a version of the theory in which the neurons are organized in an infinite, self-similar, tree-like structure, described by the p-adic numbers. The master equation, the operator formulation and the path integral carry over unchanged, and finite networks are exact restrictions of the continuum theory, not approximations of it. The main change is in the linear response. In the Euclidean theory every short-range connectivity kernel reduces to the same local operator, the Laplacian. In the p-adic theory the connectivity kernel itself gives rise to a nonlocal 

p-adic diffusion operator, the generator of a p-adic heat equation, and different kernels give different operators. For couplings that decay by a fixed factor per level of the hierarchy this is the Taibleson– Vladimirov operator, and the critical theory is an ultrametric Reggeon field theory whose upper critical dimension is twice the decay exponent of the coupling. Hence the rate at which the coupling decays up the hierarchy, and not the dimension of space, decides whether mean-field exponents apply. We obtain a Ginzburg-type criterion, loop-corrected hierarchical Wilson–Cowan equations, log-periodic corrections to scaling, power-law correlations, and the counterparts of the Buice–Cowan results on the phase transition and on self-organization. Exact simulations of the spike model on a tree support the predictions. The avalanche-size exponent takes the mean-field value three-halves when the coupling decays slowly, and decreases continuously toward the directed-percolation value when it decays fast. The simulations also show that the hierarchical network is a small-world network when the decay exponent is smaller than the dimension: connections are highly clustered, while path lengths and spreading times grow slowly, roughly as the logarithm of the number of neurons. The Buice–Cowan theory in its local diffusion limit does not have this property. In a Euclidean space it requires long-range coupling as an additional hypothesis, whereas in the p-adic setting long-range coupling is the only possibility. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

There are two central hypotheses about the functioning of the brain. The first is that the brain has a hierarchical organization [1, 2]. The second, the critical brain hypoth-esis , is that it operates near a phase transition, where a delicate balance between ordered (synchronized) and dis-ordered (random) states occurs; the neuronal avalanches observed by Beggs and Plenz support this view [3]. Since deep neural networks (DNNs) are inspired by the brain, both hypotheses are also relevant for them [4–6]. Sporns and co-workers proposed a precise form of the hierarchy hypothesis: cortical connections are organized in self-similar, fractal patterns across multiple nested scales [7–9]. This proposal provides a specific geomet-ric mechanism, nested and scale-free self-similarity, by which the brain builds its structural and functional hi-erarchy. It has three aspects. (i) Nested connectivity :pathways link neuronal populations across scales, from whole brain regions down to local minicolumns. (ii) Self-similarity : the patterns of connections repeat from one scale to the next. (iii) Small-world architecture : cortical

networks combine a high local clustering of connections with short paths between distant regions [10, 11], which allows efficient communication at a low wiring cost. As we explain below, nested self-similar connectivity is ex-actly the structure encoded by the p-adic numbers. It was shown in Ref. [12] that the classical Wilson–Cowan model, in which the neurons are organized as the points of RN and interact through Gaussian-type kernels, is not compatible with the small-world property. In this paper we examine the same question for the B-C theory. Buice and Cowan (B-C) [13] gave a systematic statis-tical field theory for the critical brain hypothesis. Start-ing from a Markovian “effective spike” model, they con-structed a Doi–Peliti path integral [14, 15]. They showed that its zero-loop (mean-field) limit is a Wilson–Cowan equation and that loops give systematic corrections con-trolled by a Ginzburg-type criterion. They also argued that the critical point lies in the directed percolation (DP) universality class [16–19]. In B-C the neurons live in RN with N = 2 or 3. The hierarchy therefore enters, at best, through the choice of kernel. A central problem is to develop new versions of B-C statistical field theories compatible with hypotheses about the hierarchical orga-nization of the brain. This is the objective of this work; however, here, we take only a first step toward it. arXiv:2610.36400v1 [cond-mat.dis-nn] 26 Oct 2026 2Another motivation is the correspondence between neural networks (NNs) and statistical field theories (SFTs). This correspondence is a promising approach for understanding the dynamics of DNNs [20–24]. Since DNNs are inspired by the structure and function of the biological brain, it is natural to conjecture that SFTs of B-C type can play a central role in understanding how large DNNs work. It is relevant to note that B-C de-veloped such a correspondence for cortical networks in the limit of infinitely many neurons, combined with a continuum limit in which the neuron label becomes a position in space [13, 25]. The correspondence between DNNs and lattice SFTs, with a large but finite number of neurons, has been studied intensively [26, 27]. How-ever, standard principles of statistical mechanics suggest that understanding the dynamics of DNNs requires un-derstanding their thermodynamic limit. Based on that, the author has proposed that the continuous SFTs de-veloped by B-C should be taken as the basis for a cor-respondence between DNNs and SFTs; see Ref. [28] and the references therein. The present work supports this proposal. In the field-theoretic approaches to DNNs, the natural large parame-ter is the width n of the layers, or the number of neurons. At fixed depth, the infinite-width limit is a Gaussian the-ory, and the non-Gaussian corrections come in powers of 1/n [26, 27]. The p-adic B-C theory differs in two ways. First, width and depth cannot be separated: a hierarchi-cal network of depth l in dimension N has pN l neurons, and its thermodynamic limit adds layers to the hierarchy at the same time as it adds neurons, so the infinite-width limit involves infinitely many layers. Second, the limit-ing theory is not Gaussian: its cubic couplings produce fluctuations that are not small near criticality below the upper critical dimension (Sec. V). The cutoffs of the the-ory, the depth of the tree and the size of the network, are part of the architecture, and a Ginzburg number plays the role of 1 /n . If the infinite-width limit is understood as a network with one layer and infinitely many neurons, the p-adic SFTs are of a completely different nature from the lattice SFTs of Ref. [26]. We discuss this comparison in Sec. VI A. 

Ultrametricity and p-adic numbers in physics. A dis-tance is ultrametric if it obeys the strong triangle in-equality, d(x, z ) ≤ max {d(x, y ), d (y, z )}. A non-empty set endowed with an ultrametric is called an ultrametric space. These spaces constitute the natural mathemati-cal framework for modeling hierarchical systems [29, 30]. In physics they first appeared in the theory of disor-dered systems. Parisi and collaborators showed that the pure states of the spin-glass phase of mean-field mod-els, described by replica symmetry breaking, are orga-nized in an ultrametric hierarchy [31]. Frauenfelder and co-workers proposed that the conformational substates of proteins are organized in a similar way, so that pro-tein relaxation resembles a random walk on a tree-like landscape of local energy minima [32]. The p-adic num-bers are the prototypical ultrametric spaces on which one can do analysis: they carry a measure, a Fourier transform, and pseudodifferential operators [33–37]. This made it possible to formulate replica symmetry breaking 

p-adically [38], and to describe relaxation in hierarchi-cal energy landscapes by p-adic diffusion equations [39]. Since then, p-adic models have been used in statistical mechanics, quantum field theory and string theory, and the theory of complex systems [30, 33]. p-Adic quan-tum field theory began in the 1980s under the influence of Vladimirov and Volovich [33], and it has produced an extensive literature [30, 33]. Most of this work concerns formal models, whose connection with measurable phe-nomena is indirect. To the author’s knowledge, the p-adic B-C theory is one of the first p-adic statistical field theo-ries that is derived, without free modeling choices, from the microscopic dynamics of a concrete system, here the master equation of a spiking network, and whose predic-tions can be compared with simulations and, in principle, with experiments. 

p-Adic statistical field theories and neural networks. 

In a series of papers, the author and collaborators have developed a correspondence between NNs and SFTs in which the fields are defined on a p-adic space instead of a Euclidean one [23, 24, 28, 40, 41]. In this framework, a field is a real-valued function on the boundary of an infinite tree-like structure that provides the architecture of a deep network. The correspondence was first estab-lished for deep belief networks [23]: whereas a restricted Boltzmann machine corresponds to a spin glass, a deep, hierarchical architecture corresponds to an ultrametric spin glass, which is described naturally with p-adic num-bers. Restricting the energy functional of a p-adic SFT to a finite-dimensional space of test functions gives a dis-cretization that is exact, and that yields a new class of networks, the p-adic convolutional deep Boltzmann ma-chines [40, 41]. Because of the weight sharing imposed by the symmetries of the tree, these networks have O(N )parameters for N neurons, instead of the O(N 2) of a fully connected machine. The same ideas were applied to dy-namical models: p-adic cellular neural networks, which are the continuum limits of large hierarchical versions of the Chua–Yang networks [42]; hierarchical Wilson– Cowan models [12]; and continuous random networks, for which a dynamic mean-field theory and a criticality condition were obtained [24, 28]. The exact discretiza-tion just mentioned is the mechanism used in Sec. III A of the present paper. A natural way to make a network intrinsically hier-archical is to index neurons by p-adic numbers [33–36]. Working in dimension one ( N = 1) for simplicity, the unit ball Zp is the boundary of a rooted tree in which each vertex has p children. The ultrametric distance |x − y|p

records the depth of the last common ancestor of x and 

y.We use the following terminology throughout. The lev-els of the tree play the role of the layers of the hierarchy, and we use the words level and layer synonymously. A vertex at level k, together with all the neurons that de-3scend from it, is a ball of radius p−k. We refer to it simply as a ball , or as a structure of level k: the ball is built from the p balls of level k +1 below it, just as higher structures in the brain are built from lower ones. The hierarchical distance between two neurons is |x − y|p: if it equals p−k,the smallest structure containing both neurons is of level

[13] as integrals over QNp with respect to the Haar measure. The purpose of this paper is to work out what changes and what does not. 

Results and comparison with the B-C theory. Our starting point is the observation that the stochastic model of B-C, its operator formulation and the result-ing discrete action do not refer to any geometry. The geometry enters only in the continuum limit, where B-C place the neurons at the points of RN . We show that if the neurons are instead organized in a tree-like structure, the same limit places them at the points of QNp , and the sums over neurons become integrals with respect to the Haar measure. In contrast with the Euclidean case, the finite network is not an approximation of the continuum theory: the discrete action is exactly the restriction of the continuum action to a finite-dimensional space of func-tions, a feature shared by other p-adic statistical field theories [23, 24, 28, 40]. Mean-field theory is again a Wilson–Cowan equation, now hierarchical, and the loop expansion of B-C carries over formally unchanged. The first substantial difference appears in the linear re-sponse. In the B-C theory, the expansion of the Fourier transform of the connectivity kernel at small momenta produces a local diffusion term, the Laplacian, the same for every short-range kernel. No such expansion exists over QNp . This does not mean that there is no p-adic diffusion. The theory of p-adic heat equations is vast, and there are many more such equations than in the Eu-clidean case [30, 34, 36]. What happens is that each con-nectivity kernel gives rise, in a natural way, to its own nonlocal diffusion operator, a W -operator, which is the generator of a p-adic heat equation. In the Euclidean theory the kernel is reduced to a universal local opera-tor; in the p-adic theory the kernel itself determines the 

p-adic diffusion operator, a W -operator [36, Chap. 2]. For kernels with compact support, the support of the kernel determines which neurons are truly connected. If the connected neurons form a ball, only its total activity becomes marginal at the critical point, and there are no scale-free correlations across the hierarchy. We also con-sider regional networks , in which the neurons occupy a disjoint union of finitely many (at least two) balls, as in the ultrametric transition networks of Ref. [43]. Each ball represents a region of the cortex in which the spike model holds with its own constant parameters, and the regions are coupled by constant interregional connections. When all regions share the same decay rate and firing-rate func-tion, this is the B-C model with a block-structured con-nectivity. Region-dependent parameters go beyond the B-C model, whose master equation has a single firing-rate function and a single decay rate for all neurons. The crit-icality of a regional network is decided by a finite matrix, and its critical behavior is of mean-field type (Lemma 1, Sec. IV A). On a bounded network, the linear dynamics is a Markov process exactly when the distance to criticality is nonnegative, and at the critical point this process be-comes conservative: branching exactly compensates de-cay (Sec. IV C). For isotropic kernels that decay by a fixed factor per level of the hierarchy, w(∥x∥p) ∝ ∥ x∥−N −σp at large distances with σ > 0, the operator is the Taibleson– Vladimirov operator of order σ, which we show to be the unique isotropic and homogeneous choice. The exponent 

σ is a parameter of the connectivity, not of the dynamics: each time one climbs one level of the hierarchy, the cou-pling between two neurons is multiplied by p−(N +σ), and the total coupling that a neuron receives from the neu-rons at that level is multiplied by p−σ . Thus σ measures how fast the coupling decays with hierarchical distance, and it can be read off from the connection weights. It is a new physical parameter with no counterpart in the B-C theory. Throughout the paper σ denotes this decay exponent. The second difference concerns criticality. B-C argue that the critical point belongs to the DP class, with upper critical dimension 4, and they invoke the high connectiv-ity of cortex to justify mean-field exponents in dimension 2 or 3. In the p-adic theory the effective action at criti-cality is an ultrametric Reggeon field theory whose upper critical dimension is Nc = 2 σ. Whether mean-field expo-nents apply is therefore decided by the decay exponent of the hierarchical coupling and not by the embedding dimension. Unlike Euclidean DP with long-range spread-ing [44], there is no competing local (Laplacian) term, so every σ > 0 defines a distinct class. The Ginzburg-type criterion of B-C becomes a statement about the level of the hierarchy that fluctuations reach before they relax, and the discrete scale invariance of the hierarchical neu-ron space, which is invariant only under the dilations 

x 7 → p−1x, produces log-periodic corrections to scaling, which a Euclidean theory cannot produce. In the mean-field regime the avalanche-size exponent is 3 /2, as in the B-C theory; for N < 2σ we predict deviations that de-pend on σ, and we confirm them by direct simulation of the spike model on a tree (Sec. VIII A). The third difference concerns the small-world property. For a network with weighted coupling we use two tests: the clustering and the path length of a sparse network whose connections are drawn with probabilities propor-tional to the coupling, and the time that activity needs to reach half of the neurons in the spike model. With both tests, the hierarchical network is a small-world network when σ < N : the clustering is high, and path lengths and spreading times grow slowly, roughly as the loga-rithm of the number of neurons (Sec. IX). For σ > N 

it is not. The B-C theory with the kernels of the classi-cal model, Gaussian or exponential, which for practical purposes have compact support, is not small-world: the 4spreading time grows in proportion to the linear size of the network. This agrees with Ref. [12], where the incom-patibility is traced to the fact that RN has no nontrivial compact subgroups, while in QNp every ball is one. A fi-nite Euclidean network can be made small-world, by our tests, only by leaving the classical model: by imposing a coupling with a power-law tail on a ring. In RN long-range coupling is thus an additional hypothesis, imposed on a theory whose generic limit is local diffusion. In QNp

all diffusion operators are nonlocal, long-range coupling is the only possibility, and it comes with the nested struc-ture of the hierarchy. The threshold σ = N differs from the threshold σ = N/ 2 for mean-field avalanches: the small-world property is necessary for the exponent 3 /2in our simulations, but it is not sufficient. Finally, we give the p-adic counterparts of the phase transition and of the self-organization mechanisms dis-cussed in Ref. [25]. The linear spectrum resolves exactly layer by layer. The zero-loop propagator is computed ex-plicitly and has power-law tails in hierarchical distance, so that correlations away from criticality are not expo-nentially truncated in space, in contrast with the Eu-clidean prediction. Power spectra of local averages scale as ω−(2 −N/σ ) below a crossover frequency set by the size of the averaging region. Standard self-organizing mecha-nisms, such as synaptic depression, tune the distance to criticality but leave σ unchanged. Throughout, we dis-tinguish results that are exact at zero-loop order from heuristic arguments and from conjectures. In particular, the analysis below the upper critical dimension, which re-quires the renormalization group, is not carried out here, which is why we regard this work as a first step. Reading guide. The paper is addressed to physicists, neuroscientists and researchers in artificial intelligence. It does not mean that we present a rigorous mathematical theory of the B-C SFTs; that remains a very difficult open problem.

In general one may consider a network topology consisting of a finite num-ber of clusters, each of them a finite rooted tree, with connections between the clusters. In the simulations of Secs. VIII A and IX we use a single finite rooted tree, which is acceptable and convenient. A hierarchical network is not a tree. A tree is a partic-ular architecture: a graph whose edges are the connec-tions. Hierarchical architectures are tree-like structures, topologies made of trees, such as a single rooted tree (ZNp ), a union of rooted trees with no root ( QNp ), or a finite number of connected clusters. In them the trees organize the neurons; they are not the connections. The neurons are the leaves, the internal vertices are modules (balls), and the couplings (11) join every pair of neu-rons, with a strength fixed by the level of their nearest common ancestor [Fig. 2(a)]. In particular, the model is not directed percolation on a Cayley tree (Bethe lattice). There, the units sit on all the vertices, activity spreads along the edges, and the number of sites within graph distance d grows exponentially in d. That lattice is ef-fectively infinite-dimensional, and DP on it is mean-field. Here the ball of radius pk has Haar volume pkN , so the neuron space has finite dimension N . This is why the upper critical dimension Nc = 2 σ of Sec. V is finite, and why fluctuation effects appear for N < 2σ.There is also a structural difference from the Euclidean case. On aZN the lattice action agrees with the con-tinuum action only as a → 0, because Riemann sums are approximations and convolution does not preserve piecewise-constant functions. On GNl the identity (16) holds for every l. The discrete theory is the continuum theory restricted to the finite-dimensional space Dl.

Remark 1 (Firing-rate function versus activation func-tion) . In neural-network and rate models, an activation function (a sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, . . . ) maps a unit’s (or node’s) input to its output (or state), determined by the inputs connected to the node and their weights. That output can take negative values; e.g., tanh represents deviations from a baseline rate in the symmetric range (−1, 1). The function f in the effective spike model dif-fers in three ways. 1. It is a transition rate, not a state. In the mas-ter equation (6), f (si) is the probability per unit time that neuron i emits another effective spike. It has units of inverse time and must be nonnegative wherever it is evaluated. The state of the neuron is the spike count ni, not f .2. It becomes an activation function only in mean field. In the Wilson–Cowan equation (21), obtained at zero-loop order, f plays the role of a determin-istic activation function acting on the mean input 

w ⋆ a + I. Beyond zero-loop order, f enters through all its derivatives, as the vertices Vn,m of Eq. (29), and it fixes the statistics of the fluctuations, not only the mean response. 3. Criticality constrains it at a point. The require-ments f (I) = 0, f ′(I) > 0 and f ′′ (I) < 0 concern the behavior of f at the operating point, not its global shape. The hyperbolic tangent fails them. Nonnegativity forces I = 0, where tanh ′′ (0) = 0. The cubic aggregation vertex then vanishes at zero-loop order and the leading saturating term is quar-tic (tanh ′′′ (0) = −2), which is the setting of tricrit-ical directed percolation rather than DP. Nonnegativity and analyticity together have a conse-quence. A real analytic function with f (0) = 0 and 

f ′(0) > 0 is negative for small s < 0, so the inputs must satisfy s ≥ 0. We therefore take w ≥ 0 and I = 0. As B-C note, inhibition cannot be distinguished from the decay rate α in the effective spike model [13], so net inhibition is represented through α. Models with negative weights require a nonnegative extension of f to s < 0 (e.g. f = 0 there), which is not analytic at s = 0.

> B. Isotropy, self-similarity and universality

The two hypotheses of Lemma 3 are idealizations of features of cortical hierarchies, and each mirrors a famil-iar Euclidean symmetry. 

Isotropy: no preferred branch. Each g ∈ GL N (Zp)maps every ball onto a ball of the same radius and pre-serves inclusions, so it acts as an automorphism of the tree: it fixes the balls centered at the origin and per-mutes, consistently at every level, the branches below each vertex. It also preserves the hierarchical distance. Invariance under this group means that the coupling be-tween two neurons depends only on their hierarchical dis-tance, i.e. on the level of their last common ancestor, and not on the ball (structure) of the hierarchy to which they belong. Physically, all the balls of a given level are equiv-alent: the pattern of connections inside a ball of radius 

pk, and between that ball and the rest of the network, is the same for every ball of radius pk, so no subtree is wired differently from its siblings. For N ≥ 2, isotropy also means that the N “directions” of branching are equiva-lent; the anisotropic case is discussed in Remark 2. For 

N = 1, condition (1) is automatic ( GL 1(Zp) = Z× 

> p

), and homogeneity alone forces Dσ . Isotropy is the hierarchical counterpart of rotational invariance in RN , which makes a Euclidean kernel depend only on |x − y|.

> V. CRITICALITY: ULTRAMETRIC REGGEON FIELD THEORY

Physical picture. We consider the network without external drive, f (I) = 0 [Eq. (39)], so that the silent state 

a ≡ 0, where a = ⟨φ⟩ is the mean activity (Sec. III C), is absorbing : once all neurons are silent, they stay silent. The absorbing state is an idealization. Here “silent” means that there are no effective spikes, the activity that propagates through the network, not that there is no elec-trical activity at all. Real cortex is never silent: even at rest it receives weak input and fires spontaneously. This corresponds to a small source f (I) > 0, which turns the absorbing state into a stationary state of low activity, 

a∗ ≃ f (I)/μ (Sec. X D). Resting cortical activity is de-scribed by this weakly driven state near μ = 0, in which spikes produced by the input start avalanches that spread and die out; the absorbing state is its limit as I → 0. A single spike triggers on average m = f ′(0) ˆ w(0) /α further spikes (the branching ratio), and μ = α(1 − m). For 

μ > 0 ( m < 1) every avalanche of activity dies out, and the network returns to the silent state. For μ < 0 ( m > 1) activity can sustain itself. Its growth is stopped by the 

saturation of f : the firing rate is bounded, f ≤ fmax , so the response grows less than proportionally to the input, and f ′′ (0) < 0 [Eq. (24)]. The network then settles in an active state of density ρ > 0. The value μ = 0 separates the two phases. This is a nonequilibrium phase transition into an absorbing state. When the neurons occupy Eu-clidean space RN , as in B-C, it belongs to the universality class of directed percolation (Appendix B). Here the neu-rons occupy QNp ; the transition is of the same type, but its critical behavior is governed by the hierarchical con-nectivity, through the operator W . At μ = 0 avalanches of all sizes occur, and their statistics follow power laws. Near μ = 0 the activity is small and slowly varying, while its correlation length and time diverge. Mean-field the-ory (the Wilson–Cowan equation) neglects the fluctua-tions of this sparse activity, and the question is whether they change the scaling laws. To answer it we expand the action about φ = ˜ φ = 0, which, since a ≡ 0 and ˜ a = 0, is the expansion about the mean field (Sec. III D), in powers of the fields, and keep only the terms that matter at large scales. The quadratic terms, ˜ φ(∂t + μ + W )φ, describe the linear dynamics of Sec. IV: propagation of activity through the hierarchy and its decay. The cubic terms describe the two elementary processes that the linear dy-namics misses. Branching , ˜ φ2φ: the spikes triggered by one active neuron are correlated, which is the source of fluctuations; their strength is proportional to the activ-ity itself (multiplicative noise). Aggregation , or satura-tion, ˜ φφ 2: two active inputs to the same neuron produce less than twice the response, which limits the growth for

Comparison with field theories of artificial neural net-works. In the field-theoretic approaches to DNNs, the natural large parameter is the width n of the layers, or the number of neurons [20–22, 26, 27]. At fixed depth, the infinite-width limit is a Gaussian theory, and the non-Gaussian corrections come in powers of 1 /n ; in Ref. [26] the effective expansion parameter is the depth-to-width ratio. Other works regularize the perturbative integrals with a cutoff in input space and obtain the Wilsonian running of the couplings by requiring that physical pre-dictions do not depend on it [21, 58]. The p-adic setting differs in two ways. First, width and depth cannot be separated. The network GNl has depth l and pN l neu-rons, and the thermodynamic limit l → ∞ (Sec. III A) adds layers to the hierarchy at the same time as it adds neurons: the infinite-width limit involves infinitely many layers. Second, the limiting theory is not Gaussian. The continuum action has the cubic couplings of Eq. (59), and for N < 2σ the fluctuations they produce are not small near criticality. The cutoffs Λ = l and L are part of the architecture, and the Ginzburg number (81) plays the role that 1 /n , or the depth-to-width ratio, plays in the works above. In addition, the fluctuations here come from the stochastic spiking dynamics, not from the ran-dom initialization of the weights. If the infinite-width limit is understood as a network with one layer and in-finitely many neurons, the p-adic SFTs are of a com-pletely different nature from the lattice SFTs of Ref. [26]. This supports the proposal of Ref. [28] that continuous SFTs of B-C type, rather than lattice SFTs with a large but finite number of neurons, should be the basis of the correspondence between DNNs and SFTs.

> VIII. AVALANCHES

An avalanche is the cascade of activity triggered by a single spike in an otherwise quiescent network. Two conditions make the background quiescent: ¯n = 0, i.e. there are no spikes initially, and f (0) = 0, i.e. there is no spontaneous firing, so that the quiescent state is ab-sorbing. The seed spike may make other neurons fire, each of these may trigger further spikes, and so on, un-til the activity dies out and the network returns to the absorbing state. An avalanche is thus a transient excur-25 sion from the quiescent state; it changes the activity but not the parameters α, f ′(0) and w, and hence not the distance μ to criticality. Avalanches are a probe, not a mechanism: their statistics, for example the mean size 

⟨S⟩ = 1 /μ R and the cutoff of the size distribution, reveal how close the network is to the critical point, but they do not change μ. Only a slow, activity-dependent plasticity acting on the parameters, i.e. a change of the synaptic strengths driven by the activity itself, such as synaptic depression, can bring the network there (self-organized criticality, Sec. X G). The size S of the avalanche is the total number of spikes, and its duration T is the time until extinction. In the language of directed percolation, an avalanche is a cluster grown from a single seed (Ap-pendices B 1–B 2), and its statistics are controlled by the distance to the DP transition: 

• in the absorbing phase ( μ > 0), a spike has on average fewer than one descendant; avalanches are finite, and P (S) is cut off exponentially beyond a cutoff size Sc: P (S) ≃ S−τS G(S/S c), where G(u)tends to a constant for u ≪ 1 and decays exponen-tially for u ≫ 1. The statement that τS departs from 3 /2 for N < 2σ

Model and method. The network is the tree Gl with 

p = 2, N = 1 and depth l = 40 (2 40 neurons, so that the boundary of the network is not reached on the simu-lated time scales, except marginally for the smallest σ). Two neurons i̸ = j have a last common ancestor in the tree; the neurons below it form the smallest ball contain-ing both. If this ball has 2 k neurons ( k = 1 , . . . , l ), i.e. if the common ancestor lies k levels above the leaves, then |i − j|2 = 2 k−l. The coupling between them is

f (si)/[f ′(0) si]. An avalanche starts from a single spike and ends when no spike is left. Its size S is the total number of spikes. Each run starts from a single spike, as in the spreading method of Grass-berger and de la Torre [63], and the critical point is located by requiring that N (t) be a pure power law. The exponents δ and ϑ are obtained by least-squares fits on logarithmic scales; τS is obtained by maximum likelihood for a power law truncated to the fitting win-dow [64], with bootstrap errors, and independently from the scaling relation τS = 1 + δ/ (1 + ϑ + δ) [60]. Whether the two networks differ at larger 

σ, where the Euclidean theory crosses over to ordinary directed percolation, is an open question. 

Comparison with the Euclidean model. The chain is more than a control: it is the Euclidean counterpart of the tree, with the same number of dimensions ( N = 1), the same local rules and the same total coupling. The two networks behave differently for a simple reason. On the chain a spike excites only its nearest neighbors, so the activity spreads as a compact patch and new spikes keep landing on neurons that already receive input. This crowding is what the saturation of f penalizes, and it drives the exponents away from their mean-field values: 

τS ≃ 1.11, far from 3 /2. On the tree with small σ the cou-pling decays slowly with the hierarchical distance, most new spikes land far from the active neurons, crowding is rare, and the avalanches are those of a branching process. As σ grows the activity stays inside small balls, crowding returns, and τS falls toward the Euclidean value. Hence in the Euclidean theory with short-range coupling the dimension of space decides whether the avalanches are mean-field, and the Beggs–Plenz exponent 3 /2 requires 

N ≥ 4; in the p-adic theory the decay exponent σ decides, and a network with N = 1 gives 3 /2 when σ ≤ 1/2. The shift of the critical point shows the same ordering: bc

grows from 1 .11 at σ = 0 .25 to 3 .16 at σ = 1 .5, and is largest for the chain, bc = 4 .85. Mean-field theory is therefore a better guide for a hierarchical network with slowly decaying coupling than for the Euclidean chain. Section IX relates this difference to the small-world prop-erty. This comparison is limited to one tree ( p = 2, 

N = 1) and one value of s0; other values of p and N were not simulated. Thus an estimate of σ from connectivity data, from how the coupling decays with hierarchical distance, pre-dicts whether the avalanche exponents are mean-field (σ ≤ N/ 2) or not ( σ > N/ 2); the size of the departure is, at present, known only from simulations.  

> IX. THE SMALL-WORLD PROPERTY

A network has the small-world property if it combines a high clustering of connections with short paths: the mean number of steps between two nodes grows as the logarithm of the number of nodes, as in a random graph, and not as a power of it, as in a lattice [10]. Cortical networks have this property [11]. In this section we ask whether the networks described by the p-adic theory and by the B-C theory have it.  

> A. Two tests for a network with weighted coupling

In the effective spike model every neuron is coupled to every other neuron, with a weight w that depends on the distance. Path lengths in the usual sense are then trivially equal to one, so the definition has to be adapted. We use two tests. 1. Sparse network. Each neuron draws k connections, and the probability of a connection to a given neu-ron is proportional to w. On the resulting network we measure the clustering coefficient C (the proba-bility that two neighbors of a neuron are connected to each other) and the mean path length ℓ.2. Spreading time. We simulate the effective spike model itself, with the full weighted coupling, in the active phase. One spike is placed in a network of 

n neurons, and T1/2(n) is the mean time at which half of the neurons have fired at least once. Runs in which the activity dies out are discarded. The network is small-world if C stays finite while ℓ and 

T1/2 grow as ln n, or as a power of ln n. 

> B. A simple estimate

Consider the kernel (53), w ∝ ∥ x∥−N −σp , and a ball of level m, which has pmN neurons. The total coupling of one neuron to the neurons outside the ball is proportional to p−mσ . The coupling of the whole ball to its exterior is therefore proportional to 

pmN p−mσ = pm(N −σ). (85) If σ < N this grows with m: the larger the structure, the more strongly it is connected to the rest of the network, at every level. Once a ball is active, its activity passes to the next level in a time that does not grow with m, and the time to reach n = plN neurons is at most of order 

l ∝ ln n. If σ > N the quantity (85) decreases with m:large structures are almost isolated, activity must wait a time of order pm(σ−N ) to leave a ball of level m, and the spreading time grows as the power n(σ−N )/N . The clustering is high in both cases, since most of the coupling 28 of a neuron stays inside the smallest balls that contain it. This estimate gives the threshold 

σ < N (86) for the small-world property. It is the same threshold that separates the two regimes of long-range percolation on a hierarchical lattice [65] and on a Euclidean lat-tice [66, 67]. It is also the condition under which the random walk generated by the Taibleson–Vladimirov op-erator is transient, since R ∥k∥−σp dN k converges at k = 0 exactly when σ < N . (Heuristic.)

> D. Comparison with the B-C theory

The master equation and the action of B-C are written for a general weight function, so the formalism does not exclude a small-world network. The restriction enters in the continuum analysis of the critical point. There the connectivity is assumed to be short range, its Fourier transform is expanded as ˆw(q) ≃ w0 − 12 w2q2, which gives the diffusion term D∇2, and the Ginzburg criterion uses the moments w2, w 4, . . . [Eqs. (50)–(52) of Ref. [13]]. These hypotheses exclude the small-world property. Akernel with power-law tail |x|−N −σ produces a small-world network only for σ < N [66, 67], and then its moment of order N is infinite. A finite fourth moment forces σ > 4 > N for N ≤ 3. (A finite second mo-ment alone does not suffice when N = 3: a tail with 2 < σ < 3 has a finite second moment and gives a small-world network, while the critical behavior remains that of DP.) Under the hypotheses of B-C, activity passes only to nearby neurons, the time to reach n neurons grows as the power n1/N , and the network is not small-world. This is the case represented by our short-range ring. By our tests, which concern finite networks, the prop-erty is recovered within the B-C formalism if the hypoth-esis is dropped and the kernel is taken to have a power-law tail: w bounded for |x| < 1 and w(x) ∝ | x|−N −σ

for |x| ≥ 1, with σ < min( N, 2). The cutoff at short distances is necessary, because the pure power law is not integrable at the origin. With it, w is integrable for every 

σ > 0, although its second moment is infinite for σ ≤ 2. Integrability is what the mean-field theory requires. The Wilson–Cowan model on a general locally compact group was studied in Ref. [12] under the hypothesis that the kernels are integrable. The existence proof uses only the inequality ∥w ⋆ a ∥∞ ≤ ∥ w∥1∥a∥∞, so that it holds on RN

as well: for an integrable kernel and a Lipschitz firing-rate function the Wilson–Cowan equation has a unique global solution in L∞. Hence the long-range Euclidean model is well defined at the mean-field level. In our sim-ulations the neurons are on a lattice, r ≥ 1, and the kernel is summable. The B-C theory in its diffusion limit describes networks that are not small-world. The small-world property requires long-range cou-pling. In the Euclidean setting this coupling must be imposed as an additional hypothesis, on a theory whose generic limit is local diffusion. In the p-adic setting all diffusion operators are nonlocal (Sec. IV A), so long-range coupling is the only possibility, and the property holds for σ < N . What distinguishes the p-adic theory from a finite Euclidean network with a power-law coupling is the hierarchy: nested balls, discrete scale invariance and log-periodic corrections, none of which the Euclidean kernel has. 

Relation to the compactness argument of Ref. [12]. 

Reference [12] treats the small-world property of the Wilson–Cowan model in the continuum, with a different and stronger criterion: the interaction between any two groups of neurons must be non-negligible, w(x) ≥ ε > 0for all x. The kernels must also be integrable, since this is the hypothesis under which the Wilson–Cowan equations are well posed on a general group. The two conditions to-gether force the group to be compact. The group ( RN , +) has no nontrivial compact subgroups, which is a conse-quence of the Archimedean axiom. Hence the classical model, whose Gaussian or exponential kernels have com-pact support for practical purposes, is incompatible with the small-world property, and by this criterion so is every integrable kernel on RN , including one with a power-law tail. In QNp the balls are compact subgroups. On a ball every positive radial kernel satisfies the criterion, and the theory restricted to a ball is again a theory of the same type (Proposition 1). The two approaches answer different questions and are consistent. The criterion of Ref. [12] concerns the contin-uum model on the whole space. Our tests concern finite networks of increasing size, and they separate the kernels on a ball further: path lengths and spreading times grow logarithmically only for σ < N . The Euclidean ring with power-law coupling used in our simulations does not con-tradict the compactness argument. A ring is a compact group, but it is not a subgroup of RN : it is obtained by imposing periodic boundary conditions, and the power-law coupling is a further hypothesis foreign to the classi-cal model. In the p-adic setting the space does not have to be changed: every finite network is a ball, and every ball is a compact subgroup. 

Small-world property and avalanches. The threshold 

σ = N is not the threshold σ = N/ 2 of Sec. VIII. There are three regimes. For σ ≤ N/ 2 the network is small-world and the avalanche exponents are mean-field. For 

N/ 2 < σ < N the network is still small-world, but the avalanche exponents are not mean-field: at σ = 0 .75 we found τS ≃ 1.37 (Table II). For σ > N the network is not small-world and the exponents approach the short-range values. In our simulations the small-world property is thus necessary for the exponent 3 /2, but not sufficient, and the avalanche exponents alone cannot be used as a test of it. 

Relation to fast transition modes. In ultrametric models of transition networks, the hierarchical organi-30 zation of the states produces fast transition modes that are absent in an ordinary Markov chain [43]. The loga-rithmic spreading time found here for σ < N is an effect of the same kind, since activity leaves each ball at a rate that does not decrease with its size. We have not made this connection quantitative. 

Limitations. The tests were made for one prime, N =1, one value of b and network sizes up to 2 18 . The laws quoted above describe the range studied; in particular, the data do not distinguish ln n from a power of ln n for 

σ close to N .

> E. Power spectra of local field potentials

The review derives the 1 /ω 2 spectrum of local field potentials (LFP) and of the electroencephalogram (EEG) from the propagator of the homogeneous mode, i.e. at zero momentum [R(150)–R(152); there the momentum is denoted p, which in this paper is the prime]. An electrode averages activity over a region. In the p-adic model we take that region to be a ball B of radius pk. The Fourier transform of its indicator is exactly pkN Ω( pk∥ξ∥p), so the ball average retains precisely the modes with ∥ξ∥p ≤ p−k,the levels coarser than the electrode, with no leakage. 

Gaussian approximation. We treat the fluctuation 

δφ = φ − a∗ about the stationary state of Sec. X D as a Gaussian random field. This is the linear noise approxi-mation of van Kampen’s system-size expansion [72]: the action is expanded about a∗ and only the terms quadratic in δφ are kept, so the fluctuations obey a linear equa-tion driven by noise whose covariance is evaluated at a∗,and are therefore Gaussian. The neglected cubic terms are the vertices whose one-loop effect is measured by the Ginzburg criterion of Sec. VI, so the approximation is controlled where that criterion holds: for N > 2σ up to the critical point, and for N < 2σ when μ is not too small. In addition, the electrode signal is an average over many neurons; when the ball B contains many correla-tion volumes (radius of B much larger than ξ⊥), it is a sum of many weakly dependent contributions and is close to Gaussian by the central limit theorem. Close to μ = 0 with N < 2σ the fluctuations are not Gaussian, and the results below do not apply.

> G. Self-organized criticality

In the review (Sec. 12.9 there), the homogeneous equi-librium and its stability are αφ 0 = f (w0φ0 + h0) and

Plasticity. In neuroscience, synaptic plasticity is the ability of synapses to change their strength in response to the activity of the neurons they connect [74]. It acts on several time scales. Short-term plasticity, such as synaptic depression, lasts from milliseconds to seconds: repeated firing temporarily depletes the resources (neu-rotransmitter vesicles) that a synapse can release, and weakens it until they recover. Long-term plasticity lasts from minutes to much longer. It includes Hebbian rules, in which a synapse is strengthened when the two neu-rons it connects are active together, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity, in which the sign of the change de-pends on the order of the pre- and postsynaptic spikes. Homeostatic plasticity slowly rescales synaptic strengths to keep firing rates within a range. In the model, plastic-ity is a slow dynamics of the connectivity w, and hence of μ, driven by the activity a or by the correlations C;the mechanisms below are of this kind. 

What must be tuned. By Eq. (88), criticality ( μ = 0) is a single scalar condition on the layer profile {Wk}. It is not sufficient on its own. Every level must also re-main stable, ϱγ ≤ 0 in Eq. (90). Otherwise a hierarchical pattern instability precedes the DP transition. 

Synaptic depression. Section VIII argues that only a slow, activity-dependent plasticity can bring the network to the critical point. The standard example is short-term synaptic depression: Levina, Herrmann and Geisel [75] showed that networks with depressing synapses self-organize to criticality, with the avalanche exponent 3 /2, over a wide range of parameters. μ. We ask what this mechanism does in a hierarchical network. Conclusion. In the p-adic B-C theory, the standard self-organizing mechanisms tune the distance to critical-ity μ but leave the hierarchy exponent σ untouched. This matters because μ and σ control different things: μ de-cides how close the network is to the critical point, while 

σ decides which critical behavior it shows there, mean-field avalanches ( τS = 3 /2) for σ ≤ N/ 2 and anomalous ones for σ > N/ 2. Plasticity can therefore explain why cortex is near criticality, but not which exponents it dis-plays: these are fixed by the architecture. Experiments that alter plasticity should change the distance to criti-cality, and hence the cutoff and the mean avalanche size, but not the critical exponents. Changing σ requires a mechanism that depends explicitly on the level, such as 35 a wiring cost or conduction delay that grows with hier-archical distance. Whether σ is tuned dynamically by such a mechanism, or fixed by development, is an open problem (Sec. XI).  

> XI. DISCUSSION AND OPEN PROBLEMS

The p-adic theory as an extension of the B-C picture. 

Buice and Cowan argued, using field-theoretic methods, that their effective spike model with an absorbing state has a phase transition in the directed-percolation univer-sality class, and that the avalanche-size exponent takes its mean-field value 3 /2, the value observed by Beggs and Plenz [3, 13], when fluctuations are negligible: ex-actly for N ≥ 4, the upper critical dimension of DP, and approximately in cortex, by their Ginzburg argument (Sec. V). In the p-adic model the upper critical dimen-sion is Nc = 2 σ, and the exponent 3 /2 holds for N ≥ 2σ.Since the Taibleson–Vladimirov operator is defined for every σ > 0, the p-adic theory gives a one-parameter family of ultrametric DP classes, with Nc ranging over (0 , ∞). At σ = 2 its power counting and mean-field ex-ponents coincide with those of the B-C model. In this sense the p-adic theory extends the B-C picture, and the 3/2 law no longer requires N ≥ 4: on a hierarchical net-work it holds even for N = 1 when σ ≤ 1/2. In Eu-clidean L´ evy-flight DP, by contrast, Nc = 2 σ only for 

σ < 2, since ∇2 takes over for σ ≥ 2 [44] (Sec. V). More generally, the p-adic B-C theory provides a framework to study criticality in large deep neural networks for which a thermodynamic-continuous limit is available: in hier-archical architectures this limit exists and is exact, and the criterion σ ≤ N/ 2 and the Ginzburg number then decide whether the critical behavior of the network is mean-field.

Not DP on a Cayley tree. The tree enters the two models in different ways. In DP on a Cayley tree (Bethe lattice), the units sit on all the vertices of the tree, in-ternal ones included, and activity spreads only along its edges. Distance is graph distance, and the number of units within distance d grows exponentially in d; the lat-tice is effectively infinite-dimensional, and DP on it is mean-field. In the p-adic model the tree is not the graph along which activity spreads. Only its leaves are neu-rons, and the internal vertices label the modules (balls) of the hierarchy. Every pair of neurons is coupled directly, with a strength fixed by the level of their last common ancestor, and a ball of radius r contains a number of neurons proportional to rN , a power of the radius. The neuron space therefore has finite dimension N , and the theory has a finite upper critical dimension Nc = 2 σ, like Dyson’s hierarchical model [53, 54]. The simulations of Sec. VIII A show branching-process avalanches at small 

σ and departures from them as σ grows. 

Relation to Ref. [24]. That work treats Gaussian-noise-driven continuous networks (Martin–Siggia–Rose type). There, criticality is defined through the largest Lyapunov exponent in a double-copy system, on both real and p-adic neuron spaces. The present theory is Poissonian (Doi–Peliti) and has an absorbing state, and its criticality is DP-like. Comparing the two notions of criticality on the same p-adic architecture is a natural next step: when do the edge of chaos and the absorbing-state transition coincide? 

Disorder and Griffiths phases. Hierarchical networks with quenched structural heterogeneity display extended critical (Griffiths) regions [76]. The p-adic model is translation-invariant under ZNp and has a sharp transi-tion. Level-dependent quenched disorder in w or α in-terpolates between the two pictures. B-C’s remark that spatiotemporal disorder preserves DP [19] should be re-visited in the ultrametric setting.

Refractoriness. The two- and three-state Cowan ac-tions (B-C Appendix C) transfer directly to QNp .Whether refractoriness drives the model toward an ultra-metric version of dynamic isotropic percolation is open.

> Appendix A: Field theory of branching processes: a primer

This appendix explains the field-theoretic tools used in the main text. It is written for readers from neuroscience or machine learning who are not familiar with quantum field theory (QFT). We work with a simple branching process on a network that contains all the ingredients of the effective spike model of Sec. III. At each step we in-dicate the corresponding objects of the main text. Stan-dard references are Refs. [14, 15, 19, 52, 77, 78].  

> 1. Core concepts

What a branching process is. A branching process is a model of a population in which each individual (a “parti-cle”), independently of the others, disappears or produces new individuals at random times. The classical example is the Galton–Watson process of population genetics [79]. In our setting a particle is an effective spike , i.e. a spike that still influences the network [13], and particles live on the nodes i of a network (neurons, or small groups of neurons). We use a single branching process through-out this appendix. Its dynamics consist of three kinds of elementary random events: decay: Ai → ∅ rate α, 

branching: Aj → Aj + Ai rate βK ij ,

saturation: Ai + Ai → Ai rate λ. (A1) Here Ai denotes one effective spike at node i, and the rates are per particle (for saturation, the total rate at node i is λn i(ni − 1)). 

• Decay. An effective spike stops influencing the net-work. Each spike does so at rate α, so its influence lasts on average 1 /α .

• Branching. An effective spike at node j causes a new spike at node i. For each spike at j this hap-pens at rate βK ij , where Kij ≥ 0 is the strength of the connection from j to i. This is the only event that increases activity. 

• Saturation. When two effective spikes are present at the same node, one of them is removed, at total rate λn i(ni − 1). This limits growth. With decay and branching only, one obtains the classical, linear branching process: each spike independently has on average m = β P 

> i

Kij /α offspring during its lifetime, and in the supercritical regime ( m > 1) activity grows exponentially without bound. Saturation is the simplest way to keep activity bounded, as real firing rates are.

Absorbing state and criticality. If there are no parti-cles, nothing can happen: the empty network is an ab-sorbing state . Whether activity survives depends on the

Why fluctuations matter. The simplest description of the process is a deterministic rate equation for the mean activity, derived below; see Eq. (A5). It ignores fluctua-tions. The number of events in a short time is random, with a variance proportional to the number of particles. Relative fluctuations are therefore large when activity is low, which is exactly the situation near an absorbing state. A systematic theory of fluctuations is needed, and field theory provides one.

> 6. Universality

Coarse-graining and relevance. Near a critical point, correlations extend over lengths and times much larger 40 than the microscopic ones. The large-scale behavior can then be studied by coarse-graining: integrating out fluctuations on short scales and rescaling. This is the renormalization group (RG). Under this procedure each coupling in the action grows, shrinks or stays constant. Growing couplings are relevant , shrinking ones irrelevant ,and the others marginal . Irrelevant couplings do not af-fect the large-scale behavior. Consequently, microscopi-cally different models that share the same relevant cou-plings have the same critical exponents. They belong to the same universality class .

Power counting. Whether a coupling is relevant can be read off from dimensions. With diffusive scaling, [x] = −1 and [ t] = −2 in momentum units. After a rescaling of φ and ˜ φ that makes the branching and sat-uration couplings equal (both = g/ 2), [ φ] = [ ˜ φ] = N/ 2and [ g] = 2 − N/ 2. The cubic couplings are therefore relevant for N < 4, while the quartic term λ ˜φ2φ2, with dimension 2 −N , is irrelevant near N = 4. The remaining action,

> Appendix B: Directed percolation and Reggeon field theory

This appendix explains the universality class to which the critical point of the effective spike model belongs. Directed percolation (DP) is a simple lattice model. Reggeon field theory (RFT) is the field theory that de-scribes its large-scale behavior; see Refs. [19, 52, 78].  

> 1. Directed percolation as a model of spreading

The lattice model. In this appendix, p denotes a prob-ability, not a prime number, and N denotes the number of spatial dimensions of the lattice (the time direction is not counted); it plays the role of the dimension N of the neuron space in the main text. Place sites on a lattice in which one direction plays the role of time (Fig. 9). Each site is connected to two sites in the next time step, by bonds that are open with probability p and closed with probability 1 − p, independently. A site at time t + 1 is active if at least one open bond connects it to an ac-tive site at time t. Starting from a single active site (the 

seed ), the active sites form a cluster that spreads forward in time. Because bonds can only be followed forward in time, the percolation is directed .

Bonds are synapses that transmit a spike with probability p, and a cluster started by one spike is a neuronal avalanche .For a machine-learning reader, each time step is a layer of a deep feedforward network with random binary con-nections and “OR” units: a unit fires if at least one of its inputs fires. The question “does a signal injected at the input reach arbitrarily deep layers?” is exactly the question of whether a DP cluster survives.

> 4. Rapidity reversal

In the neural reading, the exponent governing how long an avalanche lasts equals the one governing how fast activity decays after global stimulation.
