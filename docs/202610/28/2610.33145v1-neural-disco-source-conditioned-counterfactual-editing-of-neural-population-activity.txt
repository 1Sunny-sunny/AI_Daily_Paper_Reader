# Neural-DISCO: Source-Conditioned Counterfactual Editing of Neural Population Activity 

Nico Policzer ∗ James Ho ∗ Joseph Soo ∗ Xin Tang †

Michael Smith Laboratories and Department of Computer Science University of British Columbia Vancouver, BC, Canada 

## Abstract 

Understanding how neural activity encodes behavioral and sensory variables is a key challenge in systems neuroscience. This requires models that go beyond pre-dicting activity from labels and instead support source-conditioned counterfactual editing: given a recorded source response, we should be able to edit one measured behavioral or stimulus label and predict how neural activity would change while preserving other observed labels and source-specific variability not explained by the labels. Here, we introduce Neural-DISCO , a disentangled representation learning framework for counterfactual modeling of neural activity. Neural-DISCO partitions the latent space into label-specific components, each associated with an observed behavioral or stimulus label, together with a residual latent that cap-tures variability not supplied through observed labels. Across simulated and real neural recording datasets, we show that this structure enables selective editing of measured labels to generate counterfactual neural responses while preserving the remaining content of the original activity. Further, to gain insight into how behavioral and stimulus labels are encoded at single-neuron resolution, we pair this disentangled framework with feature attribution methods that identify which neurons are most important for each label. We find that disentanglement enhances the recovery of known functional cell roles in both synthetic and real data. To-gether, these results support Neural-DISCO as a framework for counterfactual modeling of neural activity and provide a practical interface for probing how neu-ral populations encode behavior and sensory information. ## 1 Introduction 

Modern neural recording technologies record hundreds to thousands of neurons while animals move, make decisions, and receive structured sensory input. These data make it possible to relate neural population activity to simultaneously recorded behavioral and stimulus labels, a central goal in systems neuroscience [1–9]. Many questions, however, are not purely predictive. We often want to ask how a neural population response would change if a measured variable were altered—for example, if the animal occupied a different position, reached in another direction, or ran at a different speed—while keeping the remaining source-specific state as intact as possible. We call this problem source-conditioned counterfactual editing , borrowing counterfactual terminology from causal inference while using it operationally rather than claiming causal identification from observational data alone [10]. Given a source response x and observed label values (y1, . . . , y K ), the goal is to replace one label value ya by ˆya and generate neural activity for the edited condition while

A model of p(x′ | ˆya, y −a) can sample plausible activity consistent with the target labels, but unless it is conditioned on the source response, it generally marginalizes over internal state, spontaneous movement, and unmeasured task variables [3, 13, 14]. This source-conditioned counterfactual setting instead requires representations that retain residual content of the source sample. We note that only measured labels are editable; unmeasured variation may be preserved in the source residual but is neither identified nor directly editable. We introduce Neural-DISCO , a DISentangled learning model for source-conditioned COunterfactual editing of neural population activity (Fig. 1). Each measured behavioral or stimulus label is encoded by its own embedding network into corresponding label-specific latents, while a separate residual encoder maps the source spike counts to a residual latent. A generator then decodes the concatenated latents into firing rates. To reduce observed-label leakage into the residual latent, we corrupt the residual with Gaussian noise during training and penalize its activation norm. This bottleneck biases the model to route observed factors through their designated label latents while preserving residual source-specific variability in the residual latent. At test time, Neural-DISCO encodes a source response, replaces one label-specific latent with the embedding of a target value, keeps the residual and other label-specific latents fixed, and decodes the edited representation into firing rates. The same factorization also supports neuron-level interpretation. Because labels enter the generator through label-specific blocks, we compute integrated gradients to obtain label-specific attribution scores for each neuron [15]. We evaluate Neural-DISCO in three settings that probe complementary failure modes: a rat neural activity simulator with known functional cell classes and ground-truth edited rates; macaque reaching electrophysiology, where edited activity is evaluated by held-out behavioral decoding; and a mouse visual encoding Neuropixels dataset, where running-speed and grating stimulus orientation edits are evaluated jointly with stimulus preservation and independent unit-level tuning metrics. Across these settings, Neural-DISCO improves the balance between edit fidelity and residual preservation relative to classical Generalized Linear Model (GLM) baselines. ## 2 Related work 

Neural latent-variable models and behavior-conditioned generation. Latent-variable methods such as GPFA, LFADS, and demixed PCA summarize population activity in low-dimensional representations [16–18]. Recent generative models, including LDNS, NEDS, pi-VAE, and GNOCCHI, model neural activity conditionally on behavioral labels or learned neural codes [19–22]. Neural-DISCO instead targets source-conditioned editing: the edited sample should encode a target label while retaining residual content from a particular source response. 

Residual neural structure and behaviorally targeted dynamics. Single-trial neural variability reflects internal state, spontaneous movement, unmeasured task variables, and recording noise [2, 3, 9, 13, 14]. Related methods identify task-specific subspaces [23], separate behaviorally relevant from other neural dynamics [24–27], or distinguish shared and private neural–behavioral representations [28, 29]. Neural-DISCO is complementary: it provides an editable factorization that makes label encodings directly addressable while preserving residual source-specific structure. 

Disentangled representation learning and counterfactual editing. Disentangled representation learning traditionally includes approaches based on constrained VAEs, mutual-information objectives, and factorial latent priors [30–32]. However, unsupervised disentanglement is not identifiable without inductive biases or supervision [33], motivating our supervised factorization. Counterfactual or attribute editing in generative models often aims to vary selected attributes or labels while preserving source content [34–36]. Our bottleneck design is closest in spirit to LORD and biolord, which use asymmetric noise regularization to separate observed labels from residual latents [37, 38]. Neural-DISCO adapts this principle to neural spike counts with a Poisson observation model, multiple behavioral or stimulus labels, source-conditioned editing, and neuron-level attribution. 

Neuron-level interpretation. Classical neuroscience interprets neurons through tuning and modula-tion analyses relating firing rates to stimuli or behavioral state [39–42]. Recently, xCEBRA provides a complementary approach to identifiable attribution maps for neural time series [43]. BrainDiVE synthesizes images predicted to activate cortical regions [44, 45], while MIG-Vis visualizes the stimulus semantics of neural latent groups through diffusion [46]. These methods produce stimulus images; Neural-DISCO predicts edited neural responses while retaining source residual structure.

Counterfactual semantics. We use “counterfactual” operationally. Outputs should be interpreted as model-based edits, not as identified causal effects from observational recordings. Poisson sam-pling after label latent replacement yields the corresponding interventional distribution under three assumptions: the true Poisson spike generator factorizes as in Appendix A.5; interventions leave the unedited labels and source state fixed; and the model recovers the true label maps, generator, and source-state encoding. 

3.3 Neuron-level label attribution 

We quantify label-specific contributions to each neuron’s pre-softplus generator output using in-tegrated gradients [15], along a straight path from zeroed observed labels and a zero residual to the source input. We first average signed attributions across 32 randomly selected source samples, then take the mean absolute value over each label’s input dimensions or the residual dimensions. Appendix A.4 gives the precise definition. 

3.4 Baselines and ablations 

We compare with a no regularization baseline (w/o reg.), a zero residual baseline, pi-VAE [21], and NEDS [20], and Gaussian and Poisson GLMs. The GLMs linearly project the observed labels to predict neural activity under their corresponding distributions. The Gaussian GLM is included as a linear editing baseline; we use it to edit by replacing the projection of the source label with the projection of the target label. The zero residual control removes Neural-DISCO’s source residual during editing. The no regularization baseline is an architecture-matched ablation without any infor-mation bottleneck in the residual ( λ = σ = 0 ). pi-VAE, NEDS and the Poisson GLM do not expose the same source-conditioned residual-editing interface. For counterfactual evaluation, we therefore use their target-conditioned generation interface with the edited labels. These comparisons test whether source-conditioned residual preservation provides benefits beyond strong target-conditioned baselines; they should not be read as claiming that these baselines were designed for the same editing interface. For the Allen Visual Coding evaluation, speed editors share the training-fold mean-speed target; methods within each task share decoders trained on real training activity. Checkpoints are selected using validation folds. Scores from the held-out test epoch are averaged across folds, then across sessions for the 32-session analysis. 54 Experiments 

We evaluate whether Neural-DISCO supports source-conditioned counterfactual editing, as well as standard label-conditioned generation. The experiments address three questions. First, when the data-generating factors and counterfactual firing rates are known, does the model change the requested label while preserving non-target and unobserved structure? Second, in real motor cortical recordings without ground-truth counterfactual spikes, does generated activity decode the target behavioral label rather than remain source-like? Third, in a larger visual cortex dataset, can the same factorization average out a specified behavioral label or edit the encoding of a specific stimulus label, while preserving held-out stimulus and behavioral information, and producing unit-level attributions aligned with independent biological metrics? For our non-simulated datasets, there is no ground-truth neural response for performed edits. We therefore use an indirect decoding-based evaluation strategy. We use decoders trained only on real neural activity data to evaluate whether generated activity encodes both the target label, as well as labels unobserved by the model which should be conserved through the residual latent. Unless otherwise stated, values are means across ten folds; ± values and error bars denote the standard error of the mean (SEM). For our Allen Visual Coding dataset editing, we report the mean and SEM across 32 sessions. Appendix A gives preprocessing and training settings; Appendices A.12 and A.13 report mixed-selectivity and sampling controls. 

4.1 RatInABox simulation 

We first evaluate Neural-DISCO on a controlled RatInABox simulation dataset, where an agent moves in a 2×1 meter enclosed environment and both functional cell classes and ground-truth counterfactual firing rates are known [52]. The population contains 100 place cells [53], 100 head-direction cells [54], and 100 speed cells [55], whose rates depend on position, head-direction and speed respectively. Neural-DISCO receives position and head direction as observed labels, while speed is withheld and can only be represented through the residual latent. This creates a direct test of both label-specific editing and residual preservation. 

Attribution recovers simulated cell classes. For each label, we use the corresponding neuron-level attribution score to classify the matching cell type and report area under the ROC curve (AUC). Position attribution should identify place cells, head-direction attribution should identify head-direction cells, and residual attribution should identify speed cells as speed is unobserved. Cell-type recovery is higher at the strongest bottleneck than at λ = 0 (Fig. 2b). The residual representation is also organized by held-out speed (Fig. 2c), consistent with the residual pathway preserving source-specific structure not supplied by the observed labels. 

Counterfactual edited rates match ground-truth targets. The simulator allows direct comparison between generated rates and the true rates under an edited behavioral label state. We evaluate two edits. In the position-shift edit, the source trajectory is confined to the left half of the environment and the position label is shifted to the right half, while head direction and speed are unchanged. The desired output should match target place-cell rates, while preserving source speed-cell and head-direction cell rates. Neural-DISCO follows the shifted place-cell target while preserving speed-cell activity, whose corresponding behavior is unobserved, through the residual latent (Fig. 2d,e). In the head-direction edit, head direction is reversed while position and speed are unchanged. The model should change head-direction-cell activity, preserve place-cell activity, and preserve speed-cell activity through the residual. Neural-DISCO again tracks the edited target rates for the manipulated label while retaining non-target and held-out components (Fig. 2f,g). The no regularization model without the bottleneck shows the expected failure mode: the residual pathway can carry label information, so replacing an observed-label embedding does not reliably make the decoded activity target-like. As an additional diagnostic, we train variants in which each behavioral variable is withheld in turn and must be represented through the residual. These diagnostic tests yield the same conclusion. Appendix A.1 reports these withheld-factor tests; Appendix A.16.1 tests generalization when position–heading combinations are withheld during training. 64.2 Macaque reaching electrophysiology 

We next evaluate Neural-DISCO on real motor and premotor cortical recordings from a center-out reaching task in the Neural Latents Benchmark [56]. The dataset contains 137 recorded units. Unlike the simulator, the recordings contain unobserved variability, recording noise, and correlated behavioral structure, and there is no ground-truth neural response for a hypothetical edited reach. We therefore evaluate edited activity through behavioral decoding rather than direct rate error. We also benchmark label-conditioned generators and use inconsistent behavioral targets to check whether decoding simply tracks the requested labels (Appendix A.9). 

Edited activity decodes as the target reach. For each held-out source trial, we select a target trial with matched task structure and a different reach trajectory. Here we jointly replace the hand-position, hand-velocity, and eye-position latents with target-trial label latents to test whether the generator can produce a target-like behavioral episode while retaining the source residual. Synthetic control neurons provide an attribution sanity check. This dataset does not provide ground-truth behavioral importance labels for individual units. We therefore add synthetic constant-rate control neurons as a negative-control population. These controls are Poisson spike trains with constant behavior-independent firing rates matched to the dataset mean firing rate. They should receive low attribution to behavior, whereas recorded motor and premotor units are more likely to contain movement-related structure [40, 41]. We use the total attribution to all three behaviors in our analyses. Real-neuron recovery AUC increases with λ over the positive range (Fig. 3d). This pattern is consistent with the counterfactual results: bottlenecking the residual discourages leakage of observed behavioral information and makes label-specific attribution more accurate. This is a sanity check rather than biological ground truth, because not every recorded unit is expected to be modulated by the observed labels.

Finally, we evaluate Neural-DISCO on 32 recording sessions from the Allen Visual Coding Neuropix-els dataset [58, 59]. These recordings sample multiple visual and related brain areas while mice view visual stimuli and run on a wheel, a behavioral variable known to modulate visual cortical responses [42]. ## 5 Discussion 

Neural-DISCO provides a source-conditioned interface for counterfactual editing of neural population activity. The key design choice is a supervised factorization with a bottlenecked residual: observed labels remain directly addressable, while source-specific variability that is not supplied through those labels can still be preserved. This design targets two common failure modes. A purely label-conditioned generator cannot preserve residual structure from source spiking, whereas an unconstrained residual pathway can leak the edited label and prevent the edit from taking effect [37]. The experiments support this edit–preserve trade-off. In RatInABox, where edited rates and functional cell classes are known, Neural-DISCO edits position or head direction while preserving withheld speed, and its attributions recover simulated cell classes. In macaque reaching recordings, generated activity decodes as the target reach, whereas the unregularized ablation remains source-like. In the multi-session Allen Visual Coding dataset, Neural-DISCO is able to edit orientation and speed while preserving unobserved labels. Further, its label attributions align with dataset-provided unit metrics. 

Neural-DISCO for Neuroscience. Neuronal responses to stimuli are not determined solely by the presented stimulus, but are modulated by latent aspects of brain state such as arousal, locomotion, and attention [42, 60, 61]. Neural-DISCO addresses this by providing a flexible editing framework in which the relationship between observed labels and neural activity is conditioned on the preserved source-specific residual latent. Two source responses with identical labels can therefore yield different counterfactual edits if they differ in their residual neural state. We believe this capability can make Neural-DISCO a useful tool for neuroscientists seeking to understand how both observed and unobserved aspects of brain state modulate neural representations. We explore this on the Allen Visual Coding dataset by reversing the editing operation: we keep the label inputs fixed, replace a source response’s residual with that of another response with the same orientation and similar running speed, and measure how each neuron’s predicted orientation tuning changes (Appendix A.14). Across neurons, we find that the variability in Neural-DISCO’s predicted firing rates over different source residuals recovers the extent to which temporal frequency, which is withheld from the model, modulates each neuron’s firing. This proof of concept demonstrates that Neural-DISCO counterfactuals can expose testable hypotheses about how unobserved factors of brain state modulate neural responses. 

Limitations. Neural-DISCO counterfactuals are model-based predictions under a learned factor-ization, not causal effects from observational data alone [10]. As in prior counterfactual modeling [62], interpretations are most reliable within the training support and when edited labels can vary independently; edits violating dependencies among behavior, stimuli, and internal state should be treated as extrapolations. Attribution scores likewise measure model sensitivity rather than proving causal or exclusive encoding by individual neurons. The residual bottleneck can introduce a trade-off: a weak residual channel may harm reconstruction and preservation of unobserved labels, whereas an overly strong residual channel can leak observed-label information and make edits ineffective. Our capacity bound and sensitivity analyses characterize this trade-off but do not guarantee disen-tanglement, especially under correlated labels, missing variables, or dataset shift, consistent with known limitations of disentanglement without sufficient inductive bias or supervision [33]. The model is static, without temporal or transition dynamics. Finally, in real neural data, validation remains indirect because decoder-based assays cannot replace ground-truth edited responses from perturbation experiments, matched repeated trials, or more realistic simulators. 

Broader Impact. Neural-DISCO is intended for in-silico hypothesis generation in computational neuroscience, using available neural recordings. A.1 Additional RatInABox Figures Withheld Behavior Flows Through Residual We perform a diagnostic test to verify that held-out behavior is routed through the residual latent. We train three models on our RatInABox dataset, each receiving two observed labels while the third behavioral variable is held out. The three settings correspond to withholding position, head direction, or speed. In Fig. 5, we visualize the residual latent across all models and find that the residual is consistently organized by the withheld behavior, while the observed behaviors are not strongly separated. In Fig. 6, we quantify the decodability of each label from the residual latent, again showing that the withheld behavior is more decodable from the residual than the observed behaviors. Together, these support our findings that the residual latent carries information primarily about unobserved source-specific factors. 

RatInABox Attribution Heatmaps. In Fig. 9, we plot attribution heatmaps learned by our model for all RatInABox neurons. We include both the standard model, and the unregularized model without the noisy residual bottleneck. We observe the expected block structure: in the standard model, position, head-direction, and residual attributions are concentrated on place, head-direction, and speed cells, 15 respectively. In contrast, the unregularized residual control shows broader residual attribution and weaker factor specificity. 

Example Counterfactual Rates across Cell Types . In Fig. 10 we plot the firing rates of three example cells, one from each cell type, before and after both counterfactual edits. In the position-shift edit, the model predicts the target place-cell rate while maintaining the source head-direction and speed-cell rates. In the head-direction edit, generated rates track the target head-direction response while retaining place- and speed-cell structure. All three cell types are compared with their simulator target rates. 

Ablated Model Position Shift Decoding In Fig. 11, we plot the source and target position shift movement trajectories, alongside our model’s counterfactual prediction and our ablated model’s counterfactual prediction. To extract trajectories from our model’s predictions, we train a position decoder on real neural activity, and then decode from both model’s predictions. While our model successfully encodes the target trajectory, the ablated model decoded trajectory looks much less similar to the target. Quantitative decoding results for this evaluation are in Fig. 2e. 

A.2 Additional Macaque Reaching Figures 

In Fig. 12 we plot the decoding R2 of our ablated model against both source and target trajectories for our macaque reaching edit task. Across behaviors, we observe that the ablated models’ encoded neural activity remains source like - decoding each behavior reveals high R2 against the original behaviors, while low, negative R2 against the edit targets. A.6 Hyperparameter selection and reporting 

We selected dataset-specific hyperparameters using validation diagnostics and preliminary pilot runs, then fixed one configuration for each dataset–model pair before running the cross-validation experiments reported in the main text. The final configurations are reported in Table 12. The 

λ-selection criterion favors high validation edit fidelity and low supplied-label decodability from the clean residual, averaged across ten validation folds. Reconstruction loss provides an additional diagnostic; checkpoint selection uses validation loss. The unregularized condition is an architecture-matched ablation in which the residual bottleneck is disabled by setting both the residual activation penalty and residual noise to zero. This ablation tests the necessity of the bottleneck mechanism and was not used to choose the reported Neural-DISCO hyperparameters. We also report sweeps over the residual regularization coefficient λ to evaluate how bottleneck strength affects attribution and residual leakage, as in Fig. 4, Fig. 3, and Fig. 2. These sweeps are sensitivity analyses rather than hyperparameter-selection curves for the reported cross-validation results. Initialization and training use seed 314159 + fold index, held fixed across λ. Fold indices run from 0 to 9. The sweeps retain residual noise σ = .02 for RatInABox and Allen and .04 for MC Maze, including at λ = 0 ; the zero-penalty point therefore differs from the λ = σ = 0 ablation.

A.16 RatInABox synthetic dataset experiment details Dataset generation. We use the RatInABox generator [52] to generate synthetic neural spiking activity, simulator firing rates, and associated behavioral variables. The synthetic environment has aspect ratio 2:1 and scale 1 m. Dataset generation uses random seed 42. The simulation timestep is 50 ms, the duration is 20 min, the initial position is (0 .5, 0.5) , and the mean speed is 0.2 m/s. We generate four trajectories: • Train: standard random-walk trajectory. • Inverted: the same trajectory as train , but temporally reversed. • Shifted: a trajectory spatially confined to the left side of the environment, x ≤ 1.• Cfact_shifted: the same as shifted , but with a +1 offset along the x-axis. 

Synthetic cell types. The synthetic population contains 300 neurons: 100 place cells, 100 head-direction cells, and 100 speed cells. 

Loaded arrays and observed variables. Each generated RatInABox file contains 24,000 bins. Spike counts and simulator rates have shape (24000 , 300) , position has shape (24000 , 2) , head direc-tion has shape (24000 , 2) , and velocity has shape (24000 , 2) . Neural-DISCO receives position and head direction as observed editable factors. Speed is withheld from the observed-factor embeddings, so speed-related information can only be preserved through the residual latent. 

Splits and counterfactual edits. RatInABox uses contiguous sample splits rather than trial-grouped splits. Each fold contains 17,280 training samples and 1,920 validation samples. The held-out test loader uses the first 20% of the inverted trajectory, corresponding to 4,800 samples. For counterfactual generation, the model keeps the source residual and swaps the configured target behavior blocks. In the position-shift edit, the target position comes from the shifted counterfactual trajectory while head direction and speed are held fixed. In the head-direction edit, head direction is reversed while position and speed are held fixed. Since the simulator provides ground-truth rates under the edited variables, generated rates are compared directly against the edit’s simulator target rates by cell group: place cells, head-direction cells, speed cells, and all cells.

A.17 MC Maze Experiment Details Dataset. We use the MC Maze reaching dataset from the Neural Latents Benchmark / DANDI dataset 000128/0.220113.0400 , subject sub-Jenkins . Spikes are loaded from 

train_spikes_heldin ; no additional unit filtering is applied beyond the NLB held-in unit se-lection. The dataset contains 137 recorded units together with paired behavioral trajectories. Spike counts are binned at 50 ms, yielding 14 bins per trial in the benchmark representation. We use the NLB train split for 10-fold cross-validation and reserve the public NLB validation split as the held-out test set for decoding analyses. 

Observed factors and preprocessing. The observed editable factors are hand position, hand velocity, and eye position, each represented as a two-dimensional trajectory. We do not apply additional spike smoothing beyond the benchmark binning. Behavioral targets are normalized per coordinate before training and decoding. 

Splits. The NLB train split contains 1,721 trials and the public validation split contains 574 trials. After flattening 14 time bins per trial, these correspond to 24,094 train-split samples and 8,036 held-out samples, respectively. Cross-validation is grouped by full trials rather than individual bins so that all time bins from a trial remain in the same fold. In fold 0, the model split contains 21,672 training samples and 2,422 validation samples. 

Training details. The MC Maze Neural-DISCO model is trained for 100 epochs with train/validation/test batch sizes of 512/256/256, learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −5, and scheduler decay factor γ = 1 . The behavior latent dimension is dy = 96 for each observed factor and the residual latent dimension is dres = 128 . The factor encoder, residual encoder, and generator have depths of 3/4/4, with residual penalty λ = 0 .48 and residual noise standard deviation σ = 0 .04 . The unregularized control uses the same architecture and optimizer settings with λ = 0 and σ = 0 .

Source-target pairing and counterfactual construction. For each held-out source trial, we choose a target trial with matched task context and a different reach trajectory. Pairing uses the 

context_matched strategy: candidate targets must match the source trial on trial_version ,

num_targets , num_barriers , and active_target , and must differ in target direction by at least 

30 ◦. Among the eligible candidates, the target trial is chosen by nearest standardized delay and reaction time. Pairing uses seed 0. On the public validation split, this procedure retains 557 source-target pairs from 574 trials. In the main counterfactual experiment, the hand-position, hand-velocity, and eye-position blocks are jointly replaced with the target-trial values while the source residual is held fixed. 

Decoder training and attribution controls. Downstream MC Maze decoders are trained only on real training-split spikes and behavior. We use a KNN decoder on CEBRA embedding with the offset10-model , batch size 1000, learning rate 10 −4, temperature 1.0, output dimension 8, 15,000 training iterations, cosine distance, time_delta conditioning, time offset 10, KNN with 

K = 36 , cosine metric, and seed 0. For paper figures, the same trained decoder is applied to source spikes, target spikes, and generated activity. Generated activity is obtained by Poisson sampling from the counterfactual Neural-DISCO rates before downstream decoding. For attribution sanity checks, recorded neurons are augmented with synthetic constant-rate control neurons, and we report AUC for 26 distinguishing recorded units from these controls using the total attribution score to all behaviors for each neuron. 

A.18 Allen Visual Coding Neuropixels experiment details Spike binning and preprocessing. We use only spiking and behavior during drifting-grating stimulus presentations, filtering out gray periods in between drifting stimuli. For speed editing, running speed and stimulus orientation are used as observed factors, while drifting-grating temporal frequency is withheld from the observed-factor embeddings for preservation analyses. We remove presentations with blank or invalid orientation labels. In session 756029989, the filtered dataset contains 378 training/CV presentations and 222 held-out test presentations. Unit filtering uses strict inequalities 

SNR > 1 and isi_violations < 0.5, leaving 672 units in session 756029989. Spike trains and running speed are binned at 50 ms resolution over each 2 s drifting-grating presentation, yielding 40 bins per presentation. 

Data splitting. Train/test splitting is by drifting-grating epoch: the first two drifting-grating epochs are used for model training and cross-validation, and the final epoch is reserved as held-out test data. Cross-validation splits are grouped by presentation_id using deterministic contiguous 

np.array_split partitions of sorted presentation ids. In fold 0 of session 756029989, the training split contains 13,600 bins from 340 presentations, and the validation split contains 1,520 bins from 38 presentations. The held-out test split contains 8,880 bins from 222 presentations.

Training time. Training the Allen 10-fold models across the reported λ values takes approximately 1 GPU hour on an H200 GPU.
