# Abstract 

Decoding simultaneous and proportional finger kinematics from high-density surface electromyography (HD sEMG) remains challenging. Although data-driven methods have shown promising performance for finger movement decoding, their limited physiological interpretability remains a relevant limitation, particularly because physiologically grounded elements are often absent from their architectures. This study proposes and evaluates a spiking neural network (SNN) that incorporates spinal-inspired structures and mechanisms to predict multi-joint finger kinematics from HD sEMG signals. The spinal-inspired SNN includes mechanisms such as a spike-encoding computational model with input-noise variability, a phenomenological representation of Henneman’s size principle, and excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance. HD sEMG and kinematic data were collected from 23 healthy participants performing grasping tasks under different elbow and wrist postures. Performance was assessed using six prediction metrics and compared against six baseline machine learning and deep learning methods. Non-parametric statistical analyses were performed to evaluate differences in the physiological explorations and performance comparisons. The proposed method showed a large and significant (𝑝 < 0.05, rank-biserial correlation 𝑟 𝑟 𝑏 > 0.7) improvement over all baseline methods across all accuracy metrics. Performance improved significantly with increasing spike encoder firing rates up to approximately 128 Hz (encoder amplitude 𝜃 = 250, with no further significant improvements in the coefficient of determination ( 𝑅 2) at higher firing rates), and more regular input spike trains compared with a Poisson-like encoder. In contrast, the E/I balance did not significantly affect the decoding performance of the algorithm. The firing rate and inter-spike interval statistics of the SNN differed significantly across motor tasks. These results demonstrate that incorporating neurophysiological principles into SNNs improves prediction performance while providing additional physiological interpretability for simultaneous and proportional finger-kinematics decoding. This approach represents a promising step toward physiology-informed neural networks, with potential applications in motor rehabilitation.

# 1 Introduction 

The human hand plays a central role in performing daily tasks, supporting not only grasping and object manipulation but also haptic exploration [1], skilled tool use [2], expressive actions through manual gestures [3], and social communication through gesture and touch [4]. These functions rely on the precise coordination of multiple fingers, while individuated arXiv:2610.33979v1 [cs.HC] 25 Oct 2026 Spinal-inspired SNN for finger-joint kinematics estimation from HD sEMG A Preprint 

finger control enables highly skilled actions such as writing [5], drawing or painting [6], and musical performance [7]. Natural hand movements arise from the interaction between independent finger control and synergistic neural commands that coordinate the fingers into smooth, refined motor patterns [8]. Therefore, accurately decoding hand and finger kinematics from neuromuscular signals is highly relevant for developing intuitive human-machine interfaces and neurorehabilitation strategies. Data-driven methods have been extensively applied in motor rehabilitation, using muscle or brain signals as interfaces [9–11]. Despite their potential impact, several challenges remain in both neurorehabilitation interventions and data-driven approaches. On the one hand, regarding interventions, it is still unclear: (i) how to interpret the underlying neuromuscular or neurophysiological mechanisms involved during the intervention [12]; (ii) how to evaluate their effectiveness using neural-based metrics to support the development of personalized strategies; and (iii) which neural-based measures could serve as reliable references for guiding interventions [13]. On the other hand, data-driven methods face additional challenges, including: (i) a strong dependence on large amounts of data [14], which are often difficult to obtain in clinical settings; (ii) performance variability associated with signal processing choices, feature extraction, and model architecture [10]; and (iii) the limited incorporation of physiologically grounded elements into their architectures, which restricts their interpretability from a neuromuscular perspective. These challenges highlight the need to develop methodological alternatives based on physiologically inspired approaches that can improve the simultaneous and proportional decoding of kinematic variables during movement execution. A promising solution involves designing methods with more physiologically accurate representations within data-driven frameworks to address challenges associated with interventions and data-driven approaches. Recent studies have proposed physics-informed deep learning approaches to predict muscle force and joint kinematics [15–18], demonstrating that incorporating physical laws into training can match or exceed the performance of traditional machine learning methods. Beyond traditional deep learning, SNNs offer a more biologically plausible framework that represents neural information as discrete spike events while incorporating membrane potential dynamics, subthreshold synaptic integration, and the temporal accumulation of inputs [19]. However, most SNN applications in sEMG signal processing have focused on gesture classification tasks [20, 21], often relying on relatively simple architectures with limited biological realism and primarily targeting neuromorphic implementations [21–24]. Although these approaches were evaluated in upper-limb motor tasks involving various hand movements, their application to gesture discrimination limits knowledge of the ability to predict natural, continuous movements. More recently, SNNs have been applied to the continuous regression of finger kinematics from sEMG, using spiking transformers for low-latency prosthetic control [25], heterogeneous populations of leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons with variable thresholds and time constants to encode sEMG [26], and benchmarks of temporal models across subjects [27]. In a related direction, shallow SNNs have decoded individual finger forces from motor unit spike trains and spike-encoded intramuscular EMG recorded with high-density microelectrode arrays [28]. However, these approaches target computational efficiency, encoding robustness, or cross-subject generalization, without organizing the network according to the principles of spinal motor control. In parallel, other studies have explored physiologically grounded computational models as alternatives to purely data-driven approaches. For example, Maksymenko et al. proposed the concept of a myoelectric digital twin [29], a physiologically realistic and computationally efficient simulation framework that generates synthetic data for training deep learning algorithms. In a related direction, Caillet et al. [30] implemented a motor neuron pool model calibrated using HD sEMG signals, which described force-generation dynamics through a Hill-type muscle model driven by the identified motor neuron activity. The latter approach enabled the extraction of subject- and muscle-specific neuromechanical information, providing a more physiological description of motor unit activation and improving muscle force prediction. Similarly, motor neuron pool models with explicit recruitment [31] and biophysically detailed simulators of spinal cord circuits [32, 33] describe how descending commands are transformed into motor unit activity and muscle force. Still, they operate as forward-simulation models rather than as trainable decoders. These studies support the development of computational methodologies that move beyond conventional data-driven approaches by integrating biophysical and neuromechanical models, thereby enabling a more comprehensive representation of complex biological processes. However, to the best of our knowledge, the use of physiologically inspired models for the simultaneous and proportional decoding of continuous hand and finger kinematics remains less explored. In this study, we introduce a spinal-inspired SNN that incorporates neurophysiological principles of spinal motor control and motor neuron pool organization. The method integrates a spike-based encoding of the sEMG input, a phenomenological representation of motor neuron recruitment inspired by Henneman’s size principle, E/I balance, and stochastic input variability. We evaluated how these physiologically inspired components influence the prediction of 15 hand joint angles from HD sEMG signals recorded from three motor tasks and multiple upper-limb postures. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to incorporate principles of spinal motor control into a trainable SNN to enable simultaneous and proportional decoding of continuous finger joint movements. 2Spinal-inspired SNN for finger-joint kinematics estimation from HD sEMG A Preprint 

# 2 Methods 

2.1 Experimental dataset 

Twenty-three healthy volunteers participated in the study. Of these, twelve identified as male and eleven as female, with twenty-one being right-handed and two left-handed (both male). All recordings were performed on the right hand. Participants were recruited across different age ranges, from 20 to 70 years, organized in 10-year intervals. Each age group included at least two male and two female participants. Overall, the sample had a mean age of 42.30 ±14.85 years, body mass of 75.04 ±14.23 kg, height of 167.91 ±9.28 cm, and forearm circumference of 26.29 ±2.70 cm. All participants were fully informed about the experimental procedures and voluntarily signed an informed consent form prior to their inclusion in the study. The experimental protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Campinas (UNICAMP), under protocol number CAAE: 80299124.2.1001.5404, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. Metadata describing the database is available in [34]. 

2.1.1 Signal acquisition 

Simultaneous recordings of HD sEMG signals were obtained from proximal forearm muscles — Flexor Digitorum Superficialis (FDS) and Extensor Digitorum Communis (EDC) — together with hand kinematics estimated using a multicamera system [35] (see Figure 1A). Specifically, muscle activity was acquired using two electrode grids of 64 channels each (8 × 8 configuration, 10 mm inter-electrode distance; GR10MM0808, OT Bioelettronica, Italy), operating in monopolar mode with a sampling frequency of 2052 Hz, using the wearable HD sEMG acquisition platform described in [36], based on two 64-channel RHD2164 amplifiers (Intan Technologies, USA). Kinematic recordings were performed using five high-speed cameras (DFK-37BUX287, The Imaging Source 𝑇 𝑀 ) equipped with 5 MP lenses (TCL 0814, The Imaging Source 𝑇 𝑀 ), arranged around a cubic structure of 0.34 m 3. The cameras captured hand motion at 100 Hz (FPS) with a resolution of 720 × 540 pixels, using IC Capture software (The Imaging Source 𝑇 𝑀 ). Simultaneously, to regulate the frequency of movement execution, a virtual interface was presented to the participants on a monitor located approximately 1 m in front of them. This interface displayed a virtual hand indicating both the type of movement and the frequency at which the task should be performed. The interface was developed using the Unity Engine (Unity Technologies, USA) and presented movements at a frequency of 0.5 Hz. Synchronization between the acquisition systems and the virtual interface was ensured through a command station that sent trigger pulses to the cameras and simultaneously transmitted a synchronization signal via Wi-Fi over TCP/IP to the sEMG system and Unity’s interface. 

2.1.2 Procedures 

The acquisition procedure began with an initial assessment of the participant’s physical characteristics, history of injuries, and physical activity. Subsequently, each participant completed tests of laterality and manual dexterity using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [37] and the Nine Hole Peg Test [38]. However, the results of these tests are out of the scope of the present report. Prior to data acquisition, participant preparation included cleaning the forearm skin with light abrasion using an exfoliating paste, followed by application of 70% alcohol. The two HD sEMG electrode grids were placed over the FDS and EDC muscles following a standardized protocol [39], and secured with adhesive bandages to ensure steady contact throughout the experimental session. Reference electrodes were positioned on the wrist using a water-moistened conductive band. Additionally, prior to kinematic recordings, the camera system was spatially calibrated for 60 s using a ChArUco board (see Figure 1A). Participants performed finger movement tasks in a seated position (see Figure 1A). The tasks included a one-finger pinch (1FP), a two-finger pinch (2FP), and a full-hand grasp (FG). The tasks were combined with five wrist postures, corresponding to the combined rotation of the forearm and wrist (supination–pronation) from 0 ◦ (supination) to 180 ◦

(pronation) in 45 ◦ increments, and two elbow postures (flexed and extended), resulting in a total of 30 experimental conditions (tasks × wrist postures × elbow postures) (see Figure 1B). For conditions involving an extended elbow, slight variations in the distance between the participant and the monitor were required. Due to the cameras’ limited field of view, participants were positioned slightly farther back under these conditions to ensure the hand remained within the frame, thereby avoiding the need for system recalibration. For each participant, the order of task execution was fully randomized, and each condition was performed for 30 s. Note that this corresponds to a total of 15 repetitions per task, given that the movement frequency was set to 0.5 Hz in the interface. Participants were instructed to follow as closely as possible the sinusoidal finger movements displayed by the virtual hand in Unity. 2.2 Data pre-processing 

• HD sEMG signals : The initial and final 2 s of each recording were discarded to exclude the transient at task onset, during which participants synchronized their movements with the virtual hand; the same duration was removed at the end for symmetry, which, at the 0.5 Hz movement frequency, corresponds to one complete movement cycle at each end. The remaining data were subsequently divided into training (20 s), validation (3 s), and test (3 s) sets. HD sEMG signals were then preprocessed independently within each set using a zero-phase notch filter centered at 60 Hz to attenuate power-line interference, followed by a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth band-pass filter with cutoff frequencies of 10 and 500 Hz. Preprocessing each set independently prevented information leakage between the training, validation, and test sets. Muscle activation envelopes were subsequently obtained by computing the RMS of each HD sEMG channel using a normalized rectangular sliding window. Min–max scaling was applied to the RMS signals, with the scaling parameters estimated exclusively from the training set and then applied to the validation and test sets. 

• Joint Kinematics : For the reconstruction of three-dimensional (3D) finger kinematics from the recordings obtained with the five cameras, the MediaPipe Hand framework [40] was first used to independently process the recordings from each camera, detecting and tracking 21 virtual hand skeleton landmarks in 2D coordinates. Subsequently, Anipose [41] was used to reconstruct 3D trajectories from 2D trajectories. The reconstruction initially relied on calibration recordings acquired prior to the experiments to estimate the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of each camera. Then, the triangulation was performed to obtain the 3D coordinates ( 𝑥 , 𝑦 , 𝑧 ) of each marker at every time point. Triangulation retained MediaPipe detections with a confidence score ≥ 0.6 and applied the spatiotemporal optimization of Anipose with segment-length constraints. 3D reconstruction was assessed by the reprojection error of the camera calibration and of the hand landmarks, the latter reflecting the agreement of the 2D detections across cameras. Joint angles were computed for the carpometacarpal (CMC), metacarpophalangeal (MCP), thumb interpha-langeal (IP), and proximal (PIP) and distal (DIP) interphalangeal joints, yielding three angles per finger (see Figure 2). The computation of angles at the MCP and thumb carpometacarpal joints employed an orthogonal projection strategy, as these joints are not constrained to a fixed plane due to abduction/adduction, which can affect flexion/extension estimation. To this end, two vectors were defined using three markers: proximal, central (target joint), and distal. These vectors were then projected onto a plane perpendicular to a direction vector ®𝑑 , defined toward the MCP of the nearest adjacent finger. For the thumb, ®𝑑 was estimated using virtual markers located at the wrist. The joint angle was then computed between the projected vectors using the

The angle was computed directly between two vectors defined by three markers, also using the 𝑎𝑡𝑎𝑛 2(·) function. For all joints, 0 ◦ corresponded to full extension (aligned segments), and angles increased with flexion. A similar biomechanical model was recently applied with a marker-based optical motion-capture system [42]. Following angle estimation, the kinematic data were resampled by linear interpolation to match the sEMG sampling rate, and the same 2 s trimming and division into training (20 s), validation (3 s), and test (3 s) sets as for the HD sEMG signals were applied. Finally, the angle signals were standardized using z-score normalization, with the mean and standard deviation computed exclusively from the training data and subsequently applied to the validation and testing sets. 

2.3 The spinal-inspired spiking neural network 

An overview of the implementation of the spinal-inspired SNN ( 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 ) and its relationship with biological processes occurring in the central nervous system is presented in Figure 2. The method was implemented in Python using PyTorch [43] (v2.5.1). 

2.3.1 Spike Encoding: 

Various encoding schemes have been proposed for SNNs, particularly in sEMG-based gesture recognition and neuromorphic implementations. Among these schemes, temporal and rate-based encoders are commonly used [20, 21, 23, 44]. However, although these approaches are computationally effective, they do not adequately capture the biological properties of neuronal firing patterns. Therefore, in this work, we propose an encoding model based on a non-homogeneous renewal point process (Poisson or Gamma) [45, 46]. This model emulates a neuron that integrates incoming input over time through an internal state variable. Spike generation occurs when this variable exceeds a stochastic threshold with unit mean, and the variability is determined by the process order [47]. Following each spike event, both the accumulated state and the threshold are reset, ensuring independence between successive events. As input to the encoder, the normalized root-mean-square (RMS) signal from each HD sEMG channel is used. This signal is scaled by a constant gain ( 𝜃 ), which converts the normalized RMS amplitude into an instantaneous firing rate. Processing is performed in a temporal manner for each channel, evaluating the integration dynamics of the proposed neuronal model at each time step (one sEMG sample, 1 /2052 s ≈ 0.49 ms). Additionally, the model incorporates an order parameter that controls the regularity of the spike train: when the order is 1, the behavior corresponds to a Poisson process (highly irregular), whereas higher order values yield more regular discharge, resulting in a Gamma point process. Because the threshold has a unit mean regardless of the order, this parameter modifies spike-train regularity without altering the mean firing rate. In this way, different input variability (noise) in event generation can be effectively adjusted. As a result, the encoder produces spike trains organized in a matrix of dimensions 𝐶 × 𝑁 , where 𝐶 represents the number of sEMG channels and 𝑁 denotes the number of temporal samples. This representation can be interpreted as a population of input spike trains over time, whose firing rate is directly influenced by the amplitude of the input signal (see Figure 2B). The properties and pseudo-code of the spike encoder are provided in Section 2 of the Supplementary Material .

2.3.3 Architecture: 

The SNN architecture (Figure 2B) consists of an input stage based on spike encoding, as previously described, followed by two hidden layers of LIF neurons that emulate spinal circuits, and a final stage comprising a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that generates a continuous representation for finger movement prediction. The input layer consists of 128 neurons modulated by an amplitude parameter 𝜃 (spike encoding). The two hidden layers, responsible for extracting spatiotemporal patterns, contain 128 and 64 LIF neurons, respectively. In the output stage, the network summarizes temporal information by using the membrane potential of the last hidden layer averaged over a 100 ms integration window (50 ms step, 50% overlap) for each neuron. Neuronal states were reset to zero at the start of each window, and the membrane potential was taken after the reset (Equation 7). This representation is then fed into a two-layer MLP: the first layer projects the features from the last SNN layer into a 64-dimensional space, followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation and a dropout layer with a rate of 0.2 to improve generalization. Finally, a second linear layer maps these representations into a 15-dimensional space corresponding to all finger joints, thereby producing continuous outputs for joint-movement decoding. The corresponding kinematic target for each window was taken to be the joint angle at the last sample of that window. The number of neurons in the hidden layers was experimentally tuned to balance the model’s ability to represent the input patterns and its computational cost, avoiding an excessively complex architecture. 7Spinal-inspired SNN for finger-joint kinematics estimation from HD sEMG A Preprint 

2.4 Physiologically inspired components 

In addition to the physiological incorporation introduced in the spike-encoding model, other modifications were implemented to analyze behavior under a more biologically inspired representation of spinal circuits. 

• Exponential distribution of neurons : This adaptation was implemented by modifying the firing threshold, and membrane and synaptic time constants of the neurons in the second layer of the SNN. The thresholds followed an exponential distribution (Figure 2B) between 𝑉 𝑡 ℎ𝑟 − 2 and 𝑉 𝑡 ℎ𝑟 + 2, where 𝑉 𝑡 ℎ𝑟 is the baseline threshold tuned as a hyperparameter and corresponds to the midpoint of this range. The offset of ±2 was selected empirically to keep all thresholds positive within the explored values of 𝑉 𝑡 ℎ𝑟 . The same exponential interpolation scheme was also applied to the membrane and synaptic time constants of the second layer ( 𝜏 𝑚𝑒𝑚 

and 𝜏 𝑠𝑦𝑛 , ranging from 16 to 6 and from 8 to 3 time steps, respectively; i.e., ≈7.8–2.9 ms and ≈3.9–1.5 ms), following the same neuron ordering used for the threshold distribution: neurons with the lowest threshold (recruited first) were assigned the longest time constants, while neurons with the highest threshold (recruited last) were assigned the shortest ones. This graded organization is consistent with the inverse relationship between motor neuron size, input resistance, and membrane time constant described within Henneman’s size principle [49]; thus, this distribution constitutes a phenomenological representation of the size principle, whereas the first layer retained fixed time constants ( 𝜏 𝑚𝑒𝑚 ≈ 4.9 ms, 𝜏 𝑠𝑦𝑛 ≈ 2.4 ms). 

• Spike encoder firing rate : Increasing the amplitude ( 𝜃 ) of the modulating signal in the spike encoder is directly proportional to an increase in the firing rate at the encoder output (see Section 2 of the Supplementary Material ). 

• Variability of input spikes : The spike encoder implemented in the SNN allows control over the variability of spike trains through the order parameter. Accordingly, different levels of variability in the encoding process were analyzed. This enables indirect control of the noise level at the SNN input: lower orders generate more irregular spike trains (i.e., higher noise), whereas higher orders produce more regular patterns associated with lower noise levels. For instance, when the order is 1, the spike encoder yields a Poisson point process, with a coefficient of variation (CoV) equal to 1, indicating fully irregular firing activity. • Excitation/inhibition balance : To implement the E/I balance, the sign of the synaptic weights in the first layer of the SNN was fixed per presynaptic input channel (spikes from the spike encoder for each channel) rather than per individual connection, so that each input projected exclusively excitatory or exclusively inhibitory connections to all postsynaptic neurons, consistent with Dale’s principle [50]. Sign was assigned through a random permutation of the input channels, while connection magnitudes reused the network’s default initialization and remained the only trainable component, via the reparameterization 𝑤 = sign × | 𝑤 𝑟 𝑎𝑤 |;gradient descent could therefore adjust magnitude but never invert sign, so the assigned E/I balance was exactly fixed throughout training. 

2.5 Hyperparameter tuning and training 

The hyperparameter selection process focused on four key variables: 𝑉 𝑡 ℎ𝑟 , 𝜃 , the encoder order, and the E/I balance. These hyperparameters were optimized using Optuna (v4.9.0) [51], an open-source framework for automated hyperparameter optimization, over the following search space: 𝑉 𝑡 ℎ𝑟 from 3 to 6 (step of 0.1); 𝜃 of 200, 250, 300, 350, and 400; order of 5, 15, and 25; and E/I balance of 65/35, 70/30, 80/20, or without balance (WB). The search was guided by the tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) sampler [52] with Optuna’s default settings. Each optimization study was run sequentially under a fixed budget of 60 trials, and the objective function was defined as maximizing the variance-weighted 𝑅 2 computed on the validation set. To ensure reproducibility, the seed of both the sampler and the model initialization was fixed across all studies. Each trial followed the training protocol described below. Tuning was performed separately for each task in five participants, one randomly selected from each age group (20–70 years, in 10-year intervals), with the elbow extended and the wrist at 90 ◦, the neutral forearm position of the protocol (15 Optuna calibrations). These participants were also included in the final analysis, given that tuning used only the validation set and test sets were never used for model selection. The hyperparameter combinations evaluated in each calibration (Optuna trials) were ranked according to the variance-weighted 𝑅 2, and the final combination for each task was selected from the best-ranked configurations across participants. Section 4 of the Supplementary Material presents the hyperparameter optimization results and the selection procedure. The SNN was trained end-to-end with backpropagation through time and a surrogate gradient [53], implemented directly in PyTorch. Training was performed using the AdamW optimizer, with a constant learning rate of 𝜂 = 10 −3 and a decoupled weight decay of 𝜆 = 10 −3. The learning rate was kept fixed across all epochs. The training process was carried out for a maximum of 100 epochs, incorporating an early stopping criterion if no improvement in the validation 8Spinal-inspired SNN for finger-joint kinematics estimation from HD sEMG A Preprint 

loss was observed for 13 consecutive epochs, where any decrease in the validation loss counted as an improvement. The final trained model corresponds to the weights from the epoch that achieved the lowest validation loss. The cost function used was the L1 loss, corresponding to the mean absolute error (MAE), computed over mini-batches of size 64, with the training batches shuffled at each epoch and the last incomplete batch dropped. Model training and evaluation were repeated using three different seeds to assess robustness to model initialization. This procedure was applied to all methods, and results were averaged across seeds for each participant and task. The training setup was based on previous studies and empirical tuning [54]. 

2.6 Data Analysis 

Six reference methods were implemented in this study to compare performance in simultaneous and proportional hand-movement prediction against the proposed physiologically inspired method. Specifically, the methods considered were Ridge Regression (RR), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Histogram-based Gradient Boosting (HGB) Regression, Support Vector Regressor (SVR), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and a 2D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), configured according to previous reports [54–56]. Detailed configurations for each method can be found in Section 3 of the Supplementary Material . For RR, KNN, HGB, and SVR, hyperparameters were selected using a protocol similar to that followed in the study by [55]. The MLP and CNN implemented here correspond to the methods proposed and evaluated in [54]. RR, KNN, HGB, SVR, and MLP use as input nine features extracted from 100 ms windows of the sEMG signals, with 50% overlap: RMS, mean absolute value, waveform length, standard deviation, zero crossings, maximum absolute value, logarithmic detector, mean frequency, and median frequency, with the latter two computed from the fast Fourier transform of the same window, yielding a 1152-dimensional input (9 features × 128 channels) per window. The CNN instead receives the band-pass-filtered sEMG as input over the same 100 ms window (205 samples), yielding a 1 × 205 × 128 input per window. The segmentation of the data into training, validation, and test sets was kept consistent with that used in the proposed method. Likewise, the MLP and CNN followed the same training scheme as 

𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 , whereas RR, KNN, HGB, and SVR were fitted on the same training set using their standard procedures, ensuring a fair comparison across methods. 

2.6.1 Ablation analysis 

To quantify the contribution of each physiologically inspired component, 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 was compared with three variants whose hyperparameters were also optimized with Optuna, following the same procedure as for 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 . The conventional SNN ( 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝐶𝑜𝑛𝑣 ) [57] shared the LIF architecture and MLP readout of 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 , but used identical neurons in both layers (fixed thresholds and time constants), unconstrained synaptic weights, and the adaptive temporal encoder proposed by Sun et al. [20], which emits a spike when the absolute difference between consecutive samples of the filtered sEMG exceeds an adaptive level (the mean plus a multiple of the standard deviation of the sample-to-sample differences). The other two variants were obtained by removing one of the two main components of 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 :

𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑆 𝑝𝑘𝐸𝑛𝑑 retained the spike encoder but used identical thresholds and time constants in the second layer, whereas 

𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝐻𝑒𝑡𝑒𝑀 𝑁 retained the heterogeneous second layer but replaced the spike encoder with the adaptive temporal encoder. For this analysis, all 30 experimental conditions were included.

2.6.3 Performance and interpretability assessments 

Multiple performance metrics were employed to characterize both the effects of the physiological adaptations and the comparison with baseline methods. Specifically, the following metrics were used: root mean square error (RMSE), coefficient of determination ( 𝑅 2), Pearson correlation coefficient ( 𝜌 ), MAE, and maximum cross-correlation ( 𝑋𝐶 𝑚𝑎𝑥 ). For RMSE, 𝑅 2, 𝜌 , and MAE, each measured joint angle was compared with the prediction obtained four windows later (200 ms) to compensate for the prediction delay, whereas 𝑋𝐶 𝑚𝑎𝑥 was computed over all lags without this shift; both procedures were applied identically to all methods. The lag at which the cross-correlation reached its maximum (𝐿𝑎𝑔 𝑋𝐶 ) was also reported as an estimate of the prediction delay of each method. The 200 ms shift was chosen because it corresponds to the median variance-weighted 𝐿𝑎𝑔 𝑋𝐶 across methods and trials (interquartile range: 150–250 ms). To obtain a global measure of performance in the simultaneous estimation of all finger joints, a variance-weighted version of these metrics, computed on the non-standardized joint angles, was adopted. This approach assigns greater importance to outputs with larger ranges of motion, which are more informative for evaluating the decoder’s ability to predict multiple degrees of freedom simultaneously. In the analyses of the physiological adaptations, only the 𝑅 2 and 𝜌 

metrics were considered, due to their ease of interpretation and widespread use in evaluating the efficiency of decoding methods [58]. Additionally, to assess the physiological interpretability of 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 through its internal activity, the instantaneous population firing rate, estimated as the smoothed composite spike train (sCST; the sum of the spike trains of all neurons in a layer, normalized by the number of neurons and smoothed by zero-phase filtering with a 25 ms Hann window), and several statistical properties of the inter-spike interval (ISI) distribution were computed, including the mean, CoV, skewness, and kurtosis. Population synchrony, the coherence between the sCSTs of two equally sized, non-overlapping groups of neurons from each layer, and the gain between layers were also computed (see Section 6 of the Supplementary Material ). These internal activity measures were compared across tasks in the conditions with extended elbow and 90 ◦

wrist posture, to isolate the effect of the task, since these properties may also be modulated by elbow and wrist posture. 

2.6.4 Statistical analysis 

The normality of the data distribution for each condition was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As most conditions did not meet the assumption of normality ( 𝑝 < 0.05), non-parametric statistical analyses were employed. To evaluate overall performance differences among conditions during the physiological explorations, the Friedman test was applied, considering the repeated-measures nature of the data. All tests used one value per participant (n = 23), obtained by averaging across seeds and, for the method comparison, taking the median across the 30 conditions. The hypotheses are as follows: (i) for the firing rate of the spike encoder, the alternative hypothesis states that higher firing rates lead to improved performance compared to lower firing rates; (ii) for input spike variability (input noise), the alternative hypothesis states that a reduction in noise results in a significant improvement in performance; (iii) for the E/I balance, the alternative hypothesis states that decoding performance differs across the evaluated E/I configurations; and (iv) for the comparison across methods, the alternative hypothesis states that 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 achieves higher performance than the baseline methods. For the internal activity measures of the SNN, the alternative hypothesis states that they differ across motor tasks, with each SNN layer analyzed separately. The effect size for the overall analysis was assessed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance ( 𝑊 ). When significant differences were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with a Bonferroni correction applied to the resulting 𝑝 -values to account for multiple comparisons; the adjusted 

𝑝 -values are reported. For the post-hoc comparisons, effect size was quantified using the rank-biserial correlation coefficient ( 𝑟 𝑟 𝑏 ), which measures the magnitude of the difference between paired conditions [59]. Effect sizes were interpreted according to the following thresholds: small (0.1– <0.4), medium (0.4– <0.6), and large ( ≥0.6) [60]. All tests adopted a significance level of 𝑝 < 0.05. Descriptive values are reported as the median and interquartile range (IQR).

# 4 Discussion 

In the present study, we proposed a spinal-inspired SNN that significantly improved the simultaneous and proportional prediction of finger kinematics from HD sEMG signals compared with traditional machine-learning and deep-learning approaches. Notably, this improvement was achieved with substantially fewer trainable parameters than the MLP and CNN, suggesting that it is not attributable to a larger model size, in line with recent evidence that shallow SNNs can decode finger-level motor output with a small memory footprint [28]. The results showed that incorporating neurophysiological principles—specifically, a more physiologically plausible spike-encoding model and a phenomenological representation of Henneman’s size principle—not only enhanced the biological plausibility of the architecture but also directly improved decoding performance, whereas imposing different E/I balances on the synaptic weights did not significantly alter this performance. Joint kinematics were evaluated across fifteen finger angles (three joint angles per finger), enabling the simultaneous and proportional estimation of the kinematics of all fingers. The proposed method showed significant improvements across both elbow postures, most wrist positions, and all three motor tasks (see Figure 3 of the 

Supplementary Material ). The problem of decoding kinematics with multiple degrees of freedom has recently gained considerable attention due to its potential applications in motor rehabilitation, making it an active area of research. Various methods have been proposed, primarily data-driven approaches such as deep learning [54, 61, 62]. However, most of these methods have been evaluated on a limited number of degrees of freedom. Other approaches have been developed for the simultaneous and proportional decoding of multiple degrees of freedom, showing improved performance over more conventional methods [10, 63]. Although these methods have demonstrated effectiveness in predicting movements in patients with reduced muscular activity [10], they still present several computational and methodological challenges. For example, their incorporation into personalized rehabilitation sessions may be compromised due to the lack of neurophysiological outputs that these methods can generate; the large amount of data required for proper performance, where data augmentation strategies have been implemented [64], which may introduce biases in the data or signal without an adequate physiological representations [14]; or the limited performance that these methods may present under specific conditions, such as posture or limb position [65]. Although the use of classical architectures is not inherently incorrect, we hypothesize that methods with more physiological representations and a closer correspondence to the structures of the central nervous system may improve performance in decoding multiple degrees of freedom across different conditions (postures, positions, and tasks). Additionally, such methods may produce more physiologically meaningful outputs that make the decoding interpretable, much as motor unit firing properties provide insight into the neural control of impaired muscles [66, 67]. Therefore, incorporating these models, together with other physiologically grounded representations, may have a significant impact on the next generation of physiology-informed neural networks. The ablation analysis indicated that the spike encoder was the main contributor to decoding performance, as replacing it reduced the median 𝑅 2 by approximately 0.15, regardless of whether the second layer had heterogeneous or identical neurons (Section 3.2). This is likely because the two encoders pass different information to the network. The proposed spike encoder converts the sEMG envelope, which is related to the neural drive to the muscle [69], into a firing rate, much as a motor neuron pool conveys this drive through its cumulative discharge rate [70]. The adaptive temporal encoder, in contrast, is a threshold-based temporal-contrast method that encodes changes between consecutive samples rather than the signal level [44], so the network can only infer the activation level from how often spikes occur within each integration window. Conversely, the heterogeneous second layer improved performance only when combined with the spike encoder, whereas adding it to the adaptive temporal encoder slightly reduced 𝑅 2. In motor neuron pools, recruitment thresholds that increase across neurons allow the pool to encode a wide range of input intensities, since higher-threshold neurons are recruited only as the input increases [31, 49]; similarly, heterogeneous time constants 18 Spinal-inspired SNN for finger-joint kinematics estimation from HD sEMG A Preprint 

improve learning in SNNs, particularly in tasks with rich temporal structure [71]. The scaled thresholds and time constants of the second layer follow these principles, but they can only take advantage of them when the network input directly encodes the intensity of muscle activation, as the firing rate of the spike encoder does. To analyze the effects of the neurophysiological elements incorporated into the network, we evaluated the prediction performance of each element, using the variance-weighted metrics as a reference. In particular, no further significant improvements in 𝑅 2 were observed when the spike encoder inputs to the SNN exhibited a firing rate greater than or equal to approximately 128 Hz ( 𝜃 = 250) (Figure 5). It should be noted that these encoder rates exceed the discharge rates typically observed in human motor units during voluntary contractions [72, 73]; thus, 𝜃 should be interpreted as a gain that sets the input drive to the network rather than as a physiologically calibrated firing rate. The absence of further improvements above this level suggests that, once sufficient input drive is provided, performance is no longer limited by the input. Subsequently, we demonstrated that fully noisy inputs to the proposed SNN, such as those imposed by a CoV of 100% (order 1 in the spike encoder), yielded significantly lower performance than more regular inputs. Moreover, the deterministic encoder (CoV of 0%) performed similarly to moderately regular encoders (CoV ≤ 26%). Thus, input noise did not improve decoding; rather, highly irregular spike trains likely degraded the rate information available within each integration window. This contrasts with studies in which some degree of stochasticity benefits neural computation, including stochastic facilitation in neural systems [74] and improved generalization and robustness of SNNs with noisy neuron dynamics [75]. In the present method, however, noise was introduced only as variability of the input spike trains, rather than being exploited by the neuron dynamics or the learning rule [75]. Importantly, decoding performance was preserved for theoretical CoV values between 14 and 26%, which lie within the discharge variability reported for human hand motor units during steady voluntary contractions (approximately 13–30%) [72]. This indicates that physiologically realistic levels of spike-train irregularity can be incorporated at the network input without compromising decoding performance. Furthermore, imposing different E/I weight ratios, including more excitatory regimes (up to 80/20), did not significantly affect decoding performance (Figure 7). This result indicates that biologically plausible E/I constraints, such as the predominantly excitatory regimes widely present in the central nervous system [76], can be incorporated without compromising prediction performance. However, the exploration of balance effects in physiological networks, particularly for understanding the input–output properties of neurons, remains an open research area [68]. In line with these findings, the proposed SNN enabled the characterization of neuronal activity properties, which can be regarded as interpretable latent descriptors of the proposed SNN, since their absolute values did not match physiological ranges (see below). In this sense, the proposed method addresses interpretability rather than explainability: whereas explainability methods provide post hoc explanations of black-box models, interpretable models have internal variables that are meaningful by construction [77], as is the case for the firing rates and ISI statistics of the SNN. In particular, the firing rate and ISI statistics (Figure 9), as well as the synchrony and sCST coherence (Section 6 of the Supplementary Material ), differed significantly across motor tasks, indicating that the network firing properties depended on the decoded task. Moreover, the sCST coherence increased with the number of pooled neurons and was concentrated at low frequencies (Section 6 of the Supplementary Material ). This pattern is consistent with the common synaptic input that characterizes motor neuron pools [70, 78], which, in the proposed SNN, arises because all neurons receive the same encoded sEMG channels. Nevertheless, since the hyperparameters were optimized independently for each task and the tasks differ intrinsically in their muscle activation patterns, these differences reflect both the task and the configuration that best decodes it, two effects that cannot be fully disentangled in the present analysis. Likewise, this analysis was restricted to a single elbow and wrist posture; how posture modulates the internal activity of the SNN was beyond the scope of this study and will be investigated in future work. These internal descriptors could also be relevant for rehabilitation. Duan et al. [67] estimated motor neuron intrinsic properties in vivo in individuals with incomplete spinal cord injury and found a longer refractory period in the tibialis anterior muscle than in non-disabled controls. The proposed method could complement such approaches by providing population-level descriptors directly from the sEMG envelope during dynamic finger movements, provided that it is further informed by the physiological mechanisms that shape motor neuron firing, such as refractoriness and spinal inhibitory circuits. Whether these descriptors reflect motor impairment in clinical populations remains to be tested. However, despite incorporating physiologically inspired principles, the proposed SNN’s firing rates were substantially higher than those typically reported experimentally for human motor neurons [72, 73], particularly in layer 2 during the 1FP task. Although the SNN effectively captured the qualitative aspects of neuronal firing properties, its absolute magnitudes limit direct biological interpretation. This suggests that the improvement in decoding performance achieved with the physiologically inspired architecture did not necessarily correlate with a proportional increase in quantitative biological realism. To enhance the SNN’s validity, incorporating spinal mechanisms—such as an explicit refractory period (e.g., informed by in vivo estimates [67]) and recurrent or reciprocal inhibition [79]—could help align neuronal 19 Spinal-inspired SNN for finger-joint kinematics estimation from HD sEMG A Preprint 

dynamics with experimentally observed ranges, resulting in a more biologically plausible representation of spinal motor control. Despite the encouraging results, some limitations should be acknowledged. For instance, the exploration was conducted using a controlled set of tasks and postures; therefore, the method’s robustness under more dynamic conditions and in populations with severe motor impairments still needs to be validated. Also, the study employed a small number of electrode grids (two) for HD sEMG recordings. The grids were placed over the flexor and extensor muscles of the forearm, specifically the FDS and EDC. However, other studies have evaluated computational methods by adding two additional electrode matrices near the wrist [63], which may be beneficial for use in individuals with stroke or spinal cord injury. Nevertheless, for transradial amputees, our experimental approach, based on the remaining flexor and extensor musculature, may offer an advantage.

# S1 LIF Neuron 

The characteristics of the LIF neuron model are shown in Figure S1. The model does not explicitly generate spikes; instead, it records the time at which the membrane potential reaches a threshold (15 mV), after which it is reset to 0 mV. In this illustrative simulation, the neuron was driven by a constant input current of approximately 1.45 nA. The frequency–current (f–I) curve (panel C) shows an approximately proportional increase in firing rate with input current, reflecting the absence of an explicit refractory period. The normalized temporal responses indicate that synaptic current evolves faster than the membrane potential, due to its smaller time constant. In contrast, the membrane potential exhibits slower and smoother dynamics, consistent with its role as a temporal integrator. # S2 Spike encoding properties 

The spike encoding patterns are shown in Figure S2. The figure shows an illustrative example of the encoder output for one participant in one motor task; absolute firing rates therefore depend on the normalized amplitude of that recording and are not directly comparable with the population averages reported in the main text. The Poisson process (panel A) exhibits an exponential ISI distribution with high temporal variability, whereas Gamma processes (panels B–C) increase regularity, reducing ISI dispersion as the process order increases. Accordingly, the Poisson process shows higher CoV and standard deviation, while Gamma processes progressively decrease these variability measures (panels D–E). # S5 Full performance comparison 

The performance of simultaneous and proportional prediction across all tasks and finger joint angles is shown only for the 𝑅 2 metric (Figure S3), as it provides a representative measure of continuous kinematic decoding [15, 16]. Experimental conditions are identified by a code (M 𝑥𝑦𝑧 ) indicating elbow posture (extended and flexed), task type (one-and two-finger pinch and full grasp), and wrist position (from 0 ° to 180 °). To facilitate interpretation, the figure was organized according to elbow posture and task type, while the last digit of each code represents the wrist posture: 1 for 0°, 2 for 45 °, 3 for 90 °, 4 for 135 °, and 5 for 180 °. The right side of the figure shows the percentage of finger joint angles for which the proposed method significantly outperformed HGB, with the 15 finger joint angles representing 100% for each condition. These per-condition and per-joint comparisons used one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 > HGB), with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction across the 450 task–joint comparisons of each metric. Overall, 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 significantly outperformed the best baseline method (HGB) in 45% of the 450 task–joint combinations and achieved a higher median 𝑅 2 in 81% of them, with statistically significant differences favoring 

𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 in most FG conditions (see Panel C). Among the evaluated tasks, FG exhibited the highest proportion of significantly improved joints, reaching values between 67% and 93% across wrist postures. In contrast, 1FP consistently showed the lowest percentage of significantly improved joints, ranging from 0% to 33%, whereas 2FP presented an intermediate behavior, with improvements varying substantially across wrist postures (approximately 13–87%). At the joint level, significant improvements were predominantly observed for the index, middle, and ring fingers, particularly during the FG task, where their MCP, PIP, and DIP joints improved in at least 8 of the 10 FG conditions. In contrast, the thumb joints exhibited fewer significant differences, while the pinky joints showed improvements primarily during the FG task. These findings indicate that 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 provides its greatest advantage during coordinated multi-finger movements, while simpler pinch tasks exhibit a more heterogeneous improvement pattern. Regarding limb posture, no consistent effect of elbow configuration was observed, as both the extended and flexed elbow conditions exhibited comparable proportions of significantly improved joints (45% and 44%, respectively). In contrast, wrist posture appeared to influence the relative performance of 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 . The supinated wrist posture (0 °) resulted in the lowest proportion of significantly improved joints (30%), particularly during the 1FP and 2FP tasks, whereas the pronated posture (180 °) showed the highest proportion (59%), with the most consistent improvements observed during the FG task. Overall, these findings suggest that 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 provides greater benefits for the central fingers and coordinated multi-finger grasping tasks, with its strongest relative advantage occurring under the pronated wrist posture. These observations are based on the overall trends shown in the figure; however, more detailed analyses are required to further characterize the influence of individual wrist and elbow postures on decoding performance. 

# S6 Complementary neural analysis across tasks 

To complement the neuronal characterization of the main text, three population measures were computed for each layer of 𝑆𝑁 𝑁 𝑃ℎ𝑦𝑠𝑖𝑜 from the spike trains of the test set (approximately 3 s), using the same conditions (extended elbow and 90 ◦ wrist posture) and one value per participant (averaged across seeds). The sCST was obtained by summing the spike trains of all neurons in a layer, normalizing by the number of neurons, and smoothing with a 25 ms Hann window. Population synchrony was quantified with the Golomb measure ( 𝜒 2), defined as the variance of the population-averaged spike train divided by the mean variance of the individual spike trains, which ranges from 0 (asynchronous) to 1 (fully synchronous) [80]. The common input to each layer was estimated as the magnitude-squared coherence, averaged over 0–5 Hz, between the composite spike trains of two randomly selected, non-overlapping groups of neurons of equal size (sCST coherence) [78]. This coherence was computed for pool sizes (number of neurons per group) ranging from 2 to half of the layer, and its spectrum is shown for the largest pool size. Finally, the gain was defined as the ratio between the mean sCST of layer 2 and that of layer 1, and quantifies the change in the population activity from the first to the second hidden layer. Differences across tasks were assessed for each layer with the statistical procedure described in the main text. All measures differed across tasks (Figure S4). Synchrony was low in both layers (median 𝜒 2 ≤ 0.02), indicating predominantly asynchronous firing, but differed across tasks in layer 1 ( 𝑝 = 0.03, 𝑊 = 0.16) and layer 2 ( 𝑝 < 0.0001, 

𝑊 = 0.47). In layer 2, 1FP showed higher synchrony (0.019 [0.018, 0.024]) than 2FP (0.016 [0.016, 0.018]) and FG (0.016 [0.016, 0.017]) ( 𝑝 < 0.0001, 𝑟 𝑟 𝑏 ≥ 0.93), whereas in layer 1, 2FP showed slightly higher synchrony than FG ( 𝑝 =0.02, 𝑟 𝑟 𝑏 = 0.64). The sCST coherence differed across tasks in layer 1 ( 𝑝 = 0.02, 𝑊 = 0.17) and layer 2 ( 𝑝 = 0.04, 𝑊 =0.14): in layer 1, 1FP showed lower coherence than 2FP (0.74 [0.68, 0.77] vs. 0.79 [0.74, 0.85]; 𝑝 = 0.007, 𝑟 𝑟 𝑏 = 0.70), whereas in layer 2, it showed higher coherence than FG (0.69 [0.59, 0.74] vs. 0.58 [0.47, 0.65]; 𝑝 = 0.02, 𝑟 𝑟 𝑏 = 0.64).
