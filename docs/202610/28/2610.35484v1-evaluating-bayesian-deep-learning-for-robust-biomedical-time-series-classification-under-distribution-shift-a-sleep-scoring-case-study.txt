# Abstract 

In biomedical time-series classification, machine learning models often suffer from deteriorating accuracy and cali-bration due to distribution shift. We investigate to what de-gree Bayesian-inspired deep learning improves robustness through Bayesian model averaging (BMA). Using rodent sleep scoring as a case study, we evaluate BMA under dis-tribution shifts in genotype, laboratory, and species. We systematically compare nine approximate inference meth-ods spanning single- and multi-basin approaches across two neural network backbones on in-distribution (ID) data and five out-of-distribution (OOD) datasets. In our results, multi-basin methods perform best, outperforming baselines and single-basin methods. Compared with point estimates, BMA generally improves OOD accuracy and calibration across both architectures, while yielding only modest ID gains. It also often improves selective prediction and OOD and artifact detection, but, interestingly, not inter-rater dis-agreement detection. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Machine learning has demonstrated high predictive accu-racy across a wide range of biomedical time-series classifi-cation tasks, including cardiac rhythm classification from electrocardiograms (Hannun et al., 2019), seizure detec-tion and seizure-type classification from electroencephalo-graphic recordings (Tang et al., 2022), and sleep staging from electrophysiological recordings (Miladinovi´ c et al., 2019; Perslev et al., 2021). However, in practical appli-cations, models are often applied to data from different populations, recording sites, sensor types and placements, or annotation practices, resulting in distribution shift rela-tive to the training data (Ovadia et al., 2019; Dockès et al., 2021; Wagh et al., 2022). Moreover, several of these factors may change simultaneously, producing structured shifts that may not be well represented by the synthetic corrup-tions commonly used to study distribution shift (Koh et al., 2021). Generalization beyond the training distribution is therefore critical for reliable deployment. As both predictive accuracy and model confidence often degrade under distribution shift (Ovadia et al., 2019), reli-able deployment necessitates well-calibrated probabilities to flag potential model errors. A well-calibrated classi-fier assigns confidence levels that match the observed fre-quency of correct predictions (Guo et al., 2017). Such prob-abilities can support risk-aware use of machine-learning models, including selective prediction, in which uncertain cases are deferred for expert review (Amodei et al., 2016; Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017). Bayesian neural networks represent model uncertainty by inferring a posterior distribution over the model parame-ters or the function space directly and form predictions by marginalizing over the corresponding posterior (Blundell et al., 2015; Rudner et al., 2022). Because exact Bayesian inference is generally intractable for neural networks, prac-tical Bayesian model averaging (BMA) relies on scalable approximations. Many of these approaches characterize uncertainty within a single posterior basin, whereas deep ensembles (DEs) and related multi-basin methods aver-age predictions across independent solutions (Lakshmi-narayanan et al., 2017; Wilson and Izmailov, 2020). However, whether approximate BMA systematically im-proves reliability under distribution shift remains unsettled. Large-scale comparisons have found that model averag-ing, particularly DEs, can provide strong predictive perfor-mance and uncertainty estimates under increasing distribu-tion shift (Ovadia et al., 2019). However, a more accurate approximation of the Bayesian posterior does not neces-sarily translate into greater robustness under covariate shift (Izmailov et al., 2021), and adding local posterior struc-ture to DEs can improve out-of-distribution (OOD) per-formance while reducing in-distribution (ID) performance (Jordahn et al., 2025). At the same time, most prior work on Bayesian deep learning relies on standard benchmarks and vision applications (Papamarkou et al., 2024). Whether these findings extend to naturally occurring distribution shifts in biomedical time-series classification is therefore an open question. We address this question using automated rodent sleep scoring from electrophysiological recordings as a case 1arXiv:2610.35484v1 [cs.LG] 26 Oct 2026 study. Several strong sleep-scoring models already exist (Miladinovi´ c et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; Brodersen et al., 2024), yet the task combines several challenges relevant to reliable deployment. Recordings vary across laborato-ries, acquisition systems, genotypes, experimental inter-ventions, and species, with several factors varying simul-taneously across cohorts (Miladinovi´ c et al., 2019). More-over, rodent sleep scoring lacks universally accepted scor-ing conventions, and signal artifacts can complicate anno-tation (Rayan et al., 2024). These characteristics make ro-dent sleep scoring a useful test case for evaluating approx-imate BMA under structured, naturally occurring distribu-tion shifts and for assessing whether predictive uncertainty identifies OOD recordings, artifacts, and disagreement be-tween independent scorers. Although prior work has ex-amined generalization across several of these shifts, to our knowledge no sleep-staging study has systematically com-pared a broad range of single- and multi-basin approximate BMA methods under natural distribution shift. Our contributions are as follows: • We conduct a systematic comparison of nine scalable posterior approximation and model-averaging meth-ods across two complementary neural network archi-tectures. The methods are the last-layer Laplace ap-proximation (LLLA) (Kristiadi et al., 2020); stochas-tic weight averaging (SWA) and stochastic weight averaging-Gaussian (SWAG) (Izmailov et al., 2018; Maddox et al., 2019); variational gradient descent (VGD) and mean-field variational inference (MFVI) (Ghosh et al., 2025; Blundell et al., 2015); stochas-tic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD) (Welling and Teh, 2011); DEs, MultiSWA, and MultiSWAG (Lak-shminarayanan et al., 2017; Wilson and Izmailov, 2020). • We evaluate classification accuracy and calibration for a large in-the-wild dataset and five naturally shifted OOD cohorts spanning differences in genotype, labo-ratory and acquisition configuration, signal montage, and species. • Beyond aggregate predictive performance and calibra-tion, we assess whether predictive uncertainty sup-ports OOD, artifact, and inter-scorer disagreement de-tection, as well as selective prediction. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Automatic sleep staging and generalization Sleep stag-ing has been approached using several model architectures. In human sleep staging, prominent examples include con-volutional neural networks (CNNs) (Perslev et al., 2021), convolutional-recurrent models (Supratak et al., 2017; Ole-sen et al., 2021), transformer-based models (Phan et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2024), and, more recently, foundation models with sleep staging as a downstream task (Thapa et al., 2026). Rodent sleep-scoring models span simi-lar architectures, including convolutional-recurrent mod-els (Yamabe et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2025), temporal convolutional networks (Liu et al., 2022), and classifiers that combine discriminative models with a hidden Markov model (HMM) to capture sleep-stage transitions (Miladi-novi´ c et al., 2019; Brodersen et al., 2024). Across these architectures, modern sleep-staging models have achieved high classification performance on their respective evalua-tion datasets. Several studies have investigated robustness across cohorts, populations, recording sites, acquisition systems, and sig-nal montages in human sleep staging (Perslev et al., 2021; Guillot et al., 2020; Guillot and Thorey, 2021; Guo et al., 2024), as well as across experimental conditions, record-ing configurations, laboratories, genotypes, and species in rodent sleep scoring (Barger et al., 2019; Miladinovi´ cet al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; Brodersen et al., 2024). De-spite these efforts, generalization beyond the source do-main remains a central challenge and an important barrier to widespread clinical adoption (Rossi et al., 2025). 

Uncertainty quantification in sleep staging Sleep stag-ing can be ambiguous, as reflected by disagreement be-tween expert scorers (Guillot et al., 2020; Bakker et al., 2023; Fiorillo et al., 2023). Probabilistic predictions pro-vide a natural way to represent this ambiguity by assigning probabilities across sleep stages rather than a single hard label (Stephansen et al., 2018). This motivates a distinc-tion between aleatoric uncertainty arising from ambiguity in the data and epistemic uncertainty arising from limited model knowledge (Kendall and Gal, 2017; van Gorp et al., 2022). Uncertainty estimates have also been used to flag ambiguous predictions for human review (Brodersen et al., 2024; Heremans et al., 2024). A smaller body of work has used approximate BMA to quantify epistemic uncertainty, generally focusing on one or a small number of posterior approximation methods. Re-cent studies have predominantly used MC dropout (Fiorillo et al., 2021; Vainikka et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026), while Yu et al. (2026) additionally use variational inference and model ensembling. In related work, Rossi et al. (2025) use disagreement within a model ensemble to identify ambigu-ous signal windows. 

Bayesian model averaging under distribution shift 

Prior work shows that the benefits of approximate BMA under distribution shift depend on both method and eval-uation setting. Ovadia et al. (2019) found that predictive accuracy and uncertainty quality deteriorate with increas-ing shift and that DEs are generally among the most robust approaches. SWAG has shown strong calibration and OOD detection (Maddox et al., 2019), while MultiSWAG can 2outperform DEs and MultiSWA under severe image cor-ruptions (Wilson and Izmailov, 2020). Evaluations on re-alistic shifts similarly find that multi-basin approximations often improve generalization and calibration, although the gains depend on the task and architecture (Seligmann et al., 2023). Moreover, adding local posterior structure to DEs can improve OOD metrics at the cost of ID performance (Jordahn et al., 2025), and more accurate posterior approx-imation does not necessarily improve robustness under co-variate shift (Izmailov et al., 2021). However, much of the empirical Bayesian deep-learning literature focuses on standard benchmarks (Papamarkou et al., 2024), while sleep-staging studies have not compared a wide range of approximate BMA methods under distribu-tion shift. Together, these gaps motivate evaluating single-and multi-basin approximate BMA methods in rodent sleep scoring, where several naturally occurring shifts provide a challenging test of robustness.

# 4 EXPERIMENTS & RESULTS 

We first describe the experimental setup, then compare the MAP backbones with existing sleep-scoring methods and evaluate posterior approximations for both ID and OOD data. Finally, we assess predictive uncertainty for OOD, ar-tifact, and scorer-disagreement detection and for selective prediction.

4.2 MAP models are strong baselines 

We first compared the U-Sleep and CNN-GRU MAP mod-els with Somnotate and Spindle on the ID WT test set. Ta-ble 3 shows strong and comparable classification perfor-mance across all four models: MF1 ranges from 89 .2 to 

90 .5 and Cohen’s κ from 88 .6 to 91 .1, with CNN-GRU MAP achieving the highest mean MF1 and Somnotate the highest κ. The MAP models achieve substantially higher LPD than both Somnotate and Spindle ; CNN-GRU MAP also obtains the lowest Brier score, while U-Sleep MAP lies between Somnotate and Spindle. Thus, the MAP mod-els are competitive with established sleep-scoring models and provide strong deterministic baselines for the follow-ing comparison of posterior approximation methods.

4.5 Uncertainty has task-dependent utility 

Finally, we evaluated predictive entropy for detecting OOD recordings, artifacts, and inter-rater disagreement, and for selective prediction. Table 6 reports the detection AUROCs and rejection AURC; mean entropy values are provided in Appendix Table 15. Most BMA methods improve OOD detection relative to MAP, consistent with previous work (Jordahn et al., 2025; Wilson and Izmailov, 2020). For U-Sleep, the best AUROC increases from .72 for MAP to .83 

7for MultiSWA and MultiSWAG, while for CNN-GRU it in-creases from .75 to .85 for MultiSWA. Spindle and Som-notate also perform well, with AUROCs of .88 and .86 ,respectively. For artifact detection, the best AUROC in-creases from .44 to .66 for U-Sleep for SGLD, and from 

.54 to .71 for CNN-GRU, achieved by MultiSWA. In con-trast, disagreement detection shows no systematic improve-ment: AUROCs remain between .71 and .77 , and several BMA methods match or underperform MAP. Finally, se-lective prediction improves for some, but not all, methods. MultiSWAG reduces OOD AURC from .054 to .026 for U-Sleep, while the CNN-GRU DE reduces it from .055 to 

.036 .Overall, predictive entropy has task-dependent utility. BMA generally improves OOD detection, often improves artifact detection and selective prediction, but does not sys-tematically improve disagreement detection. One possible explanation is that predictive entropy combines different sources of uncertainty, whereas scorer disagreement may more directly reflect ambiguity in the target or annotation process (van Gorp et al., 2022; Bakker et al., 2023). We therefore additionally decomposed predictive entropy into aleatoric and epistemic components, but found no consis-tent improvement in disagreement detection: aleatoric un-certainty did not consistently outperform total predictive entropy, while epistemic uncertainty was generally less in-formative (Appendix Table 14). 

# 5 DISCUSSION & SUMMARY 

Across two neural network architectures and five shifted datasets, approximate BMA provides modest ID gains but substantially larger improvements under distribution shift. Multi-basin methods are consistently more robust under shift across both classification and probabilistic metrics, extending previous findings to biomedical time-series clas-sification (Ovadia et al., 2019; Wilson and Izmailov, 2020; Seligmann et al., 2023). However, the utility of predic-tive uncertainty is similarly task-dependent, and no sin-gle method dominates across all metrics and architectures. BMA generally improves OOD detection and often artifact detection and selective prediction, but does not systemat-ically improve inter-rater disagreement detection. Thus, improved predictive probabilities do not necessarily trans-late into uncertainty estimates that are equally informative for every downstream task. From a practical perspective, the strongest multi-basin methods require several indepen-dently trained models, whereas SWAG provides a single-trajectory alternative with consistent OOD gains across both architectures (Maddox et al., 2019). Finally, while multi-modal BMA are found to generally improve both ac-curacy and calibration, one has to balance the increase in predictive performance against the significant increase in computational cost. 

Limitations and future work Our conclusions are lim-ited to rodent sleep scoring and five relatively small OOD cohorts. In addition, because several sources of distribu-tion shift co-occur within cohorts, we cannot reliably iso-late the effects of individual shift factors, as can be done in controlled simulations. Future work should investigate whether our findings generalize to additional biomedical time-series tasks and distribution shifts. Overall, approximate BMA, particularly multi-basin meth-ods, improves the robustness of biomedical time-series classifiers under distribution shift, but reliable deployment also requires uncertainty estimates to be validated for the specific decisions they are intended to support.

# A Experimental setup 

A.1 Datasets 

Table 7 summarizes the main characteristics of all datasets used in this paper, including the source of the dataset, the types of electrophysiological signals, and the shift. The WT and NT1 dataset has been collected at H. Lundbeck A/S across multiple research studies. 

Signals and preprocessing For the WT and NT1 cohorts, we used one electrocorticographic (ECoG) channel, one depth local-field-potential (LFP) channel, and one electromyographic (EMG) channel (Table 7). For the SPINDLE cohorts, we used two ECoG channels and one EMG channel. Consequently, the second neural input channel represents LFP in the WT and NT1 cohorts but ECoG in the SPINDLE cohorts, introducing an additional shift in signal type. For both neural network backbones, all channels were resampled to 128 Hz, centered by their median, scaled by their interquartile range, and clipped to [−20 , 20] . The literature baselines retained their method-specific preprocessing proce-dures, described in Appendices B.2 and B.1. 

Annotations The WT and NT1 datasets were annotated by a single scorer using Y = {wake , NREM , REM }. In contrast, the SPINDLE cohorts were annotated independently by two scorers using the expanded label set 

YSPINDLE = Y ∪ { Awake , A NREM , A REM },

where Awake , ANREM , and AREM denote artifact-containing windows assigned to the corresponding sleep stage. 

A.2 Model training, selection, and evaluation 

Model training Training schedules were implemented in blocks of 10 5 processed labels, with approximately 3.5 million labels in the full training set. The MAP models were trained for 2000 blocks, corresponding to approximately 57 passes through the training set. VGD, MFVI, and SGLD used 3000 blocks, corresponding to approximately 85 passes, while SWA and SWAG used five transition blocks followed by 200 collection blocks after initialization from the MAP solution. We use this block notation below whenever the exact optimization or collection schedule is relevant. The same unit was used for the loss curves in Figure 3 and to express the early-stopping patience for the SPINDLE model (Appendix Section B.2) and MAP model-complexity tuning (Appendix Section B.3). 

Model selection Hyperparameter tuning was based on mean validation MF1. This is visualized in Figure 2 for the MAP model-complexity tuning and in Figure 4 for the MAP prior variance and posterior approximation hyperparameters. For each method, we tuned one main method-specific hyperparameter while holding all other settings fixed. 

Posterior inference For sampling-based posterior approximations, we used S = 30 Monte Carlo samples, as summa-rized in Table 10. DE and MultiSWA used M = 10 independently trained components, while MultiSWAG used M = 10 

components with S = 30 samples per component. 

Evaluation In addition to the metrics reported in the main tables, we report accuracy, expected calibration error (ECE), and predictive entropy ( H) in Tables 11 and 12. This section describes the implementation and hyperparameter tuning of the Somnotate, Spindle, U-Sleep, and CNN-GRU baselines. Table 8 summarizes the main characteristics of the models. 

B.1 Somnotate 

We utilized Somnotate using the authors’ published code (Brodersen et al., 2024), which is dual-licensed under GPLv3 for academic use. Somnotate combines LDA with a three-state Gaussian HMM applied to multitaper spectrogram features. 

Spectrogram construction We evaluated the 1-s temporal resolution used by Brodersen et al. (2024) and a 4-s resolu-tion matching the annotations in this study. For the 1-s setting, each 4-s annotation was expanded to four 1-s intervals, and the four corresponding posterior state-probability vectors were averaged for validation. Spectral features were computed using the authors’ multitaper implementation, followed by the log( P + 1) transformation and per-recording standardization. Model fitting and predictions The model was fitted to the WT training set using the authors’ implementation. Hard pre-dictions were obtained by Viterbi decoding over the complete recording, while probabilistic predictions were the forward– backward posterior marginals

B.2 Spindle 

We implemented Spindle following Miladinovi´ c et al. (2019). The model combines a CNN operating on spectrograms with a three-state HMM for temporal modeling. We did not implement the separate artifact detection CNN, as all predictive evaluations used the common three-class label space {Wake , NREM , REM }.

Spectrogram preprocessing Following Miladinovi´ c et al. (2019), signals were represented at 128 Hz and transformed using 256-sample Hamming windows with a 16-sample step. The squared magnitude of the one-sided FFT gives a fre-quency resolution of 0.5 Hz and 32 time frames per 4-s scoring window. Spectrograms were computed continuously over each recording before division into scoring windows. For the two ECoG/LFP channels, frequencies from 0.5–24 Hz were retained, yielding 48 coefficients. The published CNN architecture requires 24 frequency inputs, but the corresponding reduction is not specified by Miladinovi´ c et al. (2019); we therefore averaged adjacent coefficients pairwise in the linear-power domain. The resulting 24 bands were log-transformed and standardized within each recording. For EMG, spectral power from 0.5–30 Hz was summed, log-transformed, standardized over time, and repeated across the 24 frequency rows. The CNN input contained the target 4-s window and two neighboring windows on each side, giving an input size of 3 × 24 × 160 .

CNN training We reproduced the CNN architecture of Miladinovi´ c et al. (2019), including the two fully connected layers with dropout probability 0.5. Training used the batch-dependent class-weighted cross-entropy described by the authors. Because the exact mapping from class ratio to weight is not specified, we used inverse-count weighting, wc = 1 /N c, with weights recomputed for each mini-batch. We used Adam with β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .99 , a mini-batch size of 100, and no weight decay. The authors used a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5. We additionally evaluated 10 −5 and 10 −4 and found no meaningful differ-ences in convergence or validation performance, and therefore retained the authors’ setting. Following Miladinovi´ c et al. (2019), training used early stopping after 20 consecutive validation evaluations without an improvement of at least 10 −4,corresponding to a patience of 100 blocks. 14 HMM inference The CNN predictions were post-processed using a three-state HMM. Following Miladinovi´ c et al. (2019), we assumed equal state priors when converting the class-balanced CNN outputs to HMM emission scores. The CNN softmax probabilities could therefore be used directly, since division by a uniform state prior introduces only a common multiplicative factor. We likewise used the uniform initial-state distribution π = (1 /3, 1/3, 1/3) .The original implementation assigns zero probability to some transitions, including Wake →REM and REM →NREM. Both occurred in the WT training annotations, so we instead estimated the full 3 × 3 transition matrix from the WT training labels. As for Somnotate, hard predictions were obtained using Viterbi decoding and probabilistic predictions using forward– backward posterior marginals based on the same emissions, transition matrix, and initial-state probabilities. Accordingly, MF1, Cohen’s κ, and accuracy were computed from the Viterbi sequence, whereas LPD, Brier score, and ECE were computed from the posterior marginals. 

B.3 Architecture selection for the neural network backbones 

We next defined the two neural network backbones used for MAP estimation and the subsequent posterior approximation experiments. We used average validation MF1 as the model selection criterion. 

Architecture-sweep training protocol All configurations in the architecture sweeps were trained with Adam using a learning rate of 10 −4 and weight decay set to 10 −5. Training was stopped when the validation cross-entropy had not improved by at least 10 −4 for 100 consecutive blocks, and the checkpoint with the lowest validation cross-entropy was retained for comparison. This early-stopping protocol was used only for architecture selection. 

B.3.1 Adapting U-Sleep to rodent sleep scoring Depth adaptation U-Sleep was originally developed for human sleep staging using 30-second epochs (Perslev et al., 2021), whereas sleep stages in the present study were scored in 4-second epochs. For a fixed number of context epochs and sampling frequency, the temporal input is therefore shorter by a factor of 30 /4 = 7 .5.The original U-Sleep encoder reduces temporal resolution through factor-two downsampling at each encoder level. Re-taining all 12 encoder and decoder levels would therefore compress the shorter rodent input substantially more than in the original architecture. We reduced both the encoder and decoder from 12 to 9 blocks. Removing three factor-two down-sampling stages reduces the total downsampling factor by 23 = 8 , approximately compensating for the 7.5-fold reduction in epoch duration while otherwise retaining the original U-Sleep encoder–decoder design.

# C Implementation and Tuning of Approximate Inference Methods 

This section describes the implementation and tuning of the posterior approximation and predictive-averaging methods. Figure 4 summarizes the primary sensitivity analyses, and Table 10 reports the selected hyperparameters, optimization budgets, and predictive-integration settings. 

C.1 SWA and SWAG 

Initialization and collection For each backbone and random seed, we initialized the SWA and SWAG collection tra-jectory from the corresponding final MAP checkpoint described in Section B.4. We first ramped the learning rate from the MAP training learning rate to the target collection learning rate over five transition blocks, during which no parameter iterates were retained. We then continued optimization for 200 collection blocks at the collection learning rate and stored one parameter iterate after each block. SWA was constructed by averaging all 200 iterates. For SWAG, the diagonal covariance component was estimated from all iterates, while the final R = 100 iterates were used for the low-rank component. Because both backbones contain batch-normalization layers, we recomputed the running statistics using 10% of the training data after changing the parameter values. This was performed once for the SWA solution and separately for each sampled SWAG model. Final SWAG predictions were obtained by averaging S = 30 parameter samples. 

Collection learning rate selection The collection learning rate controls the extent to which the continued SGD trajectory explores the local parameter region around the MAP solution. We evaluated 

ηcollect ∈ 10 −4, 3 × 10 −4, 10 −3, 3 × 10 −3, 10 −2 .

For each candidate learning rate, SWA and SWAG were constructed from the same trajectory. We therefore selected one collection learning rate per backbone and used it for both methods, enabling a paired comparison of the SWA point estimate and the corresponding SWAG approximation. Based on the validation sensitivity analysis in Figure 4(b), we selected ηcollect = 10 −3 for CNN-GRU and ηcollect = 3 × 10 −4 for U-Sleep.

C.3 Variational gradient descent 

VGD used the fixed-variance Gaussian approximation and objective defined in Section 3.1. Gaussian perturbations were applied to model weights and biases, while normalization parameters remained deterministic. For each backbone, VGD retained the Gaussian prior selected for the corresponding MAP model but was optimized independently from a random initialization rather than initialized from the MAP solution. We optimized the variational mean for 3000 blocks using Adam with a learning rate of 10 −4. The expected objective gra-dient was estimated using Strain = 1 Gaussian perturbations per optimization step. Validation performance was estimated using Sval = 10 posterior samples, and final posterior-predictive probabilities were computed using the common sampling budget of S = 30 .

Posterior-variance selection We treated the fixed posterior variance σ2 as the principal VGD-specific hyperparameter, although Ghosh et al. (2025) show that both the learning rate, the number of Monte Carlo samples and the posterior variance control the sharpness of the solution. We evaluated σ2 ∈ { 0, 10 −6, 10 −5, 10 −4, 10 −3}, where σ2 = 0 served as a deterministic reference. Based on mean validation MF1, we selected σ2 = 10 −4 for CNN-GRU and σ2 = 10 −5 for U-Sleep (Figure 4(c)).

C.7 MultiSWA and MultiSWAG 

We constructed MultiSWA and MultiSWAG from the same M = 10 independently trained MAP models used for the corresponding DE. Starting from each MAP checkpoint, we applied the same optimization trajectory, collection learning rate, collection length, low-rank dimension, and batch-normalization recalibration procedure as for the corresponding single-model SWA and SWAG methods described in Section C.1. No additional hyperparameters were tuned for either multi-basin method. MultiSWA equally averaged predictions from the M resulting SWA point estimates. For MultiSWAG, we drew S = 30 

parameter samples from each SWAG component, matching the sampling budget used for single-model SWAG. Predictions were first averaged over samples within each component and then averaged equally across the M components. With 

M = 10 , this corresponded to 300 parameter samples in total.
