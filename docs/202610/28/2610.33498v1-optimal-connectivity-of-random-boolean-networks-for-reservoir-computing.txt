Abstract. Reservoir computing shifts the focus from training a sys-tem’s internal dynamics to designing dynamics that expose useful com-putational capabilities via linear projection. We study random Boolean networks as reservoirs: cellular automata with random recurrent wiring and a random rule at each node. Their main parameter is the connectiv-ity, the mean number of edges into a node. Without input, these networks pass from ordered to chaotic dynamics at a connectivity of 2, where their dynamics are expected to facilitate computation. How the input is con-nected is itself a design factor: if the input shares the network’s edges, the connectivity sets both the dynamics and how many nodes receive the input, the input fan-out. We set the input fan-out separately and com-pare 7 ,560 reservoir designs, all combinations of six design factors, each built as many random reservoirs, on recalling the majority or the parity of a few input bits seen several steps earlier. The optimal connectivity falls from 2 .5 to 1 .75–2 as more nodes receive the input, but hardly de-pends on the task or on the other design factors, except self-loops, which connect a node to itself and raise the optimal connectivity. A measure computed from the reservoir states without a trained readout peaks at a connectivity that also falls with the fan-out and rises with self-loops, but does not replace training a readout. Reservoirs of one design vary widely, so a design should be judged by its typical reservoir. 

## 1 Introduction 

In reservoir computing, the input drives a dynamical system, the reservoir, and only a linear readout of its states is trained [6,9]. Thus the design of the reser-voir limits what the system can compute. Random Boolean networks (RBNs) generalize cellular automata (CA), with random instead of local wiring and a rule drawn for each node. CA with a rule per cell have served as reservoirs [12], and Snyder et al. call RBNs used as reservoirs Boolean reservoirs [13]. Without input, an RBN with unbiased rules is ordered when its connectivity, the mean in-degree ¯k of its nodes, is below 2 and chaotic above it [7,4]. Reservoirs are expected to perform best near this transition [1,8]. arXiv:2610.33498v1 [cs.NE] 24 Oct 2026 2 C. M. S. Vibe et al. 

A reservoir, however, is driven: at every step the input enters the rules of the nodes it reaches, so the critical value of the undriven network need not be the optimal connectivity ¯k∗ of a reservoir, the ¯k at which it is most accurate. Earlier results differ. Snyder et al. draw the input and recurrent edges from one shared pool, so that ¯k also sets how many nodes receive the input, the input fan-out r (Sect. 2); their trained 500-node reservoirs are most accurate at the largest ¯k tested, 8, at the shortest delay, and at ¯k between 3 and 4 at long delays [13]. In a second study they wire the input to r separate nodes and vary r and ¯k ∈ { 1, 2, 3}; on their tasks, accuracy falls least at ¯k=2 as the window and the input stream grow [14]. Echlin et al. also set the fan-out separately and find ¯k∗=2 among {1, 2, 3}, except in large reservoirs with many input nodes, where ¯k∗=1 [5]; in threshold networks, Calvet et al. find ¯k∗=4 [2]. Each study varies a few design factors and fixes the rest, so ¯k∗ has not been measured while the input fan-out and other factors vary together. We therefore ask how ¯k∗ depends on the design of the reservoir and on the task. We expect ¯k∗ to vary with every design factor, the input fan-out among them, and with how much memory and computation the task demands. As in the second study of Snyder et al. and in Echlin et al., we give the input its own edges, to r reservoir nodes drawn at random, so that the input fan-out is set apart from ¯k. We vary six design factors jointly: the input fan-out, ¯k, state updates per input bit, initialization, input injection, and self-loops. The classical RBN has no self-loops; Montagna et al. replace one in-edge of a node with a self-loop, preserving its random rule, and find that the undriven network has more cyclic attractors the more nodes have a self-loop [11]. Our self-loops replace an in-edge in the same way, so at a given ¯k a network with self-loops has fewer edges between distinct nodes. In a driven reservoir, the input variance is also expected to affect the dynamics; we hold it fixed (Sect. 3.2) and vary the demands of the task instead. Each task recalls a short window of past input bits, either its majority or its parity. Majority and parity are two ends of a scale from memory to computation: the majority can still be guessed when some bits of the window are lost, while the parity falls to chance when a single bit is lost. Memory and nonlinear computation share the bounded capacity of a dynamical system [3]. Boolean reservoirs are often judged without training a readout [13,14], by two measures computed from the reservoir states alone [8]. Kernel quality (KQ) is computed by driving the reservoir with streams that differ and counting the linearly independent states that result, so a high value means that distinct input histories give distinct states; generalization rank (GR) uses streams that are identical at selected bit positions, so a low value means that histories which agree there end in similar states. ## 2 Input Fan-Out in Boolean Reservoirs 

In the Boolean reservoirs of Snyder et al. [13] there is one input node, and the source of each in-edge of a reservoir node is drawn uniformly from the input and reservoir nodes together, one shared pool ( I∪R)→R. Each of the ¯k|R| pool edges has the input node as its source with probability 1 /(|R|+1), so a reservoir has on average about ¯k input edges: the input fan-out grows with ¯k. ( |R|=25, one input node) we reproduce their S-curve: KQ (Sect. 3.3) rises from low to full rank as ¯k grows. We then change only the input wiring. Drawing the input edges separately from the recurrent edges, as many on average as in the shared pool, changes the curve little at low ¯k, so there KQ is limited by how few nodes receive the input, not by the two kinds of edges sharing one pool. A one-to-one mapping from r input nodes to

The replication details are in Appendix A.2, and the code behind every curve is available (Sect. 3.4). The rest of the paper uses this mapping over the full design grid (Sects. 3 and 4.2).

3.1 Model Input. A sample is one stream of bits, u(t) ∈ { 0, 1} at step t. The input fan-out

Graph. The network has input nodes I and reservoir nodes R, which form one Boolean node set V = I ∪ R with state x(t) ∈ { 0, 1}|V |, whose reservoir part is xR(t) (Fig. 1). Two kinds of fixed edges connect them. The input edges 4 C. M. S. Vibe et al. 

I→R are one-to-one: each bit is copied to r ≤ | R| = 512 input nodes, each with a single edge to a distinct reservoir node drawn at random (Sect. 2), and input nodes receive no edges. The recurrent edges R→R number exactly ¯k|R|, so the connectivity ¯k is the mean in-degree of a reservoir node. The wiring mode sets how these edges are spread over the nodes. In the homogeneous mode every node has in-degree ki = ¯k, which requires an integer ¯k. In the heterogeneous mode the ¯k|R| edges are drawn without replacement from kmax =25 possible in-edges per node, so the in-degree ki is hypergeometric, at most kmax , with a mean ¯k

that can be fractional. At ¯k ≤ 8 no node reaches kmax , and input edges do not count toward it. In both wiring modes each node draws ki distinct sources uniformly from the reservoir, and exactly a fraction s of the nodes has a self-loop, which replaces one of its sources. The design grid uses the heterogeneous mode; a second grid in the homogeneous mode, at ¯k ∈ { 1, . . . , 8} with one tick, no self-loops and random initialization, tests whether the in-degree distribution moves ¯k∗.

Rules. Each reservoir node i has a rule, a random lookup table (LUT) over its in-neighbours, fi : {0, 1}ki → { 0, 1}. A node that receives the input reads its input node first, so its rule is fi : {0, 1}ki+1 → { 0, 1}, as for R1 in Fig. 1. The table entries are drawn once, i.i.d. from Bernoulli( p) with p = 0 .5, for which the critical connectivity 1 /(2 p(1 − p)) is 2 [4]. A reservoir’s graph, LUTs and initial state are drawn from one seed (Sect. 3.4).

3.4 Experiments Design grid. A design takes one level of each design factor in Fig. 2, and the grid is full factorial: 504 combinations of input fan-out, injection, ticks, initialization and self-loops, each at 15 values of ¯k, give 7 ,560 designs. 

Reservoirs. Each design is built as 20 reservoirs, each with its own random wiring and rules, seeded by the design and the reservoir’s index, so no two designs share a reservoir. Every reservoir is evaluated on all 16 task variants (Sect. 3.2) on the same 2 15 patterns and split, and measured by KQ and GR, so task variants are always compared on the same reservoirs. A design’s design accuracy on a task variant, or accuracy for short, is the median over its 20 reservoirs. Every choice of the analysis (the construction of Uτ , each task variant’s best design and the ¯k at which self-loops are compared, below) was made on a selection run of the same grid with 12 other reservoirs per design, so the choices and the reported numbers come from different reservoirs. Held-out patterns alone would not suffice, since a design’s accuracy varies far more between its reservoirs than between pattern sets (Appendix A.3). 

Floors. The floor of a task variant is the best accuracy without a reservoir, predicting from the last input bit (final-state readout) or from the count of ones among the pattern bits (summed readout). The floor is 66–71 % for summed majority and chance (50–51 .5 %) otherwise. Optimal Connectivity of Random Boolean Networks 7

Operating point. The operating point of a task variant is the r, P and ¯k

of its best design. Ticks, initialization and self-loops vary freely there, giving 42 designs per task variant. ¯k is fixed as well because with only r and P fixed many designs are at the floor, where the other factors show no difference. When ticks or initialization are compared, self-loops are held equal, as ¯k is, since a self-loop replaces one of its node’s ki sources and so changes the number of edges between distinct nodes. Self-loops are compared on each combination of the other factors, placed at the ¯k where it is most accurate on the selection run (its own ¯k∗), against the same combination without self-loops, placed likewise. 

Analysis. Accuracies are in %, and differences between them are absolute. The analysis makes two choices. First, input fan-out and injection are compared by the best design over the whole grid, and ¯k by mean curves (design accuracy averaged over all designs at one fan-out and ¯k and over a task’s four variants, with the optimal connectivity ¯k∗ at a curve’s peak). Every other factor is compared at the operating point, since fan-out and ¯k determine whether a design rises above its floor, and over the whole grid their effects are larger than those of the other factors. Second, designs are always compared by design accuracy, the median defined above, since reservoirs of the same design differ widely; a design is never judged by its best reservoir. 4.3 Other Design Factors and Variation Between Reservoirs 

At the operating point (Table 1), a second tick lowers accuracy by 3–11 % on every readout and task. Self-loops lower accuracy, more on the majority task than on parity; with self-loops on a tenth of the nodes ( s=0 .1), no median decrease exceeds 2 .2 %. A Beta regression of single-reservoir accuracy finds a larger decrease at s=1 than at s=0 .1 on every readout and task (Appendix A.3). The initialization changes design accuracy by at most 0 .5 %. Reservoirs of the same design differ in their random wiring and rules. On the majority task these differences account for 17–45 % of the variance of single-reservoir accuracy over the grid, and on parity for 16–65 %, most on final-state parity at d=10 (63–65 %). In each task variant’s best design, the best of the 20 reservoirs is more accurate than the median one by 6–28 %, more than the median effect of any design change at the operating point. Single reservoirs reach 100 % on every task variant (at least 99 .86 % on test), on parity even in designs whose median is about 51 %. 

## 5 Discussion 

What moves the optimal connectivity. Against our expectation, ¯k∗ moves only with the fan-out and self-loops; the task, the delay, injection, ticks and initialization hardly move it. Snyder et al. [13] find parity’s ¯k∗ at or above that of the majority task and a lower ¯k∗ at longer delays; here neither appears where accuracy rises above chance, but their reservoirs use a shared pool, and their readout is trained online and evaluated at every step. With a self-loop on every node, each node has one edge from another node fewer, yet ¯k∗ rises by only 0 .25– 0.75, not by 1. Either a node’s own previous state partly replaces the missing edge, or self-loops move the transition between order and chaos, since a self-loop carries a change in its node’s state to no other node; neither explanation was tested. 

Connectivity and input. The critical value ¯k=2 of RBNs [7,4] is close to the optimal connectivity only when many nodes receive the input (fan-out 64 and up); with few input nodes, reservoirs are most accurate above ¯k=2, where the undriven network is chaotic. The classical critical point applies here only approximately: nodes that receive the input have ¯k+1 inputs on average, one of them the injected bit, and a driven network is not a closed system. One untested interpretation: the more nodes the input reaches, the more changes it injects, and the lower ¯k must be to keep the network at the same point between order and chaos. Since ¯k∗ depends on the fan-out, studies with different fan-outs can find different values of ¯k∗, which may explain part of why earlier optima range from ¯k=1 to 8. Two earlier findings agree in direction with the fall of ¯k∗ as the fan-out grows: Echlin et al. [5] find ¯k=1 best in large reservoirs with many input nodes, and Snyder et al. [14] find that their more chaotic reservoirs reach their best accuracy with a smaller fan-out. In a shared pool, where ¯k also sets the fan-out, an optimum mixes the effects of ¯k and fan-out [13], and ∆ summed over the fan-out cannot show how ¯k∗ changes with fan-out [14]. Estimating the optimal connectivity. Two estimates of ¯k∗ need no trained readout: ¯k=2, and the ¯k at ∆’s peak, which falls with fan-out and rises with self-loops, as ¯k∗ does. On the final-state readout, the ¯k at ∆’s peak is the better estimate at fan-out 4 and 16. On the summed readout, at fan-out 4, the ¯k at ∆’s peak and ¯k=2 are both more than 3 % less accurate than ¯k∗ on the majority task; from fan-out 16 up, each is within 0 .8 %. Neither estimate replaces training a readout. 

Accuracy. The explanations in this paragraph fit the results but were not tested. The task changes how accurate a design can be: final-state parity at 

d=10 stays at 50–57 % at every fan-out, in line with the scale from memory to computation (Sect. 1): the majority of a window can still be guessed when some of its bits are lost over the later input, its parity cannot. Under xor the input node holds the running parity of the stream (Sect. 3.1), so a window’s parity is the XOR of only two past values of the input node, which may explain why xor is the better injection on final-state parity at d=5 and override on majority. A second tick passes an input bit’s effect through two rules before the next bit arrives, so the state keeps fewer past input bits, which fits the lower accuracy on every readout and task. The initialization makes no difference, as expected if the ten washout bits remove the effect of the initial state. 

Limits. This study has one reservoir size, one input distribution and two tasks at short delays; self-loops that add an edge instead of replacing one, and a readout of every tick, are untested. Our intervals, uncorrected for multiple comparisons, cover only the variation between reservoirs of the same design, so the results hold for the explored grids. Two questions remain open: why ¯k∗ is near the critical value at high fan-out, and what sets apart the single reservoirs that reach 100 % where their design’s median is about 51 %. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In the reservoirs studied here, the optimal connectivity falls as the input fan-out grows and rises with self-loops, but hardly depends on the task or on the other design factors. The critical value ¯k=2 is close to optimal only when many nodes receive the input (fan-out 64 and up). When few do (fan-out 4), majority is most accurate at ¯k=2 .5 and up to 4 .5 % less accurate at ¯k=2, and parity shows no clear optimum. The connectivity should therefore be chosen together with the input fan-out and self-loops. The ¯k at ∆’s peak estimates the optimal connectivity on the final-state readout but not on the summed one. Both tasks are solved at least 9 % above their floors, except final-state parity at the longer delay. Majority is most accurate at an intermediate fan-out and with override injection, parity at a high fan-out; one state update per input bit is better than two on every task, self-loops on many nodes lower accuracy, and the initialization does not matter. Single reservoirs of one design differ more than a change of ticks, self-loops or initialization changes their median, so a design, and its optimal connectivity, should be judged by the median of many reservoirs. 12 C.
