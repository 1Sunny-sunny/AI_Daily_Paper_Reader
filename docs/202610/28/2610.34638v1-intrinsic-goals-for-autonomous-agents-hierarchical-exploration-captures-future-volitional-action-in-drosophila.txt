ABSTRACT 

Animals have a remarkable ability to act autonomously—driven by intrinsic motivation—without external sensory cues or extrinsic objectives, yet the compu-tational mechanisms behind this ability largely remain a mystery. To understand how biological autonomy may emerge from optimizing intrinsic goals, we use re-cent whole-brain recordings in the fruit fly ( Drosophila melanogaster ) that iden-tify neural representations of spontaneous exploratory decisions more than 10 sec-onds in advance. Combined with a neuromechanical digital twin of Drosophila ,these recordings allow us to test whether intrinsically motivated artificial agents with realistic embodiments quantitatively match fly behavior and internal repre-sentations of future volitional action during exploration. We implement a hierar-chical, intrinsically motivated agent whose policy operates over a discrete library of motor primitives—fixed low-level controllers that actuate joint dynamics— closely matching ethological theories of behavioral syllables. We train agents with no external reward using 20 intrinsic motivation algorithms spanning curios-ity, entropy, and competency methods. We evaluate frozen policies in a virtual tethered-ball assay modeled after the fruit-fly experiment, allowing us to compare spontaneous locomotor statistics and internal representations and decode future turn direction from both kinematics and neural activations on the same timescales observed in flies. We find that DIAYN—a hierarchical competency algorithm that learns a discrete set of policies maximizing behavioral entropy and the separability of their induced sensory observations—achieves the strongest joint behavioral and neural prospective coding performance with the fruit fly. Surprisingly, DIAYN’s learned policies closely capture individual variability in fly exploration strategies, suggesting that this behavioral diversity optimizes a concrete computational goal of maximizing state-space coverage at the population level. Furthermore, DI-AYN agents’ latent activity closely captures the specific sum-difference rule in interhemispheric encoding of future kinematics in biological flies. Together, these results establish hierarchical, intrinsically motivated agents as a powerful frame-work for reverse-engineering animal autonomy.

They explore their environment and learn about how their own actions affect the world before those experiences are necessarily tied to a particular extrinsic goal. Understanding the computational objectives that explain intrinsically-driven animal behavior may therefore provide principles for building artificial agents that autonomously learn useful representations and behaviors

A wide range of intrinsic-motivation objectives have been proposed as computational mechanisms for generating autonomous behavior in the context of exploration and reinforcement learning. These include objectives such as novelty (Burda et al., 2019), prediction error (Pathak et al., 2017), un-certainty (Pathak et al., 2019), state-space coverage (Liu & Abbeel, 2021), empowerment (Klyubin et al., 2008), and unsupervised skill discovery (Eysenbach et al., 2019). However, these methods are typically evaluated according to criteria designed for artificial agents, such as downstream task performance on highly specific environments. Such evaluations do not directly test for autonomy, nor do they establish whether the resulting be-havior or internal representations resemble those of biological agents—systems that already exhibit robust autonomous behavior. Biological systems therefore provide an additional and independently motivated constraint on the space of possible intrinsic objectives. Hence, a candidate objective should not only produce useful exploration, but ideally reproduce measurable properties of animal behavior and neural activity (Keller et al., 2025). Recent whole-brain recordings in Drosophila provide a particularly useful “ground-truth” target for these kinds of comparisons. Brezovec et al. (2023) recorded neural activity while head-fixed flies explored spontaneously on a spherical treadmill in darkness. They found that interhemispheric ac-tivity in a subregion of the Inferior Posterior Slope (IPS) was weakly but significantly predictive of future left-versus-right turn direction approximately 15 to 8 seconds before a turn. This activity emerged well before the onset of widespread locomotion-related activity across the brain. This pro-vides two complementary constraints on computational hypotheses of internally generated behavior; namely, the spatial and temporal dynamics of the agent’s behavior and internal representations of 

future actions. Methods in computational ethology that describe spontaneous behavior of recurring behavioral syl-lables (Wiltschko et al., 2015; Mathis et al., 2018; Hsu & Yttri, 2021; Pereira et al., 2022) motivate us to consider the level of behavioral abstraction at which the intrinsic-drive methods should operate. 2Preprint. Under Review. These syllables exhibit organizational structure across multiple timescales, motivating our imple-mentation of a hierarchical policy distribution over motor primitives. These motor primitives are then implemented at the joint-torque level by a low-level central pattern generator (CPG) (Mantziaris et al., 2020; Chun et al., 2021; Lobato-Rios et al., 2022) and kinematic replays of real fly behaviors, such as grooming (Fig. 2b). This separates exploratory action selection from the mechanics of loco-motion and allows intrinsic objectives to operate over behaviorally meaningful variables rather than individual motor commands. We extend the goal-driven modeling approach of computational neuroscience (Yamins & DiCarlo, 2016; Richards et al., 2019; Feather et al., 2025), in which candidate objectives are instantiated in artificial networks and compared against neural recordings, to intrinsic motivation (Keller et al., 2025). We require that a candidate objective account for behavior as well as neural activity. Our agents are embodied in NeuroMechFly v2, a biomechanical model of Drosophila simulated in Fly-Gym, with vision, olfaction, proprioception, and articulated body dynamics (Lobato-Rios et al., 2022; Wang-Chen et al., 2024) (Fig. 1). We train recurrent policies with 20 intrinsic drive meth-ods (IDM) spanning curiosity, entropy, and competency objectives (Fig. 2a). All methods share the same embodiment, architecture, training environment, and training budget. We then freeze each policy and evaluate it in a virtual tethered-ball assay that reproduces the biological experiment of Brezovec et al. (2023) (Fig. 1b). For each agent, we compare turning behavior with that of the flies, test whether internal representations predict future turn direction on the timescale of the IPS signal, compare this with what ongoing behavior alone predicts, ask whether its low-dimensional activity shares IPS’s sum-difference relationship to angular and forward velocity. 

Contributions To summarize, our contributions are as follows: • We implement hierarchical, intrinsically motivated agents embodied in abiomechanically-realistic fly body, in which 20 intrinsic objectives operate over seven ethologically motivated behavioral primitives. 

• We show that representations of future action can emerge from intrinsic motivation alone, without an externally specified task or reward. In particular, several intrinsically motivated agents develop internal states that predict turn direction seconds in advance, paralleling the prospective signal observed in the fly IPS. • Among the intrinsic objectives tested, unsupervised skill discovery provides the strongest computational account of spontaneous population-wide fly behavior and its neural dynamics. DIAYN, hierarchically operating on behavioral primitives, jointly repro-duces future-action predictivity, the relationship between neural and behavioral predictabil-ity, inter-animal variability in turning behavior, and low-dimensional activity structure re-lated to forward and angular velocity. • We demonstrate that behavioral predictability and average locomotor statistics alone are insufficient to distinguish candidate explanations of spontaneous action. Objec-tives that generate predictable behavior or match mean turning bias can fail to reproduce the fly’s prospective internal signal; jointly evaluating behavioral variability and internal future-action predictivity distinguishes DIAYN from these alternatives. 

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

2.1 INTRINSICALLY GENERATED AND HIERARCHICALLY STRUCTURED ANIMAL BEHAVIOR 

Animals generate spontaneous behavior in the absence of explicit external reward or immediate instructive cues. Such cases provide a valuable setting for studying internally generated action se-lection. The field of computational ethology has shown that the behavior of animals such as the fly (Berman et al., 2014; 2016; Katsov et al., 2017), mouse (Wiltschko et al., 2015), zebrafish (Mar-ques et al., 2018) and C. elegans (Kaplan et al., 2020) can be decomposed into behavioral syllables 

(Anderson & Perona, 2014). A behavioral syllable is simply a recurring, stereotyped pattern of movement that can be reused and combined with other syllables to form more complex behavior. Recent studies have shown behavior exhibits hierarchical organization across syllables and across timescales. Specifically, across species, animal behavior can be represented at multiple nested lev-els of abstraction, with fine grained behavioral states carrying predictive information over short 3Preprint. Under Review. timescales and coarser groupings being informative over longer timescales (Berman et al., 2016; Kaplan et al., 2020; Mathis et al., 2018; Hsu & Yttri, 2021; Pereira et al., 2022). Advances in whole-brain neuroimaging of awake and behaving animals has provided unprecendented access to the neural dynamics of complex behavior (Mu et al., 2019; Mann et al., 2021; Aimon et al., 2023; Brezovec et al., 2024a). For example, Mu et al. (2019) image over 200,000 cells spanning neurons and astrocytes in autonomously behaving larval zebrafish, finding that glial cells play a crucial role in behavior by accumulating sensorimotor prediction error signaled by neurons. Here, we utilize recordings in flies during autonomous behavior by Brezovec et al. (2023) showing that across bi-laterally matched, locomotion-related superclusters distributed around the brain, summed bilateral activity was highly correlated with forward velocity, whereas interhemispheric activity differences were highly correlated with angular velocity. Crucially, this asymmetry emerges much earlier in a subregion of the IPS than the rest of the brain, around 15 to 8 seconds before a turn. Using this region’s activity, future turn direction can be predicted above chance. These findings provide measurable behavioral and representational signatures of internally generated action against which computational models can be evaluated. The multiscale organization of animal behavior motivates us to test a hierarchical architecture in which intrinsic drive operates over higher-level locomotor primitives while fixed low-level controllers execute joint dynamics. 2.2 COMPUTATIONAL THEORIES OF INTRINSIC MOTIVATION 

While the complete tapestry of intrinsic motivations that drive autonomous behavior in animals is not yet understood, multiple computational theories have been proposed to capture some essen-tial qualities of exploration. We organize the methods by the primary quantity they reward into three broad families: curiosity (latent prediction-error), entropy (replay diversity), and competency (policy identifiability). This taxonomy is intended as an organizational device rather than a strict partition of mutually exclusive principles. 

Curiosity Animals are driven by a desire to understand their environment and their place within it. This can be described as information-seeking behavior, which can be formalized in a variety of ways (Kidd & Hayden, 2015). Most intuitively, an animal can learn a forward model of sen-sory or sensorimotor dynamics and use its prediction-error or uncertainty over time to quantify how much it ”understands” things and makes progress thereafter (Schmidhuber, 2009). In reinforce-ment learning, this idea was taken seriously and developed early on by Schmidhuber (Schmidhuber, 1991a;b; 1997; 1999), leading to the development of numerous variants and adaptations. In this paper, we choose a representative mix of curiosity methods, from prediction-error to information-gain to learning progress. This includes Disagreement (Pathak et al., 2019), γ-Progress (Kim et al., 2020), Intrinsic Curiosity Module (ICM) (Pathak et al., 2017), Model-based Active Exploration 

(MAX) (Shyam et al., 2019), Never Give Up (NGU) (Badia et al., 2020), Random Network Dis-tillation (RND) (Burda et al., 2019), and Curiosity-driven Exploration via Temporal Contrastive Learning (C-TeC) (Mohamed et al., 2026). The essential object in all algorithms is some predictive model, and they differ in what exactly is predicted or how that model is used to form the intrinsic reward. 

Entropy Not all kinds of exploration are best explained by a drive to understand or predict the world. On the contrary, pursuing sensory novelty for novelty’s sake means that one might not care why or how a stimulus was produced. For example, mice enjoy highly variable sensory stimuli independently of learning. This preference persists, and even increases, after familiarity with the stimulus contingencies (Olsen & Winder, 2012; Dickson & Mittleman, 2020), suggesting animals are driven by the overall entropy of experience. Thus, entropy-based methods reward novelty with respect to the state or feature distribution, encouraging broad exploration coverage independent of preditability under an internal model. This family includes Active Pretraining (APT) (Liu & Abbeel, 2021), Reinforcement Learning with Prototypical Representations (Proto-RL) (Yarats et al., 2021), and State Entropy Maximization with Random Encoders for Efficient Exploration (RE3) (Seo et al., 2021). 

Competency Animals often behave to specifically isolate the role of their actions in generating sensory observations, akin to learning a causal model of the world in which controllability plays a central role. 3 METHODS 

3.1 HIERARCHICAL AGENT ARCHITECTURE AND BIOMECHANICAL MODEL 

Our agents are embodied in NeuroMechFly v2, a neuromechanical digital twin of an adult female 

Drosophila simulated in FlyGym with MuJoCo at a timestep of 10 −4 seconds (Lobato-Rios et al., 2022; Wang-Chen et al., 2024; Wang-Chen & Ramdya, 2026; Todorov et al., 2012). The policy re-ceives compound-eye vision from pale- and yellow-type ommatidia, a bilateral olfactory signal, and a proprioceptive state of joint angles, joint velocities, and body kinematics (Fig. 1c–e; App. A.1). Control is divided hierarchically (Fig. 2). Every 100 ms, the policy selects one of seven behavioral primitives: forward, left, hard left, right, hard right, stop, and groom. The six locomotor primitives set left and right descending signals that modulate a central pattern generator (CPG), whose phase indexes step trajectories recorded from real walking flies (Mantziaris et al., 2020; Chun et al., 2021). The groom primitive executes a kinematic replay of recorded fly grooming (App. A.2). Behavior therefore arises from the policy chaining primitives across many decisions, while joint-level mechan-ics are fixed across all intrinsic objectives. Modality-specific encoders produce a 310 -dimensional feature vector that feeds separate actor and critic LSTMs ( 256 units each), followed by MLP heads. The actor outputs a categorical distribution over the seven primitives (Fig. 2a; Table 2). We train 20 intrinsically motivated policies using methods across three broad families: curiosity, entropy, and competency (Sec. 2.2; App. A.4). The intrinsic reward is the only learning signal the policy receives. We use Recurrent PPO (Raffin et al., 2021; Schulman et al., 2017) in 2,048 parallel environments, procedurally generated arenas with three odor sources and 12 to 50 obstacles which vary the visual, olfactory, and proprioceptive input per episode (Fig. 1a). All methods share the same architecture, environment, hyperparameters, and budget of 50 million policy decisions (Table 5), and all analyses evaluate the final checkpoint. As controls, we evaluate randomly initialized policies with the same architectures as well as a policy trained on an extrinsic odor navigation task (App. A.7). Because DIAYN provides the strongest joint account of the behavioral and neural signatures exam-ined below, we describe its objective here to clarify how skill discovery interacts with our hierarchi-cal motor architecture; implementation details for DIAYN and all other intrinsic-drive methods are provided in App. A.4. 

DIAYN and Hierarchical Skill Discovery DIAYN (Eysenbach et al., 2019) learns a skill-conditioned policy by maximizing the objective

The first term, H(Z),encourages a diverse skill prior and is constant in our implementation because skills are sampled uniformly. Thus, each skill learns a distinct strategy for selecting among the same shared set of motor primitives as a function of the sensory state. 4 RESULTS 

Behavioral data is processed identically for agents and flies matching the procedure of Brezovec et al. (2023). First, velocities are smoothed with a 500 ms Savitzky-Golay filter (Savitzky & Golay, 1964). Then, turns are detected as peaks in absolute angular velocity above 200 ◦/s (Brezovec et al., 2023). Each evaluation rollout or fly experiment is summarized by its turning bias, log( NR/N L).To test for a prototypical planning representation, we decode future turn direction from the ac-tivity in the −15 to −8 s window using elastic-net penalized logistic regression (Zou & Hastie, 2005; Pedregosa et al., 2011) with five-fold stratified cross-validation and report balanced accuracy (Brodersen et al., 2010). Decoder hyperparameters are tuned once on the fly data and fixed for all agents. For agents, we evaluate seven candidate internal representations: the actor LSTM, critic LSTM, policy MLP, and value MLP representations individually, together with three concatenations of these recorded features. The largest concatenation additionally includes the learned observation embedding. As a separate input-level control, we also decode from the raw proprioceptive observa-tion, with vision and odor zeroed. For the biological fly, performance is decoded from IPS activity (App. A.8). Because future turns may be predictable from current behavior alone, we fit the same decoder to mean angular and forward velocity in the same window to identify policies that converged to a relatively stable, predictable behavioral strategy (App. A.9). Finally, to compare the represen-tation geometry without assuming an anatomical correspondence, we apply PCA to each agent’s activity in its highest-performing neural layer for decoding. We test whether pairs of components relate to angular and forward velocity as the IPS hemispheres do (App. A.11). 

Neural and Behavioral Decoding Using the network activations from the models in the tethered-ball evaluation, we follow the same conceptual test used for the biological fly, asking whether inter-nal activity before a turn predicts its eventual direction and plot the prospective coding in Fig. 3a. The biological fly IPS has a neural prospective coding of 66 .5 ± 1.4% while DIAYN and NGU of the IDM-trained models approach or exceed this performance with 82 .1 ± 0.9% and 65 .4 ± 1.8% 

respectively (Fig. 3a). Many IDM-trained policies are near 50% prospective coding or had an un-defined prospective coding due to never having a detected turn or only having detected turns of a single direction. Neither condition permits stratified fold cross-validation for the accuracy calcula-tion. Analogously, we decode turn direction from behavior. Using the mean angular ( ω) and forward (v) velocity of the ball in the before-turn window, we fit a logistic regression classifier to decode the future direction of the turn and plot the prospective coding in Fig. 3c. For the flies, behavior alone predicts future turn direction with a prospective coding of 60 .5 ± 0.9% . For the IDM-trained poli-cies, many exhibit behavior that is predictive of future turn direction and decodable including MAX (92 .2 ± 5.9% ), DIAYN ( 74 .7 ± 1.4% ), γ-progress ( 72 .4 ± 1.2% ) and NGU ( 66 .3 ± 9.4% ). Nine of the 20 methods produce no turns or turns in only one direction during evaluation (Fig. 3e), itself a behavioral mismatch with the fly.

Critically, an agent whose behavior resembles the fly’s should show a similar ratio of neural to behavioral decoding accuracy: internal states should carry predictive information beyond behavior, while behavior itself is structured but not trivially predictable. Therefore, Fig. 3d considers neural and behavioral pre-dictability jointly. Among the trained agents, DIAYN and NGU are the only IDMs that match or exceed the neural and behavioral predictability of the fly. Turning Bias Models that match the mean statistics of the flies do not match the flies’ population variability. Fig. 4a shows each model’s distribution of turning biases. Models whose mean bias is close to the flies’, such as APT and the random controls, do not reproduce the fly population of turning biases. In contrast, DIAYN’s skills span a much wider range of turning biases, yielding a more similar state space coverage to the flies (Fig. 4b) based on trajectory coverage. DIAYN holds one skill per 30 -minute rollout, so its nine rollouts sample nine skills, paralleling the nine flies. CSF and Random-DIAYN use the same schedule yet show far narrower bias distributions (Fig. 4a), indicating that the fly-like spread arises from the learned objective rather than the sampling scheme. 

Internal Representations The internal activation dynamics of the models were also compared with the fly neural dynamics. As shown in Fig. 5a, neural activity in the fly IPS forms separable structures whose difference and sum is correlated with angular and forward velocity, respectively. Fig. 5b shows that trained DIAYN recovers a similar relationship, whereas this organization is absent in the random-initialized control (Fig. 5c). For trained DIAYN, the difference of the selected component pair correlates with ω (r = 0 .153 ) and their sum with v (r = 0 .413 ), comparable to the fly IPS ( r = 0 .191 and 0.429 ). In Random-DIAYN, the best pair reaches only r = 0 .021 and 0.113 .

5 DISCUSSION 

The central implication of our results is that behavioral diversity and prospective internal activity may reflect a shared computational objective. Methods that match average turning bias or generate highly predictable behavior need not reproduce the accompanying predictive neural representation (Figs. 3 and 4). DIAYN’s joint correspondence with these properties therefore motivates an ex-planation of autonomy that addresses both the diversity of spontaneous behavior and the internal organization that makes future actions predictable. In our hierarchical implementation, DIAYN learns strategies for selecting among seven motor prim-itives, so its objective concerns the organization of behavior above the level of individual motor commands. It rewards skills that induce distinguishable sensory states while encouraging high-entropy action selection within each skill. Because each skill is held fixed throughout a training or 9Preprint. Under Review. evaluation episode, we interpret it as a persistent exploratory strategy consistent with the locomo-tor handedness observed in individual flies (Buchanan et al., 2015; Ayroles et al., 2015; de Bivort et al., 2022). Brezovec et al. (2023) show wide individual variation in fly exploration strategies, as reflected in their turning biases, and remarkably, DIAYN’s learned repertoire of skills captures this variation quite well (Fig. 4). The repertoire thus offers a model of behavioral diversity across a 

population of flies. One possible explanation is that the fly brain’s limited computational capacity favors a single, relatively fixed exploratory strategy per individual, while the diversity of strategies needed for survival is realized through their distribution across individuals to maximize state-space coverage as a population . Under this hypothesis, evolutionary pressures favor diverse individual strategies that collectively maximize coverage of sensory space. Handedness and other persistent behavioral tendencies we observe in individuals could also be interpreted as individual expressions of this population-level diversity. Skill discovery may also help explain why internal activity becomes predictive of future turns. Al-though DIAYN’s intrinsic reward is evaluated at individual time steps, PPO optimizes the policy to maximize cumulative intrinsic reward over trajectories. This optimization can favor sustained patterns of behavior that keep the sensory states induced by different skills distinguishable over time. In our hierarchical controller, such patterns can arise through persistent, skill-dependent ten-dencies to select particular motor primitives. The recurrent state may therefore encode the ongo-ing exploratory strategy and carry information about turns that occur seconds later. Under this hypothesis, prospective activity reflects the ongoing organization of a behavioral strategy rather than moment-by-momemnt movements, consistent with the findings in real fruit flies in Brezovec et al. (2023). Hierarchical skill-discovery therefore provides a concrete computational framework in which animal-like behavioral diversity and future-action representations emerge together under a common intrinsic objective. 

6 CONCLUSION 

We introduce a framework for reverse-engineering animal autonomy that combines a neuromechan-ical digital twin, hierarchical control over behavioral primitives, and joint comparison with fly be-havior and neural recordings. Across 20 intrinsic objectives, DIAYN provides the strongest account of individual differences in turn decisions, prospective neural activity, and interhemispheric sum-difference encoding of kinematics. Importantly, these properties emerge entirely from optimizing a self-supervised, intrinsic goal without training on external rewards or fly data. Our results show that individual behavioral variability and shared representations of future action can arise from the same computational objective. DIAYN links these findings through persistent exploration strategies whose internal states predict future turns seconds in advance. This suggests a novel perspective on the role of individual differences in optimizing exploration at the population level, an efficient strategy for survival of a species when the capacity of individual members is limited. These results establish the importance of using both neural and behavioral data in distinguishing computational models, the role of hierarchy in designing agents that naturally express complex behavior, and the potential of embodied agents and intrinsic reinforcement learning to understand principles of animal autonomy. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

One main limitation of our approach is the choice to constrain the space of behaviors available to each policy to the predefined locomotor primitives and low-level controllers. While this reflects the hierarchical organization of animal behavior, it limits the learned behavior primarily to 2-D locomotion. Future work could learn the primitives jointly with their high-level selection, allowing the IDM to shape its low-level repertoire, or expanding the primitives to allow flight, potentially extending the pre-existing implementation of Vaxenburg et al. (2025). Another opportunity is to extend our framework to account for the whole-brain dynamics of locomo-tion (Brezovec et al., 2024a). Due to our focus on IPS, a simple training environment was sufficient to reproduce key features of those neural responses. A whole-brain pursuit would force us to con-front an ethnologically relevant environment with variable terrain, opportunities for manipulation and resources that may favor different intrinsic objectives and thereby generate a broader repertoire of behaviors—which is possible to explore within the agent-based framework we build here. 10 Preprint. Policies use the recurrent actor–critic of sb3-contrib RecurrentPPO (Raffin et al., 2021), with a shared multimodal feature extractor and separate actor and critic LSTMs (Hochreiter & Schmidhu-ber, 1997) (Fig. 2a; Table 2). A CNN (Fukushima, 1980; LeCun et al., 1998) with weights shared across the two eyes produces a 64 -dimensional embedding per eye, for a 128 -dimensional visual representation. MLPs (Rumelhart et al., 1986) embed odor ( 16 dimensions) and proprioception (64 dimensions). The odor embedding is concatenated with the raw 2-dimensional odor observation and the proprioceptive embedding with the raw 100 -dimensional proprioceptive state, yielding modality representations of 128 , 18 , and 164 dimensions and a 310 -dimensional base feature vector ϕt. This is passed to the actor and critic LSTMs ( 256 hidden units each) and to the intrinsic drive module (IDM). Separate actor and critic MLP heads follow the LSTMs, and the actor outputs a categorical distribution over the seven primitives. For skill-conditioned methods, the skill z is appended to ϕt at the input of both LSTMs. The IDM receives only ϕt, never z, which prevents the skill discriminator from being solved trivially. Linear and convolutional layers use orthogonal initialization (gain √2; 0.01 for the action head and 1 for the value head), and the LSTMs use PyTorch’s default initialization. Optimization. Policies are trained with Recurrent PPO (Raffin et al., 2021; Schulman et al., 2017) in 2, 048 parallel environments, each with a distinct random seed, using the settings in Table 5 and generalized advantage estimation (Schulman et al., 2016). Each method is trained for the same bud-get of 50 million policy decisions, summed across environments ( 5 × 10 6 s of simulated experience, approximately 244 PPO updates). The scalar reward used by PPO is supplied entirely by the in-trinsic drive module, so differences between trained policies arise from the intrinsic objective rather than from external task reward. LSTM states are reset at episode starts and carried across rollout boundaries; backpropagation through time runs over sequences split at episode boundaries (at most 

50 decisions). Truncated episodes are bootstrapped with the value of the final observation, and opti-mization epochs stop early when the approximate KL divergence exceeds 1.5 times the target. Each method is trained once, with seed 0. We evaluate the checkpoint at the end of the training budget, without selecting checkpoints on any evaluation metric.

Recorded activations. At every decision we record the input embedding ϕt, the actor and critic LSTM hidden states, and the policy and value MLP hidden representations. Activations are averaged over consecutive 0.532 s bins from rollout onset, matching the fly volume rate (1.88 Hz), giving 3,384 bins per rollout. A.7 CONTROLS 

Random initialization. Each control policy is a random initialization of a source policy so the LSTM input has the same size as in the trained policy. Random-DIAYN, Random-CSF, Random-VIC (the reference for VIC and VALOR), and Random-DADS (the reference for DADS and rDADS) retain the corresponding skill input; Random-nonskill is derived from a run of a method without a skill input. Controls are evaluated exactly as trained policies. 

Extrinsic reward. The extrinsic baseline uses the same architecture and PPO settings, but is trained on an odor-seeking task instead of an intrinsic reward. Each arena has one odor source at x ∈ [20 , 30] mm, y ∈ [−6, 6] mm and 3 to 5 obstacles, and the reward is rt = ( dt−1 − dt) −

0.01 + 10 1{dt<2 mm }, where dt is the distance to the source. Episodes terminate on reaching the source, and reward normalization is enabled. The baseline was trained for 10 M decision steps, by which it had saturated the task success rate. This final checkpoint was used for evaluation. A.8 BIOLOGICAL DATA

For agents and flies, angular and forward velocity, sampled at 50 Hz, are smoothed with a Savitzky– Golay filter (Savitzky & Golay, 1964) (25 samples, i.e. 0.5 s; polynomial order 3). Turns are local maxima of |ω| exceeding 200 ◦/s, following Brezovec et al. (2023); the turn time is the time of the peak. For flies, peaks in the first and last 40 s of each session are discarded. To compare behavior, we compute the number of left and right turns, NL and NR, for each rollout or fly, and the turning bias 

log( NR/N L). Turning bias is reported only when NL > 0 and NR > 0; otherwise it is undefined, and such rollouts are omitted from bias summaries but retained in turn counts. A.10 DECODING 

Features. For each turn, features are averaged over a window relative to the turn: −15 to −8 s 

(before the turn) or −0.4 to +0 .4 s (during the turn). For agents, the average is over the 0.532 s acti-vation bins whose left edge falls within the window. We record five internal network representations: the learned observation embedding, the actor LSTM hidden state, the critic LSTM hidden state, the policy MLP hidden representation and the value MLP hidden representation. The network decoding (neural prospective coding) analysis evaluates seven candidate feature sets: the actor LSTM, critic LSTM, policy MLP and value MLP individually, and three concatenations of layers: actor LSTM with policy MLP (A CTOR ), critic LSTM with value MLP (C RITIC ), and all five recorded internal representations (A LL ). The learned observation embedding is included in A LL . As a separate input-level control, we decode from the raw proprioceptive observations with vision and odor set to zero. For flies, features are the window-averaged activity of every voxel in the left and right IPS, concate-nated; unlike Brezovec et al. (2023), we neither restrict the analysis to the pre-motor supercluster nor use the interhemispheric difference. Behavioral features are the window means of angular and forward velocity. 

Classifier. We use logistic regression with an elastic-net penalty (Zou & Hastie, 2005; Pe-dregosa et al., 2011) and the saga solver (Defazio et al., 2014). Hyperparameters were cho-sen by nested cross-validation on the fly IPS data over C ∈ { 10 −4, 10 −3, . . . , 10 4} and ℓ1 ratio 

∈ { 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 }, and the most frequently selected configuration ( C = 0 .1, ℓ1 ratio 0.75 ) is used for all decoders of agents and flies. Features are standardized with statistics from the training fold only. 25 Preprint. Under Review.

Because the policy has no anatomical analogue of the fly’s bilaterally matched superclusters, we cannot reproduce the spatial pairing analysis of Brezovec et al. (2023) directly. Instead, we use PCA to identify low-dimensional axes of model activity and ask whether pairs of components show the same functional relationship to behavior: opposing activity associated with angular velocity and shared activity associated with forward velocity. This is a functional comparison of representational geometry, not an anatomical correspondence between model components and fly hemispheres. For DIAYN and Random-DIAYN, we fit a 10-component PCA (randomized SVD, seed 0) to the actor LSTM activations pooled across all nine rollouts ( 9 × 3,384 samples; units centered but not standardized), and scale each component to unit variance. Angular and forward velocity are inter-polated to the activation timestamps. For every unordered pair (i, j ) of components, we compute 

Sij = |r(PC i − PC j , ω )| + |r(PC i + PC j , v )| , (7) where r is the Pearson correlation over all timepoints, and select the maximizing pair, breaking ties by the smaller of the two correlations. The pair is oriented so that the difference increases with angular velocity. The same search is applied to the randomly initialized policy as a reference. The selected pairs are PCs 2 and 8 for DIAYN and PCs 5 and 10 for Random-DIAYN (Fig. 5). The biological panel (Fig. 5a) is reconstructed from Fig. 1e–f of Brezovec et al. (2023).
