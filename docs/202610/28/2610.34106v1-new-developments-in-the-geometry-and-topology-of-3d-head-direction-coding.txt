# New developments in the geometry and topology of 3D head-direction coding 

# Jean Laurens 

Ernst Strüngmann Institute of the Max Planck Society, Frankfurt, Germany 

# Abstract 

The head-direction (HD) system is a neuronal compass that represents head orientation in 3D. Its encoding of 3D head orientation can be decomposed into a multiplexed representation of 2D head tilt relative to gravity and 1D head orientation in the horizontal plane (azimuth). Defining azimuth during 3D motion is not trivial, and recent studies have indicated that the HD system implements a so-called dual-axis/tilted-azimuth (DA/TA) rule, a representation that is well supported experimentally but remains mathematically and mechanistically difficult to interpret. Here, I introduce a new formulation of DA/TA based directly on the elements of a rotation matrix describing 3D head orientation. In this formulation, TA is represented by a 2D vector, whose direction encode TA and whose norm depends on tilt, consistent with a tilt-dependent gain of HD tuning reported by experimental studies. I provide a geometrical interpretation of this formulation and use it to characterize the topology of the resulting representation. More generally, I introduce a framework in which head orientation is represented by a rotation matrix and neuronal responses arise from linear combinations of its elements followed by nonlinear transformations. Within this framework, the linear stage specifies which aspects of 3D orientation are encoded, and therefore allow relating neuronal representation to linear algebra concepts. Classical HD tuning, gravity tuning, and DA/TA can all be described within this common framework. Together, these results provide a new mathematical, geometrical, and topological perspective on 3D HD coding. 

# Introduction 

The head direction (HD) cell system, also known as the brain’s internal compass, is a distributed neural system that tracks head orientation in space (Angelaki & Laurens, 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2018; Taube, 2007). Historically, it has been described and studied primarily as a horizontal compass, encoding a single angular degree of freedom: head azimuth in the horizontal plane ( Fig. 1A , left). Over the past decade, however, a series of studies (Angelaki et al., 2020; Finkelstein et al., 2015; LaChance et al., 2020; Laurens et al., 2016; Page et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2026) has demonstrated that the HD system has fundamental 3D properties, which can be divided into two distinct and complementary aspects of 3D HD coding. 

- First, multiple studies (Angelaki et al., 2020; Finkelstein et al., 2015; Laurens et al., 2016) have shown that HD-related brain regions encode head tilt relative to the vertical ( Fig. 1A ,right). Unlike azimuth, which is described by a single angular variable, head tilt has two degrees of freedom and specifies the orientation of the head’s vertical axis relative to the allocentric vertical. Subsequent experiments (Angelaki et al., 2020) demonstrated that this Page 2 of 21 

representation is anchored to gravity, which is sensed by the otolith organs in the inner ear. Tilt coding has therefore also been referred to as gravity tuning. 

- A second line of work has addressed a different question: how is azimuth encoded when the head itself is tilted away from upright? Across laboratories, a common model has emerged, initially proposed as the dual-axis (DA) model ( Fig. 1B , see legend for definition) and subsequently described, using a different mathematical formulation, as the tilted-azimuth (TA) model ( Fig. 1C , see legend for definition). Altogether, experimental support for the DA/TA model has been obtained by several laboratories (Angelaki et al., 2020; LaChance et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2019; Page et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2026). This framework also generalizes earlier descriptions that were restricted to rotations about particular axes, including the toroidal geometry proposed by Finkelstein et al. (Finkelstein et al., 2015) and earlier observations of HD-cell activity (Taube, 2011; Taube et al., 2013). 

Conceptually, gravity tuning is rather straightforward to describe. Angelaki et al. (Angelaki et al., 2020) characterized it as a neuronal population code with Gaussian tuning to the 3D gravity signal. Gravity is sensed by the otolith organs of the inner ear as a 3D vector, and gravitational signals are further extracted from vestibular information by brainstem and cerebellar circuits (Laurens, 2022; Laurens et al., 2013). The orientation and magnitude of the gravity vector relative to the head are therefore readily available to HD-related regions, presumably through direct or indirect projections from the vestibular nuclei to the HD system (Mehlman et al., 2021).

In contrast, although the DA/TA model is well defined and strongly supported experimentally, its mathematical formulation is considerably less intuitive. Existing formulations ( Fig. 2B,C ) require complex geometrical transformations and contain a singularity at the inverted head orientation: Page 3 of 21 

when the head is exactly upside down, TA becomes undefined because the definitions in Fig. 2B,C 

admit an infinity of solutions. Furthermore, small head rotations in the vicinity of this orientation can lead to large changes in TA. This raises a general mechanistic question: how might neural circuits actually compute such a representation? A further complication is that the azimuthal tuning of HDCs progressively weakens as the head tilts away from upright and toward inversion. Empirically, this attenuation can be described by a tilt-dependent gain factor, approximating a smooth cosine-like function (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2019). Thus, existing descriptions require both a relatively complex transformation to define TA and an additional tilt-dependent modulation of its neuronal representation. 

A useful perspective on this problem comes from recent work on the representation of 3D rotations in artificial neural networks. Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2019) pointed out that familiar low-dimensional parameterizations of rotation, including Euler angles and quaternions, necessarily introduce discontinuities when represented in Euclidean spaces of four or fewer dimensions. They argued that, for neural networks, it can therefore be advantageous to abandon minimal coordinate descriptions in favor of higher-dimensional, continuous representations of rotation. 

Here, I show that this is indeed possible for DA/TA azimuth. Starting directly from a rotation matrix describing 3D head orientation, TA and its tilt-dependent gain can be represented jointly by a simple 2D vector obtained through a linear operation. The direction of this vector specifies TA, whereas its magnitude decreases continuously as the head tilts away from upright, reaching zero when the head is inverted. Thus, DA/TA becomes mathematically simpler when represented in this form, and the singularity at inversion is naturally resolved: azimuth itself remains undefined at the inverted orientation, but the magnitude of its neural representation goes to zero, such that the representation as a whole remains continuous. 

Finally, I place this formulation within a more general framework, directly inspired by Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2019), where head orientation is described by the nine elements of a rotation matrix, and neuronal responses are modeled using a linear–nonlinear (L–NL) cascade applied to these elements. I focus primarily on the linear stage of this cascade, which defines the information bottleneck of the model—that is, which information about 3D head orientation is encoded by the neuron before the subsequent response nonlinearity is applied. Within this framework, both tilted-azimuth coding and gravity tuning can be expressed using simple linear combinations of rotation-matrix elements, providing a simpler starting point for investigating how these representations could be generated by neural circuits.

Yet, as pointed out in (Zhou et al., 2019), this representation is redundant because the rows and columns of a rotation matrix are mutually orthogonal unit vectors. Any one row can be reconstructed from the other two using a vector product, and the same is true for the columns. A six-dimensional representation could therefore be obtained by retaining only two rows or two columns of the matrix, and Zhou et al. provide an additional reduction to 5 dimensions. We do not make this simplification here because the full matrix provides a convenient starting point.

The point that we will make through this manuscript is that (1) the representation is continuous and (2), provided that the non-linear stages don’t lose any information, the nature of the information represented depends on the first linear stage . Thus, our focus will be on understanding what this first linear stage represents, which in turns will allow to understand the underlying sensory processing using linear algebra concepts.

# A new expression for Tilted Azimuth 

## Background 

We have described how the R–L–NL framework accounts for classical models of HDC tuning in 1D, i.e. when the head is upright. During general 3D movements, experimental studies have shown that HDCs follow a more general model, first developed by Page et al. (Page et al., 2018) (under the name Double-Axis rule) and subsequently by Angelaki et al. (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2018) (under the name Tilted Azimuth, i.e. 𝑇𝑇 𝐴𝐴  which I will use here). 

In Page et al.’s definition, head rotation ( 𝛿𝛿Ω ) is be decomposed into a rotation ( 𝛿𝛿Ω𝑧𝑧 ) around the egocentric yaw axis 𝑧𝑧 and a rotation ( 𝛿𝛿Ω𝑘𝑘 ) around the allocentric axis 𝑘𝑘 (Fig. 1B ), and HDCs are updated by the sum of these components, i.e. 

𝛿𝛿 𝑇𝑇𝐴𝐴  = 𝛿𝛿Ω𝑧𝑧 + 𝛿𝛿Ω𝑘𝑘 

Note that this decomposition is unique except in two orientations, (1) when upright, in which case the axes 𝑧𝑧 and 𝑘𝑘 are aligned, and a rotation 𝛿𝛿Ω can be expressed as 𝛿𝛿Ω𝑧𝑧 or 𝛿𝛿Ω𝑘𝑘 indifferently; and (2) when upside down, in which case the axes 𝑧𝑧 and 𝑘𝑘 are antiparallel, and in which case 𝛿𝛿 𝑇𝑇𝐴𝐴  can’t be defined. 

I also provide a brief and straightforward extension of this model, by defining a third ‘null’ rule. In general, any 3D rotation vector will be the sum of 3 components, around that vectors 𝑧𝑧 and 𝑘𝑘 

defined above, and the third vector 𝑛𝑛 defined as 𝑛𝑛 = 𝑧𝑧 × 𝑘𝑘 , i.e. perpendicular to 𝑧𝑧 and 𝑘𝑘 . This vector can be seen as a ‘null’ axis, as rotations around it don’t update 𝑇𝑇 𝐴𝐴 . Defining it is useful e.g. for conceptualization and comparison with other models. 

In Angelaki et al.’s definition (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2018), TA is expressed as follows ( Fig. 1C ): starting from a given head orientation, one rotates the head toward upright, by the shortest possible rotation, which is through an axis orthogonal to both 𝑧𝑧 and 𝑘𝑘 (i.e. 𝑛𝑛 ) until it reaches the upright orientation. The TA at the original orientation is then defined as the conventional head azimuth of this upright orientation. 

Both definition are equivalent and can be used to compute TA at any head orientation (except upside-down). Nevertheless, both formulations rely on relatively involved geometric constructions, which complicated efforts to understand how the brain may compute or update TA. 

In addition to the TA computation itself, studies in rodents (Angelaki et al., 2020; Calton, 2005; Laurens & Angelaki, 2019) have revealed that HDCs progressively lose their directional tuning as the animal tilts beyond approximately 90°. Some studies have described this loss as an abrupt disappearance of tuning (Calton, 2005), whereas others (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2019) have reported a more gradual attenuation, approximately following a cosine function of head tilt. Within existing models, this phenomenon is typically treated as an additional gain modulation applied after the TA has been computed, introducing an extra stage of processing. Page 10 of 21

# Discussion 

Here, I have proposed a general framework for describing the representation of 3D head orientation by HDCs, together with a new formulation of Tilted Azimuth (TA). The central idea, based on (Zhou et al., 2019) is to represent head orientation directly through the elements of a rotation matrix and to consider neuronal responses as resulting from a linear transformation of these elements followed by one or more nonlinear operations. Using this approach, previous models of HDC tuning and gravity tuning can be expressed naturally, while TA admits a particularly simple linear formulation. This formulation jointly represents TA and its tilt-dependent attenuation, provides an intuitive geometrical interpretation, and leads to a continuous representation in which the singularity at the inverted orientation collapses to the origin. Together with gravity tuning, it also suggests a topology for the currently known representation of 3D head orientation. 

## R-L-NL framework 

The interest of the R–L–NL framework is not in its ability to fit neuronal data per se: there is no doubt that it has enough free parameters to fit a wide range of neuronal response properties. The usefulness in the present context, is to analyze what information is selected as the first linear stage. This is because, provided that the nonlinear stage does not discard information, the linear stage defines what the neurons encode, and allow us to analyze this information through the lens of linear algebra. 

Classical HD tuning provides a simple illustration. A von Mises tuning curve can be decomposed into a linear cosine code followed by an exponential nonlinearity. Thus, beneath the familiar bell-shaped tuning curve, the relevant linear representation is simply a cosine code for horizontal azimuth. The same approach also clarifies gravity tuning. Although gravity-tuned responses have previously been modeled using 3D Gaussian functions (Angelaki et al., 2020), the information underlying these tuning curves is considerably simpler: the egocentric gravity vector itself, readily encoded in central vestibular pathways (Laurens, 2022; Laurens et al., 2013). 

## A new formulation of Tilted Azimuth 

The main new result concerns TA. Previous DA/TA formulations (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2018; Page et al., 2018) provide well-defined geometrical rules for updating or computing azimuth during 3D head motion, but these constructions are relatively involved and become singular when the head is exactly inverted. In addition, the experimentally observed attenuation of azimuth tuning with increasing head tilt has been treated as a separate gain modulation (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2019). The formulation introduced here combines these two phenomena into a single two-dimensional vector obtained linearly from the rotation matrix. Its direction corresponds to TA, while its magnitude decreases continuously with tilt, from unity when upright to zero when inverted. Thus, TA and its tilt-dependent gain need not be treated as two separate computations: they emerge jointly from the same linear representation. 

This formulation also provides a new geometrical interpretation of the DA/TA. Conventional azimuth is typically defined in reference to the head’s forward axis, but could also be defined with reference with the head’s lateral, or other axes. When the head tilts, each such axis can be projected onto the allocentric horizontal plane to provide an azimuth estimate. The TA vector introduced Page 16 of 21 

here can be understood as the average between two such projections – or even more. This interpretation explains both the direction of TA and the decrease in vector magnitude with tilt. Thus, the new expression can be viewed not merely as an algebraic simplification, but as a geometrical description of TA as an average across multiple estimates of azimuth referenced to multiple axes within the head horizontal plane. 

## Topology 

The state space of the tilt-modulated TA signal is consequently a 2D disc rather than a circle. Combining this disc with the gravity representation provides a possible description of the topology of the full 3D head-orientation code ( Fig. 6 ). At each nonzero tilt angle, TA and the direction of head tilt define two circular variables and therefore a toroidal surface. As absolute tilt changes, the dimensions of these tori change continuously. The resulting representation can therefore be viewed as a continuous 3D manifold embedded within the higher-dimensional neural state space defined jointly by the TA and gravity variables. Interestingly, the TA is encoded by two variables and gravity by 3, implying that the neural state space is 5D, as proposed by (Zhou et al., 2019). 

An important consequence of this topology of TA concerns the singularity of azimuth at the inverted orientation. The hairy-ball theorem (Jeffery et al., 2015) implies that azimuth cannot be defined continuously over all possible 3D head orientations without a singularity. The present formulation does not make this fundamental problem disappear. Instead, the magnitude of the TA representation decreases continuously to zero as inversion is approached. At the inverted orientation, TA itself remains undefined, but its vector representation has collapsed to the origin and is therefore continuous. In this sense, the singularity is resolved by a loss of azimuth information, and a discontinuity in the neural representation is avoided. 

## Tilt-dependent modulation of TA coding 

Several studies have reported that the ability of HDCs to encode azimuth decreases when the head tilts. We have described this phenomenon as a smoot, cosine-like gain modulation in (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2019) whereas other authors have evoked a more abrupt cutoff beyond 90° tilt (Calton, 2005). The TA vector model described here directly produces a smooth tilt-dependent attenuation, comparable to that observed in our previous studies. However, the model is not incompatible with a more abrupt cutoff, which could result from an appropriate nonlinearity applied to the TA vector. Thus, the precise shape of the attenuation need not be determined by the linear representation itself. In all cases, the central feature of the proposed TA vector, i.e. a progressive loss of azimuth information as the head approaches inversion, is qualitatively consistent with previous reports of tilt-dependent attenuation. 

## Coding at the inverted orientation 

One particularly interesting feature of this model is therefore the loss of azimuth information at inversion. This property is not introduced uniquely by the present work; it is an explicit feature of the previous DA/TA framework (Angelaki et al., 2020; Laurens & Angelaki, 2018; Page et al., 2018). Taken literally, however, it raises the question of whether an animal can maintain an allocentric heading representation while fully inverted. Existing observations do not yet provide a definitive answer. HD tuning and behavioral performance degrade in rats navigating inverted Page 17 of 21 

(Taube, 2011; Valerio et al., 2010), but this may partially reflect the locomotor challenge for rats, which are poorly adapted to inverted locomotion. Conversely, azimuth-related activity has been reported in inverted bats (Finkelstein et al., 2015). One may attribute this distinction to species differences - bats and other highly arboreal species, may be more adapted to navigating inverted. However, I would caution against this conclusion: 3D head orientation should not be equated exclusively with climbing. A terrestrial animal can easily bring the head through very large tilt angles by tilting, lying on their back, etc. 

I would therefore argue that further experiments are required to elucidate HD coding in inverted orientation. Should they reveal robust azimuth coding in inverted orientation, this would require either a revision of the DA/TA model itself or the addition of a complementary directional signal, encoded differently. 

## Conclusion 

What has this work accomplished? First, in my mind, it has clarified the topology of the DA/TA representation, at least based on what we know so far. It has also introduced a new mathematical expression for DA/TA, together with a corresponding geometrical interpretation ( Fig. 5 ). I would not necessarily argue that this formulation is superior to the previous ones ( Fig. 1 ); rather, I hope that it provides another way to understand this representation, both intuitively and mechanistically. 

Since its introduction by Page et al. (Page et al., 2018), the DA/TA rule has struck me as a somewhat counterintuitive; perhaps even bizarre. Yet, a growing body of experimental evidence (including mine, (Angelaki et al., 2020)) supports it. I still find myself confronted with the same fundamental questions: what does this representation mean to the brain? Why does the brain represent head direction in this particular way? Having two distinct formulations of DA/TA ( Fig. 1B,C ) has already given us different ways of looking at the same phenomenon, and therefore different ways of understanding it. Here, I offer a third, together with a geometrical and topological interpretation. It may not yet provide a complete answer to these questions, but I hope that, alongside the work of others, it will help pave the way toward one.

Our demonstration is based on the following property of DA or TA azimuth, which is that if, starting from any orientation R, the head is rotated back to upright through the shortest rotation, then TA is equal to the traditional azimuth at that orientation, i.e. 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎 𝑛𝑛 2( 𝑥𝑥2 , 𝑥𝑥1 ).
