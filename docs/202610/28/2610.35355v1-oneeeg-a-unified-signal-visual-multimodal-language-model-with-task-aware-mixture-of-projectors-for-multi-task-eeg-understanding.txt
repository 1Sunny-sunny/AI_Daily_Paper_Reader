# OneEEG: A Unified Signal-Visual Multimodal Language Model with Task-Aware Mixture of Projectors for Multi-Task EEG Understanding 

Jiacheng Zhang, Xueyi Zhang, Xin Lu, Siqi Cai, Haizhou Li 

Abstract —Electroencephalography (EEG) is widely used in clinical assessment, brain-computer interfaces, and cognitive-state analysis, yet EEG modeling remains fragmented across task paradigms. Recent multimodal large language models (MLLMs) provide a promising interface for flexible multimodal reasoning, but their ability to understand EEG inputs remains limited. In this work, we propose OneEEG, a unified EEG-MLLM framework for multi-task EEG understanding. By training on our curated OneEEG-Instruct, a multi-task EEG instruction-tuning dataset, OneEEG jointly models numerical EEG representations and visual EEG representations, and introduces a task-aware Mixture of Projectors (MoP) to align heterogeneous EEG features across different task families with the LLM embedding space. We further establish OneEEG-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating current MLLMs and our method on multi-task EEG under-standing. Experiments show that existing MLLMs have limited ability to interpret EEG inputs, while OneEEG achieves stronger performance by integrating numerical and visual EEG repre-sentations with task-aware EEG-to-LLM projection. Extensive ablations and analyses further reveal the effects of visual EEG renderings, MoP variants, router behavior, and feature dispersion across EEG tasks. 

Index Terms —Electroencephalography analysis, multimodal large language models, biomedical signal processing, mixture of experts.

As a result, most EEG models are still developed under task-specific assumptions [8]–[10], with dedicated preprocessing procedures and model architectures tailored to individual datasets or label spaces. One line of work adopts a standard MLLM paradigm [11], [13], where the EEG encoder extracts features from numerical signals, and a shared projector maps them into the LLM em-arXiv:2610.35355v1 [cs.LG] 26 Oct 2026 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON MULTIMEDIA 2

Another line of work studies image-form EEG understanding, where EEG signals are rendered as waveform plots, spectra, or topographic maps and interpreted by vision-language models [12], [14]. However, these paradigms suffer from two important limitations. (1) Single projector. A single shared projector may be insufficient for aligning heterogeneous EEG representations with the semantic space of an LLM. Unlike natural images or text, EEG is a low-signal-to-noise physiological signal with weak intrinsic semantic information, and EEG features can be highly dispersed across different tasks. Projecting such heterogeneous features through a single mapping module may introduce cross-task interference and weaken task-specific semantic alignment. (2) Single input form. Existing approaches often support only one form of EEG input, either a frame of multiple 1-dimensional EEG signals, i.e., numerical EEG representations, or a frame of 2-dimensional visual image of EEG signals, i.e., visual EEG representations. Numerical EEG representations preserve the original signal form and retain fine-grained temporal infor-mation, while visual EEG representations capture the co-occurrence of EEG events across channels within a frame. Each has its pros and cons. In many real-world settings, such as sleep monitoring, clinical diagnosis, and brain function assessment, visual EEG representations are useful for experts to inspect and interpret EEG recordings. On the other hand, although numerical signals provide more direct access to fine-grained EEG information, an encoder pre-trained on one set of numerical signals may not be compatible with others. In contrast, visual EEG representations provide a more flexible interface for datasets with non-standard channel systems. These considerations motivate us to embrace both numerical and visual EEG representations. Therefore, we propose OneEEG , a unified EEG-MLLM framework for multi-task EEG understanding that jointly models numerical EEG representations and visual EEG rep-resentations. OneEEG adopts a dual-stream architecture, in which the numerical signal stream extracts neural features from numerical EEG representations, while the visual stream processes rendered EEG waveform images. The features from both streams are projected into the semantic space of the LLM backbone, supporting EEG-oriented question answering over both signal-derived features and visual EEG patterns. To address the heterogeneous alignment problem across EEG tasks, OneEEG further introduces a task-aware Mixture of Projectors (MoP) module. Rather than relying on a single shared EEG-to-LLM projection, MoP employs a task-aware routing mechanism over a bank of projectors to align EEG representations from different task families with the LLM embedding space through task-aware projection. By combin-ing dual-stream EEG input modeling with task-aware MoP, OneEEG provides a unified baseline for heterogeneous EEG understanding. To support the training of OneEEG, we curate OneEEG-Instruct , a multi-task EEG instruction-tuning dataset that transforms heterogeneous EEG tasks into natural-language su-pervision. OneEEG-Instruct supports dual-stream EEG inputs, covering both numerical EEG representations and waveform-based visual EEG representations, and spans diverse EEG task paradigms involving clinical, event-related, cognitive, and motor-intent understanding. Beyond aggregating heteroge-neous tasks, OneEEG-Instruct adopts a hierarchical instruction design to integrate both task-specific EEG comprehension and general signal-level perception. The task-QA tier is closely tied to downstream EEG tasks and captures dataset-specific prediction objectives, whereas the basic-QA tier focuses on task-agnostic EEG attributes that reflect low-level signal characteristics. In parallel, we establish OneEEG-Bench , an EEG-MLLM benchmark for systematically assessing EEG understanding in multimodal language models. OneEEG-Bench evaluates general closed-source MLLMs, open-source MLLMs, medical MLLMs, EEG-specialized MLLMs, and our proposed OneEEG under a meticulously designed evaluation protocol. Together, OneEEG-Instruct and OneEEG-Bench pro-vide a consistent basis for training EEG-specific MLLMs and evaluating the capability of existing multimodal language models in multi-task EEG understanding. In this section, we review the literature relevant to OneEEG, including EEG representation learning and foundation models, multimodal large language models for EEG understanding, and the development of the mixture of experts mechanism. 

A. EEG Representation Learning and Foundation Models 

EEG modeling has traditionally been developed in task-specific settings, where models are designed for individual applications such as seizure detection [1], sleep staging [4], motor imagery decoding [8], or emotion recognition [7], [15], [16]. Such task-dependent pipelines often rely on dedicated preprocessing strategies, feature designs, and supervised ob-jectives, making it difficult to build transferable EEG models across heterogeneous paradigms. To improve the generality of EEG representations, recent studies have explored EEG foundation models trained on large-scale or multi-dataset EEG corpora. Representative works, including LaBraM [17], EEGPT [18], Brant [19], CBraMod [20], BrainOmni [21], IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON MULTIMEDIA 3

BIOT [22], Neuro-GPT [23], and NeuroLM [24], adopt self-supervised or large-scale pretraining strategies to learn trans-ferable representations from EEG signals. These models share the goal of moving beyond isolated task-specific training toward general-purpose EEG encoders. Although EEG foundation models are primarily designed as representation encoders and are not directly equipped with natural-language interaction or instruction-following capabili-ties, they have substantially advanced transferable EEG repre-sentation learning and provide a strong foundation for building more general EEG physiological signal understanding systems. Our work builds on this progress by integrating EEG signal representations with multimodal language models for unified, instruction-driven EEG understanding. 

B. Multimodal Large Language Models for EEG Understand-ing 

Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have enabled strong visual-language reasoning across natural images, documents, charts, and medical images. General-purpose MLLMs, such as GPT-5.5 [25], Gemini-3 Pro [26], Qwen VL series [27], [28], and LLaVA series [29], have demonstrated impressive open-ended visual understand-ing capabilities, while medical MLLMs, such as Lingshu [30] and MedGemma [31], have extended multimodal reasoning to biomedical and clinical scenarios. However, visual EEG representations differ substantially from both natural images and conventional medical images, since waveforms, spectra, and topographic maps are visual renderings of physiological time-series signals. Their interpretation depends on temporal rhythms, frequency-band patterns, and task-specific neural phenomena. Therefore, despite the rapid progress of MLLMs, their capability for EEG understanding remains insufficiently studied, motivating systematic evaluation across heterogeneous EEG tasks. Recent studies have begun to explore EEG-oriented LLMs and MLLMs. CerebraGloss [12] constructs an instruction-tuned vision-language model for fine-grained clinical EEG interpretation based on waveform images and structured in-struction data. WaveMind [11] aligns EEG signals with tex-tual and visual modalities to build a conversational EEG model. EEG-VLM [14] investigates EEG image-based vision-language modeling for sleep stage prediction through hier-archical visual feature alignment and language-guided rea-soning. These works demonstrate the potential of language-model interfaces for EEG analysis. However, most existing efforts still emphasize either signal-to-language modeling or visual EEG interpretation alone, while unified support for both numerical EEG representations and visual EEG represen-tations remains underexplored. In addition, existing models, including general MLLMs, medical MLLMs, and emerging EEG-oriented LLM/MLLM methods, have not been compre-hensively evaluated under a unified benchmark across diverse EEG task paradigms. These gaps motivate unified signal-visual EEG modeling and systematic benchmarking for EEG understanding. 

C. Mixture-of-Experts for EEG Analysis 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have been widely studied as a mechanism for conditional computation and expert specialization [32]–[35]. By routing inputs to dif-ferent experts, MoE-based models can allocate specialized capacity to heterogeneous data distributions while avoiding the need for a single shared module to fit all patterns. This idea is particularly relevant to EEG modeling, where heterogeneity arises from multiple sources, including tasks, subjects, channels, frequency patterns, and recording proto-cols. Recent EEG studies have explored MoE designs from different perspectives. EEGMamba [36] introduces task-aware MoE to enable coverage of multiple EEG tasks in Mamba. BrainMoE [37] employs channel-wise expert routing to model channel-dependent neural information, while EEGMoE [38] explores domain-decoupled experts for self-supervised EEG representation learning. Despite these advances, existing EEG-MoE methods are primarily concerned with EEG feature extraction and repre-sentation learning. In the context of EEG-MLLMs, the key challenge further involves modality alignment: heterogeneous EEG features with weak intrinsic semantic information must be transformed into the LLM feature space. This makes the EEG-to-LLM projection stage a critical bottleneck. In contrast to encoder-level or classifier-level MoE designs, our proposed MoP introduces task-aware specialization at the projection stage, guiding different EEG task families to specialized EEG-to-LLM projections. This provides a lightweight mechanism for mitigating cross-task alignment conflicts in instruction-following EEG understanding. III. D ATA CONSTRUCTION

C. Question-Answer Annotation 

We design both OneEEG-Instruct and OneEEG-Bench un-der a closed-set QA protocol. Each question is associated with a finite candidate label set, and the answer is required to be selected from this set. In practice, we mainly use two QA formats: multiple-choice questions and binary yes/no questions, with details summarized in Table I. To cover both dataset-specific reasoning and general signal perception, we organize QA samples into two levels, with examples shown in Fig. 3. Task-oriented QA follows the original prediction objective of each source dataset, evalu-ating whether the model can perform EEG reasoning under dataset-specific label spaces. In addition, we construct basic EEG attribute QA from subsets of the source datasets. These samples focus on task-agnostic signal properties, such as amplitude level, dominant frequency band, and slow-versus-fast activity tendency, enabling the evaluation of low-level EEG characteristics beyond dataset-specific task labels. OneEEG is a unified EEG-oriented MLLM trained on nu-merical EEG signals, waveform images, and text instructions. We first present the overall architecture and the dual-stream EEG input design, then the task-aware mixture of projectors to align the EEG features with the language model space. Finally, we detail the two-stage training strategy used for projector alignment and multimodal instruction tuning. 

A. Overview of OneEEG 

Fig. 4 illustrates the overall architecture of OneEEG. Given an EEG frame and a text instruction, OneEEG constructs a multimodal input consisting of a numerical EEG representa-tion, a waveform-based visual EEG representation, and the instruction text. For our method, we utilize Qwen2.5-VL-7B [27] as our LLM backbone with the native visual encoder, and EEGPT [18] as the numerical EEG signal encoder. The signal stream encodes numerical EEG representations with a pretrained EEG encoder, and projects the resulting features into the LLM hidden space through MoP. The visual stream processes the waveform image through the native visual path-way of the MLLM, and the text stream follows the standard tokenization and embedding procedure. The resulting EEG, visual, and text tokens are concatenated and fed into the language model for answer generation. 

B. Dual-stream EEG Input Modeling

For standard-setting EEG frames, OneEEG uses both numerical and visual EEG rep-resentations. For non-standard-setting EEG frames, OneEEG omits the numerical EEG signal representation and uses only the visual EEG representation.

C. Task-aware Mixture of Projectors 

Given EEG features from the frozen EEG numerical signal encoder, OneEEG uses a task-aware Mixture of Projectors (MoP) module to map them into the LLM hidden space. As shown in Fig. 4, instead of using a single shared EEG-to-LLM projector, MoP maintains a lightweight projector bank and selects the projection pathway according to the EEG token features and the task embedding. Specifically, basic EEG attribute questions are assigned to a fixed individual projector, while task-oriented questions are routed to candidate projectors through a hard-selected router. 

1) Projector Bank: MoP maintains a lightweight EEG-to-LLM projector bank: 

P = {P1, P 2, . . . , P K , P basic },

where P1, . . . , P K are candidate projectors for task-oriented questions and Pbasic is the fixed projector for basic EEG attribute questions. Each projector is implemented as an MLP with the same architecture and maps EEG encoder features to the LLM hidden dimension: 

Pk : Rde → Rdl ,

where de and dl denote the EEG encoder feature dimension and the LLM hidden dimension, respectively. For task-agnostic basic EEG attribute questions, OneEEG bypasses the router and directly applies the basic projector: 

Heeg = Pbasic (Zeeg ), q ∈ Q basic .

For task-oriented questions, the projection path is selected from {P1, . . . , P K } by the task-oriented router.

The EEG encoder and visual encoder remain frozen, while the MoP, the visual projector, and the LoRA [52] parameters are updated. This stage further adapts the alignment modules and instruction-following components while preserving the pretrained en-coders and LLM backbone. V. E XPERIMENTS

C. In-Depth Analysis 

Beyond the main results and ablation study, we further con-duct additional analyses to better understand the behavior of OneEEG, including the effect of visual EEG representations, feature-space visualization, routing-strategy comparison, and projector-usage analysis. 

1) Effect of Visual Representations: We first analyze how different EEG visual representations affect the performance of general MLLMs. As shown in Table IV, we compare waveform images, spectrogram images, and their combination using GPT-5.5 and Qwen2.5-VL-7B without additional train-ing. Waveform images provide the most stable performance across the two models. For GPT-5.5, waveform input achieves the best Overall and Macro scores, reaching 50.39% and 53.01%, respectively. For Qwen2.5-VL-7B, waveform input also obtains the best Overall and Macro scores, with 42.73% and 48.43%, respectively. In contrast, spectrogram-only input leads to clear performance degradation, especially on Vig-ilance, where GPT-5.5 drops from 75.68% to 55.68%, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B drops from 84.32% to 76.22%. Combining waveform and spectrogram does not consistently improve over waveform-only input. These results suggest that waveform images are the most reliable visual EEG representation for current MLLMs, and we therefore use waveform images as the default visual input in our training process. 

2) Feature Visualization: We further visualize the EEG features before and after the EEG-to-LLM projection using t-SNE. As shown in Fig. 7, the frozen EEG encoder features before projection are highly mixed across task families, indi-cating that the pretrained EEG encoder alone does not directly organize features according to the downstream QA taxonomy. The shared-projector baseline slightly reshapes the feature space, but different task families remain largely entangled. In contrast, MoP-projected features exhibit clearer family-level structures, with Basic, Clinical, Motor, State, and Event samples forming more distinguishable regions. This visualiza-tion suggests that MoP helps organize EEG features into a

more task-aware projected space for the LLM. The remaining overlap between some families also indicates that fine-grained EEG representation alignment remains challenging. 

3) Effect of Routing Strategy: We further compare two routing strategies of MoP under the Phase 1 setting. Besides the hard top-1 router used in the main configuration, we also evaluate a gated top-1 router with routing noise [55], which follows a commonly used soft gating design for encouraging routing exploration during training. As shown in Fig. 8, the two variants achieve very similar aggregate performance. Hard top-1 routing obtains 59.30% Overall and 59.57% Macro, while gated top-1 routing with noise obtains 59.24% Overall and 59.15% Macro, only 0.06 and 0.42 percentage points lower, respectively. This result suggests that the gain of MoP mainly comes from the multi-projector formulation rather than a specific router realization. By assigning different EEG task families to separate projection paths, MoP reduces the interference that may arise when heterogeneous EEG features are forced to share a single EEG-to-LLM projector. The comparable performance of the two router variants further indicates that sparse task-aware projection is a robust design choice for EEG-to-LLM alignment. 

4) Router Projector Usage: We further analyze the projec-tor usage induced by the hard routing configuration of MoP. Since each sample activates one projection path, the usage map in Fig. 9 provides a direct view of how samples are mapped to projector slots. The assignments show clear task-family preferences rather than uniform usage. Specifically, Clinical and Event tasks show relatively stable and distinct projector usage, while Motor and State datasets are more likely to share projector slots. This suggests that Clinical and Event tasks are more clearly separated in the current routing space, whereas Motor and State remain harder to distinguish. The observation is also consistent with the t-SNE visualization, where Motor and State representations remain partially overlapped. Overall, the usage pattern supports the interpretability of hard routing and indicates that more discriminative signal representations may further improve motor- and state-related EEG understand-ing.
