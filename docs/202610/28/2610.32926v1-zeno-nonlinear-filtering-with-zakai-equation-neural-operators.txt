## Abstract 

State inference and parameter learning for nonlinear filters is a core computa-tion for scientific machine learning with varied applications in engineering and neuroscience. The unnormalized filtering distribution is governed by the Zakai equation, a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) that combines the for-ward Kolmogorov operator (known as the Fokker-Planck equation) with observed likelihood updates. Although solving these high-dimensional SPDEs is often im-possible analytically and intractable numerically, advances in operator learning and physics-informed machine learning have made it feasible. Harnessing these advances, we introduce the Zakai Equation Neural Operator (ZENO), a model that optimizes a neural operator against a Zakai Equation based physics-informed loss to approximate the exact unnormalized filtering distribution for latent stochas-tic dynamical systems. We use this ZENO-inferred posterior distribution in an expectation-maximization (EM) framework to perform parameter learning and state inference. We demonstrate ZENO’s ability to accurately infer the poste-rior distribution and learn its corresponding SDE parameters in three simulated stochastic dynamical systems (Double-Well bistable system, Van Der Pol (VDP) oscillator, and Lorenz system) and compare its performance against established methods for latent SDE inference and learning. We show that ZENO recovers previously observed limit cycles representing forward and backward locomotion from a whole-brain imaging dataset of immobilized C. elegans nematodes. ZENO is a novel approach to the problem of nonlinear filtering and dynamical inference through the lens of partial differential equations and operator theory. 

## 1 Introduction 

Estimating a latent dynamical process from a set of noisy observations is a fundamental problem in machine learning for science [Durstewitz et al., 2023]. For example, in neuroscience, it is thought that neural computations are implemented by a latent dynamical system that is expressed by a neural population [Saxena and Cunningham, 2019]. This problem has been well studied in signal-processing as filtering (in the online setting) or smoothing (in the offline setting) a noisy non-linear dynamical 

> Preprint. arXiv:2610.32926v1 [cs.LG] 23 Oct 2026

system [Yau et al., 2024]. The essence of filtering as Bayesian inference is to estimate the filtering distribution p(zt|X[0: t]) where zt ∈ Rn is an unobserved latent variable and xt ∈ Rm (m ≥ n)are observations at each point t ∈ [0 , T ], while for smoothing the goal is to estimate the posterior distribution p(zt|X[0: T ]) [Sarkka, 2013]. These distributions are referred to as the filtering and smoothing distributions respectively. For continuous time systems, the dynamics of the filtering distribution has been well studied as the Kushner-Stratonovich (KS) equation [Stratonovich, 1960, Kushner, 1964], a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE). A more tractable analog of the KS equation is the Zakai equation [Zakai, 1969], a linear SPDE governing the unnormalized posterior density. While solving the KS or Zakai equation provides the exact posterior distribution (post-normalization for the Zakai equation), solving these equations is often impossible analytically and intractable numerically for high-dimensional systems [Sabbar and Nisar, 2025]. Alternative methods such as state-space models [Bishop, 2006], (switching) linear dynamical systems [Linderman et al., 2017], and deep learning-based latent stochastic differential equations (SDE) [Li et al., 2020] offer a more tractable approach to inference and parameter learning by making assumptions or approximations about the posterior distribution. For example, the Kalman-Bucy filter [Kalman and Bucy, 1961] provides the exact solution to the KS equation under the assumption that dynamics are linear and observation noise is Gaussian. Thus, the need remains for powerful methods that can approximate the filtering posterior without restrictive assumptions while remaining computationally tractable. Two recent approaches for solving PDEs with neural networks offer a route around these barriers. 

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) supervise the fit with the governing equation rather than labeled solutions, constraining the network’s autodifferentiated derivatives to satisfy the equation at a set of collocation points [Raissi et al., 2019, Karniadakis et al., 2021]. PINNS are applicable when the solution is never observed, as in filtering, and because no parametric form is imposed, they can represent non-Gaussian posteriors governing nonlinear dynamics. However, PINNs are fit to a single instance of a PDE and must be retrained for each new one, whereas in filtering every observation path X[0 ,t ] drives a distinct instance of the Zakai equation. 

Neural operators learn the solution operator itself, mapping a PDE’s defining data to its solution and generalizing across instances without retraining [Lu et al., 2021, Azizzadenesheli et al., 2024], but are trained on labeled solutions which filtering never provides. Physics-informed neural operators (PINOs) resolve both obstacles, using the equation’s residual when observations are unavailable to supervise operator learning across a distribution of instances [Wang et al., 2021b, Li et al., 2024]. This is the paradigm we build on to solve the Zakai equation for nonlinear filtering. We present the 

Zakai Equation Neural Operator (ZENO): A physics-informed neural operator for nonlinear filtering that learns the solution to the Zakai equation. ZENO represents the unnormalized filtering distribution by optimizing a physics informed loss which minimizes the residual between the PINO’s posterior derivatives and the Fokker Planck operator while incorporating likelihood updates at observation times. Unlike current approaches, which use numerical solvers [Leng et al., 2026] for forecasting or a deep-splitting approach [Beck et al., 2021, Bågmark et al., 2023b,a, Beck et al., 2026], we leverage neural operators to learn a global solution over the entire observation history. Furthermore, we make no assumptions about the form of the dynamics model and instead estimate the dynamical parameters and observation model in an expectation-maximization [Dempster et al., 1977] like setup. We show that ZENO is able to achieve similar latent inference accuracy to optimal nonlinear filters in simulated dynamical systems. We then show that ZENO performs dynamical parameter estimation at the same level as other state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines. Finally, we evaluate our model on a real world neuroscience dataset and find that ZENO is able to recapitulate previously observed latent dynamical structure.

3.2 Analysis of whole-brain calcium dynamics in C. elegans 

We used ZENO to analyze neuroscience data where the true latent state is unknown and where prior work [Linderman et al., 2019, Glaser et al., 2020, Yezerets et al., 2025] provides the opportunity to compare ZENO’s latent state estimation with established methods. We tested ZENO on a dataset of whole-brain calcium dynamics in immobilized C. elegans [Kato et al., 2015]. Fig. 4A-E shows performance on an example worm (StimWorm 1). Specifically, Fig 4A shows that ZENO accurately reconstructs neural observations from the inferred latents. The latent states inferred by ZENO recapitulate reported limit cycles (Fig. 4B,C) and the inferred latents are behaviorally relevant and consistent with human-labels (Fig. 4D,E). We find that ZENO performs comparable to established state-of-the-art methods on reconstruction R2 and forecasting RMSE 1. These results establish that ZENO performs comparably to established methods on real-world datasets of scientific value and can be used for scientific discovery of latent variables from neural measurments. 

## 4 Related Work 

Switching Linear Dynamical Systems. Switching linear dynamical systems are hierarchical models that approximate a nonlinear system with mixtures of linear dynamical systems. They are often fit with Bayesian methods (e.g., expectation maximization) or with variational approximation. Their mixture of linear systems structure balances interpretability and tractability against expressivity: their discrete states and linear dynamics can be interpreted for scientific discovery [Linderman et al., 

> 1Forecasting was attempted with gpSLDS but led to inconsistent results likely due to hyperparameter tuning that we do not think accurate reflect the method.

72017] but their approximate (variational) posteriors, while tractable fit to data, may diverge from the true posterior. Depending on the choice of variational family these models may struggle to match posteriors that are multimodal or asymmetric. ZENO forgoes a hierarchical framework in favor of directly approximating the true posterior distribution. 

Latent SDEs. Latent SDEs address a similar problem using a variational autoencoder framework where the prior and posterior processes are governed by a neural stochastic differential equation and the corresponding drift and diffusion functions are modeled as neural networks [Li et al., 2020]. These models offer the flexibility and expressivity of neural networks, optimization with gradient descent, and parallelization offered by GPUs. However, balancing the data reconstruction and Kullback-Leibler (KL) loss that defines their variational objective can often be difficult, (e.g., posterior collapse, where the posterior equals the prior and becomes unusable for anything outside of reconstruction). 

Simulation-based Latent SDEs established this approach first, using the stochastic adjoint sensitivity method. This required rollouts or simulations from the variational posterior at each gradient step to estimate a path-wise KL loss, which can be expensive for long timescales and numerically sensitive.

Physics-informed Neural Networks for the Fokker-Planck and Zakai Equations. Other works have investigated using PINNs for solving the Fokker-Planck equation (FPE) [Xu et al., 2020, Zhai et al., 2020, Chen et al., 2020, Hu et al., 2024b] and Zakai equation [Bågmark et al., 2023b, Azimov and Kim, 2026]. Liu et al. [2021] solve the FPE as an inverse problem by fitting a network to the FPE solution and fitting the drift and diffusion in alternating steps. However, they use a grid based approach and only demonstrate it on one-dimensional systems. Furthermore, they assume that at least a noisy version of the PDF is accessible for training and do not handle unobserved latents. Azimov and Kim [2026] learn the FPE solution and use it for Bayesian updates to solve for the filtering distribution, but assume known drift and diffusion functions. Both methods propagate the solution step-wise. To our knowledge, our approach is the first to simultaneously perform state inference of the full FPE solution operator while learning unknown dynamics functions using EM. 

## 5 Discussion 

Here, we introduced ZENO, a physics-informed neural operator that approximates the unnormalized filtering distribution by learning the solution operator of the Zakai equation. On synthetic systems, ZENO matched or came close to optimal numerical filters for latent inference and performed on par with state-of-the-art methods for parameter learning. On whole-brain C. elegans recordings, it was comparable to baselines on forecasting and produced more behaviorally informative latents, at the cost of slightly lower reconstruction R2, which we expect relaxing the Stiefel constraint on the observation model to address. ZENO achieves similar performance to state-of-the-art methods but does not outperform them in all cases. All established methods we benchmarked against are estimating the smoothing distribution, which integrates observations both forward and backward in time, thereby providing a superior marginal distribution that can be used as a target. Future directions should investigate extending ZENO to inferring the smoothing distribution by learning the solution to the backwards stochastic PDE [Pardoux, 1982]. Additionally, it may be possible to improve the objective for fitting the drift and diffusion by casting it as an inverse problem but that is outside the scope of this work. Finally, our model uses a single set of parameters to represent the entire dynamical system even in cases where there are different dynamical regimes such as in our application to C. elegans . Models such as decomposed linear dynamical systems [Yezerets et al., 2025] handle this through mixtures of dictionaries of linear dynamical systems. A similar idea could be combined with our model by using operator splitting methods [Serrano et al., 2026]. In fact, one may be able to represent switches in regimes through jumps by generalizing the Forward Kolmogorov operator to Levy processes [Jia and Benson, 2019, Leng et al., 2026]. Overall, ZENO represents a novel approach to the problem of nonlinear filtering and dynamical inference through the lens of partial differential equations and operator theory.

## B Primer on Physics Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) 

Classical numerical solvers for partial differential equations often fail to scale to higher dimensions due to exponential scaling with the dimensionality of the domain. Instead, modern machine learning based approaches amortize the solution to a PDE by directly fitting neural networks to the derivative-solution pairs. However, these data-driven models ignore known physics and tend to need large datasets which can be expensive to generate. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) are a modern approach that represent the solution of a differential equation as a neural network and train it to satisfy both the observed data and the equation itself. This approach has been made practical by automatic differentiation in modern deep learning frameworks. 15 B.1 Formulation

## D Experimental Details 

All experiments are trained on one GPU; Models are initialized randomly and a new simulated dataset is generated per seed. However methods using the same seed use the same dataset as input. ZENO and all baselines are implemented in Python ( ≥ 3.10) on JAX ( ≥ 0.6) (Bradbury et al. [2018]): ZENO uses Equinox ( ≥ 0.13) for its networks and Optax ( ≥ 0.2) for optimization [DeepMind et al., 2020], SDE Matching uses the same stack with Diffrax (0.7) (Kidger [2021]) for its forecast rollouts, gpSLDS uses the authors’ SING implementation (Hu et al., 2025), and experiments are configured with Hydra [Yadan, 2019].

D.2 Training 

Every method receives the same standardized data, observation grid, dimensions and validation cadence. Gradient-based methods (ZENO, SDE Matching) train for 10,000 steps on the double well, Van der Pol and Kato datasets and 3,000 steps on Lorenz, with validation every 100 steps; gpSLDS is fit by variational EM for 50 iterations. Each method selects its checkpoint on the validation split with a criterion that does not use ground truth (Section D.2.1–D.2.3), and the test split is scored once, after selection, with 128 posterior and forecast samples. Baselines run in float64; ZENO in float32. For the simulated systems we ran six seeds (0–5) per dataset and method; the seed changes both the data draw and the initialization. For Kato we fit one model per worm (seed 0) and treat animals as the replicates. 

D.2.1 ZENO 

The E-step minimizes lE with Adam [Kingma and Ba, 2017] with λ = 2 × 10 −3 with an exponential decay of β = 0 .9998 per step, using full-batch gradients accumulated over chunks of 16 trajectories. The Fokker–Planck residual is evaluated at 4 time-in-interval collocation points s ∈ [0 , 1] on 24 randomly chosen observation intervals per step, and at Nz mesh-free state collocation points per (interval, trajectory) drawn from the defensive mixture with σn = 0 .3 and σb = 1 .6. Every 2,000 E-steps we run one M-step: the filter mean is read out with 64 MALA chains of 30 sweeps per site (the first third is burn-in; the step size self-tunes to an acceptance rate of 0.574 [Roberts and Rosenthal, 1998]), the Stiefel sensor is refit by orthogonal Procrustes, the drift network is refit for 400 Adam iterations at learning rate 2 × 10 −3 against the increment target evaluated at the RK2 predicted midpoint ( det_mid ), the scalar diffusion is refit from the trapezoidal increment residual, and the diagonal observation noise R is refit from the residual directions orthogonal to span( C). The drift is refit only when the sensor has moved little since the previous M-step. The sensor and a linear dynamics matrix are initialized by temporal subspace identification; the drift network is warm-started to those linear dynamics and one bootstrap M-step from the projection ˆz = C⊤(x − d) precedes any operator training. The operator itself is randomly initialized with the default uniform fan-in scheme. Checkpoints are selected by the forecast RMSE on the validation windows (16 forecast samples), the same quantity that is reported on the test split. D.2.3 SDE Matching 

We implement the diagonal-Gaussian marginal specialization of SDE Matching [Bartosh et al., 2025] in JAX. The prior is a latent SDE with a neural drift and a constant diagonal diffusion; the posterior marginals q(zt | x) = N (m(t), diag s(t)2) are produced by a network that reads the time and the state of a backward GRU run over the observations (so the encoder is a smoother), and the posterior drift follows in closed form from dm/dt , ds/dt and the diffusion (time derivatives by forward-mode differentiation). No SDE is solved during training. The objective is the initial KL plus the path energy, estimated at 4 uniformly sampled times per trajectory and multiplied by the physical duration, plus the observation negative log-likelihood at sampled times multiplied by the number of observations; there is no KL annealing or time normalization. Forecasts integrate the learned prior from the posterior at the end of the prefix with an Euler–Maruyama step of 0.01 . Table 4 lists the hyperparameters, which are identical on every dataset except for the step budget.

The RMSE of the mean forecast against the observed frames is divided by the RMSE of persistence, i.e., of repeating the last observed frame, so values below 1 beat the persistence floor of that worm. 

Behavioral decoding. After the dynamical model is frozen we fit a class-balanced multinomial logistic regression from the posterior-mean latents to the annotated behavioral states.
