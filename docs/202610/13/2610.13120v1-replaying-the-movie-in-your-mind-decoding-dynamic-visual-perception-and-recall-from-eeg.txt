# Replaying the Movie in Your Mind: Decoding Dynamic Visual Perception and Recall from EEG 

Zikai Fang ⋆, Tian-Yi Zhou ⋆, Xuan-Hao Liu, Yixi Li, Xiaoyu Xing, Yutong Pei, Bao-Liang Lu, and Wei-Long Zheng ⋆⋆ 

School of Computer Science, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, China

Abstract. Decoding dynamic visual content from EEG has advanced rapidly for visual perception, yet it remains unclear which aspects of neural representations are preserved when people internally replay dy-namic scenes. We introduce PAIR, an EEG benchmark collected from 20 participants using an interleaved, clip-wise paradigm that pairs nat-uralistic video viewing with immediate visual recall. This design enables a comparison between externally driven perception and internally gen-erated recall for the same stimulus content within a fixed trial sequence. We benchmark multiple decoding models on hierarchical semantic clas-sification at both fine-grained and coarse-grained levels, meta-feature decoding tasks covering motion, color, object number, face, and human presence, and a state-decoding task that discriminates perception from recall. Within-subject validation results show numerically similar perfor-mance for several model–task combinations, alongside higher perception accuracy in two coarse-grained comparisons after multiple-comparison correction. In contrast, perception and recall are highly separable at the state level under the present validation protocol. State accuracy remains high after alpha-band ablation, although this does not exclude ocular or other task-related contributions. 

Keywords: EEG · Visual Perception · Video Recall · Brain Visual De-coding 

## 1 Introduction 

Visual brain decoding, often regarded as the scientific realization of "mind-reading", seeks to interpret the neural patterns to infer what a person is seeing from their brain signals and how the human brain processes visual stimuli [4]. This field has witnessed a paradigm shift from decoding simple, static cate-gories [5,18] to reconstructing complex, dynamic visual experiences [7,14]. Early foundational works primarily focused on reconstructing static images [22, 26]. However, natural vision is not a sequence of frozen frames but a continuous

stream of temporal events. Addressing this challenge, recent research has piv-oted toward video reconstruction, seeking to decode continuous visual streams from brain signals [10, 17, 28]. This evolution has been fueled by the release of large-scale public datasets. In the fMRI domain, datasets such as CC2017 have established rigorous standards for spatial reconstruction [25]. In parallel, lever-aging millisecond-level temporal resolution, the EEG community has introduced benchmarks like SEED-DV, which are pivotal for decoding the rapid temporal dynamics inherent in visual processing [15]. While existing datasets have propelled the decoding of visual perception, they predominantly characterize neural responses elicited while subjects pas-sively view video stimuli, largely overlooking an equally fundamental cognitive mechanism: visual imagery. Often described as the ability to "see with the mind’s eye," imagery is central to understanding how the brain simulates re-ality [11, 19]. A pivotal yet unresolved question in cognitive science is the ex-tent of the neural overlap between these two states—specifically, whether in-ternally generated imagery reuses the same representational codes as externally driven perception or relies on distinct reconstruction processes [2]. Although this perception-imagery relationship has been scrutinized using static stimuli [20], the mechanisms underlying dynamic naturalistic imagery remain obscure. Unlike static images, dynamic mental replay necessitates the reconstruction of tempo-ral structure—motion and event evolution—in addition to semantic content [3]. Crucially, strictly controlled benchmarks that directly compare perception and imagery for the same dynamic stimuli—under aligned temporal conditions and task demands—are notably absent, limiting our ability to probe these shared or distinct neural dynamics. To bridge this gap, we introduce an EEG benchmark, called Perception And 

Imagery Recall (PAIR), collected from 20 healthy subjects engaged in a con-trolled interleaved perception–recall paradigm. Unlike passive viewing datasets, our protocol requires participants to watch naturalistic videos and immediately mentally replay the visual content, pairing EEG recordings of external perception and internal imagery for each clip. Rather than assuming a particular direction of effect [2, 9], we use this benchmark to compare semantic and meta-feature decoding during dynamic recall and perception, with matched stimulus content and a fixed trial sequence. In conclusion, our contributions can be summarized as follows: 1. We introduce an EEG dataset for dynamic video viewing and immediate imagery recall. The clip-wise paradigm pairs the two conditions for the same content within a fixed trial sequence. 2. We benchmark traditional and deep models on 20- and 4-class semantic decoding, meta-feature classification, and state decoding. 3. Our within-subject validation results show high perception–recall state sep-arability, while content-decoding differences depend on the task and model. ## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Perception–Imagery Overlap and Mental Replay 

A core debate in cognitive science concerns the extent to which visual imagery relies on the same representational substrate as perception. A synthesis of re-cent neuroimaging work suggests substantial overlap between perception and imagery in visual, parietal, and frontal regions, with imagery characterized by stronger top-down influences and the absence of bottom-up sensory drive [2]. Complementing fMRI evidence, EEG studies have begun to isolate oscillatory signatures of shared content representations; For example, Dijkstra et al. [3] re-ported that cross-generalizable representations between perceived and imagined objects emerge prominently in the alpha band over posterior sensors. These lines of work motivate paradigms that compare perception and imagery under tightly matched stimulus content and timing, especially for naturalistic dynamic scenes. 

2.2 EEG Decoding of Visual Content and Naturalistic Stimuli 

Decoding visual information from EEG has progressed from controlled, static stimuli to more complex and naturalistic settings, supported by increasingly large datasets and improved modeling [10,16]. Early demonstrations showed that EEG contains discriminative information about visual categories, enabling brain-driven visual classification [24]. More recent work has emphasized richer stimulus sets and temporal representational dynamics [6, 8, 13]. Together, these studies establish EEG as a practical modality for probing time-resolved representational content during perception and provide methodological precedents for decoding semantic structure from continuous or rapidly changing visual input. 

2.3 Deep Learning Models for EEG Decoding 

End-to-end deep learning has revolutionized EEG decoding, with architectures ranging from deep ConvNets [21] and compact CNNs like EEGNet [12] to hybrid CNN-Transformer models [23] now serving as standard backbones for spatiotem-poral feature extraction. However, while these models have achieved state-of-the-art performance in perceptual tasks, strictly controlled benchmarks that leverage them to directly compare neural representations between continuous video per-ception and dynamic mental replay remain notably absent. In this study, we utilize these advanced spatiotemporal architectures to bridge this gap, system-atically evaluating the fidelity of semantic and meta-feature preservation across these distinct cognitive states.

Experimental Protocol. Data were collected from 20 healthy subjects. Each subject participated in 3 sessions. During each session, subjects viewed 5 con-tinuous video blocks. Data Acquisition. To ensure the quality of the acquired EEG data, the exper-iments were conducted in a controlled laboratory environment to minimize noise and other environmental disturbances. 62-channel EEG signals were collected Decoding Dynamic Visual Perception and Recall from EEG 7

by an active AgCl electrode cap with an international 10-10 system. The EEG signals were acquired using the ESI NeuroScan System at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. In addition to EEG, we used a Tobii Pro Fusion eye tracker to record eye movements at 250 Hz. The present study analyzed EEG only; eye-tracking recordings were not included in the reported analyses. 

4.3 Data Preprocessing 

Raw EEG signals were band-pass filtered (0.1–100 Hz) and processed using Inde-pendent Component Analysis (ICA) to attenuate ocular and muscular artifacts. The EEG signals were downsampled to 200 Hz to reduce computational cost. The continuous recordings were then segmented into distinct epochs corresponding to the Perception and Recall conditions. Content decoding used the full 2-s per-ception and 3-s recall epochs. For raw-EEG state decoding, recall was cropped to 0.5–2.5 s, giving 2-s inputs for both conditions. Each input was standardized using its own mean and standard deviation; content-training inputs additionally received Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.05. 

4.4 Baselines and Implementation Details Baselines. We first computed Power Spectral Density (PSD) and Differential Entropy (DE) across five standard frequency bands ( δ: 1–4 Hz, θ: 4–8 Hz, α: 8– 14 Hz, β: 14–31 Hz, and γ: 31–99 Hz) using the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with a Hanning window. To validate the effectiveness of our approach, we benchmark it against sev-eral state-of-the-art decoding models, including ShallowNet, DeepNet, EEGNet, Conformer, and TSConv. Notably, we also include GLFNet proposed by [15] as a key baseline, as it effectively integrates global and local EEG features and has demonstrated robust performance in dynamic visual tasks. 

Implementation Details. Neural networks used Adam with learning rates of 10 −3 or 3 × 10 −4, weight decay 10 −4, and early stopping with patience 40 (up to 400 epochs). Batch sizes were 16 for content decoding and 128 for state decoding. SVM used an RBF kernel with C = 1 ; content-decoding SVM results are reported for raw EEG only. Models were fitted separately for each participant and recording session. For content decoding, the 250 epochs in each session were randomly divided into 200 training and 50 validation epochs. State decoding used the first 200 epochs from each condition for training and the remaining 50 for validation, preserving clip pairing across partitions. Neural-network scores are the best validation accura-cies selected during early stopping; SVM scores are validation accuracies for the fixed configuration. No independent test set was used. For reporting, the three session scores were averaged within each partici-pant, followed by the mean and sample standard deviation across 20 partici-pants. 4.5 Classification Tasks 

To comprehensively evaluate the decoding performance, we adopted the bench-mark suite established by EEG-VP [15] and extended it with State Decoding, a task unique to our dataset. This resulting suite comprises three distinct classi-fication tasks: Semantic Decoding, Meta-feature Decoding, and State Decoding. Together, they range from high-level conceptual understanding to low-level vi-sual and recall feature retrieval and mental state discrimination. 

Task 1: Semantic Decoding This task evaluates the model’s ability to dis-tinguish specific semantic concepts from neural activity. We defined the ground truth labels at two hierarchical levels of granularity: Fine-grained Classification (20-class), Coarse-grained Classification (4-class) Both classification levels were applied to the Perception and Recall conditions independently to compare the semantic distinctiveness across mental states. 

Task 2: Meta-feature Decoding Beyond semantic identity, we decoded mid-level visual and cognitive attributes. These "meta-features" serve as a probe to investigate whether specific physical properties of the stimuli are preserved in the neural representations. The ground truth labels were generated as follows: 

– Motion Speed (Fast/slow): We quantified the motion information of each video stimulus using the Optical Flow Score (OFS). The videos were then split into binary classes ( Fast/slow ) using the implemented threshold of 0.6427. 

– Dominant Color: We extracted the dominant color features from each video and categorized them into five atmosphere-oriented classes: Neutral Light , Earth & Dark , Cool Tones , Green Nature , and Warm Vibrant .

– Biological/Social Attributes: We manually annotated the presence of specific visual elements. 

• Human: Binary label indicating the presence of a human figure. 

• Face: Binary label indicating the presence of a visible face.

5.2 Semantic and Meta-feature Decoding Analysis 

To compare content decoding across conditions, we evaluated coarse-grained (4-class) and fine-grained (20-class) semantics together with meta-features. Fig. 4 illustrates performance for three representative models. Several model–task combinations yielded numerically similar scores, but dif-ferences were not uniform. After Holm correction across 84 comparisons, percep-tion exceeded recall for raw-EEG 4-class decoding with DeepNet (mean paired difference 4.40 percentage points, 95% CI [2.65, 6.15], adjusted p = 0 .0038 ) and GLFNet (5.87 points, 95% CI [4.22, 7.51], adjusted p < 0.001 ). The remaining comparisons did not reach the corrected threshold of 0.05. In particular, GCN-LSTM yielded close mean scores across conditions; nonsignificant differences do not establish equivalent performance or shared representations. All comparisons remain exploratory because they use validation-selected scores.

## 6 Conclusion 

Our benchmarking results provide initial within-subject validation baselines for dynamic visual perception and recall, with GCN-LSTM yielding the highest mean accuracy on several recall tasks. Several models yielded numerically similar validation accuracies for percep-tion and recall, although the differences varied across tasks and models. The paired analysis identified higher perception accuracy for two coarse-grained com-parisons after correction. Similar decoding scores alone do not establish equiva-lent performance or shared neural representations. State decoding yielded high validation accuracy, including after alpha-band ablation. The topographic analysis illustrates differences across anterior and pos-Decoding Dynamic Visual Perception and Recall from EEG 13 

terior sensors, without identifying cortical sources. PAIR provides a basis for fur-ther investigation of content decoding and state differences under more stringent evaluation and artifact controls. 

Limitations. Validation data were used for both epoch selection and performance reporting, so neural-network scores may be optimistically biased and model com-parisons are exploratory. The 20-participant, within-subject protocol does not establish cross-subject generalization. Content epochs also differ in duration be-tween perception and recall. Although ICA attenuated artifacts, eye-tracking control analyses were not performed; residual ocular activity, visual input, and task demands may contribute to state separability. Sensor-level maps cannot establish recruitment of specific cortical networks.
