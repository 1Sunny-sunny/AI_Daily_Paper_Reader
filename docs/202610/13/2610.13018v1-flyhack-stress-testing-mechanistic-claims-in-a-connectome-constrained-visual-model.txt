# FlyHack: Stress-Testing Mechanistic Claims in a Connectome-Constrained Visual Model 

Aashish Dhawan 

Department of Computer & Information Science & Engineering University of Florida 

# Abstract 

Connectome-constrained neural networks make biological components directly accessible to intervention, but their mechanistic conclusions can still depend on the comparison model, intervention, circuit representation and trained solution. We stress-test these dependencies in flyvis, a Drosophila motion-vision model, using its 50-model ensemble, task-matched causal CNN controls and replicated interventions. At the level of model behaviour, three-level reverse-phi reversal in flyvis is strongly period- and velocity-dependent and also appears in task-matched CNNs; neither family reproduces the fly’s stationary-sawtooth response. Adversarial direction reversal requires a perturbation bound of 0.21–0.27 times stimulus contrast in flyvis versus 0.13–0.25 in CNNs, but the gap over a parameter-matched CNN largely closes for larger CNNs. At the level of mechanism, matching attribution to the intervention matters: for held-out-mean replacement, first-order edge attribution patching reaches pooled AUROC 0.970 and Spearman 0.872, versus 0.933 and 0.126 when its zero-aligned score is evaluated against the same ground truth. Circuit conclusions also vary with representation and ensemble member. Greedy cell-type sets meet a model-relative T4/T5 tuning criterion in 25 of 50 models, while T4/T5 output silencing reduces the tested optic-flow behaviour to at most two of eight clips in 40. In six models where the cell-type search first succeeds, the corresponding connection sets can be pruned to 13–31 added classes; under an additional two-sided response-amplitude check, only two of those six first-success cell-type sets still meet the tuning threshold. These results support a narrower use of connectome-constrained models: they make mechanistic hypotheses directly testable, but the resulting claims must be scoped to the model family, intervention, representation and trained solution. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent connectomes now span the adult Drosophila brain, visual system and male central nervous system (Dorkenwald et al., 2024; Nern et al., 2025; Berg et al., 2026), making it possible to build network models whose architecture is constrained by anatomy and whose remaining parameters are fitted by simulation or training (Lappalainen et al., 2024; Shiu et al., 2024). The most developed example in vision is flyvis (Lappalainen et al., 2024): a recurrent network of 64 optic-lobe cell types, with connectivity constrained by the connectome, whose 734 free parameters were trained to estimate optical flow. Its trained ensemble predicts physiological tuning in many cell types without having been fitted to neural recordings. Such models are increasingly used to generate in-silico predictions about circuit function and behaviour. The central question in this paper is not whether flyvis is biologically constrained—it is—but how much a mechanistic conclusion changes when we change the comparison model, intervention, circuit representation or trained ensemble member. We address that question in two stages.

Second, we turn from behaviour to mechanism within flyvis. Each of its 604 type-to-type connection classes and each modelled cell type can be intervened on directly. This lets us score circuit-discovery outputs against exhaustive single-component interventional ground truth rather than against a planted circuit (Lindner et al., 2023; Gupta et al., 2024) or a faithfulness proxy (Mueller et al., 2025), and then ask whether the answer changes with intervention semantics, circuit representation or ensemble member. Directional predictions and analysis criteria were specified before the corresponding analyses; they were not externally timestamped preregistrations, and we report failed predictions as well as successful ones (Appendix A).

Evaluation artefacts (Appendix B). Two-level periodic reverse-phi constructions are phase-equivalent to translation, a one-sided retained-output metric is satisfied by degenerate sub-networks, and same-input mean-current ablation leaks stimulus information. 

# 2 Related work 

Connectome-constrained models. Lappalainen et al. (2024) trained ensembles of connectome-constrained networks of the fly visual system on optic flow and compared predicted tuning to published recordings; Shiu et al. (2024) simulated the central brain as a leaky integrate-and-fire network with connec-tome weights. Anatomically constrained task optimization can also recover response properties of individual fly motion neurons (Mano et al., 2021). Karaneen et al. (2026) retrained flyvis ensembles and found that simi-lar task performance does not reliably identify the circuit solutions whose tuning matches experiment. Beiran & Litwin-Kumar (2025) showed that connectivity alone often leaves the dynamics of connectome-constrained networks underdetermined, and that recordings from a small, well-chosen set of neurons can resolve this. Non-identifiability of circuit explanations also arises in fully enumerable networks (Méloux et al., 2025), and degenerate circuit parameters have long been documented in small biological circuits (Prinz et al., 2004; Marder & Goaillard, 2006). Our analyses are complementary to the tuning comparisons of Karaneen et al. (2026): we intervene on the released ensemble and score attribution and circuit-search outputs against those interventions. 

Motion illusions. Reverse-phi, the reversal of perceived direction when contrast inverts at each displace-ment (Anstis, 1970), is predicted by correlation-type detectors (Hassenstein & Reichardt, 1956) and has been 2characterized in fly behaviour and T4/T5 physiology (Tuthill et al., 2011; Leonhardt et al., 2017). Minimal apparent-motion experiments show complementary bright/dark computations and ON/OFF-pathway con-tributions to phi and reverse-phi responses (Clark et al., 2011; Salazar-Gatzimas et al., 2018). Flies turn in response to stationary sawtooth luminance gradients, an effect that depends on T4 and T5 (Agrochao et al., 2020); humans perceive motion in related static patterns such as the peripheral-drift and Rotating Snakes illusions (Conway et al., 2005). Rosario et al. (2026) note that recent DNN motion models can repro-duce dynamic illusions such as reverse-phi, but found that most tested optical-flow networks fail to produce human-consistent flow on Rotating Snakes. Golan et al. (2020) argue that stimuli which separate candidate models are more informative than stimuli on which they agree. 

Robustness and biological priors. Small input perturbations can change the outputs of deep networks (Szegedy et al., 2014; Madry et al., 2018), and constraining the early stages of a CNN to resemble primate V1 increases robustness (Dapello et al., 2020). We compare the robustness of flyvis with task-matched CNN families and treat any difference as a model-family effect. 

Circuit discovery and its evaluation. Circuit discovery identifies components responsible for a be-haviour by activation patching (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang & Nanda, 2024), automated pruning (Conmy et al., 2023), or gradient-based approximations such as attribution patching and integrated gradients (Syed et al., 2024; Hanna et al., 2024; Sundararajan et al., 2017). Evaluation relies on planted circuits (Lindner et al., 2023; Gupta et al., 2024) or on faithfulness metrics (Mueller et al., 2025); Miller et al. (2024) showed that faithfulness scores depend on the ablation type, and gradient-based attribution patching has been eval-uated against exhaustive patching in language models (Kramár et al., 2024). We evaluate these methods in a trained recurrent network in which single-component interventional ground truth can be computed exhaustively at both the connection-class and the cell-type level. 

# 3 Model, controls and protocol 

flyvis. We use the 50 pretrained models released by Lappalainen et al. (2024) (flyvis 1.2.0). The model represents 64 biological cell types across 721 hexagonal columns (about 45,000 neurons and 1.5 million neuron-level connections). CT1 is represented by separate medulla and lobula compartments, giving 65 parameterized type units. The 734 free network parameters comprise 65 time constants, 65 resting potentials and 604 synapse-strength scaling factors, one per type-to-type connection class; each class is a scale factor shared across all neuron-level contacts of that type pair, not an individual synapse. We call these 604 classes 

connections . A trained decoder (DecoderGAVP, 7,427 parameters) reads 34 type units and outputs optical flow on the hexagonal lattice. The models were trained on Sintel (Butler et al., 2012), and stimuli are applied through flyvis’s own renderer. Experiments that are too expensive for the full ensemble use models 000–009, the ten models ranked best by the release package’s legacy task-error scalar. That scalar is numerically EPE-like, but its exact generator, configuration, checkpoint-selection history and training-held-out status are not retained (Appendix C). 

Readout. The readout is the decoded flow vector at the central column, averaged over an analysis window that begins after the onset transient and spans whole stimulus cycles (at least 0.3 s). The decoded direction is the angle of this vector. 

CNN controls. The CNNs receive the same luminance input on the 721-column lattice, mapped to a zero-padded 31 × 31 grid by a permutation of axial coordinates, so a per-column perturbation bound means the same thing for both families. # 4 Illusions 

4.1 Reverse-phi 

Reverse-phi reverses apparent direction when contrast polarity changes across displacements, and fly exper-iments implicate interactions between bright- and dark-motion pathways (Tuthill et al., 2011; Leonhardt et al., 2017; Clark et al., 2011; Salazar-Gatzimas et al., 2018). Our stimulus follows Leonhardt et al. (2017). Alternating grating stripes stay at intermediate gray, while the other stripes are dark or bright. In matched standard trials the non-gray stripes keep their starting polarity; in reverse-phi trials they switch polarity at every 3 .944 ◦ spatial update. We test both starting polarities and average their response vectors before decoding. Spatial periods are 30 ◦ and 90 ◦, and update intervals of 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 and 25 frames at 50 Hz give 197.2, 98.6, 65.7, 39.4, 19.7 and 7.9 ◦/s. Matched controls differ only in the per-update polarity switch. This three-level stimulus is not a translated copy of its matched control: none of 836 polarity-flipped frames equals any integer translation of the control frame. The residual is, however, a spatially uniform luminance offset. After subtracting each frame’s spatial mean, the moving pattern equals a two-level contrast-inverting grating at half contrast, and a two-level contrast inversion is itself equivalent to a half-period translation (Appendix B). The three-level stimulus therefore adds a whole-field flicker and makes bright-only and dark-only views signal the nominal direction, but it does not remove the reverse-direction pattern correspondence 410 1 10 2

4.2 Stationary sawtooth gradients 

Tethered flies turn in response to stationary sawtooth luminance gradients with a 45 ◦ spatial period presented for 5 s, towards the lighter shade along the shallow gradient, and the response is strongly reduced when T4 and T5 are silenced (Agrochao et al., 2020). We rendered this stimulus in both polarities after 1 s of mean grey, with normalized luminance in [0 , 1] because the projector luminance values were not available. The prediction recorded before the run was that at least 35 of 50 flyvis models report the fly’s turning direction in the sustained window, with magnitude at least 10% of the response to a moving grating of matched contrast, and that the sign flips with polarity. It failed. Three of 50 flyvis models meet the criterion for each polarity and none for both; median sustained responses are 2.3–2.6% of the moving-grating response, and no CNN meets the criterion (Table 9). In 36/50 flyvis models the horizontal-flow sign is the same for the two polarities, and the polarity-flipping component has the fly-like sign in 23/50, close to the 25/50 expected by chance. With the four-level luminance sequence of the human peripheral-drift illusion (Conway et al., 2005), at most 14 of 50 flyvis models and 2 of 6 CNNs report the predicted direction. This negative result is consistent with the optic-flow objective suppressing a static-gradient imbalance, but our experiments do not identify that objective as the cause. # 5 Adversarial perturbations 

Attack and controls. Let x be the clean input movie and u(x) the decoded flow at the central column, averaged over the analysis window. For a stimulus moving in direction ˆd we minimise 

L(δ) = cos (u(x + δ), ˆd) subject to ∥δ∥∞ ≤ ϵ, 0 ≤ x + δ ≤ 1, (1) using projected gradient descent with a random start, step size ϵ/ 10 and 60 steps (the loss plateaued within 20 steps in a pilot). The attack succeeds if the decoded direction is more than 90 ◦ from ˆd. The minimal successful bound ϵ∗ is found by bisection in log space over [0 .002 , 0.5] to 10% precision, so reported values lie on a coarse common grid. Three controls accompany each attack: uniform noise of amplitude ϵ∗ (10 draws per cell); a naive counter-moving grating of the same period and frequency, whose minimal amplitude ϵnaive 

gives the attack advantage ϵnaive /ϵ ∗; and transfer of each successful perturbation to other models. Stimuli are gratings at 4 Hz and contrasts 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.1 and 0.05. Cells in which the clean stimulus is decoded incorrectly are excluded. 

Margins. Table 2 and Figure 2 report ϵ∗ divided by contrast. For flyvis this ratio lies between 0.21 and 0.27 at contrasts 0.05 to 0.5. For the parameter-matched CNN it is 0.14 and 0.13 at contrasts 0.5 and 0.25 (five seeds), so flyvis requires about twice the perturbation. The medium and large CNNs require 0.15–0.25, within a factor of 1.4 of flyvis, and the large CNN’s median exceeds flyvis’s at the two lowest contrasts (two seeds). At contrast 1, no bound up to 0.5 reverses any of the ten flyvis models, whereas three of the six CNN checkpoints tested at that contrast are reversed ( ϵ∗ = 0 .25, 0.30 and 0.50, one per size). Uniform noise at ϵ∗

reversed none of 660 draws. The attack advantage is close to 2 for flyvis and the larger CNNs, and 3.7–4.3 for the small CNN at contrasts 0.1 to 0.5. A square-wave grating of contrast c deviates from mean luminance by ±c/ 2, so a counter-moving component of amplitude near c/ 2 cancels it; the roughly constant ϵ∗/c and an advantage near 2 are consistent with the optimized perturbation concentrating opposite-direction motion energy at sensitive stimulus features. 

Transfer. Over all 50 flyvis models and six CNNs, transfer rates show no consistent family ordering: perturbations scaled to each source’s own ϵ∗ transfer less often between flyvis models (0.15) than between same-size CNN seeds (0.29), while at a fixed bound the order reverses (0.61 versus 0.50). Input gradients are more similar among flyvis models (mean cosine 0.14) than among same-size CNN seeds ( −0.08), but this similarity does not produce a consistent transfer advantage (Appendix D). These family-level tests therefore motivate a different use of flyvis’s biological structure: rather than treating behaviour as evidence for wiring, we use the model’s named components to test mechanistic explanations directly.

6.3 Single-connection rankings do not give a sufficient circuit 

Exhaustive single-connection interventions give single-component effects, not a description of how com-ponents act together. To test whether the top-ranked connections reproduce the behaviour, we score a connection set by the number F of eight clips (standard gratings and their backward-drifting counterparts in four directions) on which the model with all other connections ablated reproduces the full model’s decoded direction (within 45 ◦), magnitude (ratio in [0 .5, 2]) and bounded activity. Under zero ablation of the com-plement, no greedy set reaches F ≥ 6 within 60 connections in any of the ten models, and top-k sets need a median of 320 connections when ranked by IG or by the true single-connection effect and all 604 when ranked by EAP. Under same-stimulus mean replacement, greedy search reaches F ≥ 6 with a median of 4.5 connections in 16 of 20 cases, but only 3 of those sets contain any directed path from a photoreceptor to a decoder input: the small sets are produced by stimulus leakage, not by a compact circuit (Appendix F). 

# 7 Cell-type circuits 

The connection-level results show that attribution depends on intervention semantics, but they define a circuit in fine-grained type-to-type connection classes. We now ask how the mechanistic picture changes when the intervention unit is a biological cell type and when the analysis is extended across the released ensemble. The connection-level experiments and single-type diagnostics use models 000–009. Joint T4/T5 silencing and the tuning-based anatomical, random and greedy cell-type analyses use all 50 released models with the same stimuli, thresholds and search rules.

# 8 Discussion 

Across the paper, the same reliability issue appears at three levels. External behaviour is not uniquely family-specific in the tested comparisons; attribution effect sizes depend on the intervention used to define the ground truth; and criterion-preserving circuits depend on the representation and trained ensemble member. These observations narrow what the connectome constraint alone can support while preserving its main practical advantage: mechanistic hypotheses can be stated on named biological components and tested by intervention. 

Model-family comparison. The CNN controls share the task, Sintel rendering and target, loss and input lattice with flyvis but not a verified training split; they also differ in recurrence, learned time constants, parameter sharing, decoder, temporal context and training schedule, so differences between the families cannot be assigned to connectome wiring. With that caveat, the families are more alike than different on our tests: both reverse under reverse-phi in selected bands, neither responds to stationary sawtooth gradients like the fly, and the adversarial margin of flyvis exceeds that of a parameter-matched CNN but not clearly that of larger CNNs. A practical advantage of the connectome-constrained representation is experimental access: named cell types and connections can be intervened on and compared with biological perturbations. 

Interventions define the question. Zero-aligned IG and EAP identify important connections under every replacement we tested, but their effect sizes agree with the ground truth only when the attribution is constructed for the same intervention. The discrepancy is therefore largely a mismatch between the causal question and the attribution construction rather than a failure of the methods, in line with the view that the ablation baseline defines the question being asked (Li & Janson, 2024; Chan et al., 2022). Likewise, how compact a circuit looks depends on the unit of intervention and the search procedure: the cell-type and connection-class results describe how criterion-preserving structure is distributed across coarse and fine units, not an equal-unit compression comparison. 

Degeneracy across the ensemble. The greedy cell-type search meets the tuning criterion in 25 of 50 models and fails in the other 25, and T4/T5 silencing does not reduce the score to F ≤ 2 in ten models. We detect no association between either split and the release package’s legacy task-error ranking or clean-grating accuracy. For the legacy scalar, rank-biserial effects for the two splits are −0.060 and −0.168 (two-sided Mann–Whitney p = .780 and .313); for clean-grating accuracy they are −0.063 and 0 .005 ( p = .767 and 

.984), with identical group medians of clean-grating accuracy (0.9375). We use the packaged scalar only as a within-release ranking variable because its generator and held-out provenance are not retained (Appendix C); the nonsignificant comparisons do not establish equivalent task quality. Agreement with canonical T4/T5 preferred directions, in contrast, has a weak descriptive association with the legacy scalar (Section 7). This variation is consistent with retraining evidence that task performance does not uniquely identify biologically plausible flyvis solutions (Karaneen et al., 2026), with underdetermination theory (Beiran & Litwin-Kumar, 2025), and with explanation non-identifiability (Méloux et al., 2025) and biological degeneracy (Prinz et al., 11 2004; Marder & Goaillard, 2006). Mechanistic claims drawn from a single trained model are claims about one member of an ensemble of trained solutions; stimuli designed to separate these solutions (Golan et al., 2020), evaluated against recordings, are a natural next step. 

Limitations. All tests use one architecture and its released ensemble. The released models’ launch-time training split and the exact generator of their packaged task-error scalar are not recoverable. Consequently, the common original-16 evaluation in Table 1 is not a verified held-out test for flyvis and cannot establish a fair shared held-out generalization comparison with the CNNs. The CNNs were trained for 30,000 steps against flyvis’s 250,000. Most stimuli are periodic gratings; attacks use one direction and frequency per condition, a single random start and the central-column readout, and low-contrast CNN cells rest on one or two seeds. Sawtooth luminances are normalized rather than calibrated to the fly experiments, and no eye movements were simulated. Held-out means exclude target movies but reuse the same stimulus families and three calibration directions. Attribution is evaluated in the ten models ranked best by the legacy packaged scalar and two readouts, one of which (B1) is small in most models; first-order EAP is a local approximation. CUDA nondeterminism makes near-zero ranks and some greedy choices non-unique. Output silencing is a model intervention, not cell removal, and lesion-style effects should not be equated with a complete mechanism (Jonas & Kording, 2017). The matched-random comparison uses 20 draws per model. The remaining caveats on the reverse-phi stimulus, the tuning criterion and the pruned connection sets are stated with the corresponding results. 

# 9 Conclusion 

Connectome constraints make mechanistic hypotheses directly testable, but in flyvis the answers are condi-tional. The tested model-family behaviours do not isolate an effect of wiring; attribution effect sizes depend on the intervention they are matched to; and criterion-preserving circuits vary with representation and en-semble member. The value of the connectome constraint is therefore not that it makes a mechanism unique, but that it makes competing mechanistic claims concrete enough to intervene on and compare. Such claims should state the model family, intervention, circuit representation and trained solution for which they hold.

One-sided retained-output metric. A retained-output diagnostic scored a set S by M (S)/M clean on one stimulus and counted the set as successful when this ratio exceeded 0.8. Cell-type sufficiency at the decoder. In models 000–009, keeping only a greedily selected set of up to 20 cell types in addition to the photoreceptors gives F ≤ 4 in every model, and the anatomical ON and OFF pathways alone give F of 0 to 5. Because the decoder reads 34 cell types, silencing most of them also changes the decoder’s input distribution, so this test conflates the circuit’s computation with the readout. Under the tuning criterion, the decoder’s 34 input types and the decoder-optimized type sets score Ftun = 0.
