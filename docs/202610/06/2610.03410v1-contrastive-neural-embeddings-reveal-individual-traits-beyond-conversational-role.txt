Abstract
Contrastive representation learning is increasingly used to recover low-dimensional struc-
ture from neural recordings, but its output is typically validated by decoding accuracy
rather than by the geometry of the manifold it produces.
We apply CEBRA to EEG
recorded from dyads in conversation, and analyze the resulting embedding, which training
constrains to the 2D sphere. Labels describing the dyads, including the absolute differ-
ence between partners’ autism-quotient scores, decode well above chance (0.77 against a
0.55 majority baseline for binary AQ magnitude; 0.44 against 0.25 for the six-class |∆AQ|
partition). However, the two permutation controls have notable differences in results: per-
muting labels over a frozen embedding yields p = 0.001, whereas retraining the encoder
under each permutation yields p = 0.50. Only the latter tests the label rather than the
geometry. Consistent with this, spherical mixture structure and per-class dispersion track
identity rather than autism trait differences in dyads; frequency-band and non-oscillatory
activity ablation controls do not change the results. However, participant-level model does
separate from its identity-aware null (p = 0.0099) while speaker-versus-listener role analysis
performs at chance in the same embedding, indicating a manifold organized by individual
— and, in contrast with current neurolinguistics models, almost invariant to speaking vs.
listening.
Based on these results, we suggest that retraining-based nulls should be the
default for grouped-data contrastive embeddings.
Keywords: contrastive learning, representational geometry, CEBRA, EEG, dyadic inter-
action, permutation controls, autism-spectrum quotient
1. Introduction
Contrastive methods such as CEBRA (Schneider et al., 2023) learn from high-dimensional
time series by projecting task-relevant features into a low-dimensional latent space. Here we
analyse CEBRA-embedded dyadic EEG in terms of the geometry and grouping structure
of the resulting manifold, in order to separate the contributions of neurotype, activity
(speaking vs. listening), and neurotype similarity between partners. Because the objective
uses cosine similarity, the embedding is constrained to S2, and every question we ask is a
question about angular structure on a sphere.
Recent behavioural work on double empathy argues that communication difficulty be-
tween autistic and non-autistic people reflects a mismatch between interlocutors rather than

(Hamilton, 2021); we identify one specific to representation learning.
A label that is constant within a dyad is a proxy for dyad identity. An encoder that
learns which dyad a segment came from will decode that label above chance without having
represented anything about the labelled trait; deep models readily recover stable individual
and group attributes from brain data (Ryali et al., 2024; Ford et al., 2021). Distinguishing
the two requires asking whether comparable geometry arises under arbitrary regroupings.
We show that it does not. We then show that a participant-level AQ label, which is not
nested inside the recording group in the same way, does survive an identity-aware control,
and that the geometry it induces is close to invariant under the speaker/listener exchange.
2. Data and embedding
Each dyad contributes two participants and four role-specific EEG recordings (A speak-
ing, A listening, B speaking, B listening), exported as time-locked EDF files cropped to a
common onset and offset. Each dyad yields two AQ-10 scores (Lundin et al., 2019; Baron-
Cohen et al., 2001). The dyad-level label is the absolute difference |∆AQ| = |AQsp −AQln|,
binarised as AQ magnitude (low: ≤1; high: ≥2). Crucially, |∆AQ| is constant across
every sample and both role mappings of a dyad—it is a coarsening of the dyad-identity
partition. The participant-level label is that individual’s own AQ-10 score, which varies
within a dyad. We train CEBRA with a time-delta conditional objective on channel-wise
normalised inputs, using the offset10 receptive-field model and cosine similarity to pro-
duce three-dimensional embeddings on S2. Quality is summarised by final InfoNCE loss,
goodness of fit, silhouette score, and k-nearest-neighbour decoding (k = 5). Every accuracy
reported below uses grouped leave-one-dyad-out cross-validation: both role mappings of a
held-out dyad are withheld together.
3. Decoding results
Dyad-level AQ magnitude decodes well above the majority baseline.
With the speaker
recording placed first in every stacked file, so that role order is held constant by construction,
leave-one-dyad-out 5-NN accuracy is 0.766 against a majority-class level of 0.545. Taken
alone this looks like a trait effect.
It is not stable under the choice of null. We ran two controls on the same embedding and
the same labels (Table 1). The frozen-embedding control holds the trained representation
fixed and permutes AQ-magnitude labels across dyads 1000 times; the retraining control
permutes complete dyad-level label signatures, preserving class counts, and retrains CEBRA
from scratch under each permutation with identical preprocessing, configuration, seed, and
training duration.

4. Manifold organization at individual level: role invariance and
trait-level structure
We next trained CEBRA on individual participant AQ, keeping speaker and listener record-
ings separate (40 participants, 80 recordings, 6.08M samples; AQ 0–9; final loss 5.9917, good-
ness of fit 0.3343). Unlike the dyad-level result, this one separates from an identity-aware
null: across 100 participant-label permutations with a matched 20,000-sample subsample
and dyad-level holdout, real decoding was 0.2470 against a null mean of 0.1157 ± 0.0197
(p = 0.0099). Accuracy did not fall when the entire co-recorded partner was also held out,
exact-AQ accuracy rose from 0.2300 under leave-one-participant-out to 0.2437 under leave-
one-dyad-out (Table 2), so the effect is not carried by within-dyad leakage. It is nonetheless
modest: exact-AQ exceeds the 0.2007 majority baseline by ∼0.046, balanced accuracy
across ten levels is 0.188, and the AQ silhouette is −0.167. AQ grades the manifold without
partitioning it.

5. Discussion
The generalizeable result of embedding analysis is that decoding accuracy, computed on
a contrastive embedding, is not a test of the label when the label is constant within a
recording group. The frozen-embedding permutation essentially asks if the geometry that
was learned in contrastive embedding happens to contain the label partition; the answer
will be affirmative whenever the encoder has learned group identity, because the label is, in
fact, a coarse-grained equivalent of identity. The retraining permutation asks if comparable
representaitonal geometry arises from arbitrary regroupings. In our data, the two proce-
dures differ by orders of magnitude in p-value while operating on identical embeddings and
identical labels. Since grouped neural datasets are the norm rather than the exception, we
would expect this issue to occur widely wherever contrastive embeddings are evaluated by
decoding.
Interestingly, participant-level AQ embedding generalizes to fully held-out dyads and
separates from an identity-aware null, while speaker-versus-listener role is at chance in the
same embedding, indicating within-person recordings are highly similar across conversa-
tional roles. The neural manifold, then, is primarilty organized by who is being recorded

(individual neural dynamics and state space - cf. (Ahn et al., 2025; Malaia et al., 2020, 2016;
McCleod et al., 2024)) rather than by what they are doing. For dyadic neuroscience, this
means the need to adopt experimental designs in which the label of interest varies within a
recording group (either using within-dyad manipulations, or constructing repeated pairings
across partners).
Limitations.
Our sample (33 dyads at the dyad level; 40 participants at the participant
level) was over-represented for lower-AQ (neurotypical) participants, such that the classes
with larger |∆AQ| contained as few as three dyads. Thus, the six-class analysis is sample-
limited independently of any confound.
Broader impacts.
Our approach advances understanding of interpersonal neural mech-
anisms in autism and social communication more broadly. By moving beyond deficit-based
models to examine dyadic interactions, this research supports more nuanced views of neu-
rodiversity and could inform more effective, relationship-focused interventions. Geometric
representation approaches could contribute to development of objective biomarkers for so-
cial communication challenges; however, the present results are a demonstration of why such
measures should not be treated as biomarkers judged by the strength of decoding accuracy
alone.
