ABSTRACT
Understanding how neural activity represents higher-order cognition and how
these representations evolve over time has long been a central pursuit in neuro-
science. However, current analytical tools cannot easily distinguish representa-
tional plasticity from recording instability in chronic neural recordings. Here, we
introduce NeuroLens (Latent Embeddings of Neural Semantics), a self-supervised
model based on the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) framework
that learns denoised, semantically informative latents from chronic neural record-
ings. An adaptive encoder maps changing neural populations into a common la-
tent space, while a temporal predictor learns structure that supports prediction of
future latent states. By predicting in latent space, NeuroLens captures tempo-
rally predictable structure and reduces sensitivity to transient, recording-specific
variability. Across chronic intracortical data in mice and humans, the learned rep-
resentations improve decoding of decision-making and semantic task variables.
Multi-day pretraining enables generalization to future sessions, rapid few-shot
adaptation to unseen neural populations, and more stable decoding over time than
state-of-the-art baselines. Together, these results establish NeuroLens as a new
paradigm for studying how neural representations change during learning and over
long timescales.

INTRODUCTION
Learning continually reshapes the relationship between neural activity and behavior over extended
timescales (Peters et al., 2014). Understanding this process requires tracking how task-relevant
population dynamics emerge, persist, and reorganize across days (Wang et al., 2026). Advances
in chronic recordings increasingly enable such longitudinal measurements (Melin et al., 2024; Bim-
bard et al., 2025; Skromne Carrasco et al., 2026), but electrode drift, signal degradation, and changes
in the recorded neurons can obscure learning-related changes in neural activity (Karpowicz et al.,
2024). This challenge motivates methods that capture genuine representational plasticity while re-
maining robust to recording nonstationarity.
Existing approaches address recording nonstationarity through statistical and deep-learning-based
neural manifold alignment (Karpowicz et al., 2025; Gallego et al., 2020), recalibration-based test-
time adaptation (Fan et al., 2023; Feghhi et al., 2026), and transformer-based in-context learning (Le
et al., 2026). These methods improve decoding stability across recording days, but many are tailored
to specific decoding tasks or compensate for distribution shifts in observed activity without explicitly
separating task-relevant structure from recording variability. These limitations motivate a general-
purpose framework that accommodates changing neural populations while preserving biological
changes in the representations themselves.
Self-supervised learning (SSL) provides a foundation for learning such representations beyond a
specific decoding task. However, many existing approaches reconstruct observed neural activity,

which may retain recording-specific details alongside behaviorally relevant structure (Ye & Pandar-
inath, 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). For example, probe drift can alter which neurons are recorded
even when the underlying population dynamics remain stable. Reconstruction-based models must
account for these changes in the observations, although they may be unrelated to the neural com-
putation of interest (Rule et al., 2020). Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) instead
predict masked or future representations in latent space (Assran et al., 2023). This objective em-
phasizes predictable structure without requiring the model to reproduce every detail of the recorded
activity. Applied to chronic neural recordings, it may yield latents that are less sensitive to recording
instability while capturing information relevant to behavior and cognition.
Building on this principle, we introduce NeuroLens (Latent Embeddings of Neural Semantics), a
self-supervised model that learns denoised, semantically informative neural latents through causal
prediction in latent space (Maes et al., 2026). The model integrates two complementary components.
An adaptive neural encoder uses cross-attention to accommodate varying neuron counts across days
and infers neuron identities from a few calibration trials, mapping each day’s neural population into
a common latent space. A causal transformer predictor then predicts future latent embeddings, with
parameters shared across days to encourage a stable, predictive structure. These components enable
adaptation to changing neural populations while capturing temporal regularities across days.
Across chronic intracortical data in mice (Melin et al., 2024) and humans (Willett et al., 2023; Card
et al., 2024), we demonstrate that NeuroLens learns latent embeddings that improve decoding of
visual decision-making and semantic task variables. After multi-day pretraining, the model gen-
eralizes to future sessions and supports rapid, gradient-free few-shot adaptation to unseen neural
populations, outperforming state-of-the-art (SOTA) neural decoding baselines. Despite substantial
neuron loss and day-to-day drift in neural activity, interpretability analyses indicate that NeuroLens
learns temporally consistent representations with a stable effective rank over time. These results
provide a foundation for tracking task-relevant population dynamics over long timescales. The con-
tributions of this work include:
• A self-supervised framework that learns denoised, semantically informative neural repre-
sentations through prediction in latent space.
• An architecture that balances flexibility and stability through an adaptive neural encoder
and a shared temporal predictor, enabling rapid generalization to unseen neural populations.
• A framework for learning task-relevant representations that are robust to recording nonsta-
tionarity, enabling new analyses of how neural latent states evolve over time.

RELATED WORK
Large-scale models for neural analysis.
Recent advances have expanded from modeling single-
session neural dynamics to large-scale pretraining across sessions, animals, and tasks (Andre et al.,
2026). Supervised approaches such as POYO (Azabou et al., 2023) and POSSM (Ryoo et al., 2026)
enable scalable multi-session decoding through spike tokenization combined with PerceiverIO (Jae-
gle et al., 2021) or recurrent state-space models. Self-supervised approaches (Ye & Pandarinath,
2021; Zhang et al., 2024; 2025) instead learn representations by masking neural activity across time,
neurons, regions and modalities. Despite this progress, two challenges remain. First, supervised rep-
resentations are tied to predefined decoding targets, while self-supervised objectives largely focus on
reconstructing neural activity rather than capturing global structure. Second, existing multi-session
models primarily address transfer across animals, tasks, or brain regions, with limited focus on
adapting to temporal distribution shifts.
Self-supervised representation learning for neural activity.
Self-supervised learning enables
transferable representations from unlabeled data. Observation-space methods such as GPT (Rad-
ford et al., 2018) predict future inputs, while masked autoencoders (MAEs) (Radford et al., 2018)
reconstruct missing inputs. Although these methods scale effectively, they require modeling low-
level details that may be irrelevant to downstream tasks. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures
(JEPAs) instead predict masked targets in latent space, encouraging higher-level structure (Bardes
et al., 2024). I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) demonstrated semantic visual representations without
hand-crafted augmentations, while LeJEPA (Maes et al., 2026) introduced a theoretically grounded
regularizer to prevent representation collapse. In neuroscience, self-supervised models (Ye et al.,
2


2023; Zhang et al., 2025) have largely adopted reconstruction-based objectives to model neural pop-
ulation activity. Given the noise and variability in neural recordings, latent-space prediction may
emphasize dynamics that capture higher-level representations and support generalization.
Nonstationarity in chronic neural recordings.
Methods for addressing nonstationarity in
chronic neural recordings have largely been developed for task-specific decoding and fall into three
categories. First, manifold-alignment methods map activity across days into a shared latent space
using statistical or deep learning techniques; NoMAD maps activity to a stable reference mani-
fold (Karpowicz et al., 2025), while CEBRA learns consistent embeddings from temporal context
(Schneider et al., 2023). Second, test-time recalibration methods continually update decoders, with
CORP and DietCORP using language-model-corrected pseudo-labels for online adaptation (Fan
et al., 2023). Third, transformer-based methods, including SPINT and POSSM, use causal prediction
objectives (Le et al., 2026; Ryoo et al., 2026). However, these approaches have limited generality:
manifold alignment imposes restrictive assumptions about shared cross-day dynamics, recalibra-
tion requires task-specific pseudo-labels, and SPINT and POSSM rely on supervision for predefined
tasks. Learning general-purpose representations that remain stable under long-term changes in neu-
ral population composition while supporting accurate decoding therefore remains an open challenge.

DATASETS
IBL chronic Neuropixels data.
This dataset tracks a mouse learning a visual decision-making
task in which it turns a wheel to report whether a stimulus appears on the left or right side of a screen,
receiving a reward for correct choices (Melin et al., 2024). Chronic Neuropixels recordings capture
spiking activity from hundreds of neurons over 3 months as the mouse progresses from novice to
proficient performance. We analyze 53 daily sessions (∼47 hours) from this mouse, including both
trial and non-trial (spontaneous) periods. Each session contains 366 trials on average, each spanning
2 seconds around stimulus onset; non-trial periods are divided into contiguous 2-second windows.
Spikes are binned at approximately 16.7 ms (60 bins per second), and units with mean firing rates
below 1 Hz are excluded. Each recording contains 159∼498 units across approximately 6∼20
anatomical regions. Neural activity is paired with synchronized left- and right-camera videos of the
face and upper body. Behavioral variables include trial-level binary choice and time-bin-level wheel
speed, whisker motion energy from each camera, and paw and nose speeds from the left camera.
Human chronic Utah array data.
These datasets contain neural recordings from two participants
(T12 and T15) with ALS-induced anarthria as they attempted to speak displayed sentences. For
T12, chronic recordings were obtained from four intracortical Utah arrays implanted in the ventral
motor cortex (Willett et al., 2023); we retain the 128 channels in area 6v. We analyze 9,680 trials
across 23 daily sessions over four months, totaling 16.2 hours. For T15, we analyze 9,498 trials
across 45 sessions over 20 months, using all 256 electrodes in the precentral gyrus (Card et al.,
2024). For both datasets, neural features include threshold-crossing spike counts and spiking-band
power (SBP), binned at 20 ms and z-scored to mitigate recording nonstationarity (Appendix C). Each
sentence is labeled with an unaligned phoneme sequence using a 41-class vocabulary that includes
the Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) blank token. For semantic decoding, we obtain a
384-dimensional sentence embedding from all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), such
that sentences with similar meanings have similar embeddings.

METHODS
NeuroLens is a self-supervised model that learns task-relevant neural representations by predicting
future latent embeddings. Building on LeJEPA (Maes et al., 2026), it addresses nonstationarity in
chronic recordings via a day-adaptive encoder, which maps daily activity into a common latent space
by conditioning on neural population size and identity, and a shared autoregressive (AR) predictor,
which extracts temporally predictable structure to form a stable subspace across days. Training
combines prediction mean squared error (MSE) with sketched isotropic Gaussian regularization
(SIGReg) to prevent representation collapse (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025). At inference, the encoder
supports few-shot, gradient-free adaptation to unseen populations. We detail the architecture and
training objectives below.

Figure 1: NeuroLens framework. (A) Model overview. A day-adaptive encoder maps spike counts at each
time bin to latent representations, and an autoregressive (AR) predictor shared across days predicts future
latents from past latents. The model is trained using a latent prediction loss together with SIGReg loss to
prevent representation collapse. (B-C) Day-adaptive cross-attention encoder based on calibration data. At
each time bin, an MLP independently projects each neuron’s spike count into a neuron token. These tokens
serve as the keys and values of the cross-attention module. Because attention is permutation-invariant, we
distinguish neuron identities using calibration trials sampled from each recording day. From these trials, we
compute firing-rate statistics, autocorrelograms (ACGs), and population coupling to generate a signature for
each neuron. A second MLP maps these signatures to neuron embeddings, which are concatenated with brain-
region embeddings and added to the corresponding neuron tokens. Learned queries then aggregate the variable-
length sequence of neuron tokens through cross-attention, producing a fixed number of latent embeddings for
the AR predictor. Processing each time bin independently prevents temporal leakage, while calibration-based
neuron embeddings enable adaptation to previously unseen neural populations across recording days.

Gradient-based (GB) few-shot adaptation.
We adapt to data from a new day using sampled cal-
ibration trials, updating only the read-in layers of the encoder, including the spike projector fproj, the
identity network hid, and the fusion projector ffuse (Figure 1). For neural decoding, we additionally
update an attached decoder head. All other parameters, including the encoder cross-attention layers,
region embeddings, and the AR predictor, are frozen to preserve the representations and dynamics
learned during pretraining.
Full finetuning.
With sufficient data from a new session, we fine-tune the entire model, including
the decoder head for neural decoding, when few-shot adaptation may not perform well for tasks such
as speech decoding (Willett et al., 2023).

RESULTS
5.1
HIGH-LEVEL VS. LOW-LEVEL NEURAL DECODING
Brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) can decode movement and speech and enable cursor control, but
predicting high-level cognitive variables such as semantic content remains less explored. To better
evaluate the neural representations learned by different models, we compare their performance on
both high-level and low-level neural decoding tasks.
Baselines.
For IBL, we compare NeuroLens with Spike, a linear model that predicts future be-
havior from past neural activity, and causal NDT, a self-supervised transformer-based autoencoder
that predicts neural activity in the next time bin from past activity (Ye & Pandarinath, 2021). For
human data, we use only causal NDT because using linear decoders with neural activity input per-
form poorly. For all baselines, we match latent embedding size, model capacity, and training setup
6


to ensure a fair comparison (Appendix K). These comparisons test whether latent-space prediction
better captures decisions and sentence semantics than observation-space prediction.
Evaluation on IBL data.
We evaluate learned representations from single-session models on low-
and high-level decoding tasks on the same 14 days that are later held out in Sec. 5.2, each modeled
separately; see Appendix D for data splits. We train NeuroLens and causal NDT separately for each
day, freeze their parameters, and extract latents bz(d)
t
using sliding windows with stride 1 and up to
59 preceding bins. Both models use past neural activity to predict the neural activity (causal NDT)
or neural latent (NeuroLens) at the current time step, which is then used for decoding. We evaluate
representation quality with linear probing while keeping the pretrained models frozen: we fit linear
decoders to predict behavior, select pretrained checkpoints by validation decoding performance, and
report test results. We report R2 for five time-bin-level variables—wheel velocity, whisker motion
energy from each camera, and left-camera paw and nose speeds—and balanced accuracy for choice
after pooling representations across all time steps within each trial into a single vector.
Evaluation on human data.
To evaluate representation quality, we use the provided data split
(Appendix D), with the majority of recording days represented in both splits to avoid temporal
distribution shifts. We pretrain NeuroLens and causal NDT on pooled training data from both par-
ticipants in a self-supervised manner. During pretraining, we evaluate models on the validation
set using a linear probe to predict sentence embeddings, measured by cosine similarity and top-5
closed-vocabulary sentence retrieval accuracy (Appendix E.3), and an MLP to decode phonemes,
measured by phoneme error rate (PER) (Appendix E.2). We select checkpoints by aggregate val-
idation performance across these metrics. Unlike the linear-probing-only IBL experiments, strong
human decoding performance requires full-model fine-tuning. For each model, we initialize sepa-
rate T12 and T15 models from its shared checkpoint and fine-tune on each participant’s data. For
each baseline, we train phoneme and sentence-embedding decoders separately, attaching a head to
the predictor output with CTC and MSE loss, respectively (Appendix G).
Results.
For IBL data, Figure 2B shows that both causal NDT and NeuroLens outperform raw-
spike decoding, confirming the benefit of learning nonlinear neural latents via SSL. Notably, Neu-
roLens achieves higher choice decoding accuracy and comparable movement-related R2 despite
a lower effective latent rank than causal NDT (see Appendix E.1 for rank computation), indicat-
ing that latent-space models can learn more task-relevant representations while remaining far more
compact than reconstruction-based models. This is reinforced by Figure 2C, where, as the animal
transitions from novice to expert between April and June, left- and right-trial latents (choice condi-
tion) grow increasingly separable—more so for NeuroLens than for causal NDT, reflecting denoised,
more task-relevant encoding. A parallel pattern emerges in the human data: Figure 2E shows that
NeuroLens substantially outperforms causal NDT on sentence-embedding decoding while achieving
comparable performance on phoneme decoding, and that NeuroLens’s latents separate more cleanly
by semantic class than those of causal NDT, pointing to more semantically meaningful representa-
tions. Relative to causal NDT, NeuroLens achieves larger gains on choice (6%) than on movement
(2%) decoding, and on sentence-embedding (38%) than on phoneme (4-16%) decoding, suggesting
that it captures richer global, semantic structure from neural activity overall.
5.2
GENERALIZATION ACROSS TIME
Representations that generalize across days help track learning-related neural changes despite
recording drift and maintain stable BCI decoding performance over time. To this end, we evalu-
ate models on unseen future data using the various adaptation regimes described in Sec. 4.3.
Baselines.
For IBL, we compare NeuroLens with causal NDT (Section 5.1) and four additional
baselines: (1) CEBRA: a self-supervised contrastive learning method that aligns neural samples with
similar temporal contexts to capture temporally consistent neural dynamics (Schneider et al., 2023).
(2) SPINT: a supervised sliding-window transformer decoder that learns context-dependent neuron
identities from raw calibration activity to adapt to unseen neural populations (Le et al., 2026). (3)
POYO: a supervised decoder based on Perceiver IO that maps spike tokens to latent representations
via cross-attention, processes them with a transformer, and decodes behavior through a second cross-
attention module (Azabou et al., 2023). (4) POSSM: replaces POYO’s transformer with a recurrent
state-space model (SSM) for efficient real-time decoding (Ryoo et al., 2026). For human data,

Evaluation on IBL data.
Using a chronological split (Figure 3A), we pretrain all baselines on

Results.
In Figure 3B, we benchmark multi-day pretrained NeuroLens against other pretrained

5.3
TASK-RELATED REPRESENTATION DRIFT VS. RECORDING NONSTATIONARITY
Having established that NeuroLens captures task-relevant representations that generalize across tem-
poral distribution shifts, we next investigate how they evolve in chronic recordings.
Representation drift.
In chronic data, changes in neural activity can reflect learning as well as
intrinsic changes unrelated to behavior. Here, we loosely refer to these changes as representation
drift. We first examine how representational similarity changes with the temporal separation be-
tween recording days. For each day pair, we compare trial-by-trial representations using representa-
tional similarity analysis (RSA; Appendix E.4) and define representational dissimilarity as 1 - RDM
similarity, with higher values indicating more dissimilar representational geometry. As the temporal
separation increases, representations become increasingly dissimilar in the raw spike space, whereas
NeuroLens latents remain more similar. Thus, NeuroLens learns temporally consistent representa-
tions that are robust to distribution shifts in chronic neural activity.
Latent dimensionality.
We next quantify how representation dimensionality changes over time by
computing the effective rank of the causal NDT and NeuroLens latents for each recording day. As the
number of recorded neurons declines across the IBL chronic recordings (Figure 3A), the effective
rank of causal NDT decreases substantially over time, tracking the loss of recorded neurons. In
contrast, NeuroLens’s effective rank remains stable across days in both datasets (Figure 4B).
These results show that NeuroLens learns stable, compact representations that separate task-related
changes from recording nonstationarity, enabling studies of learning over long timescales.

DISCUSSION
In this work, we introduce NeuroLens, a JEPA-based self-supervised model that learns denoised,
semantically informative representations from chronic neural recordings. Across mouse and human
intracortical data, NeuroLens improves decoding of higher-level task variables, generalizes across
recording days, and enables rapid few-shot adaptation to unseen neural populations. By extracting
stable, task-relevant structure from nonstationary neural activity, NeuroLens provides a framework
for studying neural representations over long timescales. Despite its promise, this work has several
limitations. Although we analyze representation drift across days, we do not disentangle changes
due to learning and behavioral changes from intrinsic neural changes, nor do we model cross-area
interactions or region-specific representation drift. Separating these sources of variability through
joint behavioral modeling and cross-area analyses is an important direction for future work.

CALIBRATION-BASED CHANNEL DRIFT ADAPTATION
Channel Statistics.
For each recording day d, we use a fixed set of calibration trials to compute
five summary statistics for each channel and then average these statistics across trials. The choice of
statistics depends on the input data. For T12 spike count data (Willett et al., 2023), which are non-
negative and count-valued, we use the firing-rate statistics described in Appendix A: mean activity,
standard deviation, Fano factor (Churchland et al., 2010), coefficient of variation, and the fraction
of nonzero time bins. In contrast, spike-band power (SBP) is not count-valued, and the T15 dataset
provides pre-z-scored features without raw spike counts (Card et al., 2024). For these inputs, we
instead use statistics that describe the distribution’s location, spread, skewness, tail weight, and
magnitude.
Per-Day Drift Context Vector.
We normalize each statistic using constants estimated from the
training days, so that no individual statistic dominates because of its numerical scale. For each
channel, we first convert its five normalized calibration statistics into a learned channel embedding
using the same MLP layer. This shared mapping lets the model represent different patterns of activity
and recording drift in a common feature space. We then average the channel embeddings separately
within each input modality (spike counts and SBP). The resulting modality-level vectors summarize
the typical calibration distribution for that modality on a given day. Finally, we concatenate these
modality summaries and project them into a single day context vector c(d). Because the channel
embeddings are averaged, the context vector c(d) does not depend on the order of channels.

C
DATA PREPROCESSING
C.1
IBL CHRONIC NEUROPIXELS DATA
For the IBL chronic Neuropixels dataset, we analyzed neural and video recordings collected over 3
months from a single mouse (MM008) (Melin et al., 2024). We derived binned spike counts from
the neural recordings and behavioral variables from the video recordings, as described below.
Neural Activity (spikes).
For each recording session, we combined spike-sorted units using
Kilosort (Pachitariu et al., 2016) from all probes into a single neural population. We retained units
with a mean firing rate greater than 1 Hz, computed using only the training portion of each record-
ing; thus, unit selection did not use validation or test data. Recorded units were located mainly in
primary and secondary motor cortex and dorsal striatum, with smaller numbers in prelimbic and
dorsal anterior cingulate cortex. Spike times were binned into 16.7 ms intervals (∼60 Hz), and raw
spike counts were used as model inputs.
We divided each session into trial and non-trial periods. For trial data, each trial was represented
by a 2 s window spanning 0.8 s before to 1.2 s after stimulus onset (120 bins). For non-trial data,
we excluded the interval from 0.2 s before to 0.8 s after stimulus onset for every valid trial. We
segmented the remaining recording into consecutive, non-overlapping 2 s windows (120 bins).
Trial-Level Behavior.
We used the mouse’s binary choice in the visual decision-making task as
a discrete behavioral variable. On each trial, the mouse reported whether it perceived the stimulus
on the left or right side. Choice labels were therefore available only for trial windows; non-trial
windows were unlabeled.
Time-Bin-Level Behavior.
From video recordings, we extracted wheel speed, whisker motion
energy from the left and right cameras, and paw and nose speeds from the left camera using keypoint
estimation (Biderman et al., 2024). Each signal was resampled to the same 16.7 ms bins used
for spike counts. Because these measures are available during both trial and non-trial periods, we
included them in both window types. Frames with missing keypoints were assigned a speed of
zero and were not interpolated. Each behavioral variable was scaled to the range [0, 10] using its
minimum and maximum values from the training portion of the recording only. The resulting scaling
parameters were then applied consistently across all data splits and window types.
C.2
HUMAN CHRONIC UTAH ARRAY DATA.
Neural Activity (spikes and SBP).
We used channel-level features rather than spike-sorted
units. Both T12 (Willett et al., 2023) and T15 (Card et al., 2024) provide threshold-crossing counts
and spike-band power (SBP) for each electrode in 20 ms bins. We smoothed each feature separately
within each trial using a Gaussian kernel with a standard deviation of 40 ms, then combined every
five consecutive bins into a 100 ms input token. Because the sentences differ in length, trials have
variable durations.
For T12, we used the 128 electrodes in ventral premotor cortex (area 6v) and excluded electrodes in
area 44. The two features from each electrode yielded 256 input dimensions per time bin. We first
z-scored each feature within each recording session, then z-scored it again within each day using
statistics computed only from that day’s training trials. For T15, we used all 256 electrodes in the
14


precentral gyrus, which yielded 512 input dimensions per time bin. We used the publicly released,
normalized T15 features without further normalization.
Phoneme Labels.
We derived phoneme labels following the procedures of Willett et al. (2023);
Card et al. (2024). For T12, we converted each sentence to a phoneme sequence with the g2p-en
grapheme-to-phoneme package. Before conversion, we lowercased the text and removed punctu-
ation other than apostrophes. We then removed stress markers and added a silence token at word
boundaries and at the end of each sentence. This produced a vocabulary of 39 phonemes and one
silence token; we added a blank token for CTC training. For T15, we used the phoneme sequences
provided with the dataset, which use the same 40 non-blank classes. When training supervised base-
lines jointly across T12 and T15 datasets, we remapped T15 labels to the T12 class order to ensure
consistent labeling across participants.
Sentence Embeddings.
We encoded each sentence with the pretrained all-MiniLM-L6-v2
model (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019).
We averaged its contextual token embeddings and L2-
normalized the result to obtain one 384-dimensional vector per sentence. The shared encoder placed
sentence representations from both participants in the same embedding space.
D
DATA SPLITS
For both the IBL and human chronic datasets, we considered two experimental settings: (1) high-
vs. low-level neural decoding (Section 5.1) and (2) generalization across time (Section 5.2). The
two settings use the same model architectures and training procedures (Appendices K and L) and
differ only in how the data are split. In the first setting, training and test data were drawn from the
same recording days, allowing us to evaluate representation quality without requiring generalization
across days. In the second setting, we split the data chronologically: models were trained on earlier
recording days and evaluated on later, held-out days, creating a temporal distribution shift between
training and test data.
IBL chronic Neuropixels data.
For high- vs. low-level neural decoding, we focused on single-
session models. Within each session, we split the data into 70% training, 10% validation and 20%
test sets. Because all splits came from the same recording day, this setting does not introduce a
substantial temporal distribution shift. For generalization across time, we split the full MM008
dataset chronologically to introduce an across-day distribution shift. We used 39 days collected
between 2023-03-21 and 2023-05-17 for multi-day pretraining, and held out 14 subsequent days,

held-out day, we used the first 20% of trials as the adaptation set, the next 10% as the validation set
for checkpoint selection, and the remaining 70% to evaluate decoding performance.
Human chronic Utah array data.
For high- vs. low-level neural decoding, all data splits were
drawn from the same recording days. For T12 (Willett et al., 2023), we use the provided training
partition for training, hold out the last block of each training day for validation, and use the pro-
vided evaluation partition as the test set, resulting in 7,920 training, 880 validation, and 880 test
trials across 24 recording days. For T15 (Card et al., 2024), we use the provided training partition
and divide the provided evaluation partition equally into validation and test sets, resulting in 8,072
training, 704 validation, and 722 test trials across 45 days. For generalization across time, we split
each participant’s full dataset chronologically, using the earliest 80% of recording days for training
and holding out the final 20% for evaluation. For T12, we trained on 18 past days and evaluated on
the subsequent 5 days (excluding 2022-06-14 due to data quality). For T15, we trained on 36 past
days and evaluated on the following 9 days. Within each training day, we used the first 90% for
training and the remaining 10% for validation. For each held-out day, the first 20% served only as
unlabeled calibration data, and we reported decoding performance on the remaining 80%.

E.2
PHONEME ERROR RATE
We used a 41-token phoneme vocabulary comprising all phonemes, silence, and the CTC blank
token. We computed the phoneme error count by first removing repeated tokens and blanks from the
decoded phoneme sequences and then calculating the Levenshtein edit distance (Levenshtein, 1966)
between the decoded and ground-truth sequences, counting substitutions, insertions, and deletions.
We then normalized the error count by the total number of ground-truth phonemes to obtain the
phoneme error rate.
E.3
TOP-5 SENTENCE RETRIEVAL ACCURACY
Top-5 sentence retrieval accuracy measures whether a model’s predicted sentence embedding is
close enough to the ground-truth (GT) sentence to pick it out of a candidate pool: a trial counts as
correct if the GT sentence is among the 5 most similar to the prediction. We use this metric on the
T12 and T15 datasets to evaluate representation quality via the semantic decoding task, where the
target is the 384-dimensional all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence embedding (Reimers & Gurevych,
2019). We report average top-5 accuracy across held-out days. Each day has a candidate pool of 288
∼448 sentences, which keeps chance level low (1∼2%). Predicted and GT candidate embeddings
are first centered by subtracting the mean GT embedding across test trials, so we can focus on
trial-to-trial variability; candidates are then ranked by cosine similarity to the centered prediction.

Representational similarity analysis.
Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) (Kriegeskorte
et al., 2008) compares representations through their second-order structure. Rather than aligning
representations point by point, RSA first converts each representation into a representational dis-

bins within a trial). It then compares two representations by correlating their RDMs. Because this
comparison depends on pairwise relationships rather than the coordinate system itself, RSA is in-
variant to rotations of the underlying representation and can be applied across models or recording
days without a shared basis.

Linear baselines.
We use Ridge regression to decode time-bin-level behavior and logistic regres-

timesteps and concatenates them as input to the logistic regression.

Causal NDT.
Causal NDT is a self-supervised causal Transformer baseline built on (Ye & Pan-
darinath, 2021). A 2-layer MLP read-in maps neural activity at each time step to the latent space,
a causal transformer backbone (Table K.1) processes the sequence, and a linear output layer maps
each hidden state back to observation space to predict the next step’s activity. For IBL, inputs are
raw spike counts. For multi-day pretraining, each session (day) has its own read-in and output layer
during stage-1 training, since each day has a distinct number of neurons. The output layer predicts
per-neuron log firing rates, trained to predict the next bin’s spike counts under a Poisson negative
log-likelihood, with no behavioral labels used. In stage 2, only a newly initialized per-day read-in
and the decoding heads are updated, with the rest of the model frozen. For decoding, the decoder
heads and losses are the same as other baselines (binary cross-entropy for choice, MSE for time-
bin-level behavior). For the human chronic dataset, 5 consecutive 20 ms time bins are concatenated
along the channel dimension to form 1 input time patch. The spike data are projected with a 2-layer
MLP followed by a learned temporal positional embedding, and we employ separate participant-
specific output layers to reconstruct their own spike data, while keeping the Transformer backbone
shared. In Stage 1, the training objective is next-step prediction of the following time patch’s neural
features using an MSE loss; no behavioral labels are required for this stage, and the spike recon-
struction output layer is discarded after Stage 1. In Stage 2, the model is fine-tuned end-to-end on a
single participant with the task-specific decoding losses, as in Appendix G.
POSSM.
We implement POSSM (Ryoo et al., 2026) as a supervised multi-session baseline with an
event-based neural read-in, a unidirectional GRU temporal backbone, and a causal cross-attention
decoder. Unlike the shared IBL input representation, which consists of vectors of binned spike
counts, POSSM retains individual spike events within each 16.7 ms bin. Each spike is represented
by a learned unit embedding, and one learned latent token cross-attends to the spike-event tokens
within each bin. The resulting bin representation is processed by the GRU, after which an output
query cross-attends to preceding GRU hidden states to produce the downstream latent.

current bin. When producing the latent for time bin t, the decoder explicitly attends to time bins
up to time bin t −1. The unidirectional GRU may carry information from earlier states into this
explicit context, but neither the GRU nor the decoder receives neural activity from the current or
future bins to prevent data leakage. For an unseen IBL day, the recurrent and cross-attention mod-

step. The phoneme decoding model applies LayerNorm →Linear(256, 41) to every decoded step.

and applies LayerNorm →Linear(256, 384). We separately train the model to decode phonemes
and sentence embeddings because joint optimization caused substantial interference between the
two tasks.

width near the shared backbone budget. The reported model uses a four-layer GRU with hidden
size 588. For T12 and T15, we compare multiple GRU depths while matching the full 7.29-million-
parameter transformer backbone budget. The reported model-capacity-aligned model uses 8 GRU
layers with hidden size 395. The remaining architectural settings are summarized in Table K.4.
SPINT.
We implement SPINT (Spatial Permutation-Invariant Neural Transformer) (Le et al.,
2026) as a supervised multi-session decoding baseline on the IBL chronic dataset, with a calibration-
based neural read-in module and spatial cross-attention over recorded units. For each neuron, the
model takes its spike count sequence across consecutive time bins and represents that sequence as a
single token. A shared identity network encodes 64 unlabeled calibration windows from a session’s
training span, averages the identity network outputs across windows, and maps them to a per-neuron
identity vector. This neuron identity vector is added to each neuron’s projected spike count token
before the shared MLP read-in. Learned latent queries, one per behavior output, cross-attend to
the resulting neural tokens to produce fixed-dimensional query latents. This read-in module sup-
ports variable-sized populations without a learnable neuron-identity lookup table. Dynamic neuron
dropout is also employed to improve robustness to changes in the recorded population.
During multi-day pretraining, all model parameters are shared across days, while a calibration set is
sampled from each day. For IBL, the stage-1 training objective is an equally weighted sum of mean-
squared error for time-bin-level, movement-related behavior and binary cross-entropy for choice.

CEBRA.
CEBRA (Schneider et al., 2023) is a self-supervised contrastive learning framework that
learns embeddings preserving temporal proximity, trained via an InfoNCE objective, wherein each
anchor is paired with a positive sample from the same trial at a small temporal offset and contrasted
against negatives sampled from the remaining anchors in the batch using cosine similarity, without
any behavioral labels.
CEBRA is not transformer-based, so it does not use the shared backbone of Table K.1; its encoder
is a convolutional network with a 10-step receptive field and no positional embedding. We adopt
the architecture provided by the authors (Schneider et al., 2023) rather than widening it to match the
transformer parameter count. On IBL data, we use the provided model config offset10-model
(128 hidden units, 192 latent dimensions). On T12 and T15, we employ the same 2-layer MLP
22


read-in as for the other baselines, followed by 3 causal convolutions (kernel 4, receptive field 10
patched steps, width 192), so that it shares the participant-specific read-in and the decoder heads
with the other baselines. Because the encoder has no positional embedding and no cross-time mixing
beyond its receptive field, its per-time-bin embedding is window-invariant, which the other models’
positional embeddings do not permit.
The provided encoder centers its receptive field, so the embedding labelling time bin t would read
time bins [t −5, t + 5) (4 bins into the future). We shift the receptive field to end 1 bin early, so
that we use neural activity only up to t −1, to prevent data leakage and match the strictly causal
setup under which the other models are scored. Downstream, the per-time-bin embedding is the
latent: time-bin-level behaviors are decoded per time bin, and the trial-level target is decoded from
the embedding averaged over the time bins within a trial, then classified with a linear decoder. On an
unseen day, the pretrained backbone is frozen, and that day’s own read-in encoder is trained together
with a new read-in layer to accommodate the new data distribution, which aligns with CEBRA’s own
few-shot update setting (Schneider et al., 2023).

POYO.
POYO (Azabou et al., 2023) is a supervised decoder trained to decode behavioral targets.
Unlike models based on binned spike counts, it tokenizes individual spikes, each with a per-neuron
embedding and a rotary time embedding (RoPE); since timestamps enter the tokenizer for spikes
rather than via learnable time embeddings, the backbone has no positional embedding, so its Ta-
ble K.1 count reflects only the self-attention blocks. A cross-attention read-in compresses spikes
within each 16.7 ms step into latent tokens, the shared causal Transformer backbone processes them,
and a cross-attention read-out queries the result at the output times before per-target MLP decoder
heads. The objective equally weights behavior MSE and choice binary cross entropy.
Attention is masked on three levels so that no model output depends on neural activity after the
time bin it is queried at (no temporal leakage): (1) the cross-attention read-in latent attends only to
spikes preceding the end of its own time step, (2) latents attend only to earlier latents, (3) and the
cross-attention read-out query attends only to latents whose time step has ended. For an unseen day,
the read-in, backbone, and read-out are frozen, and only that session’s new unit and day embedding
rows are updated together with a fresh decoder head, following POYO’s own few-shot recipe (Az-
abou et al., 2023), as also done for POSSM (Ryoo et al., 2026). For hyperparameter tuning, we
searched the finetuning learning rate, batch size, window stride, unit dropout ratio, and different
initializations for new rows in the neuron embedding lookup table, selecting the best checkpoint
based on validation decoding performance. The other settings are summarized in Table K.7. POYO
is evaluated on IBL data only.

TRAINING DETAILS
All baselines are trained with the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) and employ the
same training setup and budgets. The checkpoint selection approach, based on validation metrics, is
also the same across all baselines to ensure fair comparisons. We describe training details for each
baseline below.
NeuroLens.
We train both the single-day and multi-day NeuroLens using a learning rate of 1 ×
10−4 and weight decay 0.05. For IBL, the 39-day model is trained on a single Nvidia A40 GPU in
21.1 hours for a total of 1000 epochs. Single-day models can be trained on a single Nvidia A40 or
A100 GPU in 1.0 ∼2.7 hours, also for 1000 epochs. Stage-2 fine-tuning (behavior and choice, 300
epochs each) requires 1.1 ∼4.0 hours per held-out session on one A40 or A100 GPU. For T12 and
T15 datasets, NeuroLens is trained under all experimental settings on a single Nvidia A100 GPU
(40 GB) for a total of 1000 epochs: approximately 20 hours for the high- vs. low-level decoding
setting and 16 hours for the generalization-across-time setting. Fine-tuning can be performed on a
single A100 or A40 GPU for 500 epochs, requiring approximately 2 ∼3 hours per run for T12 and
6 ∼9 hours for T15. For the ablation experiments, we use one A100 or A40 GPU and complete
each run in 2 ∼9 hours.
Causal NDT.
We train both the single-day and multi-day Causal NDT using the same optimizer
settings as NeuroLens. For IBL, the 39-day model is trained on a single Nvidia A100 GPU in 8.7
hours for a total of 1000 epochs. Single-day models can be trained on a single Nvidia A40 or A100
GPU in 0.5 ∼1.4 hours, also for 1000 epochs. Stage-2 fine-tuning (behavior and choice, 300 epochs
each) requires 1.2 ∼3.8 hours per held-out session on one A40 or A100 GPU. For T12 and T15
datasets, causal NDT is trained under all experimental settings on a single Nvidia A100 GPU (40
GB) for a total of 1000 epochs: approximately 16 hours for the high- vs. low-level decoding setting
and 14 hours for the generalization-across-time setting. Fine-tuning can be performed on a single
A100 or A40 GPU for 500 epochs, requiring approximately 2.4 ∼3 hours per run for T12 and 5.5
∼8 hours for T15; fine-tuning repeats over random seeds fall in the same range (2.4 ∼2.6 hours for
T12, 5.5 ∼6.2 hours for T15).
POSSM.
We train both the single-day and multi-day POSSM using the AdamW optimizer. On
IBL, the 39-day model is pretrained on a 96GB H100 GPU in ∼9.8 hours for a total of 1000 epochs
with early stopping, using batch size 64, peak learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0.05, unit dropout
0.15, gradient clipping at 1, and 15% warmup for the learning rate scheduler. Stage-2 finetuning is
run on a single GPU in ∼1 hour using two sub-stages: (1) unit identification (learning rate 3×10−4,
at most 100 epochs) followed by (2) read-in and readout fine-tuning (learning rate 1×10−5, at most
400 epochs), both with batch size 16 and weight decay 0.1. On T12 and T15 data, POSSM is trained
on a single H100 GPU in ∼2.5 hours for a total of 500 epochs, using AdamW with batch size 32,
peak learning rate 5 × 10−4, minimum learning rate 1 × 10−5, weight decay 10−3, cosine decay
with 2% warmup, and gradient clipping at 10.
SPINT.
On IBL data, SPINT is pretrained for 1000 epochs on the 39 pooled days. Training uses
AdamW optimizer with a OneCycle learning-rate scheduler, with each batch drawn from a sin-
gle day. The Stage-1 checkpoint maximizes the mean per-behavior validation R2, computed from
predictions pooled across the pretraining days’ validation set. Choice cross-entropy contributes to
pretraining but does not enter this model selection criterion. For finetuning, we employ training
batches of 16 trials. The reported choice decoding runs use AdamW optimizer with peak learning
rate 1 × 10−4, weight decay 0.05, a OneCycle schedule with 15% warmup, and gradient clipping at
1.0, for a fixed budget of 500 epochs. Training runs for the full epoch budget without early stopping.
CEBRA.
We train CEBRA using its own solver for Stage 1 and AdamW for Stage 2. On IBL,
Stage 1 is trained on one A40 or A100 GPU in ∼1 hour for a total of 12,000 iterations, on the 39
pooled days, with batch size 512 and learning rate 3 × 10−4. Stage 2 is trained on a single GPU
in 2∼14 minutes per test day per behavior target, attaching a decoder head to the frozen pretrained
encoder and training it together with the held-out day’s own read-in encoder, using learning rate
1×10−3, weight decay 0.05, batch size 16, with early stopping at up to 1000 epochs. Time-bin-level
behavior and choice decoding are independent runs from the same Stage-1 checkpoint, selected on
validation mean R2 and validation balanced accuracy respectively. On T12 ad T15 datasets, CEBRA
follows the shared two-stage training protocol in Table J.1. Stage 1 is trained on one A40 or A100

POYO.
We train POYO using the AdamW optimizer. On IBL data, Stage 1 is trained on one A40
or A100 GPU in 2.8 ∼5.2 hours across runs, for 1000 epochs with early stopping on the 39 pooled
days, using learning rate 3 × 10−4, weight decay 0.05, batch size 64, gradient clipping at max-norm
1.0, and a cosine schedule with 5% warmup. The training objective equally weights time-bin-level
behavior MSE and choice binary cross-entropy. Stage 2 is trained on a single GPU in ∼25 seconds
to 5 minutes per test day per behavior target; finetuning runs are short since they early-stop well
within the epoch budget. We freeze the read-in, backbone, and read-out, training only the held-out
day’s new unit and session embedding rows with a new decoder head, using learning rate 3 × 10−4,
weight decay 0.05, and batch size 16, for at most 500 epochs. Time-bin-level and choice decoding
are independent runs, selected by validation mean R2 and balanced accuracy, respectively.
