ABSTRACT
Extracellular electrophysiology records a different set of neurons in every session.
Neural foundation models embed each neuron and each session into their tokens,
so every new session is an input they have never seen, and they fail to general-
ize to it. A tokenizer for new sessions needs a unit that every session shares and
that carries behavior. Population activity offers such a unit. It evolves on a low-
dimensional manifold that persists across neuronal turnover and across animals
once sessions are aligned. This manifold changes regime at task events such as
stimulus onset and movement onset. Within each regime the population occupies
a state, the part of the manifold it spans between two events, and each state car-
ries its own behavioral meaning. We propose Tokenization with States (TWS),
which segments each trial, one repetition of the task, at these events and converts
every state into tokens of population geometry, with no neuron or session embed-
ding. On held-out International Brain Laboratory (IBL) sessions, TWS decodes
movement even from regime boundaries that carry no information about the tar-
get, while a per-neuron foundation model pretrained on those sessions decodes at
chance. Frozen after training on mice alone, TWS transfers to macaques and Utah
arrays with only a linear probe. On reach direction, a target that its boundaries do
not define, it achieves a Matthews correlation of 0.23, where the event time alone
achieves 0.01. For cross-session generalization, the token matters more than the
model on top.

INTRODUCTION
Every extracellular electrophysiology session records a different set of neurons. Electrode positions
shift between Neuropixels insertions, impedance changes alter unit isolation with Utah arrays, and
spike sorting yields different populations even from identical trajectories (Jun et al., 2017; Dhawale
et al., 2017; Steinmetz et al., 2021). Every new session is therefore out-of-distribution by default.
Neural foundation models are exposed to this shift, because every token they build carries the iden-
tity of one neuron and of the session it came from. Neuron overlap across sessions is typically
zero (Appendix A): no neuron label recurs, so per-neuron tokens form a vocabulary that is entirely
session-specific and collapses on unseen sessions.
A tokenizer for new sessions therefore needs a unit with two properties: (1) a single decoder can
use it in every session, and (2) it carries behavioral meaning. We look for such a unit in population
activity, the joint activity of the recorded neurons. It evolves on a low-dimensional manifold (Cun-
ningham & Yu, 2014; Gallego et al., 2017). This manifold separates into distinct regimes at behav-
ioral events such as movement onset (Vyas et al., 2020; Churchland et al., 2012).1 Elsayed et al.
(2016) showed that at movement onset population activity moves into a subspace orthogonal to the
one it occupied during preparation. (1) The manifold is shared across sessions. The same latent dy-
namics persist across months of neuronal turnover (Gallego et al., 2020) and across animals (Safaie

manifold carries behavior. Within the window between two behavioral events, it tracks what the an-

Language modeling has shown that the definition of a token matters. Whole words form a closed
vocabulary that cannot represent unseen words (Sennrich et al., 2016). Single characters carry little
meaning on their own and produce long sequences. Subword tokenizers such as BPE (Sennrich et al.,
2016) and SentencePiece (Kudo & Richardson, 2018) occupy an intermediate level of granularity.
They learn reusable, semantic units below the level of whole words from the frequency of character
sequences. Neural data show both failure modes. Per-neuron tokens form a closed vocabulary, and
single time bins, like single characters, carry little meaning on their own.
We therefore propose a new tokenizer that converts each behavioral regime of a trial, one repetition
of the task from stimulus to response, into a token of the population manifold: Tokenization with
States (TWS; Figure 1), where a state is the population activity of one behavioral regime. Many
controlled electrophysiology experiments record the time of each task event of a trial, such as stim-
ulus onset and the first movement. TWS segments the trial at these times and converts the activity of
each regime into a few tokens: the subspace that this activity spans, built from all recorded neurons
without their order, so the tokens need no neuron or session embedding. A backbone then mixes
these tokens into a prediction of behavior. What the tokens carry therefore bounds what any back-
bone can decode, and for cross-session generalization the tokenizer decides more than the model on
top.
Contributions.
• Tokens built per behavioral regime rather than per neuron prevent decoding from
collapsing on sessions with new neurons. On unseen IBL sessions TWS decodes move-
ment even when its regime boundaries carry no information about the target, while a per-

their movement labels.

RELATED WORK
Neural foundation models.
Existing multi-session models retain neuron or session identity:
POYO (Azabou et al., 2023), POYO+ (Azabou et al., 2025) and NDT2 (Ye et al., 2023) learn an
embedding for every neuron, session or context, NEDS (Zhang et al., 2025) finetunes on each new
session, and CEBRA (Schneider et al., 2023) needs a model or an input layer for every session;
FALCON (Karpowicz et al., 2024) benchmarks the recalibration this requires. Alignment meth-
ods (Gallego et al., 2020; Degenhart et al., 2020; Karpowicz et al., 2022) fit a transform to every
new session. SPINT (Le et al., 2025) is invariant to the order of the units but infers an identity for
each unit from unlabeled trials of the new session. TWS infers no unit identity and fits no param-
eter to a new session, and its tokens are the states between task events (Appendix D). Appendix C
covers further related work on neural manifolds and their behavioral meaning as well as subword
tokenization and data-driven segmentation of neural states.
3
METHOD
3.1
PROBLEM SETUP
Session s records Ns neurons over T time bins per trial and gives trial activity X(s) ∈RT ×Ns where
both Ns and the identity of the neurons change from session to session. We seek a representation
h ∈Rd of a trial that captures task structure whichever neurons the session records.
Which variables such a representation can carry. Some behavioral variables such as locomotion
and arousal occupy a low-dimensional subspace shared across the population (Stringer et al., 2019;
Musall et al., 2019) and any large subset of neurons carries them. Others such as choice (Steinmetz
et al., 2019) are decoded along directions that differ from session to session. We call the first kind
Type A and the second kind Type B. A representation without neuron identity is designed to preserve
Type A variables, while Type B variables need not survive the removal of neuron identity. TWS
therefore targets Type A variables across sessions (Appendix E).

Model versions. Four versions of one architecture separate what the architecture, pretraining and
behavioral training contribute: TWS (random init), a forward pass with random weights; TWS
pretrained, after self-supervised training that reconstructs masked state means and pushes consec-
utive state subspaces toward orthogonality (Appendix F); TWS from scratch, trained on behavior
alone; and TWS pretrained + finetuned. Every IBL model is scored on 53 held-out sessions (49
drawn at random and the 4 test sessions of NEDS (Zhang et al., 2025)). TWS and PerceiverIO train
without them. The baselines that fit a model to each session fit one to every held-out session by
design.
Evaluation. We decode seven IBL variables (Appendix K, L); the main text reports movement,
reaction time (RT), choice and block, and Appendix M all seven and the within-session scores.
Cross-session scores are leave-one-session-out: a Ridge probe on frozen embeddings, fit on the other
sessions, with no labels and no fitted parameters from the test session. Its unlabeled trials set the per-
neuron z-score, and continuous targets are standardized within each session, which uses the label
statistics of the test session. We report the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC; 0 at chance) for
discrete variables, R2 for continuous ones, and session identifiability MI(h; session)/H(session).
Baselines. POYO (Azabou et al., 2023) stands for per-neuron models; it pretrained on all IBL ses-
sions, the held-out ones included, with an auxiliary loss on movement, wheel velocity and licking.
Supervised CEBRA (Schneider et al., 2023) and NEDS (Zhang et al., 2025) fit one model per ses-
sion, NEDS with its own per-neuron and per-session embeddings. PerceiverIO (Jaegle et al., 2021)
is the POYO tokenizer without neuron or session embeddings on the TWS CNN backbone, trained
from scratch. Aligned PCA rotates each session’s PCA latents onto a shared template with that
session’s unlabeled trials (Appendix D), and PCA without alignment serves as a floor. Population
Statistics summarizes each trial by the mean and spread of its activity, and Event-time information
is the same probe on the first-movement bin alone, with no neural data.
Cross-dataset evaluation. On Steinmetz, Gallego et al. and NLB, TWS and PerceiverIO retain their
IBL weights and only a Ridge classifier is fit, on all sessions of the dataset but the test one. CEBRA
fits one model per session and POYO trains on each dataset (Appendix N). NLB pseudo-sessions
share neurons, so NLB tests transfer across trials only.
4.2
TWS TOKENS DECODE UNSEEN SESSIONS WHERE PER-NEURON TOKENS COLLAPSE
TWS decodes a session it has never seen. TWS leads every baseline on movement and on reaction
time (Table 1), and four baselines enter with an advantage. POYO pretrained on these very sessions
and still predicts the same class for every trial. Finetuning POYO end-to-end on IBL behavior does
not change this (Appendix D). CEBRA trains one model per session and also predicts a constant for
every trial, on the classes and on the continuous targets alike (Appendix O). Aligned PCA rotates
each new session onto a shared template and achieves 62% of the movement decoding of TWS and
5


56% of its reaction-time decoding. TWS fits nothing per session. With regime boundaries that carry
no information about the target, TWS still decodes movement, where POYO, NEDS and CEBRA
decode nothing (Section 4.3). On the five held-out sessions that also record video, TWS decodes
whisker motion, a variable that training never uses, twice as well as PerceiverIO, and no model
decodes pupil diameter across sessions (Appendix P).
Choice and block stay near chance across sessions. Every method, TWS included, stays near
chance on choice and block across sessions (Table 1), as Section 3 predicts for Type B variables
(Appendix E). Models that report these variables above chance on IBL, such as NEDS (Zhang et al.,
2025), finetune on each test session; our cross-session probe fits nothing to the test session. The
NEDS block score is the largest number in the two Type B columns and is still chance: its median
over the 53 sessions is 0.000 and 11% of sessions score above zero (p=0.08, one-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test).
4.3
REGIME BOUNDARIES DECIDE WHAT THE TOKENS CAN HOLD
Table 2 shows that what the tokens hold depends on the number of regime boundaries and on whether
each boundary is aligned with the recorded event time. Moving every boundary by 100 ms reduces
movement decoding by 7% and moving it by 200 ms by 9%, while boundaries that ignore the trial
reduce it by 62% when every trial is segmented at one constant bin and by 70% when each trial is
segmented at a random bin. Training does not repair a wrong segmentation: with those boundaries
40 epochs of behavioral training stay below TWS (random init) with the regime boundaries (Table 1).
Transition tokens between consecutive states change neither number, since the three state subspaces
are already nearly orthogonal (Appendix Q); Appendix R explains why the trial is not segmented
more finely.
Neural activity and the event time. TWS decodes movement from neural activity, not only from
the event time it receives. With boundaries that carry no information about the target (a fixed,
random or shuffled bin), the event time decodes nothing, and TWS retains a third of its movement
decoding. The event time also defines movement and constrains reaction time, and a probe on it
alone achieves movement MCC 0.91 and RT R2 0.44 (Table 2, Event-time information). A large
part of this movement score comes from the 11% of held-out trials in which the mouse moves shortly
before the stimulus. The label counts these trials as trials without a movement, and the event time
identifies them because it falls before the stimulus. Without these trials, TWS achieves 93% of the
movement information in the event time, and PerceiverIO, which receives no event time, achieves
less than half (Appendix R).
4.4
THE TOKENIZER, NOT THE BACKBONE, DETERMINES CROSS-SESSION DECODING ON
IBL
We separate the effect of the tokenizer from the effect of the backbone. Two tokenizers, TWS
and PerceiverIO (Jaegle et al., 2021) (the POYO tokenizer without neuron or session embeddings),
meet six backbones: pooling, MLP, CNN, Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), Mamba-1 (Gu & Dao,
2024) and Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024). Every combination trains from scratch, because PerceiverIO
has no self-supervised objective (Table 3).
Backbones that mix the tokens differ from one another by at most 5% in movement decoding, while
removing the mixing reduces it by 18%; their transfer to the other datasets is in Appendix S. Swap-
ping TWS for PerceiverIO halves decoding with the same backbone. The embeddings of the two
tokenizers also differ in what they spend their variance on. Session identity takes 31% of the Per-
ceiverIO embedding and movement 1%, against 23% and 11% for TWS (Figure 2B; Appendix T).
The backbone is interchangeable; the tokenizer is not.

4.7
BEHAVIOR AND SESSION IDENTITY OCCUPY DIFFERENT DIRECTIONS
In TWS, session identity and behavior occupy different directions of the embedding. Deleting the
directions that separate sessions leaves movement decoding almost unchanged (0.56 against 0.55).
Identifying the session from the two behavior decoding directions works no better than from two
random directions (Appendix Z). The session information that remains therefore does not interfere
with decoding. The other methods fail in two ways (Figure 3A). POYO and PCA identify the
session of almost every trial and decode movement at chance, and CEBRA reduces session identity
and loses behavior with it. TWS retains behavior with half the session information of POYO and
less than PerceiverIO with the same backbone, at the laboratory level as well as the session level
(Appendices AA, AB and AC). On Steinmetz, a dataset it never saw, the frozen TWS retains a third
of the session information of per-neuron features (Appendix AD).
4.8
WITH LABELS FROM FIVE SESSIONS, TWS OUTPERFORMS BASELINES TRAINED ON
ALL OF THEM
A decoder for a new laboratory has to be fit on sessions with behavioral labels, and TWS needs
few of them. Figure 3B shows reaction-time decoding against the number of sessions the probe
is fit on. With five sessions, TWS from scratch decodes reaction time five times better than the
best baseline of Figure 3B fit on every session. It also exceeds aligned PCA fit on every session
by 25% (Table 1), and PerceiverIO on the same five sessions fourfold (Appendix AE). Across six
models, this score follows how consistently the probe direction points across sessions, not session
information (Appendix AE).
5
DISCUSSION
We propose TWS, a tokenizer that turns the population activity between task events into tokens
without neuron or session embeddings. On unseen sessions TWS decodes where per-neuron and
per-session models decode at chance. Frozen after training on mice, TWS transfers to macaques
and Utah arrays with a linear probe fitted on each dataset, including reach direction, a target that
its boundaries do not define. The design sets the limits, and they are the price of the invariance. A
tokenizer without neuron identity need not preserve a variable whose decoding direction differs from
session to session. Averaging inside a regime gives up the time course within it, and TWS needs the
event times that a task records. Free behavior therefore requires a tokenizer that finds the regimes in
9


the data itself. TWS is a practical model for the electrophysiology datasets most laboratories hold.
It fits nothing per session, it asks only for the event times a task already records, and a probe fit on a
few sessions with behavioral labels generalizes to the remaining sessions.

NEURON IDENTITY IS SESSION IDENTITY
Neuron identity is session identity (Section 1) because of how extracellular electrophysiology works.
Each session records its own set of neurons, and the labels that name them never carry over to another
session. We verify this on the IBL Brain Wide Map and on the Gallego et al. data.
No neuron label recurs. A per-neuron tokenizer keys its vocabulary on neuron labels. In IBL these
labels never recur. Each session is an acute recording with its own probe insertion, so most sessions
also record different cells. We compare the probe tracks of the 361 pairs of sessions from the same
subject, using the Allen CCF coordinates of every channel from the aligned histology. Of these, 342
pairs (94.7%) never come within 100 µm of each other, with a median closest approach of 1.2 mm,
and 19 pairs do. The labels do not match even for those 19 pairs. Spike sorting (Kilosort (Pachitariu
et al., 2024)) runs on each session separately and assigns session-local cluster indices, and no registry
links clusters across sessions. Across all

Chronic recordings. With chronically implanted arrays, such as the Utah arrays of the Gallego et
al. and NLB data, some cells are recorded again on later days. The data do not track them. All 111
sessions of the Gallego et al. data number their units from 0 within the session and carry no field that
links a unit to another session. Dedicated tracking can follow part of the cells (Dhawale et al., 2017;
Steinmetz et al., 2021), but it is rarely available and the tracked set shrinks as the recorded population
turns over (Gallego et al., 2020). For the data these models receive, the per-neuron vocabulary is
therefore session-specific. The population manifold persists through the turnover (Gallego et al.,
2020), and that is what TWS encodes.
Neuron count. Every one of the 243 sessions has at least 128 units that pass quality control, and
we keep 128 per session (max units), so in IBL the dimensionality of the raw neural space is the
same for every session while its axes are unrelated neurons. The count does vary on the Gallego et
al. data (18 to 128 units per session, median 64, after the same cap).
Implication. A model that represents a trial as a vector in neuron-indexed space (RNs) cannot
compare two sessions. Two sessions with N1̸ = N2 live in spaces of different dimension. Even
when N1 = N2, the axes correspond to unrelated neurons. Per-neuron tokenization, as in POYO
and NEDS, answers this with learned neuron embeddings and per-session adaptation, which write
neuron identity into the representation. TWS summarizes each state over an unordered set of neurons
instead, which removes neuron identity by construction. Session identity is a different matter. TWS
reduces it and does not remove it: trained TWS embeddings still carry 0.49 of the session entropy
(Figure 3A).

REGIME BOUNDARIES VS. EVENT SEGMENTATION THEORY
We use “regime boundary” rather than “event boundary” to distinguish TWS’s boundaries from
those in Event Segmentation Theory (Zacks et al., 2007). EST boundaries are observer-internal,
driven by prediction error, and hierarchically organized; TWS boundaries are task-defined tran-
sitions (stimulus onset set by the task, movement onset measured from behavior) that demarcate
distinct computational regimes. The two concepts overlap (both mark qualitative changes in pro-
cessing) but differ in origin: exogenous task structure versus endogenous perceptual inference.
C
EXTENDED RELATED WORK
Subword tokenization as an analogy. In NLP, subword methods such as BPE (Sennrich et al.,
2016) and SentencePiece (Kudo & Richardson, 2018) solve the open-vocabulary problem by learn-
ing reusable units below the level of whole words, from how often character sequences occur. TWS
draws an analogy. Regime boundaries define units between a single time bin and a whole trial. Ev-
ery such unit marks the same stage of the task in every session, whichever neurons were recorded.
BPE merges character pairs by corpus frequency; TWS segments at task events, stimulus onset and
movement onset. The boundaries of a TWS token therefore carry domain knowledge rather than a
statistical regularity. The Grassmannian projection that follows is the neural analogue of a shared
14


embedding table: it maps variable-length and variable-neuron states into a fixed geometry that car-
ries no neuron identity and less session identity than per-neuron representations.
Behavioral meaning of the manifold. Population dynamics change regime at task events such as
movement onset (Vyas et al., 2020; Churchland et al., 2012) and move between distinct subspaces
such as the orthogonal preparatory and movement subspaces of motor cortex (Elsayed et al., 2016).
The manifold follows the task and not the animal. One monkey performing two related tasks pro-
duces less similar latent dynamics than two monkeys performing the same task (Safaie et al., 2023).
The manifold carries this meaning before the animal moves: while a monkey only plans a reach, its
dynamics already predict which target it will reach for (Safaie et al., 2023).
Grassmannian and subspace methods. Grassmannian manifolds (spaces of linear subspaces) have
a long history in computer vision for subspace tracking and video-based recognition, where each
video clip is represented as a point on Gr(k, d) and geodesic distances provide an appearance-
invariant similarity measure. In neuroscience, subspace overlap metrics (principal angles, canonical
correlations) are standard tools for quantifying representational similarity across conditions or ses-
sions (Elsayed et al., 2016). TWS uses the Grassmannian not as an analysis tool but as an archi-
tectural bottleneck: projecting each state onto Gr(kdim, d) with kdim=2 forces the representation
through a two-dimensional subspace per state. The unordered aggregation removes neuron identity
and the narrow bottleneck limits how much session-specific detail passes through. The Grassman-
nian is one lightweight way to impose this bottleneck.
Neural state segmentation. Hidden Markov models (HMMs) and switching linear dynamical sys-
tems have been used to discover latent neural states from continuous recordings (Linderman et al.,
2017). These methods learn state boundaries from data. That serves exploratory analysis, but it does
not serve cross-session transfer: boundaries learned on one neuron set need not align with those
learned on another. TWS takes task-defined regime boundaries instead, stimulus onset and move-
ment onset, which mark the same stage of the task in every session. The price is that TWS needs
trial-structured data with known event times.

D
ALIGNMENT-BASED BASELINES VERSUS TOKENIZATION
Contrastive embeddings such as CEBRA (Schneider et al., 2023) are trained on each session and
compared afterwards. Manifold-alignment methods (Gallego et al., 2020; Degenhart et al., 2020;
Karpowicz et al., 2022) map a new session’s latent trajectories onto a reference with a transform
fitted to that session’s data. Both buy invariance by fitting something to every new session. TWS
has it by construction. Each token is a basis vector of a subspace that attention over the unordered
neurons computes from the state’s population activity, and no permutation of the neurons changes
it. The task-defined boundaries then make the k-th token stand for the same computational regime
in every session. A new session needs no fitting and no alignment data. The same forward pass
encodes it, even at random initialization (Table 1). The price is that TWS needs the event times that
define the boundaries, which alignment methods do not.
The baselines in Table 1 differ in what they fit to each session. CEBRA trains one supervised and
one self-supervised encoder per session, 486 fits of 5,000 iterations for 243 sessions. POYO learns
an embedding for every neuron and every session during pretraining, over all IBL sessions, so its
test-session embeddings were fitted to those sessions’ data. TWS fits nothing per session. Under
the same leave-one-session-out probe it decodes cross-session movement at MCC 0.565 and RT
at R2=0.402, and at random initialization still at 0.274 and 0.083, while CEBRA and POYO stay
at chance on both targets (Table 1). Per-session fitting makes the embedding space of one session
internally consistent and leaves the axes of two sessions unrelated, which is what a shared linear
probe cannot use. TWS makes the axes comparable before any probe runs. Finetuning POYO
end-to-end on IBL choice and RT does not change this. Its embeddings still decode cross-session
movement at the majority rate (accuracy 0.748, MCC 0.018; RT R2=0.036; one model, scored on
all 243 sessions).
A label-free alignment baseline. We implement alignment in the one form that runs on an unseen
session. Per session, the log-transformed, z-scored spike counts are reduced to 10 principal com-
ponents. Each session’s trial-averaged latent trajectory, over all trials and aligned to the stimulus,
15


is rotated onto a common template by orthogonal Procrustes, and generalized Procrustes over all
sessions builds that template. Single trials are then projected, rotated and averaged in ten 200 ms
windows. The procedure uses no labels, and the held-out session enters through its own unlabeled
trials alone. Under the probe of Table 1, aligned PCA decodes movement at MCC 0.349 and RT at
R2=0.226, and choice and block at chance. Without the rotation the same features decode neither
target (0.002 and 0.003), so the rotation is what makes PCA transfer. TWS exceeds aligned PCA by
0.22 in movement MCC and 0.18 in RT R2 and fits nothing to the new session (Table 27).

EMPIRICAL VALIDATION OF THE TYPE A / TYPE B DISTINCTION
A variable is Type A when it survives a change of the recorded neurons. We measure that inside a
session, before any model enters. The neurons of a session go into two random halves. Every trial
of a half is summarized by statistics that do not depend on which neuron is which: the mean, the
standard deviation and the 10, 25, 50, 75 and 90% quantiles over neurons, in five windows of the
trial. A decoder is fit on the summaries of one half and scored on the summaries of the other, with
the trials cross-validated, so training and test differ in both trials and neurons. The index divides
this cross-half score by the score of a decoder that sees the full neuron vector of the same trials.
It therefore reports the share of the decodable signal that survives the loss of neuron identity, not
the strength of that signal. Every neuron is z-scored within its session first, as in TWS, and the
summaries of each half are standardized over the trials of that half, without labels. Ten neuron splits
and five folds per session, over 242 IBL sessions, 39 Steinmetz sessions, 81 Gallego et al. sessions
and 23 NLB pseudo-sessions. The index needs 60 trials for its folds and 10 trials in the smallest
class, which drops one IBL session of 50 trials. Reach direction exists only in the 84 center-out
sessions of Gallego et al., since the random-target sessions draw a new target on every trial; three of
those 84 fall below the trial or neuron minimum.

Geometry-only pretraining. Pretraining sets up the geometry of the token space before behavior
is learned. Preparatory and movement-epoch activity occupy orthogonal subspaces (Elsayed et al.,
2016), so we push the subspaces of consecutive states apart and let behavioral training fill a space
that already has this structure; Section 4.6 shows that it adds session invariance rather than accuracy.

LMSE: Mean squared error between decoded state tokens and per-state neuron mean activities. Ldiv:
Chordal distance (squared sine of principal angles between consecutive subspaces) plus pairwise
cosine repulsion across all state token pairs, jointly encouraging manifold separation. Lgeo: Negative
geodesic distance on the Grassmannian between adjacent state pairs (Sk, Sk+1), encouraging near-
orthogonality between temporally neighboring regimes, the prior stated above.
State masking pretext. During pretraining, we randomly mask one of K states (replacing its tokens
with a learnable mask embedding) in 50% of trials. The model must reconstruct the masked state
from the remaining states, learning inter-state relationships.
Behavioral finetuning. After pretraining, we add one linear head per token for each classification
task, with the logits averaged over tokens, and regression heads on the mean token ¯h = mean(h) ∈
Rd, and finetune on behavioral labels. Models are evaluated both within-session (same session,
held-out trials) and cross-session (test sessions never seen during training).
Table 6 lists the settings of every training stage. All stages use AdamW; IBL pretraining and behav-
ioral training clip gradients at norm 1. Each session enters with at most 128 units. In IBL we keep
the units that pass the IBL quality label and take the 128 with the most spikes over the session; every
IBL session has at least 128 such units. In the other datasets we keep the 128 units with the largest
variance and fill the remaining slots with zeros when a session has fewer. Pretraining further keeps
IBL units that pass firing-rate, presence-ratio and contamination thresholds, cuts at the knee of their
spike counts and keeps the 40% whose binned counts are closest to Gaussian, a selection inspired
by Bae et al. (2026). No unit selection uses behavioral labels.
Learned offsets. The offsets of Eq. 1 are trained with the other weights and bounded by a sigmoid to
∆1 ∈[5, 25] and ∆2 ∈[0, 20] bins. In the five TWS from scratch models of Table 1 they converge to
∆1 = 14.6–15.6 bins (292–311 ms after the stimulus onset) and ∆2 = 1.3–3.0 bins (26–60 ms after
the first movement). ∆1 lands after the stimulus-evoked transient and ∆2 almost on the movement
itself; we read these as the delays at which each regime settles, not as a measurement of them. The
architecture is the same in every stage: d=256, K=3 states with kdim=2, gate temperature γ=2
bins, 4 cross-attention heads, at most 128 neurons and T=100 bins of 20 ms; the backbone has 4
blocks, each a layer normalization, a kernel-3 convolution over the 6 tokens, GELU and dropout 0.1,
followed by a feed-forward layer.

BACKBONE ARCHITECTURE
The backbone receives the six state tokens and nothing else. TWS has already averaged activity
within each state (Algorithm 1, Step 2), so the backbone mixes a short sequence of state summaries
rather than time bins. Any sequence model can fill that role (Section 4.4). We use a convolutional
mixer by default: four blocks of a kernel-3 convolution over the six state tokens, with d=256. It is the
simplest backbone that mixes the tokens at all, and it leads the Mamba and Transformer backbones
by at most 0.03 in movement MCC (Table 3). Backbones that mix the tokens decode within 0.03
MCC of each other. They differ in how well they transfer to other datasets (Appendix S).

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Permutation invariance: proof and check. Let Π permute the Ns recorded neurons and apply
it to the activity X and to the coordinates {pn}. Then every TWS token is unchanged. Proof.
Normalization, fact and fcoord act on each neuron alone and so commute with Π. The cross-attention
returns a softmax-weighted sum over the neurons with no positional encoding over them and so
ignores their order. Gram-Schmidt then acts on the kdim attention outputs in a fixed order. □The
backbone sees the six tokens in a fixed order so predictions and distances between tokens inherit
the invariance. On five held-out sessions and 20 random permutations each the tokens of a trained
model agree to within 4 × 10−6 of their scale.
Scope of the permutation invariance. Each token summarizes the mean activity of every neuron
within the state. The invariance holds for the order of the neurons. Recording different neurons
changes the activity that enters the aggregation. The aggregation has no padding mask, so in a
session with fewer than 128 units the zero-filled slots enter the attention and the tokens depend on
the number of recorded units; every IBL session has 128. The backbone receives the orthonormal
basis that Gram-Schmidt returns, a point on the Stiefel manifold whose span is the Grassmannian
point. The basis therefore enters the representation alongside its span.
18


Atlas coordinates. In the neuron feature computation un = fcoord(pn) + fact(¯xk,n), atlas coordi-
nates (pn ∈R3) are missing for 230 of the 243 IBL sessions, so fcoord reduces to one learned bias
shared by all neurons. In the other 230 sessions every IBL result therefore comes from fact alone:
the tokens line up across sessions from per-state mean firing rates, with no anatomical identity.
When atlas coordinates are available, fcoord provides an additional anatomical prior.

between the per-trial Grassmannian bases of the pretrained model (before behavioral finetuning)

between consecutive pairs is encouraged by Lgeo (Section 3), but the partial structure between non-
adjacent states and the per-state dimensionality differences emerge without direct supervision.
Dimensionality differs across states. The participation ratio (PR, effective dimensionality of each
state’s representation) is 1.3 before the stimulus, 2.5 between the stimulus and the movement and
1.3 after the movement (Figure 4B), so the state that the two boundaries enclose is the one that
spreads over more directions. Reaction time follows the same order. Within a session, no principal
direction of the pre-stimulus state correlates with reaction time (|r| ≤0.03). The third direction
of the stimulus-to-movement state achieves |r|=0.26 and the third direction of the post-movement
state |r|=0.49. Movement follows the same order over the three states: the largest correlation any
direction of a state achieves is 0.08, 0.26 and 0.32. The states that the movement boundary delimits
therefore carry the timing, which is what TWS tokens keep across sessions (Section 4.2).

REGIME BOUNDARY INFORMATION PATHWAY ANALYSIS
Why two boundaries. The IBL protocol records five event times per trial, but only stimulus onset
and first movement fall reliably inside the analysis window and mark a clear shift in population
dynamics (Churchland et al., 2012). Cutting the trial more finely than these two events would also
leak the labels: a narrow window placed after the first movement stays empty whenever the mouse
does not move, and its emptiness states the movement label.
Regime boundary tokenization raises one concern: the state tokens h could pass the boundary time
bmove straight to the decoder and carry no neural content. Reaction time satisfies RT ∝bmove−bstim
with bstim constant, so a model that recovers bmove from h predicts RT without decoding anything
neural.
We decompose the information in TWS state tokens into two pathways: (i) an architectural tim-
ing prior arising from the interaction between the gate window shape and generic neural temporal
structure, and (ii) trial-specific neural content within each epoch.
Null result: constant activity carries zero boundary information.

Event-time information. The decomposition above is within-session and restricted to trials with
a first movement. To measure how much of each label the event time holds in the cross-session
setting of Table 1, we fit the same leave-one-session-out Ridge probe on a one-hot encoding of the
first-movement bin alone, with no neural data. It scores movement MCC 0.908 and RT R2=0.443,
against 0.565 and 0.402 for TWS trained from scratch (Table 1). Movement is defined by that time:
a first movement 0–2 s after stimulus onset (Table 10). RT runs from stimulus onset to the IBL
response time, which follows the first movement by a median of 0.19 s. The boundary, in contrast, is
the first-movement time wherever it falls. In 11% of the held-out trials the mouse moves within 0.5 s
before the stimulus (median 64 ms): the label is then 0 and the boundary falls before the stimulus.

boundary means a movement” scores 0.56. We therefore score the saved embeddings of Table 1
again without these trials (Table 16). TWS does not read the label from them: without them it
rises from 0.565 to 0.723, close to the 0.776 that the event time then holds, whereas PerceiverIO
changes little. The boundary-placement ablation adds that TWS keeps about a third of its movement
decoding when the boundary holds no label information (Table 2b).

BACKBONE SWEEP
We replace the backbone while keeping TWS, the pretraining and finetuning recipe, and the evalua-
tion fixed: mean pooling followed by layer normalization (no token mixing), an MLP on the pooled
tokens, a CNN (a kernel-3 convolution over the six tokens in each of four blocks), a Transformer,
Mamba-1 and Mamba-2. Table 3 gives cross-session decoding on IBL for each of them. Decoding
on IBL is the same within 0.03 MCC for every backbone that mixes the tokens. Transfer to the
other datasets differs between backbones. On Steinmetz and NLB we compare backbones trained
on all 243 IBL sessions. From the same TWS tokens, the convolutional backbone achieves Stein-
metz choice 0.155 ± 0.006 and NLB movement 0.378 ± 0.008, and the Mamba backbones land
within 0.03 of it (Mamba-1 over 4 seeds). The Transformer falls behind on both (0.122 ± 0.054
and 0.299 ± 0.060), with seed-to-seed spreads an order of magnitude wider. On reach direction,
scored with the held-out models, the Transformer (0.201 ± 0.006) stays close to the convolutional
backbone (0.232 ± 0.010). Figures 5, 6 and 7 show the representation of every tokenizer, back-
bone and training combination: its session means, the composition of its variance, and how similar
the combinations are to one another. Every trained TWS combination separates the two movement
states more than every trained PerceiverIO one (1.03–1.88 against 0.48–0.72), and the tokenizer
also decides how similar two embeddings are. Among the four token-mixing backbones trained
from scratch, the TWS embeddings agree with one another at a linear CKA of 0.87–0.92 and the
PerceiverIO embeddings at 0.44–0.64, while a TWS embedding and a PerceiverIO embedding agree
at 0.20–0.32. Backbone and tokenizer trade off in the variance as well: with TWS tokens the con-
volutional backbone keeps 23% of the variance between sessions and the Transformer 18%, against
31% and 28% for the same two backbones on PerceiverIO tokens.

SESSION DIRECTIONS AND BEHAVIOR DECODING DIRECTIONS
We ask whether the directions that separate sessions overlap the directions a linear probe uses to
decode behavior. We work on the z-scored trial embeddings of each model, first model seed. One
pooled linear regression first removes the part of every embedding that movement and reaction time
explain, so that sessions differing only in behavior, a slower mouse for instance, do not define the
session directions. The session directions are the top principal directions of the 243 session means
of this residual, as many as explain half of their variance (k). The behavior decoding directions are
the weights of Ridge probes for movement and for reaction time, fit on the 190 training sessions.
Table 28 reports two tests on the 53 held-out sessions. In the first, projecting out the k session
directions leaves movement decoding almost unchanged for every model, −3% for TWS and for
PerceiverIO, and projecting out k random directions changes nothing. Reaction time overlaps more
with the session directions: TWS loses 37% and PerceiverIO 17%. What TWS keeps still exceeds
everything PerceiverIO decodes. In the second test, the two behavior decoding directions identify
the session of a trial no better than two random directions. Session information therefore stays out
of the behavior decoding directions, and it is not fully separable from reaction time. The regression
step matters for this conclusion. Without it the session directions also carry the behavior differences
between sessions, and projecting them out costs TWS 18% of movement and 59% of reaction-time
decoding.
