Abstract
Cephalonauts One is a whole-brain 3 Tesla (3T) functional magnetic resonance
imaging (fMRI) dataset recorded while subjects listened to audio podcasts. Three
healthy subjects underwent multiple scanning sessions, each consisting of five
15-minute runs, while listening to podcasts in their native language. With 30
hours of fMRI data per subject, the current release is the deepest available fMRI
dataset using naturalistic speech stimuli. The dataset pairs brain activity with
the corresponding podcast audio, transcript annotations, and derived stimulus
embeddings. Furthermore, we introduce a brain decoding benchmark formulated
as audio segment retrieval: given fMRI activity from a held-out session, the
decoder must identify the corresponding time-aligned podcast audio segment
among candidate segments. We provide standardized splits, evaluation metrics,
and baseline decoders for this task. Finally, a scaling analysis shows that decoding
performance improves continuously with the amount of training data per subject.

Introduction
Brain-behavior links The human brain can process highly complex perceptual inputs but also
produce rich, intentional, and reflective thoughts. Extensive research has been devoted to probing,
measuring, and modelling this complex biological system, in the hope of gaining clinical insights
into brain disorders, inspiring the design of more intelligent artificial systems, and better grasping
the nature of human cognition [Mesulam, 1998]. One way of gaining information about the brain
is to link its properties – e.g., anatomical, functional, or biological – to properties of the external
world – e.g., a list of tasks an individual is able or unable to perform, or the category of stimuli
they are exposed to. Famous historical approaches demonstrated that lesions in specific frontal and
temporal regions could impair speech and comprehension, yielding early insights into the functional
organization of the language system [Broca, 1861, Wernicke, 1874].

Brain encoding and decoding More recently, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) [Huet-
tel et al., 2004] enabled non-invasive measurement of brain activity in healthy subjects. Its higher
spatial and temporal resolution compared with positron emission tomography (PET) made it possible
to characterize brain responses in greater detail. This led to early statistical analyses showing that
some brain regions respond selectively to specific stimulus categories [Kanwisher et al., 1997].
To identify regions where these effects cannot necessarily be observed so clearly, a large body
of work has predicted recorded brain activity from known stimulus features, a process known as
encoding. Encoding models characterize how stimulus features are represented across the cortex,
from retinotopic organization in early visual areas [Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008] to semantic maps
estimated from fMRI recorded while participants listened to long natural narratives [Huth et al.,
2016]. In parallel, many studies have attempted to infer properties of the stimulus from recorded
brain activity, a process known as decoding. In language neuroscience, recent decoding approaches
have moved beyond coarse stimulus labels by mapping non-invasive brain recordings to rich speech
representations. Jalouzot et al. [2025] showed that fMRI decoding of natural speech improves with
additional subject-specific data, a finding consistent with related speech-decoding results in magneto-
and electroencephalography (M/EEG) [Défossez et al., 2023].
Stimulus embeddings In fact, brain encoding and decoding can be viewed as two sides of the same
coin, as both aim to link properties of brain activity to properties of the stimulus. While early work
focused on relatively low-dimensional variables such as stimulus category or the presence of cognitive
impairments, the advent of deep learning has made it possible to access high-level high-dimensional
representations of the stimulus—so-called latent representations or embeddings—that can be used
in both encoding [Eickenberg et al., 2017] and decoding analyses [Défossez et al., 2023]. In both
settings, these representations support increasingly expressive models with greater predictive power
[d’Ascoli et al., 2026], opening new perspectives for understanding brain function.
Natural speech Language is a particularly compelling stimulus, spanning multiple levels from
acoustics to semantics and narrative context. Spoken narratives are especially useful for studying
these representations in the brain: they engage distributed cortical systems [Huth et al., 2016]
over multiple timescales [Lerner et al., 2011], while remaining easy to align with transcripts and
computational representations from modern large language models (LLMs) [Zhang et al., 2025, Khare
et al., 2025]. Prior work used brain responses to compare natural language processing embeddings,
showing that they capture different aspects of language [Toneva and Wehbe, 2019]. Other studies
have shown that modern models partly match human brain responses [Caucheteux and King, 2022], or
used controlled models to separate the effects of meaning, syntax, and context in the cortex [Pasquiou
et al., 2023]. Together, these results show that fMRI during speech listening can help study both
biological and computational language representations.

Dataset depth However, fMRI with speech stimuli remains constrained by limited per-participant
data. This is limiting for subject-specific decoders, for which performance improves with additional
within-subject data in both natural speech and visual fMRI decoding [Tang et al., 2023, Scotti et al.,
2023]. Indeed, large-scale analyses further indicate that decoding scales primarily with data per
subject [Banville et al., 2025]. Existing speech fMRI resources address different regimes: Narratives
provides up to roughly 2 hours of unique stimuli per participant [Nastase et al., 2021], whereas
LeBel et al. [2023] provide up to 16 hours for 3 participants. Hence, deep per-subject language fMRI
datasets for natural speech encoding and decoding remain scarce.

Related work
Early scaling of naturalistic fMRI datasets Public neural datasets have been central to progress
in brain encoding and decoding. Early scaling was driven by fMRI datasets based on visual stimuli.
Indeed, early decoding datasets contained roughly 1.5–3 stimulus hours per subject [Wen et al.,
2017, Chang et al., 2019]. Then, the Natural Scenes Dataset increased visual stimuli to 40 hours
per subject [Allen et al., 2022]. More recently, the Courtois NeuroMod datasets pushed further
with roughly 80 hours of movie watching per subject [Boyle et al., 2025]. This scale has supported
advances in visual encoding and decoding, including natural-image reconstruction from fMRI with
deep learning models [Chen et al., 2022, Ozcelik and VanRullen, 2023, Scotti et al., 2023].
Speech stimuli in fMRI datasets: breadth and depth However, speech fMRI datasets have often
scaled across rather than within participants. Narratives aggregates story-listening fMRI from
345 participants, with roughly 4.6 hours of unique audio stimuli across the full dataset [Nastase
et al., 2021], while Le Petit Prince provides multilingual audiobook fMRI from 112 subjects, with
approximately 1.7 hours per subject [Li et al., 2022]. Shared stimuli support group analyses and cross-
subject comparisons, but limited per-subject depth constrains subject-specific decoders. Recently,
LeBel et al. [2023] went deeper with natural story-listening fMRI from 8 participants, including an
extended subset of up to 16 hours of podcast listening for 3 participants. Using these data, Jalouzot
et al. [2025] show that natural speech decoding improves with more training data per participant.
Benchmark-ready datasets In an effort to make brain encoding and decoding more reproducible
and accessible, recent datasets provide standardized tasks, code, and baseline models. The Algonauts
Project 2025 Challenge [Gifford et al., 2024] turns CNeuroMod movie fMRI data into an encoding
benchmark with private in-distribution and out-of-distribution test sets, public leaderboards, and a
baseline model. Similar releases exist for other recording modalities: LibriBrain [Özdogan et al.,
2025] provides 52 hours from one subject, standardized splits, public code, and baseline speech-
decoding tasks. Together, such datasets support model comparison and progress tracking.
Remaining gap fMRI provides whole-brain spatial coverage and a better decoding-performance-to-
stimulus-duration ratio than M/EEG [Banville et al., 2025]. However, as summarized in Table 1, brain
decoding still lacks fMRI datasets that are both deep at the subject level and released as ready-to-use
benchmarks. Such datasets should provide aligned stimulus representations, held-out evaluation
sessions, standardized metrics, and reproducible baseline decoders.
Contributions We introduce Cephalonauts One, a deep per-subject dataset for benchmarking
decoding models of naturalistic speech from fMRI. The main contributions are:

Stimuli Subjects listened to Les Pieds sur Terre1, a popular French podcast produced by the public
radio station France Culture that features real-life stories told by ordinary people. Episodes are
approximately 30 minutes long and center on first-person true stories about themes such as everyday
life, relationships, work, personal turning points, or social change. Each episode typically features a
single main speaker, who narrates a personal experience in rich and diverse language. Although the
same journalist generally introduces the episodes, the story itself is usually delivered as a monologue
by the featured speaker. The narration is often interspersed with short musical interludes.
3.2
Brain activity recordings
MRI acquisition Data were acquired at the Paris Brain Institute on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM
Cima.X whole-body MRI scanner equipped with a 64-channel head/neck coil. Functional images
were collected with a highly accelerated 2D gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence
using simultaneous multi-slice acquisition with multiband factor 6, yielding a repetition time (TR)
of 520 ms. The protocol was designed to provide high temporal resolution while maintaining
whole-brain coverage. Functional images were acquired at 3 mm isotropic resolution. Each run
contained ∼1700 functional brain volumes. High-resolution anatomical images were acquired with a
3D T1-weighted spoiled gradient-echo Dixon sequence at 0.8 mm isotropic resolution. The same
acquisition protocol was used across all sessions and subjects. Each subject underwent 30 hours
of fMRI scanning, yielding ∼207,000 brain volumes. Additional sequence details are provided in
Appendix S1.3, including the core acquisition parameters summarized in Table S1.
Preprocessing Functional images were preprocessed with DeepPrep [Ren et al., 2025], a fully
automated fMRI preprocessing pipeline that includes standard spatial preprocessing steps such as
motion correction, spatial normalization, and anatomical-to-functional alignment. DeepPrep uses
FreeSurfer [Fischl, 2012] for anatomical processing, including cortical surface reconstruction and
segmentation. As summarized in Table 3, we release the resulting data in three complementary
spaces: subject-native T1w space, MNI152NLin6Asym space, and the fsaverage5 cortical surface.
These representations support different use cases: subject-native T1w space preserves individual
anatomy for within-subject analyses, MNI space facilitates cross-subject comparisons and compatibil-
ity with standard atlases, and fsaverage5 focuses the input on cortical gray matter while reducing
dimensionality. Broad cortical representations are particularly relevant for natural speech fMRI,
since semantic responses during story listening are distributed across large regions of cortex [Huth
et al., 2016]. Surface projections were computed from each subject’s anatomical scan to ensure
anatomically consistent mapping from volumetric data to cortex. For the volumetric T1w and MNI
spaces, we apply the corresponding brain mask before export, so each run is stored as a lighter and
more decoding-ready two-dimensional time-by-voxel array rather than as a 4D image; the masks are
also released to map these arrays back to brain space. For decoding analyses, each voxel or vertex

3.3
Stimulus
Stimulus preprocessing For each run, we release the original stimulus in two modalities: the raw
audio signal and its transcript. Word-level transcripts were generated automatically with AssemblyAI
Universal 3 Pro. We converted the word-level transcript into a sequence of regularly sampled
stimulus onsets spaced every 4 TRs, corresponding to 2.08 seconds. At each onset, we extracted the
immediately preceding context by collecting the last 20 completed words whose offsets occurred
before that time, and used their concatenation as the text stimulus. For the audio modality, we used
the same retained onsets and represented each event by the 10-second audio segment ending at that
onset. Early onsets with insufficient preceding context were discarded. As summarized in Table 4,
we release both these segment-level data and the corresponding embeddings.
Stimulus embeddings We embedded text segments with Qwen3-Embedding-8B and audio segments
with LCO-Embedding-Omni-7B, yielding 4096-dimensional and 3584-dimensional representations,
respectively [Zhang et al., 2025, Xiao et al., 2025]. This choice is motivated by the fact that they are
leading models on their respective benchmarks: MTEB multilingual for text [Muennighoff et al.,
2023] and MAEB for audio [Assadi et al., 2026]. These benchmarks evaluate general-purpose
embedding spaces across diverse retrieval and similarity tasks, matching our use of text and audio
embeddings as targets for held-out segment retrieval.
Alignment to fMRI Each retained stimulus event was paired with the corresponding fMRI measure-
ment by selecting the brain frame acquired after the event, accounting for the hemodynamic lag, set
to 2.5 seconds. This fixed lag approximates the delay between stimulus processing and the measured
BOLD response. This procedure yields aligned pairs of brain volumes and stimulus embeddings for
any machine learning tasks, as illustrated in Figure 2.A.

Decoding experiments
Cephalonauts One provides a benchmark for decoding naturalistic speech from fMRI. The task
is formulated as audio segment retrieval: given brain activity from a held-out session, the model
must identify the corresponding podcast audio segment among all other possible segments from that
session. This task fixes the evaluation target while leaving the decoding approach open: models may
use any stimuli representation, decoder architecture, or training objective, provided they are evaluated
on the same held-out retrieval problem. The proposed baseline predicts embeddings of the stimulus’
raw audio and transcript from fMRI activity and ranks candidate segments by cosine similarity in that
embedding space. Figure 2 presents the overall framework.

Baseline decoder We provide a simple decoder baseline for this retrieval task. The decoder is a one-
hidden-layer neural network trained to predict a stimulus embedding from brain activity. Each target
embedding is defined as the concatenation of the text and audio embeddings described in Section 3.3.
We train the decoder with AdamW [Kingma and Ba, 2017, Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017] using the
CLIP contrastive loss [Radford et al., 2021] with temperature 0.02, following recent brain decoding
setups [Scotti et al., 2023, Jalouzot et al., 2025]. Training uses early stopping on the validation
Median Relative Rank, with patience of five epochs, and the best validation checkpoint is used for
test evaluation. At evaluation time, candidate segments are ranked by cosine similarity between their
embeddings and the predicted embedding. All hyperparameters are reported in Appendix S2.
4.2
Results
Performance scales with training data Figure 3 shows decoding performance as a function of
training-set size. Across subjects, performance improves as more subject-specific training sessions
are added. Cosine Similarity increases, Median Relative Rank decreases, and Top-10 Retrieval
Accuracy increases over the range of training sizes. For example, Cosine Similarity increases from
approximately 0.075–0.090 at the smallest training sizes to approximately 0.144–0.155 at the largest
training sizes. Over the same range, Median Relative Rank decreases from approximately 0.11–
0.19 to 0.022–0.034, while Top-10 Retrieval Accuracy increases from approximately 3.8–6.4% to
22.3–27.3%. These gains are visible across all three metrics, indicating that the decoder consistently
benefits from additional within-subject recordings. As in the empirical scaling laws reported for
neural language models [Kaplan et al., 2020], the trajectories are approximately linear in the log-log
space of Figure 3, with performance continuing to improve up to the largest training sets and no clear
saturation over the range studied. This supports the value of deep per-subject datasets for naturalistic
language decoding.
5
Discussion
Cephalonauts One is designed as both a neuroscience resource and a machine-learning benchmark
for naturalistic language decoding. Its main contribution is to increase within-subject depth for
speech fMRI, while pairing brain activity with aligned text and audio representations. This design
creates opportunities for studying language processing, evaluating multimodal decoding models, and
developing methods that may later transfer to clinical settings. At the same time, the dataset has
important limitations: it includes only three subjects, focuses on French podcast stimuli, and evaluates
decoding under a controlled retrieval setting. We discuss these broader impacts and limitations below.
8


5.1
Broader impact
Neuroscience Cephalonauts One can support a better understanding of how the human brain
processes naturalistic speech. By pairing many hours of fMRI with aligned audio and transcript
representations, the dataset makes it possible to study language comprehension over extended
timescales, from acoustic input to semantic content. Its depth also supports subject-specific analyses.
Multimodal machine learning Cephalonauts One also provides a useful setting for multimodal
representation learning. Text and audio benefit from strong pretrained foundation models, whereas
fMRI does not yet have comparably mature foundation models. This creates an asymmetric mul-
timodal problem: one modality has rich pretrained representations, while the other remains noisy,
high-dimensional, and data-limited. The benchmark therefore supports the training and evaluation of
models that align brain activity with established text and audio embedding spaces. More broadly, it
can help study how machine learning systems handle modalities with unequal levels of representation
quality, data availability, and pretrained model support.
Potential negative impacts Brain recordings are sensitive biomedical data, and decoding cognition
could raise privacy concerns. To best protect participants privacy, access to Cephalonauts One is
gated on Hugging Face and requires users to accept terms of use before requesting access. Participants
gave informed consent for data sharing, and the terms of use prohibit re-identification attempts, contact
with study volunteers, and provision of information linking the data to protected medical information.
5.2
Limitations
Limited number of subjects Cephalonauts One prioritizes depth over breadth. It contains many
hours of recordings per subject, but only three subjects. This design supports subject-specific
decoding and scaling analyses, but limits conclusions about population-level variability and cross-
subject generalization.
Single-site acquisition All data were collected at the same imaging site, on the same scanner, with the
same MRI sequence. This improves consistency across sessions and subjects, but limits conclusions
about robustness to other sites, scanners, field strengths, and acquisition settings.
MRI acquisition sequence The acquisition protocol was optimized for temporal sampling rather
than spatial resolution. The 520 ms TR provides many brain volumes per hour of listening. This
choice came with a 3 mm isotropic functional resolution, which is lower than protocols designed for
fine-grained spatial mapping. Thus, Cephalonauts One is well suited to studying temporally dense
naturalistic speech decoding, but it is less suited to questions that require precise localization of small
cortical structures.
Restricted language and stimulus domain All subjects listened to French podcasts from a single
program, Les Pieds sur Terre. This gives coherent and naturalistic stimuli, but restricts the dataset
to French narrative speech. Models trained on Cephalonauts One may not generalize to other
languages or broader auditory environments.
Retrieval-based evaluation Our benchmark focuses on retrieval: the model must identify the
correct audio segment from a finite pool of candidates in a held-out session. This setting is already
challenging because candidates come from unseen podcast segments with similar topic, speaker, and
narrative context. However, it does not cover the full range of possible decoding tasks. We hope
that Cephalonauts One will also support future benchmarks at different levels of difficulty, from
easier tasks such as speech-versus-non-speech or coarse stimulus classification to harder tasks such
as open-ended generation of text or audio stimuli from brain activity.
6
Conclusion
Cephalonauts One provides 30 hours of whole-brain 3T fMRI per subject from three subjects
listening to French podcasts, paired with aligned audio and transcript representations. It includes
preprocessed brain data in multiple spaces, stimulus embeddings from text and audio, standardized
decoding tasks, baseline models, and scaling analyses over training duration. Decoding perfor-
mance improves with additional within-subject data, with no clear saturation over the range studied,
supporting deep recordings for naturalistic language decoding.

The decoding problem consists in learning a function fθ parameterized by θ that maps brain activity
to stimulus features, and in evaluating the performance of this function on held-out test data. We refer
to fθ as the decoder. Since we only train models on data from a single subject, we drop the subscript
p for readability.
For a given brain volume t ∈T, the model takes as input the vector of brain activity xt ∈RV
and produces as output a vector of predicted stimulus features ˆyt ≜fθ(xt) ∈RD. Using gradient
descent, θ is optimised to minimise a loss function L that quantifies the discrepancy between the
predicted embeddings ˆyt and the true embeddings yt.
A temporal lag δ can also be introduced, to account for the delay of the hemodynamic response for
instance. The full model can be written as:
ˆyt ≜fθ (xt+δ)
Additionally, the model can be trained on multiple consecutive brain volumes x, and tasked with
predicting stimulus features derived from a window of multiple consecutive stimulus segments, which
we did not explore in the present work but which constitute interesting avenues for future research
using Cephalonauts One.

Justification: Results are reported with error bars across five folds, using session indices
