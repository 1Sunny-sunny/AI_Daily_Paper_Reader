BRAIN–COMPUTER INTERFACE ACCESS

1School of Biomedical Engineering & Imaging Sciences, Kings College London, London, UK

ABSTRACT
Endovascular brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) avoid craniotomy but require precise device delivery
through anatomically variable cerebral veins. This work presents the first demonstration of in vitro
autonomous robotic navigation for endovascular BCI access in the cerebral venous system. Soft
Actor-Critic controllers were trained in silico for two sequential tasks spanning the right internal
jugular vein to the superior sagittal sinus, using geometric augmentation of one training anatomy.
Navigation was evaluated in a training anatomy and an anatomically unseen hold-out model over
250 in silico episodes and five fluoroscopy-guided in vitro robotic runs per task–anatomy condition,
comprising 1,000 simulated episodes and 20 physical runs overall. Task recurrent predictors were
also evaluated for online identification of impending navigation failure. In silico success rates for
Tasks A and B were 85.6% and 98.4% in the training anatomy and 42.0% and 91.6% in the hold-out
anatomy, respectively. Fourteen of 20 physical runs were successful (70% overall), including 80%
success for Task B in the hold-out phantom. In silico the predictors detected 99.3–100.0% of failures
with false-alarm rates of 0.8–6.7%. During in vitro evaluation, predicted risk increased before failed
episodes, but elevated probabilities during some successful runs showed reduced calibration after
transfer. These results demonstrate the feasibility of autonomous cerebral venous access and show
how online failure prediction could support human oversight, while also identifying anatomical
generalization and sim-to-real calibration as priorities before preclinical translation.

Introduction
Brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) offer a route to restoring communication and control through digital devices for
individuals with severe motor impairment arising from conditions such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), brainstem
stroke, or high cervical spinal cord injury, for whom conventional assistive technologies are frequently insufficient [1, 2].
Cortical BCIs achieve this by decoding motor intent directly from neural activity, bypassing the damaged efferent
pathways that would otherwise carry a movement command from brain to muscle, and translating that decoded intent
into control of an external device [3].
The majority of BCIs validated in human trials to date have relied on electrode arrays implanted via craniotomy [4].
This could be by penetrating the cortical surface as intracortical arrays, or resting upon it as subdural or epidural
electrocorticography (ECoG) grids. These approaches offer excellent spatial and temporal signal fidelity, but craniotomy
carries surgical risk, and the chronic foreign-body response provoked by direct cortical contact, including glial scarring
and encapsulation, can progressively degrade signal quality over the working life of the implant [5, 6].
Endovascular BCIs offer an alternative to open cranial surgery. The Stentrode (Synchron, Brooklyn, NY, USA) is a stent-
mounted electrode array delivered via the jugular vein and deployed within the superior sagittal sinus (SSS) adjacent

to primary motor cortex. This approach repurposes catheter-based delivery techniques established in interventional
cardiology and neurointervention for the placement of a chronic neural interface. Endovascular BCIs enable neural
recording via cerebral veins, avoiding craniotomy, with signal quality similar to subdural arrays and minimal vascular
complications reported to date [7]. The SWITCH and COMMAND feasibility studies have now extended to ten
participants implanted for periods of up to five years, and have shown that vascular ECoG can be recorded safely and
with long-term signal stability, although considerable inter-participant variability in recorded motor signal strength has
been observed [8, 9].
Functional performance of an endovascular BCI depends on the relationship between electrode location and task-relevant
cortex. Clinical data indicate that greater primary motor cortex coverage is associated with stronger recorded motor
signals from the Stentrode [8], meaning achieving reproducible access to patient-specific venous targets is an important
prerequisite for reliable endovascular BCI implantation. This is challenging because cerebral venous anatomy varies
between individuals and the relevant vessels can be tapered and tortuous [10]. Additionally, computational modeling
suggests that vessel tapering and deformation alter the local hemodynamic environment around stent-mounted neural
interfaces [11].
Endovascular BCI placement remains a manually performed neurointervention. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the
operator repeatedly translates and rotates the guidewire and catheter to reach a prespecified vascular target. Similar to
other endovascular procedures, manual navigation is dependent on operator skill, while repeated fluoroscopic imaging
exposes operators to occupational radiation [12]. Autonomous robotic navigation could reduce operator burden and, in
the case of endovascular BCIs, improve the reproducibility of access to prespecified venous targets, independent of an
individual operator’s skill.
Previous research into the autonomous navigation of catheters and guidewires has demonstrated in silico and in vitro
feasibility across cardiac interventions, mainly by using reinforcement learning (RL) to automate device control [12].
Recently, autonomous thrombectomy navigation has been investigated using multi-agent and world-model-based
RL approaches validated across unseen patient vasculatures in silico and fluoroscopy-guided vascular phantoms in
vitro [13, 14].
Despite these studies, current literature has focused predominantly on the aortic arch and coronary vasculature [12],
with only a small number of studies extending into the cerebral vasculature, and those exclusively in the context of the
arterial circulation [15, 16].
To our knowledge, no study has addressed autonomous navigation of the cerebral venous system. Beyond demonstrating
navigation performance, recent work has increasingly emphasized human-supervised autonomy embedded within
existing interventional workflows [17, 18]. Under this framing, autonomous navigation should be accompanied by
online monitoring that identifies when system behavior is becoming unreliable and when human intervention may be
required. This motivates the combination of autonomous venous navigation and online failure prediction investigated in
this study.
The primary aim of this paper was to demonstrate that the autonomous navigation of the cerebral venous system is
possible both in silico and in vitro. Specifically, we contribute:
• The first demonstration of autonomous robotic navigation for cerebral venous access, including fluoroscopy-
guided robotic navigation from the right internal jugular vein toward the superior sagittal sinus.
• Sim-to-real evaluation of controllers trained entirely in silico, including evaluation in an anatomically unseen
cerebral venous in vitro model.
• A task-specific recurrent failure-prediction framework for online estimation of impending navigation failure,
evaluated in silico and transferred to the physical testbed to investigate its potential for human-supervised
autonomy.

2.2
Dataset
Two publicly available 3D vascular STL models derived from MRI venography were used in this study. The models,
CNS Venography 3D SR Nevit Dilmen and 3DPX-003440 CNS Venography Nevit Dilmen, were created by Nevit Dilmen
and made available through Wikimedia Commons under a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 3.0 (CC BY-SA
3.0) license [20, 21]. The models contain the cerebral venous structures required for the navigation tasks, including the
SSS and associated venous structures relevant to endovascular BCI implantation.
The source STL models were subsequently modified for use in the navigation environment. They were imported into
3D Slicer [22], where vessel centrelines were extracted. Local vessel radii were calculated at each centreline point and
used to reconstruct a parameterized representation of the vascular lumen. This representation enabled generation of a
smooth surface mesh while preserving the underlying vascular topology and vessel diameter distribution. The resulting
surface meshes were subsequently imported into the Simulation Open Framework Architecture (SOFA, v23.12), where
they were used for autonomous navigation training and evaluation [23].
As only two anatomically distinct venous models were available, one was used for training and one as a hold-out
anatomy. Additional geometric augmentation was employed during RL training to increase morphological diversity
and reduce overfitting to a single anatomy [14]. Augmentations were applied directly to the extracted centreline
representation prior to mesh generation. These included global scaling, local stretching and compression of vessel
segments, rotation, and spatial perturbation of centreline control points while preserving vessel connectivity. Vessel
radii were transformed consistently with the modified centrelines to maintain realistic lumen geometry. Centreline
lengths were randomly scaled by ±20%, vessel radii by ±20%, and rotational perturbations of up to 30◦were applied.

2.3
Testbed
2.3.1
In silico
The open-source simulated EndoVascular Environment (stEVE) framework was used to build an in silico environ-
ment [24]. stEVE utilizes the BeamAdapter plugin for SOFA, which was used to model the vasculatures and the devices
used for navigation [25]. The devices modeled were a 0.038” J-tip guidewire (AngioDynamics, Inc., Latham, NY,
USA), and a 0.070” multipurpose (MP) catheter (Penumbra, Inc., Alameda, CA, USA).
A single-tracking (guidewire tracking only) method was employed [26]. The simulation’s fidelity to real-world device
behavior was achieved through iterative fine-tuning of parameters such as the friction between the device and vessel
wall and the device’s stiffness. Such methodology has previously allowed domain adaptation of autonomous navigation
agents from in silico to in vitro models and from in silico to ex vivo of porcine liver vasculatures [24, 27, 13].
2.3.2
In vitro
The in vitro testbed used in this study consisted of an in-house 3D-printed phantom fabricated from polylactic acid
(PLA), as shown in Figure 2. Both of the vascular models described in Section 2.2 were printed at original scale. A
mobile C-arm (Ziehm Vision, Ziehm Imaging, Nuremberg, Germany) was used to provide fluoroscopic images as an
input to a device tip-tracking algorithm that extracted the necessary coordinates for the state-based RL algorithm [28]. A
0.038” J-tip guidewire (AngioDynamics, Inc., Latham, NY, USA), and a 0.070” MP catheter (Penumbra, Inc., Alameda,
CA, USA) were used for experiments.
A robotic manipulator (Fig. 3) was used to translate and rotate the catheter and guidewire independently [13]. The
system features a modular design with independent actuation carriage units, each capable of two degrees of freedom
(rotation and translation), placed on a single lead screw rod, which was fixed on a frame made from aluminum struts.
Direct-drive hollow shaft stepper motors were used with a leadscrew nut to translate the actuation carriage unit with an
instrument along the length of the actuation system. Furthermore, the same stepper motor type was deployed to rotate
the instruments via small drill chuck couplings.

2.4.2
Soft Actor–Critic
Controllers were trained with Soft Actor–Critic (SAC), an off-policy maximum-entropy actor–critic algorithm [29],
using the recurrent architecture described in [24]. An LSTM observation embedder was followed by separate policy
and twin Q-networks. The policy was a tanh-squashed diagonal Gaussian, ensuring that sampled actions lay in the
bounds of (2). Sampled actions were used during training and the squashed mean during evaluation. Standard SAC
target-network, terminal-masking, critic, and entropy-regularized actor updates were used.

2.5
Failure Predictor
To provide an online estimate of navigation risk, a separate recurrent predictor was trained for each navigation task
using in silico trajectories from that task. The navigation policy was trained first and subsequently used to generate
successful and unsuccessful trajectories, which were stored and used to train the failure predictor a posteriori. The
predictor and navigation policy were therefore not trained jointly.

2.6
Training, experimental design and evaluation
2.6.1
Training
RL training was done entirely in silico, using the hyperparameters in Table 2. All training was completed on an
NVIDIA DGX A100 node (Santa Clara, CA, USA). One attempt at a navigation task constituted an episode. An episode
was successful when the tracked device tip entered the predefined target region equal to the local vessel diameter.
Training episodes terminated after 200 exploration steps (approximately 27 s) if the target had not been reached to lower
simulation cost.
To select the checkpoint used for final testing, evaluations were conducted every 2.5 × 105 exploration steps for
50 episodes during in silico training. Agents were allowed 450 exploration steps (equivalent to approximately 60 s)
during in silico evaluation to avoid classifying slower but recoverable trajectories as failures because of the training-time
truncation limit. The Success Rate was defined as the percentage of evaluation episodes in which the controller
successfully reached the target; for unsuccessful episodes Path Ratio was defined as PathRatio = 100

2.6.2
Experimental design & evaluation
The trained agents were evaluated separately for Tasks A and B in the training anatomy and the previously unseen
hold-out anatomy (Fig. 5). Each anatomy–task condition comprised 250 in silico episodes and five in vitro runs. Success
rate, procedure time, and path ratio were recorded for every condition. Physical runs were limited to 5 min to account
for the lower operating speed of the robotic manipulator.
Failure-prediction performance was evaluated at the prespecified visual-warning threshold of ˆqt ≥0.50. Detection rate
was the proportion of failed episodes in which ˆqt ≥0.50 at least once before failure, thereby generating a warning;
warning lead time was the number of control steps between the first warning and failure; and false-alarm rate was the
proportion of successful episodes with at least one warning. Calibration was assessed using an episode-level Brier score,
obtained by averaging (ˆqt −yt)2 over all timesteps in an episode. This measures the accuracy of the predicted failure
probabilities, with lower values indicating closer agreement between predicted risk and observed failure outcomes.
Success rate, detection, and false-alarm proportions are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Procedure
time, path ratio, and Brier scores are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas warning lead time is reported
as median [interquartile range]. Given the five physical runs per anatomy–task condition, the in vitro analysis was
descriptive and no inferential between-condition tests were performed.

Results
3.1
In silico
Agent performance across the training and hold-out anatomies is summarized in Table 3. In the training anatomy, Tasks
A and B achieved success rates of 85.6% and 98.4%, respectively. In the hold-out anatomy, Task A success decreased
by 43.6 percentage points to 42.0%, whereas Task B success decreased by 6.8 percentage points to 91.6%. Across
successful episodes, mean procedure time ranged from 10.33 s to 13.40 s. Across unsuccessful episodes, mean path
ratio ranged from 8.4% to 31.7%.
Quantitative failure-prediction results are reported in Table 4, where the predictor detected every failure in the training
anatomy. In the hold-out anatomy, the predictor was able to detect 99.3% of Task A failures and 100.0% of Task B
failures. Median warning lead time ranged from 22 to 27 steps, corresponding to approximately 2.9–3.6 s at the in
silico evaluation control rate. Maximum false-alarm rates were at most 2.3% in the training anatomy and at most 6.7%
in the hold-out anatomy. The largest calibration difference occurred for hold-out Task A, for which the Brier score was
0.0203.

episodes, the mean predicted probability remained low during the early and middle portions of the trajectory and rose
sharply as failure approached, reaching approximately 100% near episode termination for both tasks. For successful
episodes, the mean output remained close to zero throughout, consistent with the low in silico false-alarm rates in

3.2
In vitro
The in vitro navigation results are summarized in Table 5. Success occurs in 14/20 runs, corresponding to an overall
success rate of 70% (95% CI 48.1–85.5%). In the training anatomy, both Tasks A and B achieved success rates of 80%,
while in the hold-out anatomy, Task A achieved a 40% success rate and Task B achieved 80%.
For successful runs in the training anatomy, mean procedure time ranged from 85 s for Task B to 189 s for Task A.
Among unsuccessful runs, Task A had a higher path ratio than Task B in both anatomies. In the training anatomy,
the single unsuccessful Task A run reached 84% of the route, whereas unsuccessful Task B runs reached 35%. In the
hold-out anatomy, the corresponding values were 88% for Task A and 56% for Task B.
The observed physical failure modes differed by task and anatomy. For Task A, the single training-anatomy failure
occurred when the device entered the left internal jugular vein instead of the SSS. In the hold-out anatomy, one Task A
failure occurred when the device became lodged in the right internal jugular vein and two occurred at the base of the
SSS. For Task B, the single failure in each anatomy occurred when the device became lodged in a smaller branch arising
from the SSS. All hold-out anatomy failures were associated with substantial device–phantom friction.
The dashed curves in Figure 6 show task-matched predictor outputs during in vitro testing. Mean predicted risk
increased before failed episodes for both tasks and was high near failure. In contrast to in silico, the physical predictor
outputs were also elevated during successful runs, as Task A mean exceeded 50% through much of the latter portion of
successful trajectories, while Task B mean rose to approximately 40–60%. The wide CIs reflect the small number of
physical runs, but the overlap between successful and unsuccessful outputs indicates reduced predictor calibration after
sim-to-real transfer. Figure 7 maps the locations at which the armed automatic response initiated guidewire retraction in
the hold-out in vitro phantom.

Discussion
This study presents the first demonstration of autonomous robotic navigation in the cerebral venous system and evaluates
a complementary online failure predictor. Two sequential navigation tasks approximating the access route from the
superior right jugular vein to the SSS were completed in silico and in vitro under fluoroscopic guidance, including
a hold-out anatomy. The primary findings were that autonomous venous access was feasible but strongly task- and
anatomy-dependent; that simulated failure prediction generalized well to the hold-out anatomy; and that direct transfer
of the predictor to the physical testbed introduced calibration error. The work therefore establishes technical feasibility
while identifying the limitations that must be addressed before autonomous BCI delivery can be considered safe or
clinically applicable.
4.1
Autonomous navigation performance
The navigation results demonstrate that cross-anatomy generalization cannot be characterized by a single aggregate
success rate. Task B retained high in silico performance in the hold-out anatomy, decreasing from 98.4% to 91.6%,
whereas Task A decreased from 85.6% to 42.0%. Task A requires route selection at multiple venous junctions, with
greater path tortuosity than Task B, making it a more challenging navigation. The larger degradation for Task A suggests
that the geometric augmentation used during training did not span the variation in this junction sufficiently. It also
shows why validation confined to a training anatomy would have overstated the robustness of the controller.
In vitro navigation was successful in 14/20 runs. The hold-out results followed the same task-dependent pattern as
simulation: Task A achieved 40% physical success compared with 42% in simulation, whereas Task B achieved 80%
compared with 91.6%. These similar point estimates should not be interpreted as evidence of sim-to-real equivalence
because the physical estimates were based on only five runs per condition and consequently had wide CIs. Nevertheless,
successful in vitro fluoroscopy-guided navigation in both printed anatomies shows that a policy trained entirely in silico
can produce useful physical behavior in the cerebral venous route, extending prior autonomous endovascular work from
arterial navigation toward venous BCI access [15, 13, 14].

The in vitro failure modes identify specific targets for system improvement. Incorrect entry into the contralateral jugular
route during Task A may reflect state ambiguity or an incorrect policy decision at a vascular branching point, whereas
lodging at the SSS base or in smaller SSS branches was attributed to friction. The latter failures expose a limitation of
matching in silico device mechanics to a rigid printed phantom, as contact and friction can dominate progression even
when the selected route is anatomically correct. In vitro procedure times were also longer than in silico procedure times,
due to the slower robotic platform, and fluoroscopic acquisition. These differences reinforce the need to evaluate task
completion, mechanics, and timing after in vitro transfer rather than relying on in silico performance alone.
For endovascular BCIs, reaching the SSS is an enabling step rather than the complete implantation problem. The optimal
recording location depends on the relationship among venous anatomy, task-relevant cortex, and electrode coverage [8].
Future clinical systems would need to register a patient-specific implantation target derived from venography and
functional imaging, navigate to that target, and support controlled device deployment. The present experiments address
autonomous access only, as no Stentrode was deployed, no neural signals were recorded, and the accuracy of final
electrode placement relative to functional cortex was not evaluated.
4.2
Failure prediction and human oversight
In silico task-specific predictors detected 99.3–100.0% of failures with median warning lead times of approximately
20–27 control steps. The in vitro trajectories provide a more cautious result, as predicted risk rose before failed
runs, showing that the learned temporal signal retained some relevance after transfer. However, risk also rose during
successful in vitro runs and frequently exceeded the 50% visual-warning threshold, especially for Task A. This behavior
is consistent with a covariate shift in tracked device motion, timing, or image-derived coordinates between in silico and
in vitro testbeds. A detector that identifies failures but also warns during successful navigation could increase operator
workload or cause unnecessary withdrawal, limiting its clinical value. Physical calibration, task-conditional threshold
selection, and explicit uncertainty or out-of-distribution detection are therefore necessary before the output can be used
as a dependable supervisory signal.
Coupling persistent high predicted risk to guidewire retraction illustrates how online monitoring could lead to an
immediate physical response. However, it does not establish that automatic retraction is safe or beneficial. No
matched no-retraction experiments were performed, and retraction could itself prolong a procedure, cause repeated
device–wall contact, or prevent recovery from a transient but non-hazardous state. Near-term use should therefore
remain operator-armed and human-supervised, consistent with proposals for AI assistance within existing interventional
workflows [17, 18].
4.3
Limitations and future work
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only two venous anatomies were available, with one used for training
and one for hold-out evaluation, reflecting the limited availability of cerebral venous imaging datasets in the current
literature, when compared to the corresponding availability of arterial vascular datasets. Geometric augmentation
increases variation around the training model but cannot reproduce the topology and pathology of a larger patient cohort.
This limited-anatomy design is consistent with earlier feasibility studies in autonomous endovascular navigation, in
which initial single-anatomy demonstrations motivated subsequent evaluation across larger patient-derived cohorts [26].
The in vitro study included only five runs per anatomy–task condition, limiting precision and preventing meaningful
inferential comparisons. No manual expert-navigation benchmark was included, preventing direct comparison of
autonomous and operator performance in terms of success rate, procedure time, or failure modes. Future studies
should use a larger set of patient-derived venographies, preserve a multi-patient external test cohort, and repeat in vitro
evaluation sufficiently to characterize both common and rare failures.
Second, 3D-printed phantoms did not reproduce pulsatility, or physiological motion [17], although these are less
prevalent in the venous system when compared to the arterial system. The frictional behavior was a major cause of
failure and may not represent either living veins or more compliant flow phantoms. More realistic flow loops, compliant
materials, and systematic domain randomization of image and device mechanics are required to enhance the in vitro
testbed. Future testbeds should therefore investigate elastomeric materials that better reproduce venous deformation and
device–wall interaction, with physiologically representative flow conditions.
Third, navigation success rate was defined by target entry and did not measure contact force, vessel-wall stress,
perforation, dissection, or other safety outcomes. It also quantified vascular access rather than optimal BCI placement
relative to the intended cortical target and electrode coverage. No endovascular BCI was deployed, and no ex vivo or in
vivo validation was performed. Future platforms should incorporate force estimation, impose explicit mechanical safety
constraints, evaluate final implant-placement accuracy relative to patient-specific functional targets, evaluate controlled

Finally, the predictors were trained from trajectories generated by the same controller family and may not recognize
failures caused by unseen policies, tracking artifacts, or hardware faults. In vitro results show that simulation calibration
did not transfer directly. Future work should fine-tune and recalibrate the predictor using independently collected
physical data, evaluate generalization across controllers and failure modes, prospectively select alert thresholds,
and compare operator-only, warning-only, and automatic-response conditions. A controlled study is also needed to
determine whether prediction and retraction improve success, reduce force, or shorten recovery without increasing false
interventions.
5
Conclusion
This work demonstrates the feasibility of autonomous robotic access to the cerebral venous system for an endovascular
BCI route. In silico-trained controllers completed two sequential tasks from the right jugular vein toward the SSS in
training and hold-out anatomies and achieved 70% success across 20 fluoroscopy-guided physical runs. Recurrent
predictors identified nearly all simulated failures with early warning and low false-alarm rates. However, elevated risk
estimates during successful in vitro runs revealed an important sim-to-real calibration gap. The results support further
development of human-supervised autonomous venous navigation, but they do not establish autonomous implantation
or clinical safety. Larger anatomical cohorts, realistic compliant flow models, physical recalibration, force-aware safety
evaluation, and preclinical validation are required before translation.
