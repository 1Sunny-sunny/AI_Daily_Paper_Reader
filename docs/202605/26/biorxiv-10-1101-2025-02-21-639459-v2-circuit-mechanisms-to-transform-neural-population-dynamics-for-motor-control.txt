Abstract 17 

We identify the input -output transformation s performed by the cerebellar circuit and isolate the 18 

circuit properties that implement them. Extracellular recordings exploited new technology for 19 

neuron -type identification in part of the cerebellar cortex that is causal for an oculomotor 20 

behavior whose neural substrate is well -understood in monkeys . Data demonstrate how multiple 21 

classes of interneurons in the cerebellar circuit cooperate to transform incoming mossy fiber 22 

responses into Purkinje cell outputs. Computer models suggest that t emporal decomposition in 23 

the granule cell layer enable s flexible reconstruction of transformed output dynamics , regulated 24 

by cerebellar plasticity, to convert eye position -like inputs into predominantly velocity -driven 25 

Purkinje cell outputs . Convergence of mossy fiber inputs with diverse direction preferences 26 

would increase input layer dimensionality and enable learning of highly flexible direction tuning .27 

The neural circuit mechanisms suggested by our data outline a cerebellar operating principle that 28 

enables flexible input -output transformations and could generalize across cerebellar and other 29 

brain areas .30

Introduction 32 

Understanding how neural circuits process information to generate behavior is a fundamental 33 

goal of systems neuroscience. Circuit computations can be envisioned as multiple 34 

transformations 1 of neural population dynamic s2,3 - from sensory inputs 4 to intermediate 35 

representations 5 to the ultimate drive of precise muscle activations 6. The cerebellum provides an 36 

ideal platform for understanding circuit mechanisms that transform population dynamics. The 37 

cytoarchitecture of the cerebellum is highly conserved 7,8 , with a well -characterized circuit 38 

organization 9, discrete neuron types 9,10 , and crucially, well -defined inputs and a single neural 39 

population output 11,12 . It has long been thought that the cerebellar circuit performs a canonical, 40 

repeated computation 13,14 across its different regions for both motor and non -motor behaviors. 41 

Thus, identifying the circuit mechanisms that transform population dynamics in one region of the 42 

cerebellum may generalize across cerebellar regions and tasks. 43 

We focus on transformations of population dynamics in the cerebellar floccular complex, a44 

structure chosen for analysis because of the unique interpretational power provided by extensive 45 

knowledge about its neural signal processing 15 and causal connection to a quantifiable 46 

behavior 16,17 . The floccular complex is essential 17,18 for controlling smooth pursuit eye 47 

movements via disynaptic connections to extraocular motor neurons 19 . Prior recordings from its 48 

Purkinje cell output s20 –22 and mossy fiber inputs 23,24 tentatively characterized the input -output 49 

transformation of the floccular circuit. Now our goal was to understand how the complete circuit 50 

performs those input -output transformations . The circuit is known 9: i ncoming mossy fibers 51 

synapse onto granule cells, whose parallel fiber axons provide excitatory input to Purkinje cells, 52 

the sole output of the cerebellar cortex. Additional neuron types 10 , including Golgi cells, unipolar 53 

brush cells, and molecular layer interneurons (among others 25,26 ), use both feed -forward and 54 

recurrent connections 11 to modulate the activity within the pathway between mossy fibers and 55 

Purkinje cells. 56 

We take advantage of recent advances in large -scale multi -contact electrode technology 27,28 and 57 

neuron -type identification strategies 29,30 to record populations of identified cerebellar input 58 

fibers, output neurons, and interneurons during motor behavior. We reveal neural circuit 59

Using multi -contact probes, we recorded extracellular action potentials from the floccular circuit 75 

(Fig . 1a) while monkeys performed smooth pursuit eye movements (Fig . 1b). We successfully 76 

identified all major neuron types schematized in Fig . 1a, with the exception of granule cells, 77 

using expert criteria 30 (see Methods ), validated via ground -truth optogenetic identification in 78 

mice 29 .79 

The inputs and outputs from the floccular complex, mossy fibers and Purkinje cells, follow 80 

distinct low -dimensional neural state -space trajectories 2,31 (Fig . 1c). Mossy fiber trajectories 81 

align closely with the horizontal and vertical axes of the eye and terminate eccentrically in state -82 

space with magnitudes that scale with pursuit speed. In contrast, Purkinje cell trajectories show 83 

principal axes that are rotated relative to the cardinal axes and return near the origin at movement 84 

completion. For both neuron types, the first two dimensions captured a substantial portion of the 85 

response variance: 38% and 29% for mossy fibers and 23% and 20% for Purkinje cells (a 86 

considerable amount given that we are accounting for responses in 4 directions by 3 speeds of 87 

target motion). The first two components dominate the responses : for mossy fibers and Purkinje 88 

cells, respectively, the 3 rd principal component accounts for only 7% and 11% of the variance. 89 

The mean population responses of mossy fibers and Purkinje cells in the preferred and opposite 90 

directions reveal temporal dynamics that explain the difference in state -space trajectories (Fig .91 

1d). Mossy fiber responses resembled eye position traces, with persistent firing at movement 92 

termination that scaled approximately linearly with pursuit speed (preferred direction R 2 = 0.83 ± 93 

0.03). Purkinje cell responses peaked approximately 200 ms after motion onset with magnitudes 94 

that increased with pursuit speed and returned to baseline at the end of pursuit irrespective of 95 

final eccentric eye position. The shift from sustained mossy fiber responses to transient Purkinje 96 

cell responses requires the cerebellar circuit to perform a “temporal transformation.” 97 

Directional tuning properties also differed between mossy fibers and Purkinje cells (Fi g. 1e). 98 

Mossy fibers showed uniformly distributed preferred directions across the cardinal axes (circular 99 

dispersion:  𝑅 = 0.13) with essentially no modulation of firing for pursuit orthogonal to their 100 

preferred axis . Purkinje cells’ preferred directions were more strongly biased, with preferred 101 

directions close to ipsiversive and downwards ( 𝑅 = 0.58) 21,22,24,32 –34 , requiring the cerebellar 102 

circuit to perform a “directional transformation.” Purkinje cells exhibited diverse directional 103 

tuning, with varying degrees of inhibition in the anti -preferred direction and often small 104 

increases or decreases in firing in orthogonal directions. 105

The features of preferred versus non -preferred mean responses (Fig . 2a) were consistent across 114 

individual neurons of each type (Fi g. 2b). Purkinje cells, mossy fibers, and unipolar brush cells 115 

showed reciprocal responses (negative values in the anti -preferred direction) . Golgi cells showed 116 

minimal modulation in either preferred or anti -preferred directions, while most MLIs exhibited 117 

positive , non -reciprocal modulation in both directions. 118 

Principal component analysis of trial -averaged pursuit responses in each neuron’s preferred 119 

direction at 20 deg/s showed progressive changes in temporal response s across the cerebellar 120 

circuit. The two dominant components together explained 81% of the population variance (49% 121 

and 32%, respectively). The first principal component (Fig . 2c, x -axis inset ) resembled eye 122 

position, with a near monotonic increase over time and sustained deviation. The second principal 123 

component (Fig . 2c, y -axis inset ) exhibited an early peak followed by a return to near baseline ,124 

resembling a combination of eye velocity and acceleration . The third principal component 125 

accounted for 2.5% of the variance. 126 

In the two -dimensional space defined by the first two principal components (Fig . 2c), Golgi cells 127 

are concentrated near the origin. Purkinje cells show near zero weights in the first dimension but 128 

consistently positive weights in the second dimension. Mossy fibers and unipolar brush cells 129 

show the opposite trend, with weights centered near zero in the second dimension but strongly 130 

positive in the first dimension. Much of the within -neuron -type variation in the spatial 131 

distribution shown in Fig . 2c results from heterogeneity in response magnitude rather than 132 

differences in temporal properties within neuron types. Angular distributions in the two -133 

dimensional space (Fig. 2d) differ across neuron types, reflecting distinct relative contributions 134 

of the two principal components to neuron firing. 135

Before turning to computational analysis, we draw conclusions based on the data in Fig . 2. 136 

Specific ally, features of each cerebellar interneuron type suggest that they could not contribute to 137 

the temporal input -output transformation within the cerebellar circuit , as well as how they might 138 

contribute to the directional transformation .139 

● Golgi cells showed essentially no temporal modulation of firing during ipsiversive pursuit 140 

with regular firing at low rates (mean rate = 12 spikes/s, mean “CV2 35 ” = 0.25 ± 0.01) (Fig .141 

2e). We conclude that Golgi cells provide tonic but temporally and directionally (Fig. 2b, c) 142 

unmodulated inhibition of granule cells. 143 

● Molecular layer interneurons showed preferred directions opposite to those of their 144 

connected Purkinje cells. Thus, a molecular layer interneuron’s firing, related primarily to 145 

eye velocity in its preferred direction (Fig . 2f), provides inhibition that suppresses the firing 146 

of Purkinje cells in their anti -preferred direction. The presence of velocity signals in both 147 

molecular layer interneurons and Purkinje cells argues for a shared upstream transformation 148 

available to both populations in parallel, rather than independent intrinsic mechanisms 149 

reimplemented in each cell type. Molecular layer interneurons displayed minimal modulation 150 

of firing rate during pursuit in their anti -preferred direction (Fig. 2b) and therefore have little 151 

impact on the temporal dynamics of Purkinje cell preferred -direction responses but possibly 152 

greater impact on their direction tuning .153 

● Unipolar brush cells showed clear positive and negative modulation in their preferred and 154 

anti -preferred directions (Fig. 2b) , had fully sustained modulation in both directions after 155 

pursuit termination and, like mossy fibers, responded primarily to a combination of eye 156 

position and velocity (Fig . 2g). Regression analysis showed stronger eye position encoding 157 

for unipolar brush cells compared to mossy fibers (histograms in Fig . 2g, standardized 158 

coefficient, β = 0.65 ± 0.07 versus 0.40 ± 0.06, t(114) = -2.36, p = 0.02). The dominant eye 159 

position response of unipolar brush cells suggests that they are not part of the transformation 160 

to velocity -related Purkinje cell or molecular layer interneuron responses .161 

Finally, the temporal transformation cannot be explained by synchrony in multiple mossy fibers 162 

inputs to granule cell s because we found no evidence for mossy fiber synchrony other than the 163 

amount expected from covariation of mossy fiber firing rates during pursuit ( Extended Data Fig .164 

2). 165 

A data -driven computational approach for predicting granule cell temporal 166 

representations 167 

The responses across neuron types imply that the temporal input -output transformation in the 168 

floccular complex occurs in the granule cell layer. Given the inaccessibility of granule cells to 169 

extracellular recordings 29 , we adopted a computational approach : we trained a deep -learning 170 

model of the mossy fiber -to -Purkinje cell pathway with explicit granule cell dynamics (Fig . 3a). 171 

Our goal was to discover the crucial computational features necessary for successful temporal 172 

transformation before mapping the successful computational features onto potential cerebellar 173 

circuit and cellular mechanisms. 174

A biologically motivated model of granule cell temporal transformations 193 

We used the evidence for short -term plasticity (STP) in the mossy fiber to granule cell 194 

synapses 36,37 as the basis for a model that could create a temporally -decomposed granule unit 195 

basis set using biological principles. To keep the model computationally tractable, we extended 196 

the classic Tsodyks -Markram STP framework 38 to include a dual transmitter pool . Each granule 197 

unit receives inputs from 4 randomly -weighted mossy fiber/unipolar brush cell s and implements 198 

STP through a “Dual Pool Synapse” with ( 1) a “fast” pool of neurotransmitter that is readily 199 

releasable and ( 2) a “slow” pool of neurotransmitter that replenishes the fast pool ( see Methods ). 200 

The resulting granule units exhibited a broad range of temporal filtering properties that agreed 201 

well with previously proposed “spectral timing” models of granule cell activity 39 –41 . The step -202 

responses of model granule units also agreed with those predicted by the deep -learning model 203 

(Fig . 3e), as did the model’s responses to mossy fiber and unipolar brush cell inputs during 204 

pursuit (Fig . 3f) . The STP model showed emergent correlations between rise and decay times 205 

(t( 49 ) = 2.2 , p = 0.03) , consistent with the deep -learning model. Other e mergent properties of the 206 

STP granule unit basis set are ( 1) g ranule unit population activity (Fi g. 3g) increased with pursuit 207 

speed to allow Purkinje cell modulation to increase with speed and ( 2) granule unit timing was 208 

consistent across pursuit speed to enable generalization across pursuit speeds. 209 

The mean response of a population of 100 granule units (Fig . 3g) resembled the mean of the 210 

temporal profiles observed in experimentally recorded Purkinje cells (Fi g. 3h, blue curves). 211 

Thus, it was not surprising that o ptimizing the output weights of the granule units via the 212 

LTP/LTD learning rule provided an excellent fit to the responses of our population of Purkinje 213 

cells (Fig . 3h, mean R2=0.9 1). Further , the STP basis set generalized well when trained on the 214 

responses to a single target speed and tested on the other 2 target speeds (Fig . 3i), Again, note 215 

that we fitted each Purkinje cell individually and we report averages of the time varying firing 216 

rates and the mean R 2 for simplicity. 217

Analysis of other plausible basis sets emphasized our main conclusions about the properties 218 

needed in the granule cell basis set: temporal decomposition of sustained inputs with scaling and 219 

consistent timing of each unit’s responses across target speeds. The other models share some of 220 

these features in a minority of their units and therefore are able to reproduce the time varying 221 

Purkinje cell firing to some degree, albeit less well than the STP basis set when using the 222 

LTP/LTP learning rule (Extended Data Fig . 3, 4). The other basis sets also perform reasonably, 223 

though less well than the STP basis set , when we used least squares optimization as the learning 224 

rule (Extended Data Fig . 5-7). 225 

The details of our optimization procedure appear in the Methods . In brief, f or each granule unit 226 

model, we optimized the hyperparameters that bound granule unit parameters rather than the 227 

parameters themselves. For each optimizer iteration, we: (1) sampled random parameter values 228 

within these bounds, ( 2) computed each granule unit ’s response to its 4 mossy fiber/unipolar 229 

brush cell inputs, ( 3) optimized parallel fiber -to -Purkinje cell weights to match recorded Purkinje 230 

cell firing, ( 4) calculated mean squared error, and ( 5) adjusted hyperparameters to potentially 231 

improve the next fit. Directly optimizing individual granule unit parameters would have yielded 232 

perfect fit s with limited mechanistic insight. 233 

A cerebellar circuit model that reproduces the directional and temporal response 234 

properties of individual Purkinje cells and molecular layer interneurons 235 

We subscribe to Feynman’s view: “What I cannot create, I do not understand”. Thus, our next 236 

step was to create a circuit model of the cerebellar cortex with realistic architecture and elements 237 

that show biomimetic neural responses. 238 

Our model used weighted combinations of modeled granule units and measured responses of 239 

molecular layer interneurons to transform the temporal firing rate responses and direction tuning 240 

of mossy fibers and unipolar brush cells into the firing of individual Purkinje cells during pursuit 241 

eye movements. The model (Fig . 4a) consisted of 86 mossy fibers and 31 unipolar brush cells 242 

with the firing profiles from our recorded population, 1,000 granule units based on the STP 243 

model, 250 molecular layer interneuron s (augmented from our recorded n=23 population), and 244 

our 101 recorded Purkinje cells. Each granule unit used the STP model to transform i nput from 4 245 

randomly -chosen mossy fibers and unipolar brush cells (Fig . 4a) into velocity -dominant ,246 

temporally decomposed, and directionally diverse granule unit activity (Fig. 4b). The 247 

convergence of randomly weighted inputs with different preferred directions created granule 248 

units with diverse and irregular direction tuning. The LTP/LTD learning rule (see Methods )249 

delivered excellent f its of both direction tuning and temporal dynamics of the 250 molecular 250 

layer interneurons (exemplar shown in the top row of Fig . 4c); the mean R 2 for molecular layer 251 

interneurons was 0.7 1 (Fig . 4e). Note that the preferred directions of molecular layer 252 

interneurons were rotated by 180° to account for the opposite preferred directions relative to their 253 

Purkinje cell neighbors (Fig . 2f). 254

Discussion 376 

The transformation of neural population dynamics is a ubiquitous problem across brain circuits. 377 

Our study identified potential circuit mechanisms in one part of the brain – the cerebellar 378 

floccular complex – that could enable two simultaneous transformations along the orthogonal 379 

axes of space and time. We suggest that the transformation of the input’s eye position /velocity 380 

population dynamics into the output’s eye velocity /acceleration dynamics occurs through (1)381 

decomposition of the incoming signals into a temporal basis set in the structure’s input layer 382 

followed by (2) flexible reconstruction, potentially under the guidance of climbing fiber inputs, 383 

into any temporal pattern of Purkinje cell firing output . We suggest that the granule cells also 384 

reorganize the representation of direction in their mossy fiber inputs, and then excitatory and 385 

inhibitory inputs to Purkinje cells can reconstruct a more varied representation of direction in the 386 

outputs. The computation s in the cerebellar cortex seem to be set up to be incredibly flexible and 387 

therefore can reproduce many combination s of directional tuning and temporal dynamics. 388 

Our results are based on one specific cerebellar region, but the ubiquity of the cerebellar circuit 389 

suggests that the computational strategies we uncovered have the potential to apply to many 390 

behaviors across the cerebellum and potentially throughout the brain. The floccular complex is 391 

ideal for our analysis because it provides context that gives strong interpretational power to our 392 

results. It is causally required for control of smooth pursuit eye movements 17 , it is connected 393 

disynaptically to extraocular motor neurons 19 , and prior recordings had characterized its input -394 

output transformations 21,24 . We have taken the next step by recording the responses of the 395 

interneurons that nominally perform the input -output transformations, using new technology to 396 

identify neuron types in the cerebellar cortex from extracellular recordings 29,30 , and inferring 397 

from both data and computation how the circuit could compute the transformation s.398 

Based on the data, we concluded that the requisite temporal transformation probably occurs in 399 

the input layer. In a shorthand, we can think of the mossy fiber inputs to the floccular complex as 400 

signaling mostly eye position: identified downstream neurons, including molecular layer 401 

interneurons and Purkinje cells 52 , show a temporal transformation to predominantly eye velocity 402 

and acceleration. The presence of velocity signals in both molecular layer interneurons and 403 

Purkinje cells argue s for a shared upstream transformation available to both populations in 404

We circumvented the challenge that granule cells are inaccessible to conventional recording 410

We obtained an excellent basis set, good fits to Purkinje cell temporal dynamics, excellent 419 

generalization of learning for target speed, and realistic learned timing using biologically 420 

motivated short -term plasticity at the mossy fiber to granule cell synapse. Alternative basis sets 421 

(see Extended Data Figures) also can support accurate fits, provided the optimization procedure 422 

is allowed to select a small subset of units that exhibit the requisite properties . While many 423

biological 56 . For this reason, we used an LTP/LTD learning rule to determine parallel fiber -to -426 

Purkinje cell weights , a strategy that distributes weights broadly across granule units. 427 

An engineer might solve the temporal transformation problem differently. For biology, a non -428 

intuitive strategy of decomposing continuous time -varying input signals into a temporally 429 

distributed representation and then reconstructing a continuous, but temporally transformed, 430 

output has the advantage that it enables a broad range of transformations. It also affords 431 

temporally specific learning of sensory -motor input -output transformations 41 . We note that 432 

temporal decomposition solutions have been proposed in multiple other papers 40,41,44,53,57 but 433 

based on theory rather than on the neural data we used to come to a tightly constrained solution. 434 

We think it is a strength that the properties of our proposed basis set align with features 435 

demonstrated as computationally desirable in prior theoretical work .436 

We also note that prior reports of granule cell responses using calcium imaging are difficult to 437 

relate to our hypothesis because ( 1) the temporal resolution of imaging is limited relative to 438 

extracellular neurophysiology and ( 2) most experimental paradigms did not explicitly establish 439 

the causal behavioral role of the recorded regions or quantitatively constrain the relevant 440 

behavior. Nevertheless , t he relatively dense coding of sensory stimuli in these datasets 58 –60 , along 441 

with heterogeneous responses across granule cells, would be consistent with formation of a 442 

distributed basis set like the one we propose. 443

Our analysis provides a roadmap for asking whether the cerebellar cortex performs a “universal 488

existing data in the eye -blink regions in mice 66 and rabbits 67 suggests a temporal basis set 490

support many different temporal transformations on the time scale of movements. Indeed, the 494 

principle of combining varied inputs onto granule cells to create a basis set with a wide range of 495 

representations of cerebellar inputs could extend beyond time, for example to direction in our 496 

model. It would maximize the flexibility in learning of appropriate cerebellar output to guide 497 

behavior. Multiple plasticity mechanisms 68,69 , including meta -plasticity 70 , could further 498 

generalize the framework of a high -dimensional basis set to the specific demands of different 499 

behaviors and cerebellar areas. 500 

In summary, we describe, for the first time in a behaving vertebrate, how a complete circuit with 501 

input, output, and interneurons could perform specific neural computations. Our analysis points 502 

to biologically plausible neural circuit mechanisms that can transform neural population 503 

dynamics across space and time and suggests that these circuit mechanisms could be a 504 

fundamental feature of neural circuits throughout the cerebellum and the rest of the brain. 505

procedures, all performed with aseptic technique. For each surgical procedure, animals were 517 

deeply anaesthetized with isoflurane. Animals received peri - and post -operative analgesia until 518 

they had completely recovered. In the first of multiple surgical procedures, head restraint 519 

hardware was attached to the animal’s skull to allow measurement of the animal’s eye position 520 

without concomitant head movements. In a later surgery, we sutured a small coil of wire to the 521 

sclera of one eye 75 , allowing measurement of the animal’s eye position using the search coil 522 

technique 76 . Following these two surgical procedures, animals were trained to perform smooth 523 

pursuit eye movements during discrete trials of target motion in exchange for fluid reward. The 524 

experimental paradigm is described in detail below. Once the animal had demonstrated excellent 525 

smooth pursuit tracking abilities, as evidenced by minimal intervening saccadic eye movements, 526 

we affixed a stainless -steel recording cylinder above a craniectomy, allowing access to the 527 

floccular complex of the cerebellum with microelectrodes. The position of this cylinder was 11 528 

mm lateral to the midline, angled at 26° relative to the frontal plane, and pointed towards the 529 

interaural axis. 530 

Behavioral task 531 

Each day animals were seated in a dark room, 30 cm in front of a CRT monitor (80 Hz refresh 532 

rate, 2304 x 1440 pixels, 480 x 310 mm). Their heads were attached to the chair via the 533 

previously implanted restraint hardware. Horizontal and vertical eye position signals from the 534 

search coil system were separately digitized at 1,000 Hz. We computed the velocity of the 535 

animal’s eye movement offline using a 2 nd order causal Butterworth low -pass filter with a cutoff 536 

frequency of 10 Hz. As we were principally interested in the relationship between floccular 537 

neural responses and smooth pursuit eye movements, we removed any saccadic eye movements 538 

from ongoing pursuit using an automated procedure 32 . The occurrence of a saccade was 539 

identified offline using eye velocity (50 deg/s) and eye acceleration thresholds (1,250 deg/s 2). 540 

Onset and offset of each saccade were determined as the time when the animal’s eye velocity or 541 

acceleration fell below both thresholds for more than 10 ms. In all analyses, we treated eye 542 

kinematics during saccades as missing data. 543

Neurophysiology recordings 561 

All recordings were made in the ventral paraflocculus and flocculus, a region we call the 562 

floccular complex. These regions have been shown to be crucial for the execution of smooth eye 563 

movements 17 . In addition, electrical stimulation of this region of the cerebellum drives smooth 564 

movement of the eye towards the side of stimulation 20,71 . We acutely inserted either single 565 

tungsten microelectrodes (FHC, 1 -2 MΩ) or, more commonly, custom designed Plexon S -Probes 566 

into the brain each day through the previously implanted recording cylinder. Plexon S -Probes 567 

featured 16 tungsten recording contacts, each 7.5 μm in diameter. The 16 contacts were arranged 568 

in two columns (50 x 50 μm separation between adjacent contacts). Almost all of our recordings 569 

come from the S -Probes. 570 

We identified the floccular complex by its strong activity during smooth pursuit eye movements, 571 

presence of infrequent Purkinje cell complex spikes, and the depth relative to the tentorium. 572 

Upon arriving in the floccular complex, we waited a minimum of 30 minutes, up to several 573 

hours, before beginning neurophysiological recordings. This initial period maximizes the 574 

stability of the recording by minimizing drift of neural units across the recording contacts. 575 

Neurophysiological data were recorded using the Plexon Omniplex system. We used analog 576 

Butterworth low -pass hardware filters (4 th order, 6 kHz cutoff) prior to digitization to minimize 577 

any interference from the eye coil system and prevent aliasing. Wideband neural activity on each 578 

contact was recorded at 40 kHz, synchronized to behavioral data using high speed TTL pulses, 579 

and stored for later offline analysis. 580

Expert identification of cerebellar neuron types 603 

A detailed discussion of the methodology for identification of cerebellar neuron types in our 604 

recordings has been published previously 30 . Briefly, we began by identifying a subset of units as 605 

ground -truth Purkinje cells based on their unique extracellular properties. Purkinje cells receive 606 

excitatory inputs from parallel fibers as well as strong inputs via climbing fibers from the inferior 607 

olive that drive post -synaptic Purkinje cell complex spikes. During complex spikes and for 10 or 608 

more milliseconds thereafter, Purkinje cells do not fir e simple spikes, resulting in a stereotypical 609 

complex -spike -induced pause in simple spikes. We included only Purkinje cells that had a 610 

complex -spike -induced pause. 611 

We identified molecular layer interneurons by establishing monosynaptic functional inhibitory 612 

relationships between these neurons and simultaneously recorded ground -truth Purkinje cells. 613 

Therefore, our sample of molecular layer interneurons constitutes principally MLI -1’s, classified 614 

by others based on their genetic 25 and connection profiles 81 . Golgi cells were identified by their 615 

low firing rate responses, characteristic extracellular waveforms 82 , and presence within the 616 

granule cell layer. Mossy fiber inputs to the cerebellar cortex were identified based on the 617 

presence of a negative -after -wave 83 (NAW). The NAW corresponds to the postsynaptic response 618 

of granule cells within the cerebellar glomerulus 84 . We note that while a NAW is sufficient to 619 

identify mossy fibers 29 , it is possible to record from mossy fiber axons that do not show marked 620 

NAWs. To ensure that our sample contained only known mossy fibers, we excluded from 621 

analysis putative mossy fibers that lacked a NAW. Unipolar brush cells were identified based on 622 

established functional responses recorded by in vitro studies showing that unipolar brush cells 623 

elongate the timescales of discrete mossy fiber input over 10s to hundreds of milliseconds 85,86 .624 

We considered units to be unipolar brush cells if triggering their firing off simultaneously 625 

recorded mossy fiber bursts yielded temporally -elongated responses. We validated our expert -626 

labeling approach using a deep -learning classifier trained on ground -truth optogenetically -627 

identified recordings in mice 29 .628

Approach to evaluate different m odels of granule cell dynamics 713 

Given the inability to record cerebellar granule cells via extracellular recordings 29 , we sought an 714 

unbiased approach to evaluate different models of granule cell dynamics. We created 5 model 715 

granule unit basis sets, one that emulates short term synaptic plasticity and four others suggested 716 

in the literature or by reviewers (see below). For each model basis set we optimized the mapping 717 

from mossy fiber and unipolar brush cell inputs, as measured in one of the cardinal directions , to 718 

Purkinje cell outputs as measured in their preferred direction of pursuit. The logic is the same as 719 

described above: focusing on Purkinje cell responses in the preferred direction allows us to 720 

reduce the model by eliminating temporal effects of molecular layer interneuron firing. 721 

As we were chiefly interested in the characteristics of the basis sets formed by each model, the 722 

parameters of individual granule units were chosen random ly . Each free parameter of the model 723 

(described below) was drawn from a bounded uniform distribution whose hyperparameters 724 

(lower and upper bounds) were optimized to reproduce Purkinje cell firing rates. We used black -725 

box optimization (adaptive differential evolution) to identify the lower and upper bounds of the 726 

uniform distribution describing each free parameter. We minimized the mean squared error 727 

between the reconstructed and measured Purkinje cell activity across our complete sample of n = 728 

10 1 Purkinje cells . For each iteration of the optimization algorithm, we constructed a random 729 

selection of 100 granule units, with each unit's parameters drawn from their respective 730 

distributions. We used an LTP/LTD rule to determine the weight matrix between granule units 731 

and Purkinje cells (described below) and averaged the mean squared error across five replicates 732

Analysis of spike timing synchrony among mossy fibers 933 

We evaluated the millisecond -scale synchrony between pairs of simultaneously -recorded mossy 934 

fibers using techniques we developed to assay Purkinje cell simple -spike synchrony 32 . Briefly, 935 

we assayed the probability of the two mossy fibers firing in the same millisecond during a 936 

smooth pursuit trial,  𝑃 𝑟 [𝑀 𝐹 1(𝑡 ) ∩ 𝑀 𝐹 2(𝑡 )]. This raw measure of synchrony is the combination 937 

of synchrony that is expected due to temporal covariations in the firing rates of both mossy fibers 938 

as well as potential synchrony that is due to network effects independent of rate -based changes. 939 

To separate these two potential sources of millisecond -scale synchrony, we subtract from each 940 

raw measure of synchrony the expected synchrony under the null hypothesis that the two neurons 941 

are independent. We compute the null hypothesis by jittering the exact timing of each spike 942 

within spike triplets. Subtraction of the null hypothesis from the measured synchrony provides a 943 

rate -corrected measure of synchrony, equivalent to the covariance between the two neurons, as a 944 

function of time during pursuit. Deviations of the covariance curve from zero would indicate 945 

changes in synchrony that are unexpected given the instantaneous firing rates of the two neurons 946 

under study. 947
