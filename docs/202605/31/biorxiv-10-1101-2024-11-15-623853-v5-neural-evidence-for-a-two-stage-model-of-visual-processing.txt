Abstract 26 

In some forms of postdictive phenomena , later events influence the perception of earlier ones, 27 

suggesting that conscious perception may be preceded by extended periods of unconscious 28 

processing. An example is the Sequential Metacontrast (SQM) paradigm , in which vernier 29 

offsets are unconsciously integrated over several hundred milliseconds before conscious 30 

perception . Obviously, t he integrated percept can only emerge after each individual element in 31 

the stream has been processed. Thus, t he SQM provide s a unique opportunity to dissociate 32 

unconscious from conscious stages of visual processing , as these stages are well separated in 33 

time. Using EEG during the SQM, we identified two distinct stages of neural activity: an early 34 

occipital EEG activity pattern (~200 ms after the initial vernier) associated with unconscious 35 

processing, and a later centro -parietal EEG pattern (~400 -600 ms after SQM onset) associated 36 

with the integrated percept and the behavior al report . We propose that the transition between 37 

these neural patterns marks the shift from unconscious encoding of individual visual stimuli to 38 

their integrated percept .39 

Introduction 40 

Perception feels like a continuous stream : we seem to consciously experience the world at each 41 

moment of time . However, several visual phenomena challenge this idea. For instance, in a42 

classic apparent motion phenomenon , two dots of different colors are flashed at separate 43 

locations with a brief delay (Kolers & Von Grünau, 1976) . Rather than perceiving two distinct 44 

flashes one after the other , we perceive a single moving dot that changes color midway .45 

Logically, the color change can only be perceived after the second dot is presented, implying 46 

that later events influence how we perceive earlier ones. The se postdictive effects provide a 47 

unique opportunity to study the neural correlates of both conscious and unconscious processing, 48 

as well as the transitions between the two .49 

A prime tool for studying these processes is the Sequential Metacontrast (SQM) 50 

paradigm (Figure s 1A and 1 B). In this paradigm, a sequence of vertical lines is presented ,51 

creating the illusion of two diverging motion streams expanding from a central point ( Otto et 52 

al., 2009, 2010) . When one of the lines contains a horizontal vernier offset (i.e., the lower 53 

segment of the line is slightly shifted leftward or rightward relative to the upper segment) , all 54 

the other lines are perceived as having the same offset, even though they are actually straight .55 

When the stream contains two vernier s with opposite offsets, the offsets integrate : participants 56

In this study, we used electroencephalography ( EEG ) decoding to investigate the neural 85 

mechanisms involved in both the unconscious integration of vernier offsets and the subsequent 86 

emergence of a conscious percept . Our approach was guided by the following rationale : in the 87 

SQM, regardless of the number and spatiotemporal location s of the verniers, all offsets are 88 

integrated unconsciously , and only the outcome is consciously perceived (Herzog et al., 2020) .89 

Neural activity encoding information about the number or location of individual offsets should 90 

therefore reflect unconscious processing, whereas neural activity encoding the integrated 91 

outcome should reflect processes linked to the content of the conscious percept .92 

Results 93 

Unconscious feature integration within the SQM stream 94 

In the conditions with only a single vernier in the stream (V conditions ; Figure 1A and 1C ),95 

offset discrimination was above 50% (Figure 1D, left panel) . This effect was consistent 96 

regardless of where the offset appeared in the stream : whether the offset occurred in the central 97 

line or in the second or fourth flanking lines, mean accuracy remained high (70% ± 7%). Paired 98 

t-tests comparing each of these V conditions with a no vernier (NV) control condition resulted 99 

all in significant differences (all p < .001, Cohen’s d’ > 3.3; Figure 1D, left panel). 100 

In contrast, when two opposite vernier offsets (a vernier and an anti -vernier) were 101 

included in the stream (V -AV condition, Figure 1C), mean accuracy was 47% ± 9%, 102 

comparable to the NV condition (paired t -tests between the V -AV and NV conditions: all p >103 

.05; Figure 1D, right panel). Thus, conscious perception does not depend on the spatiotemporal 104 

location and number of offsets in the stream, as there is no conscious percept of the individual 105 

elements. 106

Neural correlates of unconscious processing 185 

Up to this point, we have focused on conditions featuring a single vernier, whose offset is 186 

consciously perceived throughout the entire stream. Even though the spatiotemporal location 187 

of the vernier was not conscious ly perce ived , it could still be decoded via two distinct EEG 188 

topographies. Next , we analyzed conditions involving two opposite vernier offsets, separated 189 

by varying intervals (V0 -AV2: 50 ms, V0 -AV4: 150 ms; Figure 1C), using LDA to distinguish 190 

these conditions from a condition featuring only straight lines (NV). 191 

In the V-AV conditions, behavioral performance was around 50% (Figure 1D) . As 192 

previously suggested, this may reflect either that both offsets are fully integrated, resulting in 193 

the perception of a straight line as in the NV condition (Drissi -Daoudi et al., 2019) , or that there 194 

is an equal probability that one of the two vernier offsets ”wins” the integration process in a 195 

given trial  (Menétrey et al., 2023) .196 

The decoder successfully discriminated V0 -AV2 or V0 -AV4 trials from NV trials (AUC 197 

> 0.5, one -tailed cluster -based permutation test, p < .05; Fig ure 4A). In both analyses, t he 198 

significant decoding window s (170 -1000 ms ; Figure 4B ) exhibited a similar sequence of EEG 199 

activation patterns (Figure 4C) , with topographies (Figure 4 D) closely resembling those 200 

observed in the V0 vs. NV decoding analysis (Figure 3B) . Similarly, t he significant decoding 201 

onset s and the transition dynamics around 370 ms (Figures 4 B and 4E ) closely mirrored those 202 

found in V0 vs. NV conditions (Figure s 2B and 3C for comparison) . The effect was also robust 203 

across participants (positive correlation with the occipital topography within the time window 204 

associated with the first cluster: 18/18 participants in V0 -AV2 vs. NV and 14/18 in V0 -AV4 205 

vs. NV; positive correlation with the parietal topography within the time window associated 206 

with the second cluster: 16/18 participants in V0 -AV2 vs. NV and 18/18 in V0 -AV4 vs. NV). 207 

Thus, EEG activation patterns distinguished the presence of two offsets from no offsets, even 208 

though the individual offsets were integrated and not perceived separately .209 

Furthermore , cross -condition decoding revealed that a decoder trained to detect the 210 

vernier in the V0 condition (vs. NV) still successfully decoded , with similar onset and temporal 211 

dynamics, V0 -AV2 or V0 -AV4 conditions from NV condition (AUC > 0.5, one -tailed cluster -212 

based permutation test, p < .05 , Figure 4 F), indicating shared neural representation s between 213 

conditions with a single vernier and two opposite vernier offsets. 214

occurred at different times (V0 -AV2 vs. V0 -AV4), could be decoded from V0 trials, where 237 

only a single vernier was shown and consciously perceived (AUC > 0.5, one -tailed cluster -238 

based permutation test, p < .05; Fig ure 5A). The corresponding decoding windows (from 300 239 

ms for V0 -AV2 vs. V0, and from 340 ms for V0 -AV4 vs. V0) were compatible with the 240 

latencies observed when de coding conditions in which a single vernier was physically presented 241

reliably discriminated from each other (AUC > 0.5, one -tailed cluster -based permutation test, 243 

p < .05; Figure 5A). That is, EEG activation patterns contained information about the distinct 244 

spatiotemporal location of the second vernier, which was decodable starting from 420 ms 245 

onward . These results suggest that neural representations of the second vernier persisted despite 246 

the second offset not being typically consciously perceived as a distinct event at its specific 247 

spatiotemporal location (Otto, 2006; Drissi -Daoudi et al., 2019) .248 

The average activation pattern contributing to these decoding results (Figure 5B) 249 

resembled the occipital topography identified when contrasting conditions with one or two 250 

offsets from the condition without any offset (Figures 3B and 4D). This topography dominated 251 

and remained stable throughout the significant decoding windows and across the three analyses 252 

(V0 vs. V -AV and V0 -AV2 vs. V0 -AV4; see Figure 5B, effect size plot s). To further validate 253 

this finding, we correlated the activation patterns obtained here with the two prototypical 254 

topographies derived from averaging across all V or V -AV vs. NV decoding analyses (Figures 255

Neural correlates of the integrated percept 296 

To directly assess the involvement of the parietal topography in conscious processing , we 297 

applied LDA to decode correct vs. incorrect reports of the offset direction in the condition s298 

featuring only a single vernier (V conditions). As shown in Figure 6, the classifiers successfully 299 

decoded the performance in all single -vernier conditions (Figure 6A; AUC > 0.5, one -tailed 300 

cluster -based permutation test, p < .05). However, significant decoding emerged at later 301 

latencies —480 ms for V0, 460 ms for V2, and 570 ms for V4 — compared to the earlier latencies 302 

observed when decoding V or V -AV conditions against the NV condition. 303 

In addition, t he topographies obtained by averaging the main activation patterns across 304 

the entire significant decoding window ( Figure 6B) were clearly distinct from those identified 305 

in the previous decoding analyses (V0 vs. V -AV and V0 -AV2 vs. V0 -AV4; Figure 5B). 306 

Correlations with prototypical activation patterns confirmed that decoding of the correctly 307 

reported direction was driven by a single EEG activation pattern (Figure 6C) , which in this case 308 

aligned with the parietal topography identified when decoding conditions with one or two 309 

offsets from trials without any offset (Figures 3B and 4D). This topography exhibited latency 310 

shifts as a function of the vernier’s temporal position within the stream and appeared to persist 311 

longer when the vernier occurred later in time (see also Supplementary Figure 3 for analogous 312 

effects in V –AV conditions, depending on whether the first or second vernier dominated the 313 

integrated percept). 314 

Thus , th e later decoding of correct versus incorrect responses —compared to the earlier 315

Discussion 339 

Previous studies have proposed that in the SQM , and in related postdictive phenomena ,340 

conscious perception is preceded by a period of unconscious processing (Choi & Scholl, 2006; 341 

Wu et al., 2009; Elliott & Giersch, 2016; Sergent, 2018; Keuninckx & Cleeremans, 2021; 342 

Herzog et al., 2016, 2020) . In the SQM, a sequence of lines and vernier offsets is unconsciously 343 

integrated for up to 450 milliseconds before a conscious percept emerges (Drissi -Daoudi et al., 344 

2019; Vogelsang et al., 2023) . Here , we used time -resolved decoding analysis to identify EEG 345 

markers of this unconscious integration stage and to track the transition toward the final 346 

integrated percept .347 

We found that each vernier offset in the stream could be decoded from EEG activity 348 

time -locked to the moment it was shown, with decoding emerging around 200 ms later (Figure 349 

2). This was the case even though the consciously perceived vernier offset does not depend on 350 

the number or specific spatiotemporal location of the verniers within the SQM stream. 351 

Importantly, individual verniers remained decodable even when two opposite verniers were 352 

presented and their offsets were integrated (Figure 4). These results indicate that, even when 353 

individual vernier offsets are not consciously perceived, their presence and temporal position 354 

can still be inferred from neural activity. 355 

The decoding of individual vernier offsets was driven predominantly by occipital 356 

activity (see Figure s 3 and 4 ), with a topography resembling the N170/VAN ERP component 357 

(see also Supplementary Figure 4 for complementary ERP analyses) . Although this component 358 

has often been proposed as an early correlate of conscious perception (Ojanen et al., 2003; 359 

Koivisto & Revonsuo, 2010; Railo et al., 2011; Dembski et al., 2021) , several studies have 360 

related it to response to unseen stimuli (Sterzer et al., 2009; Harris et al., 2013; Cohen et al., 361 

2024) . Our results suggest that the N170/VAN may reflect an unconscious rather than a 362 

conscious processing stage, consistent with studies linking th is component to the unconscious 363 

accumulation and integration of visual information (Fahrenfort et al., 2017; Noorman et al., 364 

2025) .365 

The N170/VAN -like component was also observed when decoding between conditions 366 

involving one or two verniers (Figure 5) , even though both conditions produced the same 367 

integrated percept , namely, a single vernier offset perceived throughout the stream. Because 368 

these conditions differed in the location or number of physical offsets, this finding suggests that 369 

the topography reflects differences in the sequence of unconsciously processed events, 370 

supporting its role as a neural correlate of unconscious integration. It is also worth noting that 371

The P300 is often described as a neural correlate of conscious access (Sergent et al., 388 

2005; Del Cul et al., 2007; Kouider et al., 2013; Weaver et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2019; Noorman 389 

et al., 2025) and ha s been linked to late -access models of consciousness , where conscious 390

2022) . However, recent studies suggest that, rather than reflecting the neural correlates of 393 

conscious perception, the P300 -like component may index post -perceptual processing stages, 394 

such as decision -making and response -related activity, because it is often absent in no -report 395 

paradigms or for task -irrelevant but consciously perceived stimuli  (Pitts et al., 2014; 396 

Schlossmacher et al., 2020; Cohen et al., 2020; Sergent et al., 2021) . In our task, a direct motor 397 

contribution can be ruled out, as responses occurred much later, around 900 ms . Nevertheless, 398 

a post -perceptual interpretation remains possible, and future studies incorporating no -report 399 

paradigms will be important to further clarify the role of this component. 400 

Here, we suggest that the critical shift from unconscious processing to the unified 401 

integrated percept is not reflected in the P300 -like component itself, but rather in the transition 402 

from the occipital to the parietal topography. This interpretation is further supported by the 403 

close temporal alignment between this transition (Figures 3 and 4) and the window of 404

of a vernier offset initiates an unconscious processing window, while subsequent offsets or the 431 

termination of the SQM stream may accelerate the collapsing of the sequence into a unified 432 

conscious percept (Wolff & Akyürek, 2026) . Although the exact nature of these non -linearities 433 

remain s unclear, our results indicate that the temporal dynamics of unconscious and conscious 434 

processing stages are flexible and may be influenced by subtle details of the stimulus sequence, 435 

in contrast to standard ERP analyses, which often treat processing latencies as fixed. 436

In general, the fact that neural activity is time -locked to a stimulus (see Figure 2) does 437 

not imply that conscious perception occurs at that moment (the ‘vehicle problem’, Dennett & 438 

Kinsbourne, 1992; Herzog et al., 2020; Dainton, 2023; White, 2023) , just as a neuron that 439 

encodes “red” is not itself red. This contrasts with theories assuming a close mapping between 440 

stimulus timing and the moment of perception (e.g., the ‘brain -time’ view, Holcombe, 2014) 441 

and implies that neither the transition between the two topographies nor the duration of the 442 

parietal topography necessarily reflects neural events involved in conscious perception itself. 443 

Instead, these neural signatures may act as precursors to consciousness or, as mentioned, reflect 444 

post -perceptual processes operating on the content of the percept. Nevertheless, this transition 445 

marks the shift from the unconscious encoding of the physical, retinotopic properties of the 446 

vernier offset —which are not perceive d consciously —to the integrated representation of the 447 

stream, which forms the basis of conscious perception in the SQM . Thus, while the exact timing 448 

of conscious perception cannot be inferred directly from these topographies, they reveal the 449 

sequential neural processes that enable the emergence of a coherent percept. 450 

In line with this, throughout the manuscript we use the term s conscious processing and 451 

conscious perception to refer to the stage in which an integrated percept of the entire SQM 452 

stream has emerged. Th ese term s are used in contrast to the unconscious processing of 453 

individual offsets in the SQM, which participants cannot perceive individually. We must 454 

clarify, however, that by these term s we do not intend to imply or distinguish between 455 

phenomenal consciousness (i.e., the felt quality of a conscious mental state) and access 456 

consciousness (i.e., the functions enabled by conscious processing, such as behavioral reports) ,457 

as our paradigm is not designed to address this distinction (Block, 2007; Michel & Doerig, 458 

2022) . As mentioned, we use the term s broadly to include also post -perceptual, decision -459 

making processes associated with the emergence of an integrated percept. 460 

The presence of two distinct topographies, and their temporal dynamics, is compatible 461 

with a recently proposed two -stage model of perception in which a prolonged period of 462 

unconscious processing precedes the emergence of a unified conscious percept (Herzog et al., 463 

2016, 2020) . In this framework, the first stage involves the precise encoding of each vernier 464 

offset and its temporal position, with this information maintained in a high -capacity buffer 465 

where integration operates unconsciously. This stage aligns with the independent neural 466 

encoding of individual verniers observed in our decoding results and reflected in the occipital 467 

topography (Figures 3, 4 and 5 ). In the second stage, the unconscious processing window 468 

enables a form of sense -making: the brain resolves the buffered information into the most 469

duration, speed, and direction (Herzog et al., 2016) . Such dynamics do not contradict the 476

& Öğmen, 2007) . Detection, object recognition, and even action can unfold during unconscious 478

Mitroff & Scholl, 2005; Van Gaal et al., 2012; but see also Biderman & Mudrik, 2018; Mudrik 480

While many previous findings support the long -lasting unconscious integration of visual 482

integration may indeed vary across trials, with fluctuations in the relative dominance of the first 491 

or second vernier. Importantly, regardless of whether integration is uniform or non -uniform, 492 

our results show that the spatiotemporal location of each individual vernier, although not 493 

consciously accessible as a separate event because all elements are integrated within the SQM 494 

stream, can nevertheless be decoded from a specific occipital topography. Considering the role 495 

of pre -stimulus oscillatory activity, future studies could further investigate the contribution of 496 

specific frequency bands to the unconscious and conscious stages involved in SQM processing, 497 

as the present analyses focused exclusively on ERP -based decoding. 498

Stimuli and experimental procedure 527

and a lower segment (the length of each segment is 26. 6 arcmin, with a width of 1.2 arcmin) 531

arcmin). The central line and all subsequent pairs of lines were presented for 27.8 ms each, with 534 

an interstimulus interval (ISI) of 20.8 ms separating each presentation. The entire stimulus 535 

sequence consisted of one central and 5 flanking lines, lasting a total duration of 270.8 ms 536 

(Fig ure 1A). 537 

Participants were instructed to always covertly attend to the right stream. In a 538 

randomized order, 6 different conditions were presented (Fig ure 1C): In the no vernier control 539 

condition (NV), all lines were straight. In three experimental conditions, one line of the attended 540

Statistical evaluation of decoding analyses 674 

For all decoding analyses, either with temporal or cross -condition generalization, the statistical 675 

evaluation of the 2D matrices of cross -temporal decoding results ( participants x time x time) 676 

employed cluster -based permutation approaches and surrogate analysis, following established 677 

methodologies (Nichols & Holmes, 2002; Maris & Oostenveld, 2007; Kayser et al., 2016) .678 

Clusters were defined as consecutive time points where the decoding successful ly exceeded 679 

chance levels (chance = 0.5, paired t -test with α = 0.05). The cumulative t -values within each 680 

cluster were then compared to the maximum sum derived from surrogate clusters (permutations 681 

= 10'000). Time points were considered statistically significant if the probability in the 682 

surrogate data was <0.05 for the corresponding clusters. 683

EEG activation patterns 685 

We estimated activation patterns from the LDA classifiers using the regression -based approach 686 

(eq. [8] in Haufe et al., 2014) . Briefly, this method applies an ordinary least squares regression 687 

to derive a pattern that, when multiplied by the discriminant signal obtained via LDA, best 688 

approximates the original EEG data used as input to the decoder (electrode x time points x 689 

pseudotrials). This approach yields a ‘forward model’ that allows for neurophysiologically 690 

interpretable patterns, addressing the known issue of non -interpretable decoder weights (Haufe 691 

et al., 2014; Grootswagers et al., 2017) .692 

We assessed the temporal dynamics of the activation patterns in the context of each 693 

decoding analysis (V0, V2, or V4 vs. NV, Fig ure 2; V0 -AV2 or V0 -AV4 vs. NV, Figure 4; V0 -694 

AV2 or V0 -AV4 vs. V0, V0 -AV2 vs. V0 -AV4, Fig ure 5; correct vs. incorrect reports in V0, 695 

V2, and V4, Fig ure 6; see also Supplementary Figure 1 for V0 vs. V2 or V4, and V2 vs. V4 ). 696

of these EEG activation patterns . These activation patterns were then averaged across 701

for overall amplitude differences. Next, w e calculated pairwise dissimilarities between all time 703 

points within the significant decoding window using the Euclidean distance —a standard 704

Decoding analyses between V conditions 1058 

As supplementary analyses, we used linear discriminant analysis (LDA , see Methods ) to 1059 

discriminate between conditions presenting a single vernier (V0, V2, or V4), irrespective of 1060 

behavioral responses (i.e., whether participants correctly or incorrectly reported the vernier 1061 

offset). Although these conditions differed physically, because the single vernier appeared at 1062 

different spatiotemporal locations, they did not differ perceptually (see Otto, 2006; Drissi -1063 

Daoudi et al., 2019) : in all cases, the offset is integrated into the motion stream and can be 1064 

reported. The aim was therefore to confirm that the occipital topography, which we associated 1065 

with unconscious processing of the individual offset, contributed most strongly to decoding 1066 

performance. 1067 

The classifiers (V0 vs. V2 or V4, and V2 vs. V4) successfully discriminated between 1068 

all pairs of conditions ( Area Under the Curve, AUC > 0.5, one -tailed cluster -based permutation 1069 

test, p < .05 ; Supplementary Figure 1A). In addition, the onset and duration of significant 1070 

decoding windows varied systematically depending on the conditions being compared. When 1071 

V0 was included in the comparison, decoding began around 200 ms, similar to previous 1072 

analyses comparing V0 vs. NV (Figure 2) and V -AV conditions vs. NV (Figure 4). By contrast, 1073 

decoding onset was delayed to around 300 ms when comparing V2 and V4, resembling the 1074 

decoding latencies observed for V2 vs. NV. Finally, when V4 was one of the compared 1075 

conditions, decoding windows lasted longer, extending beyond 800 ms. 1076 

Moreover, as expected, these additional analyses confirmed the dominant contribution 1077 

of the occipital topography within the significant decoding windows (Supplementary Figure 1078 

1C), indicating that decoding was driven primarily by differences in the topography we 1079 

associate with unconscious processing. However, we also observed smaller contribution s from 1080 

the parietal topograph y (Supplementary Figure 1C) . This may reflect the fact that the decoded 1081 

conditions involved similar processes occurring at temporally shifted time points, as shown by 1082 

the cross -condition decoding (Figure 2C ) and the clustering analysis (Figure 3A ) with the V 1083 

conditions vs. NV . Consequently, the EEG activation patterns underlying decoding may 1084 

represent a mixture of occipital and parietal topographies across time (Supplementary Figure 1085 

1B) . Alternatively, the later dominance of parietal topography observed in comparisons 1086 

involving V4 (Supplementary Figure 1C) may indicate that the temporal dynamics of parietal 1087 

activity vary across conditions, potentially persisting longer in the V4 condition , in line with 1088 

non -linear temporal dynamics observed across other analyses (see Discussion). 1089

Our decoding analyses showed that decoding performance was driven either by the presence of 1211 

two successive distinct maps —namely, the occipital and parietal topograph y (for V or V -AV 1212

dominated when perceptual reports differed (i.e., correct vs. incorrect reports in V conditions; 1217 

see Figure 6). 1218 

In the Discussion, these two distinct topographies are compared with well -known event -1219 

related potential (ERP) components. In particular, we relate the occipital topography to the 1220 

N170/VAN component and the parietal topography to the classic P300.  In this additional plots ,1221 

we further validate the involvement of these specific ERP components. 1222 

Condition -specific ERPs were first computed for each participant by averaging EEG 1223 

epochs across trials within each condition. A pre -stimulus baseline correction was applied using 1224 

the interval from −450 to −50 ms relative to stimulus onset. This resulted in a channels × time 1225 

ERP matrix for each participant and condition. Overall, inspection of the grand -averaged ERPs 1226 

revealed highly similar patterns across conditions (examples are shown for V0 and NV 1227 

conditions in Supplementary Figure 4A). 1228 

To isolate condition -specific activity, differential ERPs were computed by subtracting 1229 

the NV condition from each experimental condition (Supplementary Figure 4B). Difference 1230 

maps were then normalized by the across -subject standard deviation to estimate effect sizes. 1231 

The results revealed strongest effects at different latencies depending on condition, with 1232 

differences emerging later in V2 and V4, consistent with the decoding onset reported in Figure 1233 

2B. Moreover, the strongest effects aligned with the two temporal windows identified by the 1234
